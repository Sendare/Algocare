Causes of Emergencies

Introduction and Classification of Emergency Causes

An emergency in nursing practice is any sudden, unexpected health crisis that threatens life, limb, or organ function, requiring immediate intervention. Understanding why emergencies happen helps nurses anticipate complications, prepare equipment, and act quickly before a patient deteriorates.

  • Medical Emergencies: Arise from internal disease processes, metabolic failures, or organ system breakdown. Common examples include acute myocardial infarction, severe asthma attacks, diabetic ketoacidosis, and massive stroke.
  • Traumatic Emergencies: Result from external physical forces applied to the body, causing structural damage. Examples include road traffic accidents, deep stab wounds, severe burns, and falls from a height.

A common mistake in busy clinical settings is treating every rapid deterioration purely as a medical issue without checking for hidden trauma, such as a missed internal bleed following a fall.

Environmental and Community-Level Triggers

Emergencies frequently originate outside the hospital walls, driven by environmental factors and community hazards. Nurses must recognize these triggers because they often present as mass casualty incidents or simultaneous admissions of multiple patients with identical symptoms.

  • Extreme Weather: Floods, severe heatwaves, and windstorms cause injuries, displace communities, and disrupt safe drinking water supplies.
  • Biological Hazards: Outbreaks of infectious diseases like cholera, Lassa fever, or meningitis can overwhelm local healthcare facilities very quickly.
  • Chemical Exposures: Agricultural pesticide poisoning, industrial chemical leaks, and fumes from poorly ventilated generators cause acute respiratory and neurological collapse.

When multiple patients arrive from the same community showing sudden breathing difficulties, nurses should immediately suspect a shared environmental toxin or gas exposure rather than isolated individual illnesses.

Accidental and Man-Made Crises

Human activity, mechanical failures, and safety lapses account for a vast number of acute hospital admissions. Recognizing the mechanism of these accidents guides the immediate resuscitation priorities.

  • Road Traffic Accidents (RTAs): The leading cause of severe polytrauma, head injuries, and long bone fractures on Nigerian highways due to speeding, bad roads, and vehicle maintenance failures.
  • Domestic Accidents: Severe burns from cooking gas explosions or kerosene adulteration, accidental poisoning from household chemicals stored in beverage bottles, and electrocution from faulty wiring.
  • Occupational Incidents: Falls from construction sites and machinery crush injuries resulting from inadequate safety gear.

Underlying Pathophysiological Triggers

Every clinical emergency shares common physiological pathways that lead to cellular death if left unchecked. Understanding these mechanisms bridges the gap between the primary cause and the outward symptoms.

  • Hypoxia: A lack of oxygen at the cellular level, caused by airway obstruction, severe asthma, or cardiac arrest, leading rapidly to brain damage.
  • Circulatory Shock: A failure of the cardiovascular system to perfusion vital organs with oxygenated blood, resulting from heavy bleeding, severe dehydration, or overwhelming infection (sepsis).
  • Metabolic Acidosis: A dangerous buildup of acid in the blood when the body breaks down fats instead of glucose, commonly seen in uncontrolled diabetes.

Treating the symptom alone, such as giving pain relief without restoring blood pressure in shock, will inevitably lead to treatment failure.

Vulnerable Populations and Contextual Risk Factors

Certain groups in the Nigerian community face a higher risk of experiencing acute emergencies due to biological, socioeconomic, and environmental vulnerabilities.

  • Children Under Five: Highly susceptible to severe dehydration from diarrheal diseases, acute respiratory infections, and severe malaria leading to profound anemia and convulsions.
  • Pregnant Women: At high risk for sudden hemorrhage, hypertensive disorders like eclampsia, and obstructed labor.
  • The Elderly: Prone to falls resulting in hip fractures, strokes, and sudden cardiac events due to underlying chronic illnesses.
  • Low-Income Communities: Face delays in reaching healthcare facilities due to transportation barriers, financial constraints, and out-of-pocket payment models for emergency care.

Rapid Assessment and Etiology Identification

When an emergency patient arrives, the nurse must quickly determine the underlying cause using systematic assessment frameworks. The primary survey follows the ABCDE approach: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure.

  • Airway: Check for obstruction from the tongue, blood, or foreign bodies.
  • Breathing: Assess rate, depth, and symmetry of chest movement.
  • Circulation: Evaluate pulse strength, capillary refill time, and active bleeding.
  • Disability: Check level of consciousness using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale.
  • Exposure: Undress the patient briefly to inspect for hidden injuries, rashes, or trauma.

Once immediate life threats are stabilized, the secondary survey involves taking a focused history from the patient or relatives using the SAMPLE mnemonic: Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, and Events leading to the incident.

Initial Nursing Management and Stabilization

Immediate nursing care focuses on reversing the underlying threat and supporting failing organ systems before definitive medical treatment begins.

  • Restore Oxygenation: Administer high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask if hypoxia is present.
  • Establish Access: Insert large-bore intravenous catheters (gauge 16 or 18) quickly to allow rapid fluid resuscitation or emergency drug administration.
  • Control Bleeding: Apply direct manual pressure and compression bandages to external hemorrhage sites.
  • Positioning: Place an unconscious, breathing patient in the recovery position to prevent airway obstruction, or elevate the legs for a patient showing early signs of shock.
