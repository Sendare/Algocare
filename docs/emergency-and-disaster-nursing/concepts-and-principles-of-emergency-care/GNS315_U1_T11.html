Global Burden of Disasters

Introduction to Disasters and Global Health

A disaster is a sudden, severe event that overwhelms local capacity and causes widespread human, material, economic, or environmental loss. In global health, we look at how these events disrupt the basic foundations of community life, from clean water systems to functioning hospitals. Disasters do not just happen overnight; they are often the collision of a natural hazard, like an earthquake or flood, with human vulnerability, such as poorly built housing or poverty. When local resources cannot cope with the scale of destruction, the event becomes a disaster requiring outside help. Nurses must understand that health impacts extend far beyond immediate physical injuries. They include long-term mental health trauma, the breakdown of chronic disease management, and the threat of infectious disease outbreaks when sanitation fails.

Natural Versus Man-Made Disasters

Disasters are broadly split into two categories based on their origin, though the line often blurs in practice.

  • Natural disasters: Events caused by natural environmental processes. Examples include earthquakes, hurricanes, tsunamis, volcanic eruptions, and severe droughts.
  • Man-Made (Technological or Anthropogenic) disasters: Events caused by human intent, error, or structural failures. Examples include industrial chemical spills, nuclear accidents, major transport crashes, and armed conflicts.

While natural hazards are difficult to prevent, their impact is heavily influenced by human choices, such as deforesting hillsides or building on floodplains. Conversely, man-made disasters are preventable through strict safety regulations, proper maintenance, and peaceful conflict resolution.

Direct and Indirect Health Impacts

The toll of a disaster on human health is measured in two distinct ways.

  • Direct impacts: Immediate physical trauma such as crush injuries from collapsed buildings, lacerations, burns, and drowning.
  • Indirect impacts: Secondary health crises that emerge days or weeks later, including waterborne disease outbreaks, severe psychological distress, malnutrition, and the rapid deterioration of chronic conditions due to missed medications.

In many major emergencies, indirect causes claim more lives than the initial event itself. When public health infrastructure collapses, people with routine medical needs can no longer access insulin, dialysis, or emergency obstetric care.

Vulnerability Factors in Low-Resource Settings

Disasters do not affect all populations equally. Communities in low-resource settings suffer disproportionately high death tolls and slower recovery times. Several intersecting factors drive this vulnerability:

  • Poverty: Forces people to live in hazard-prone areas, such as steep hillsides or low-lying floodplains, in poorly constructed housing.
  • Lack of Infrastructure: Weak road networks delay emergency vehicle access, and fragile electrical grids cause immediate blackouts in clinics.
  • Displaced Populations: Refugees and internally displaced persons live in crowded camps with poor sanitation, raising infection risks.
  • Marginalized Groups: Women, children, the elderly, and individuals with preexisting disabilities often face barriers in receiving timely warnings or evacuation assistance.

The Role of Nurses in Disaster Response and Preparedness

Nurses form the backbone of healthcare response during emergencies, working across prevention, active response, and community recovery. Preparedness involves participating in hospital disaster drills, educating communities on emergency kits, and identifying local high-risk populations. During an active disaster, nurses triage incoming mass casualties, manage makeshift treatment areas, and provide psychological first aid. In the recovery phase, they monitor for infectious disease outbreaks, support mental health initiatives, and help rebuild primary healthcare services. Clinical judgment and flexibility are essential, as nurses often must care for many patients with minimal supplies and equipment.
