Local Burden of Disasters

Understanding Local Disasters and Community Context

A disaster is a sudden, calamitous event that seriously disrupts the functioning of a community or society, causing widespread human, material, economic, and environmental losses that exceed the affected community's ability to cope using its own resources. In the local Nigerian context, disasters can be sudden-onset emergencies like flash floods or structural fires, or slow-onset crises such as severe droughts and desertification in the northern regions. Nurses working at the primary healthcare level must understand that local disaster vulnerability is deeply tied to poverty, rapid and unplanned urbanization, and fragile infrastructure. When an emergency strikes, local health facilities are often overwhelmed within the first few hours before national or international help can arrive.

Epidemiology and Common Disasters in Nigeria

Nigeria faces a diverse and recurring profile of natural and human-made disasters. Flooding is perhaps the most widespread environmental hazard, displacing thousands of coastal and riverine residents annually during the rainy season. Communal clashes and insurgency, particularly in parts of the North-East and North-Central zones, have generated massive populations of internally displaced persons (IDPs). Epidemics of vaccine-preventable and waterborne diseases—such as cholera, Lassa fever, and measles—frequently surge in overcrowded settlement areas. Additionally, human-made disasters like petroleum pipeline explosions, heavy road traffic accidents along major commercial routes, and building collapses in densely populated cities contribute significantly to local morbidity and mortality.

Health System and Socio-Economic Impacts

The aftermath of a disaster extends far beyond immediate physical injuries. Economically, disasters destroy local markets, farmlands, and small businesses, plunging vulnerable families deeper into poverty. For the health system, facilities face physical destruction, disruption of power supplies, and critical shortages of essential medicines and safe water. Nurses frequently encounter secondary health crises, including malnutrition, severe psychological trauma, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and interruptions in chronic disease management for conditions like hypertension and diabetes.

Vulnerable Populations and Risk Factors

Disasters do not affect everyone equally; their burden falls disproportionately on socially and physically vulnerable groups.

  • Pregnant women: Face heightened risks of pregnancy complications and lack of clean delivery environments.
  • Children: Particularly under-five years, are highly susceptible to dehydration from diarrhea, severe acute malnutrition, and separation from caregivers.
  • Older adults: Often have reduced mobility, pre-existing chronic illnesses, and difficulty evacuating quickly.
  • Persons with disabilities: Frequently encounter barriers during emergency communication, physical evacuation, and accessing shelter facilities.
  • Low-income urban dwellers: Often live in informal settlements constructed in hazard-prone areas like river channels or near industrial zones.

The Role of Nurses in Disaster Risk Reduction and Response

Nurses are central to all phases of the disaster management cycle: mitigation, preparedness, response, and recovery. In the preparedness phase, nurses conduct community education on hazard awareness, participate in emergency drills, and help families create simple household disaster plans. During active response, nurses triage mass casualties, provide emergency first aid, manage emergency shelters, and monitor for infectious disease outbreaks. In post-disaster recovery, they support community rehabilitation, provide mental health counseling, and advocate for rebuilding safer, more resilient health infrastructure.

Local Emergency Management Frameworks and Challenges

In Nigeria, disaster management is coordinated at various levels by agencies such as the National Emergency Management Agency (NEMA) and State Emergency Management Agencies (SEMAs), alongside the Nigerian Red Cross Society. However, operationalizing these frameworks at the grassroots level often faces significant obstacles. Common challenges include delayed communication channels, poor inter-agency coordination, inadequate funding, poorly maintained transport fleets, and a lack of specialized training in disaster nursing for frontline health workers. Overcoming these bottlenecks requires strengthening community-based disaster resilience and ensuring active grassroots participation in local planning.
