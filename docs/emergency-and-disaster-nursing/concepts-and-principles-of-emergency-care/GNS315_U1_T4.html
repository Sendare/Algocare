Principles of Emergency and Disaster Care

Introduction to Emergency and Disaster Nursing

Emergency and disaster nursing requires rapid critical thinking, adaptability, and decisive action under intense pressure. Unlike routine ward care where timelines are measured in hours or shifts, emergency care operates on seconds and minutes. Practitioners must quickly differentiate between stable and life-threatening conditions using limited baseline information.

Disaster nursing expands this scope from individual patient management to community-level response. When local health systems are overwhelmed by floods, building collapses, or disease outbreaks, nurses coordinate mass triage, manage makeshift triage centers, and allocate scarce resources ethically. The clinical environment is inherently unpredictable, demanding strict adherence to safety protocols despite chaotic surroundings.

Core Principles and Ethics of Emergency Care

Ethical dilemmas frequently arise during high-stress medical crises. The foundational principle of beneficence drives providers to save lives, yet resource scarcity forces difficult decisions regarding distributive justice. When supplies or personnel cannot meet total demand, care must be allocated to maximize the greatest good for the greatest number of people.

Consent protocols also adapt during acute emergencies. Under the doctrine of implied consent, an unconscious or severely incapacitated patient is presumed to want life-saving intervention. Autonomy remains paramount when patients possess decision-making capacity, but preserving life supersedes complex administrative procedures when delay would cause permanent harm or death.

The Disaster Management Cycle

  • Mitigation: Long-term measures taken to prevent natural or man-made hazards from developing into disasters or to lessen their damaging effects.
  • Preparedness: Continuous planning, training, stock-piling supplies, and conducting simulations before an incident occurs.
  • Response: The immediate activation of emergency plans, search and rescue operations, triage, and direct medical treatment.
  • Recovery: Restoring infrastructure, stabilizing community health services, and addressing long-term psychological and economic rebuilding.

Triage Principles in Mass Casualty Incidents

When patient volume overwhelms available medical capacity, standard emergency department queuing fails. The START system (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) enables rapid categorization within sixty seconds per patient using physiological markers: respiration, perfusion, and mental status.

  • Red (Immediate): Life-threatening injuries with a high probability of survival if treated immediately (e.g., tension pneumothorax, major hemorrhage).
  • Yellow (Delayed): Serious injuries requiring medical care, but stable enough to wait without immediate threat to life or limb.
  • Green (Minor/Walking Wounded): Minor injuries where treatment can be delayed hours or days.
  • Black (Expectant/Deceased): Fatal injuries incompatible with life or patients with catastrophic injuries where survival is unlikely given available resources.

Rapid Primary and Secondary Patient Assessment

Emergency assessment relies on the systematic ABCDE framework to uncover and treat life threats sequentially.

  • Airway: Assess patency, clear obstructions, and apply cervical spine stabilization if trauma is suspected.
  • Breathing: Evaluate respiratory rate, depth, chest symmetry, and oxygen saturation.
  • Circulation: Check central and peripheral pulses, skin color, temperature, and capillary refill.
  • Disability: Perform a rapid neurological check using the AVPU scale (Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive) or Glasgow Coma Scale.
  • Exposure: Completely expose the patient to identify hidden injuries while preventing hypothermia.

Once immediate resuscitation needs are stabilized, the secondary survey proceeds with a focused head-to-toe physical examination and a detailed AMPLE history (Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events preceding the incident).

Emergency Resuscitation and Life Support Guidelines

Immediate intervention for cardiac or respiratory arrest follows standardized Basic Life Support (BLS) and Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) protocols. High-quality chest compressions—delivering a depth of at least 5 cm at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute—form the foundation of effective resuscitation.

Minimizing interruptions in chest compressions directly correlates with return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). When automated external defibrillators (AEDs) or manual defibrillators are available, rhythm analysis should occur with minimal delay. In Nigerian clinical practice, resource constraints require proficiency in manual bag-valve-mask ventilation and basic airway adjuncts while waiting for advanced mechanical support.

Disaster Response and Incident Command Systems

Chaos is the primary enemy during a major disaster. To maintain order, healthcare facilities and emergency services utilize the Incident Command System (ICS). ICS provides a standardized, scalable management hierarchy that integrates personnel, facilities, equipment, and communications under a unified commander.

Communication breakdown is a frequent hazard during multi-agency responses. Utilizing plain language instead of radio codes prevents misunderstandings between police, fire, and medical personnel. Nurses assigned to command roles must maintain clear lines of authority, track resource deployment accurately, and prevent rogue rescue attempts that endanger responder safety.

Post-Disaster Recovery and Psychosocial Support

Once the acute rescue phase concludes, the recovery period begins. This phase focuses on rebuilding health infrastructure, monitoring environmental sanitation, and preventing secondary disease outbreaks like cholera or malaria, which frequently follow floods and displacement.

Psychosocial support is equally critical for both survivors and healthcare workers. Acute stress reactions, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are common following catastrophic events. Nurses facilitate psychological first aid, encourage social connection, and monitor team members for burnout and compassion fatigue, ensuring long-term workforce resilience.
