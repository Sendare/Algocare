Principles of Emergency and Disaster Care

Introduction to Emergency and Disaster Nursing

Emergency and disaster nursing requires rapid critical thinking and decisive action under intense pressure. Unlike routine clinical care where patient loads are predictable, emergency nurses face sudden surges of critically ill or injured individuals with limited initial information. The primary goal is to stabilize life-threatening conditions swiftly, minimize permanent disability, and efficiently manage available resources. Nurses in this field must stay calm, adapt to chaotic environments, and collaborate seamlessly with multidisciplinary teams including paramedics, physicians, and security personnel.

Core Principles and Ethics of Emergency Care

In high-stress emergency situations, ethical dilemmas arise frequently. Nurses are bound by the principle of beneficence, striving to do what is best for the patient, alongside justice, ensuring fair allocation of care when resources are scarce. Consent laws often shift in true emergencies under the doctrine of implied consent, which assumes an unconscious or gravely incapacitated patient would want lifesaving treatment.

  • Autonomy: Respecting a competent patient's right to refuse treatment, even in urgent scenarios.
  • Non-maleficence: Doing no harm and preventing avoidable complications during hurried interventions.
  • Veracity: Communicating honestly and clearly with patients and distressed family members about procedures and risks.

The Disaster Management Cycle

Disaster management is an ongoing cycle rather than a single event response. Understanding its phases helps healthcare systems minimize loss of life and property.

  1. Mitigation: Long-term measures taken to prevent or reduce the impact of future disasters, such as building earthquake-resistant hospitals.
  2. Preparedness: Developing disaster plans, training personnel, stockpiling medical supplies, and conducting community drills.
  3. Response: The actual deployment of emergency services, medical triage, and rescue operations during or immediately after the event.
  4. Recovery: Rebuilding infrastructure, restoring health services, and addressing the long-term physical and psychological needs of the community.

Triage Principles in Mass Casualty Incidents

When patient volume overwhelms local medical resources during a mass casualty incident (MCI), standard triage shifts from doing the greatest good for the individual to doing the greatest good for the greatest number. Nurses must rapidly sort patients based on the severity of their injuries and their immediate chance of survival using structured systems like START (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment).

Rapid Primary and Secondary Patient Assessment

Emergency assessment relies on structured mnemonics to ensure life threats are found and treated instantly. The primary survey follows the ABCDE approach: Airway (with cervical spine protection), Breathing, Circulation, Disability (neurological status), and Exposure/Environment. Nurses do not move to the next step until the previous life threat is managed. Once the patient is stable, the secondary survey begins, involving a head-to-toe physical examination, detailed history using the SAMPLE mnemonic (Signs/Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events leading up to the incident), and baseline diagnostics.

Emergency Resuscitation and Life Support Guidelines

Immediate intervention during cardiac or respiratory arrest relies on established Basic Life Support (BLS) and Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) guidelines. High-quality chest compressions—pushing hard and fast with minimal interruption—form the foundation of successful resuscitation. Nurses must coordinate rhythm checks, defibrillation for shockable rhythms like ventricular fibrillation, and the administration of emergency medications such as epinephrine. Team communication using closed-loop methods prevents confusion and ensures smooth, continuous resuscitation efforts.

Disaster Response and Incident Command Systems

During large-scale disasters, normal hospital hierarchies can break down without a structured management framework. The Incident Command System (ICS) provides a standardized, scalable approach to command, control, and coordination of emergency response. It establishes clear lines of authority, assigning distinct roles such as Incident Commander, Public Information Officer, and Logistics Chief. This structure prevents duplication of effort, ensures clear communication channels, and integrates multiple agencies—such as police, fire, and health services—into a unified, efficient response.

Post-Disaster Recovery and Psychosocial Support

Once immediate physical threats subside, the focus shifts to community recovery and psychological healing. Disasters frequently trigger acute stress reactions, post-traumatic stress, and severe grief among both survivors and healthcare workers. Nurses play a crucial role in providing psychological first aid (PFA)—offering calm, compassionate presence, practical assistance, and basic emotional support without forcing trauma processing. Additionally, healthcare facilities must conduct thorough debriefings to evaluate response performance, identify systemic gaps, and improve preparedness for future events.
