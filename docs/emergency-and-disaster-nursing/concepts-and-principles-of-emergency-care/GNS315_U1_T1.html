Emergency Care Concepts and Definitions

Introduction to Emergency Nursing and Care

Emergency nursing is a specialized field focused on the immediate assessment, diagnosis, and treatment of patients experiencing acute illness or severe trauma. Unlike routine clinical settings where care is planned over days or weeks, emergency environments demand rapid decision-making under conditions of extreme uncertainty and time pressure. In many Nigerian tertiary hospitals, emergency departments (EDs) face high patient volumes, infrastructural deficits, and delayed presentations due to financial or transportation barriers. Consequently, emergency practitioners must master both advanced clinical skills and resource improvisation without compromising patient safety. The primary goal is stabilization, prevention of secondary injury, and seamless transition of the patient to intensive care, the operating theater, or discharge.

Core Concepts and Philosophy of Emergency Care

The philosophy of emergency care rests on three pillars: universal access, rapid intervention, and efficient patient flow. Universal access dictates that emergency departments must treat any presenting individual regardless of socioeconomic status, ethnicity, or ability to pay—a principle legally upheld in many jurisdictions. Rapid intervention prioritizes life-threatening conditions over non-urgent ailments through structured prioritization. Patient flow management prevents bottlenecks, ensuring that diagnostic results, consultations, and bed allocations happen swiftly. Together, these concepts form a framework that values human life above administrative delays and demands systemic resilience from all healthcare personnel.

Key Terminology and Definitions

Clear communication in emergency care relies on precise terminology. Understanding these terms prevents miscommunication during critical handovers:

  • Triage: The sorting and classification of patients based on the severity of their illness or injury to determine priority of treatment.
  • Golden Hour: The critical first sixty minutes post-trauma during which prompt surgical or medical intervention gives the highest chance of survival.
  • Acute: A condition characterized by rapid onset, severe symptoms, and a short course.
  • Stabilization: The process of bringing vital signs and physiological parameters within acceptable, non-lethal limits.
  • Moulage: The practice of applying simulated injuries for emergency drills and training scenarios.

The Emergency Triage Process

Triage is the gatekeeper of the emergency department. Because resources are finite and patient arrivals are unpredictable, trained nurses must rapidly evaluate arriving patients to assign clinical priority. Globally recognized systems like the Canadian Triage and Acuity Scale (CTAS) or the Manchester Triage System (MTS) categorize patients from immediate resuscitation to non-urgent. In resource-constrained settings, triage nurses frequently rely on rapid visual assessment, vital sign screening, and structured questioning to spot occult deterioration. Effective triage prevents crowding in treatment bays, ensures critically ill patients are seen first, and reassures waiting patients that their turn will come.

Initial Assessment and the Primary Survey

The primary survey follows the systematic ABCDE framework to identify and immediately manage life-threatening conditions. Speed is paramount; any compromise discovered in one step must be addressed before moving to the next.

StepFocusNursing Action
AAirwayCheck patency, clear secretions, insert adjuncts if needed, maintain cervical spine precautions.
BBreathingAssess rate, depth, chest rise, breath sounds, and administer high-flow oxygen.
CCirculationCheck pulses, capillary refill, skin color, control major external hemorrhage, establish large-bore IV lines.
DDisabilityEvaluate neurological status using AVPU (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or Glasgow Coma Scale, check pupils.
EExposureExpose the patient fully to find hidden injuries while preventing hypothermia with warm blankets.

Secondary Survey and Comprehensive Evaluation

Once immediate life threats are controlled during the primary survey, the nurse conducts a secondary survey. This phase involves a head-to-toe physical examination, detailed history taking, and review of diagnostic investigations. The nurse gathers the AMPLE history: Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, and Events preceding the incident. Head-to-toe inspection checks for lacerations, deformities, swelling, and tenderness across every anatomical region. Vital signs are reassessed, and baseline laboratory or imaging requests are processed. Missing this systematic check can cause occult fractures, internal bleeding, or closed head injuries to go unnoticed until irreversible damage occurs.

Legal and Ethical Principles in Emergency Care

Emergency nursing operates under intense legal and ethical scrutiny. The doctrine of implied consent assumes that an unconscious or incapacitated patient would consent to life-saving emergency treatment if they were able. Conversely, a competent adult retains the right to refuse care, presenting a complex ethical dilemma when refusal threatens life. Nurses must also navigate documentation accuracy, maintaining patient confidentiality while cooperating with legal authorities in cases of trauma, gunshot wounds, or suspected assault. In many Nigerian clinical environments, police reports are historically demanded before treating gunshot victims; however, current legislative frameworks and professional ethics mandate that saving life supersedes administrative or law enforcement delays.
