Emergency Assessment

Introduction to Emergency Assessment in Nursing

Emergency assessment is a rapid, structured evaluation designed to identify and manage life-threatening conditions immediately. Unlike routine clinical evaluations that follow a leisurely pace, emergency nursing requires a high-stakes, fast-paced mindset. When a patient arrives in critical distress, nurses must quickly filter out non-essential data and focus entirely on immediate survival threats.

The core philosophy of emergency assessment relies on prioritizing interventions before a formal diagnosis is even made. Stabilization always comes before investigation. Nurses use swift sensory cues—listening for stridor, looking for cyanosis, feeling for weak pulses—to form a rapid mental picture of clinical instability.

The Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The primary survey is the universal starting point for every emergency patient. It uses the ABCDE approach to systematically catch and treat life threats in order of urgency. If a problem is found at any step, the nurse must address it immediately before moving down the line.

  • Airway: Check if the airway is open and clear. Look for tongue obstruction, foreign bodies, or secretions.
  • Breathing: Assess chest rise, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation. Ensure adequate gas exchange is occurring.
  • Circulation: Check central pulses, capillary refill, skin color, and control any major bleeding.
  • Disability: Perform a rapid neurological check using AVPU (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or the Glasgow Coma Scale.
  • Exposure: Briefly expose the body to look for hidden injuries while protecting the patient from hypothermia.

Resuscitation and Immediate Life-Saving Interventions

When the primary survey reveals an immediate danger, the nurse transitions straight into resuscitation. This phase is dynamic and hands-on. Actions happen simultaneously with assessment rather than waiting for a doctor's order in many emergency protocols.

Common immediate interventions include clearing a blocked airway with suction, applying high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask, initiating chest compressions, or applying direct pressure to massive hemorrhages. Every second delayed during this phase drastically increases mortality risk for unstable patients.

The Secondary Survey: Comprehensive Head-to-Toe Assessment

Once the patient is stable and life threats from the primary survey are managed, the nurse begins the secondary survey. This is a methodical head-to-toe physical examination designed to catch every other injury or illness.

The nurse inspects, palpates, auscultates, and checks every body region from the scalp down to the toes. This includes checking pupil reactivity, listening to breath sounds in all lung fields, feeling the abdomen for tenderness or rigidity, and inspecting extremities for deformities or pulses. A missed injury here can easily turn into a severe complication later.

Obtaining a Focused Emergency History (SAMPLE & OPQRST)

Gathering patient history in an emergency must be quick and targeted. Nurses use two popular mnemonic tools: SAMPLE for general background and OPQRST for specific pain complaints.

  • SAMPLE: Signs/Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events leading up to the incident.
  • OPQRST: Onset, Provocation/Palliation, Quality, Region/Radiation, Severity, Time.

Family members or bystanders are often vital sources of this information when the patient is confused, in shock, or unconscious.

Triage Principles in Emergency Care Settings

Triage is the process of sorting patients based on the severity of their illness or injury to ensure that those with the highest risk of death are treated first. In busy emergency departments or mass casualty events, nurses cannot simply see people on a first-come, first-served basis.

Using systems like the Emergency Severity Index (ESI), nurses evaluate vital signs, expected resource needs, and stability. A patient with chest pain and dropping blood pressure goes straight to the front, while a patient with a minor sprained wrist waits safely until resources permit care.

Continuous Monitoring and Reassessment

An emergency assessment is never a one-time event; it is a continuous loop. Clinical conditions can change within seconds. A patient who looked stable five minutes ago can suddenly crash due to internal bleeding or a blocked airway.

Nurses must frequently repeat the primary survey and vital signs, especially after giving medications, performing procedures, or moving the patient. Vigilant reassessment catches subtle downward trends before they become full-blown cardiac or respiratory arrests.
