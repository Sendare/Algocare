Emergency Assessment

Introduction to Emergency Assessment in Nursing

Emergency assessment requires a rapid, systematic approach to identify and manage immediately life-threatening conditions. Unlike routine clinical evaluations where a comprehensive history comes first, acute care prioritizes immediate stabilization. The primary goal is finding physiological instability before it causes irreversible damage or cardiac arrest. In busy Nigerian emergency departments, where patient loads often outstrip staff numbers, this structured framework prevents subtle deteriorations from being missed amidst the chaos.

The Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The ABCDE mnemonic guides the systematic evaluation of any critically ill or injured individual. You assess and intervene on each step sequentially before moving to the next. - **Airway**: Assess patency, clear obstructions, and consider a cervical spine adjunct if trauma is suspected. - **Breathing**: Evaluate rate, depth, symmetry of chest expansion, and oxygen saturation; administer supplemental oxygen if needed. - **Circulation**: Check pulse rate and quality, capillary refill, skin color, and control major hemorrhages. - **Disability**: Perform a quick neurological check using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or Glasgow Coma Scale. - **Exposure**: Briefly expose the patient to locate hidden injuries or rashes, then prevent hypothermia with warm blankets.

Triage Principles in Emergency Care Settings

Triage is the sorting of patients based on the severity of their illness or injury to ensure that those with the most critical needs receive care first. In high-pressure environments, effective triage prevents bottlenecks and optimizes resource allocation. - **Immediate / Red**: Life-threatening injuries or illnesses requiring instant intervention to survive. - **Delayed / Yellow**: Serious conditions requiring medical attention, but stable enough to wait a short time without immediate risk. - **Minimal / Green**: Minor injuries where treatment can be delayed significantly without worsening outcomes. - **Expectant / Black**: Catastrophic injuries where survival is unlikely even with maximal medical care.

Obtaining a Focused Emergency History (SAMPLE & OPQRST)

Once immediate life threats are managed during the primary survey, you gather targeted historical data using standard mnemonics. The **SAMPLE** history captures crucial background: Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, and Events leading up to the illness. For patients presenting with pain, the **OPQRST** framework clarifies the complaint: - **Onset**: When did the pain start and what caused it? - **Provocation**: What makes it worse or better? - **Quality**: Is it sharp, dull, crushing, or burning? - **Region/Radiation**: Where is the pain located and does it spread? - **Severity**: Rate the pain on a scale from zero to ten. - **Time**: Has the intensity changed over time?

The Secondary Survey: Comprehensive Head-to-Toe Assessment

The secondary survey begins only after the primary survey is complete, life-saving interventions are underway, and the patient is stable. This systematic head-to-toe examination uncovers non-immediate injuries or illnesses. Inspect the head and neck, listen to breath and heart sounds, palpate the abdomen for tenderness or rigidity, inspect the extremities for pulses and deformities, and inspect the posterior surfaces if spinal trauma is ruled out or cleared log-roll techniques are used.

Continuous Monitoring and Reassessment

A patient's physiological status in an emergency is dynamic. Stabilization is never a one-time event. You must continuously monitor vital signs, neurological status, and response to interventions. Reassess the primary survey whenever the patient is moved, when interventions change, or if clinical status deteriorates. Subtle changes in respiratory rate or level of consciousness often serve as the earliest warning signs of impending circulatory collapse.
