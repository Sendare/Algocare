Triage System

Introduction and Definition of Triage

Triage is the systematic process of prioritizing patients based on the severity of their condition, resource availability, and the likelihood of survival. Derived from the French verb *trier*, meaning to sort or select, the practice serves as the critical gateway to emergency departments and disaster zones alike. When patient influx overwhelms available medical personnel and equipment, indiscriminate treatment fails. Triage ensures that those with life-threatening emergencies receive immediate intervention, while individuals with minor ailments are safely directed to wait or receive alternative care. In busy Nigerian tertiary hospitals, where accident and emergency units frequently experience massive overcrowding, effective triage prevents clinical deterioration in the waiting room by identifying hidden instabilities early.

Core Principles and Objectives of Triage

The foundational philosophy of triage rests on doing the greatest good for the greatest number of people, particularly when resources are severely strained. Unlike routine clinical care, which prioritizes the individual patient currently in front of the practitioner, mass casualty triage sometimes requires withholding extensive resources from unsalvageable patients to preserve supplies for those with higher survival probabilities. - **Safety**: Protecting both incoming patients and staff from secondary hazards or violence. - **Speed**: Rapid initial assessment completed within seconds or minutes of arrival. - **Equity**: Objective, protocol-driven categorization free of bias or social standing. - **Efficiency**: Optimal utilization of available diagnostic and therapeutic interventions.

Common Triage Scales and Categorization Protocols

Standardized scales remove subjectivity from patient sorting, ensuring reliable communication among multidisciplinary teams. Different settings employ specific instruments tailored to their patient demographics. | Scale Name | Typical Tiers | Primary Focus | |---|---|---| | **Emergency Severity Index (ESI)** | 5 Levels (1 to 5) | Resource utilization and clinical urgency | | **South African Triage Scale (SATS)** | 5 Levels + Triage Early Warning Score | Vital signs and physiological parameters | | **Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment (START)** | 4 Categories (Red, Yellow, Green, Black) | Disaster field sorting based on respiration, perfusion, and mental status | The South African Triage Scale is widely adapted across various sub-Saharan African healthcare facilities because it incorporates physiological scoring, making it robust even when diagnostic technology is limited.

The Role and Responsibilities of the Triage Nurse

The triage nurse acts as the clinical gatekeeper of the healthcare facility. This position requires advanced assessment skills, sound clinical judgment, and emotional resilience. Responsibilities extend far beyond assigning a number or a color. The nurse must perform rapid yet accurate primary surveys, initiate approved standing orders for pain relief or diagnostic tests, manage the waiting room environment, and communicate effectively with anxious relatives. In busy Nigerian emergency rooms, the triage nurse frequently encounters aggressive crowds or stressed family members, demanding exceptional de-escalation skills alongside clinical competence.

Triage Assessment and Rapid Decision-Making

Decision-making at the triage desk occurs under conditions of high uncertainty and time pressure. Nurses rely on pattern recognition, clinical intuition backed by experience, and structured algorithms. The assessment begins the moment the patient enters the field of vision—noticing skin color, respiratory effort, and gait. A formal ABCDE approach (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure) is then executed swiftly. Pitfalls to avoid include premature closure, where a nurse fixes on an obvious minor injury while ignoring a subtle, life-threatening internal condition, and anchoring bias, where a patient's previous diagnosis colors the current evaluation.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Triage

Triage operates at the intersection of clinical ethics, human rights, and jurisprudence. The principle of distributive justice guides resource allocation, ensuring that care is distributed fairly without discrimination based on wealth, tribe, religion, or social status. Legally, the triage process establishes a duty of care; once a patient is assessed at the triage desk, the hospital-patient relationship is legally activated. Consequently, abandoning a patient in the waiting room without appropriate re-assessment or failing to monitor a deteriorating individual constitutes negligence. Nurses must document triage times and re-assessment intervals meticulously to protect patient safety and provide a legal record of care.
