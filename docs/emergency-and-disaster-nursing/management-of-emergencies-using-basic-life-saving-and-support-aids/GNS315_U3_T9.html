Management of Victim Enroute to Hospital

Introduction to Enroute Victim Management

Transporting a sick or injured person from an accident scene to a hospital is a high-risk phase of care. Enroute management bridges the gap between pre-hospital rescue and definitive emergency department treatment. During transit, the moving ambulance becomes a dynamic clinical environment where vibration, road noise, and limited space make standard assessments harder. The primary goal is simple: prevent further injury and keep vital organ systems functioning until medical handover. Nurses and paramedics working in mobile units must anticipate sudden clinical shifts rather than just reacting to them after they happen.

Pre-Transport Stabilization and Preparation

Moving a patient before they are stable is a common mistake that worsens internal injuries. Before the ambulance moves off, the team must secure the airway, control major external bleeding, and splint obvious fractures. Essential equipment like suction machines, oxygen tanks, and monitor leads must be tested and firmly mounted. A poorly secured oxygen cylinder can turn into a dangerous projectile if the driver has to brake suddenly. Taking two extra minutes to check connections on the ground saves lives once the vehicle is moving.

Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABC) Monitoring in Transit

Continuous tracking of ABCs forms the backbone of safe patient transport.

  • Airway: Ensure the endotracheal tube or supraglottic device remains in place. Constant vibration can easily dislodge artificial airways.
  • Breathing: Check chest rise, listen for equal breath sounds, and monitor oxygen saturation levels through pulse oximetry.
  • Circulation: Palpate peripheral pulses, check capillary refill time, and observe skin color and temperature for signs of shock.

If the oxygen saturation drops during a bumpy stretch of road, always check the physical connection of the sensor before assuming the patient's lungs are failing.

Secure Patient Positioning and Immobilization

Proper positioning protects fragile bones and prevents airway obstruction. Spinal precautions require a rigid spine board, cervical collar, and head blocks until spinal injury is ruled out clinically. For unconscious patients without trauma, the recovery position (lateral decubitus) prevents aspiration of saliva or vomit. If a patient experiences severe respiratory distress, elevating the head of the stretcher slightly helps ease breathing, provided spinal injuries are absent. Straps must be fastened securely across the chest, hips, and knees to prevent the patient from sliding during sharp turns.

Management of Equipment, Infusions, and Lines

Intravenous lines and medical devices must function reliably despite the motion of the vehicle. Gravity drips are unreliable in moving ambulances because changing road angles alter flow rates; infusion pumps should be used whenever possible. All lines must be clearly labeled and secured so they do not catch on stretcher rails or blankets. Nurses must check infusion sites regularly for swelling, which indicates infiltration caused by needle movement. Keep spare batteries handy for all powered medical devices before leaving the pickup location.

Continuous Assessment and Recognizing Clinical Deterioration

A stable vital sign reading at the accident scene does not guarantee stability ten minutes later. Nurses must look beyond monitor screens and observe the patient's face, skin color, and level of consciousness. A sudden drop in blood pressure or an unexplained rise in heart rate often signals hidden internal bleeding or worsening shock. Monitoring trends over time is far more valuable than looking at a single isolated number. Early recognition of decline allows the team to call ahead and request specialized trauma teams to stand by at the receiving hospital.

Communication and Handover Protocols with Receiving Facilities

Safe transit ends with a structured and professional handover to the hospital team. Enroute, the crew should radio ahead using the MIST format: Mechanism of injury, Injuries found, Signs and vital numbers, and Treatment given. This gives the emergency department time to prepare blood products or trauma bays. Upon arrival, give a concise verbal report directly to the receiving nurse or doctor while transferring care. Never leave the bedside until the hospital staff have assumed full responsibility for the patient's monitoring and equipment.
