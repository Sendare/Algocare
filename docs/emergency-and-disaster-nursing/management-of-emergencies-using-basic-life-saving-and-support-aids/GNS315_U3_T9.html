Management of Victim Enroute to Hospital

Introduction to Enroute Victim Management

Enroute victim management is a critical phase of emergency nursing and pre-hospital care in Nigeria, bridging the gap between the scene of an accident or medical emergency and definitive hospital care. During transit, a patient remains vulnerable to secondary injuries and physiological deterioration due to vehicle motion, environmental stressors, and the dynamic nature of their underlying condition. Nursing students must understand that effective enroute management requires anticipation, rapid critical thinking, and meticulous organization. The primary goals are to maintain physiological stability, prevent further harm, and ensure that the patient arrives at the receiving facility in the best possible condition. This phase involves continuous monitoring, timely interventions, and seamless communication with the receiving hospital. In resource-constrained settings, nurses must maximize the use of available mobile equipment while recognizing their limitations. Ultimately, the quality of care provided enroute can significantly influence patient morbidity and mortality rates, making it an indispensable component of emergency nursing practice.

Pre-Transport Stabilization and Preparation

Before an ambulance or transport vehicle moves, thorough pre-transport stabilization and preparation are vital. Rushing to transport an unstable patient without adequate preparation often leads to catastrophic outcomes enroute. The nurse must perform a rapid primary survey following the ABCDE approach to identify and correct immediately life-threatening conditions. All interventions, such as controlling major hemorrhages, securing an advanced airway if indicated, and establishing reliable intravenous access, must be completed before departure whenever possible. Equipment functionality should be meticulously checked, including suction machines, oxygen tanks with adequate pressure, and battery levels of monitors and infusion pumps. Furthermore, secure fastening of the patient on the stretcher and proper bundling of all loose items inside the vehicle prevent traumatic displacement during sudden stops or rough terrain common on Nigerian roads. Preparation also involves ensuring that all necessary documentation and medication supplies are readily accessible throughout the journey.

Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABC) Monitoring During Transit

Continuous monitoring of Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABC) forms the bedrock of safe patient transit. The nurse must maintain constant vigilance over the patient's airway patency, especially in victims with altered mental status or facial trauma, ensuring endotracheal tubes or supraglottic devices remain correctly placed and secured. Breathing is assessed by monitoring respiratory rate, chest rise and fall, oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry, and auscultating breath sounds despite ambient vehicle noise. Circulation is evaluated through regular pulse checks, capillary refill time, blood pressure measurements, and continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) rhythm monitoring where available. Engine vibrations and road bumps can sometimes cause artifacts on monitors; therefore, clinical correlation through physical palpation and observation is indispensable. Any sudden deviation in vital signs requires immediate troubleshooting of equipment, followed by targeted clinical interventions to restore optimal oxygenation and hemodynamic stability before reaching the hospital.

Secure Patient Positioning and Immobilization Techniques

Secure patient positioning and immobilization are vital to prevent secondary spinal injury, pressure ulcers, and displacement during transit. Trauma victims with suspected spinal cord injuries must remain firmly secured on a rigid spine board or vacuum mattress with appropriate cervical collars and head blocks in place. However, nurses must remain mindful of the risks of prolonged immobilization on rigid boards, such as pressure sore development and respiratory restriction, padding bony prominences adequately. For non-trauma medical or surgical patients, positioning should match their clinical requirements, such as the recovery position for unconscious patients to prevent aspiration, or semi-Fowler's position for those with respiratory distress or congestive heart failure. All patient straps across the chest, pelvis, and limbs must be fastened securely to the stretcher. Regular checks of the restraints are necessary to ensure they are not too tight, which could compromise circulation or nerve function, while still offering adequate protection against sudden vehicular deceleration.

Management of Equipment, Infusions, and Lines in Transit

Managing equipment, intravenous infusions, and medical lines during transport presents unique logistical challenges for the emergency nurse. Infusion pumps should be operated on battery power, and IV lines must be secured properly to prevent accidental dislodgement caused by patient movement or vehicle jolts. Gravity-fed infusions require careful height adjustment and observation, as vehicle acceleration and deceleration can alter flow rates. Chest tubes, urinary catheters, and nasogastric tubes must be secured to the patient's body and kept below the level of insertion if gravity drainage is required, ensuring collection bags are hooked securely to the stretcher frame. The nurse must systematically check all access points and infusion sites regularly to detect early signs of infiltration, extravasation, or disconnection. Organizing lines neatly prevents tangling and allows for rapid identification of specific medications or fluids in an emergency situation.

Continuous Assessment and Recognizing Clinical Deterioration

Continuous assessment and the ability to recognize clinical deterioration early are hallmarks of competent enroute nursing care. The physiological status of a victim can change rapidly due to internal bleeding, tension pneumothorax, or sudden neurological decline. Nurses must practice ongoing reassessment using structured formats like vital sign trends and level of consciousness checks using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale. Observing subtle signs such as increasing restlessness, cool and clammy skin, tachypnea, or a narrowing pulse pressure can indicate developing shock long before a catastrophic drop in blood pressure occurs. When deterioration is recognized, the nurse must act swiftly—administering rescue breaths, adjusting oxygen delivery, or titrating emergency medications according to established protocols while coordinating with the driver regarding transit urgency. Vigilance and proactive problem-solving prevent minor issues from escalating into cardiac arrest enroute.

Communication and Handover Protocols with Receiving Facilities

Effective communication and structured handover protocols with the receiving facility ensure continuity of care and prepare the emergency department team for the incoming patient. As the ambulance approaches the hospital, the nurse should provide a concise, pre-arrival radio or phone report using a standardized format such as MIST (Mechanism/Medical history, Injuries/Illness, Signs/Symptoms, Treatment given). This allows the receiving team to mobilize appropriate personnel, blood products, or surgical suites before the patient arrives. Upon arrival, a face-to-face verbal handover must be conducted with the receiving nurse and physician, accompanied by the transfer of all patient records, identification documents, and personal belongings. The handover should highlight baseline vitals, recent interventions, response to treatment, and any critical events that occurred during transit. A thorough and professional handover minimizes information gaps and promotes a collaborative transition of care.
