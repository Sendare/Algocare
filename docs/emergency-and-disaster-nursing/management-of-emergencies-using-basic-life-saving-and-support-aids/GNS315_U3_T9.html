Management of Victim Enroute to Hospital

Introduction to Enroute Victim Management

Enroute victim management bridges the critical gap between pre-hospital rescue and definitive hospital care. During transit, trauma or acutely ill patients remain exposed to motion, vibration, and environmental stress that can exacerbate unstable physiological states. The primary objective shifts from field triage to maintaining life-sustaining interventions while navigating transport challenges. In Nigerian emergency care settings, where transit times can be prolonged due to traffic congestion or infrastructural deficits, this phase demands high vigilance. Every movement inside a moving ambulance imposes physiological demands on the victim, making the mobile environment an extension of the resuscitation room.

Pre-Transport Stabilization and Preparation

Moving an unstable patient prematurely guarantees disaster. Before the ambulance wheels turn, essential stabilization must occur on-site. This includes securing a patent airway, controlling external hemorrhage, establishing reliable intravenous access, and ensuring fractures are splinted. Securing equipment is equally vital; loose oxygen cylinders or monitors become lethal projectiles during sudden stops or rough terrain driving. The golden rule dictates that no patient should be loaded until their immediate life-threatening conditions are addressed as far as field resources allow.

Airway, Breathing, and Circulation Monitoring During Transit

Continuous monitoring of the ABCs forms the backbone of transit care. Engine noise and vehicle vibration make auscultation difficult, requiring reliance on electronic monitors, chest rise observation, and capnography where available. Endotracheal tubes can easily dislodge from the vibrations of unpaved roads, making regular checks of tube depth and securement mandatory. Circulation checks involve monitoring peripheral pulses, capillary refill, and blood pressure trends. Any sudden drop in oxygen saturation or abrupt loss of end-tidal carbon dioxide demands an immediate safety stop to troubleshoot the airway or breathing apparatus.

Secure Patient Positioning and Immobilization Techniques

Proper positioning protects the victim from secondary injury and manages specific clinical states. Spinal immobilization using a long spine board, cervical collar, and head blocks remains standard for suspected spinal trauma. - **Recovery Position (Lateral)**: Ideal for unconscious, breathing patients to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. - **Supine with Spinal Precautions**: Necessary for polytrauma victims requiring spinal alignment. - **Semi-Fowler's Position**: Beneficial for patients with isolated respiratory distress or chest trauma without spinal injury. - **Trendelenburg Position**: Historically used for shock, though now largely discouraged as it compromises respiratory effort and increases intracranial pressure.

Management of Equipment, Infusions, and Lines in Transit

Managing intravenous lines and medical hardware inside a confined moving vehicle requires strict organization. Infusion pumps are preferred over gravity drips because vehicle motion and changing bag heights alter flow rates unpredictably. All lines must be clearly labeled, secured against accidental traction, and kept accessible for rapid flushing or medication administration. Oxygen delivery systems must be secured in dedicated brackets, with backup cylinders verified before departure. Battery-powered devices like suction units and monitors must be fully charged, as power outlets in older transport vehicles can be unreliable.

Continuous Assessment and Recognizing Clinical Deterioration

The clinical condition of an injured or ill person is dynamic and can destabilize without warning. Nurses must perform serial assessments rather than relying on a single baseline evaluation taken at the accident scene. Recognizing subtle shifts—such as a widening pulse pressure, increasing tachypnea, restlessness, or decreasing level of consciousness—allows for proactive intervention before overt arrest occurs. Frequent checks of skin color, temperature, and moisture provide rapid feedback on tissue perfusion. Documenting these trends provides the receiving trauma team with vital clues regarding the patient's physiological trajectory.

Communication and Handover Protocols with Receiving Facilities

Effective transition of care relies on structured communication between the mobile team and the receiving hospital. Advance notice allows emergency department staff to prepare blood products, surgical teams, or specialized imaging. Using standardized frameworks like the **MIST** (Mechanism, Injuries, Signs, Treatment) or **SBAR** (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) acronyms ensures concise, accurate reporting. Upon arrival, a verbal handover must occur directly to the receiving physician or senior nurse, accompanied by a completed transport documentation sheet detailing interventions performed enroute.
