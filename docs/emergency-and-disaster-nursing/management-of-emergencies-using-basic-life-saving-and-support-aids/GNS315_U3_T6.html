Mechanical Ventilation

Introduction and Indications for Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving supportive therapy that physically assists or replaces spontaneous breathing when a patient's respiratory system fails to maintain adequate gas exchange. The primary goal is to normalize arterial blood gases, rest fatigued respiratory muscles, and minimize myocardial oxygen consumption.

Indications for initiating ventilatory support generally fall into two broad physiological categories: acute respiratory failure and the need for airway protection.

  • Acute Hypoxemic Respiratory Failure: Characterized by a failure of oxygenation, often seen in conditions like severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and pulmonary edema.
  • Acute Hypercapnic Ventilatory Failure: Inability to eliminate carbon dioxide effectively, caused by neuromuscular disorders, central nervous system depression, or severe status asthmaticus.
  • Airway Protection: Required for patients with altered mental status, Glasgow Coma Scale scores below 8, or severe maxillofacial trauma where the upper airway is compromised.
  • Prophylactic Support: Utilized following extensive major surgeries or in patients experiencing profound hemodynamic instability.

Basic Physiology and Mechanics of Breathing

Normal spontaneous breathing relies on negative pressure ventilation. When the diaphragm and intercostal muscles contract, the thoracic cavity expands, creating a negative intrapleural pressure that draws air into the lungs.

Mechanical ventilators, by contrast, typically operate using positive pressure ventilation. Air is forcefully pushed into the lungs during inspiration, raising intrathoracic pressure. While effective for gas exchange, this positive pressure alters normal hemodynamics. High intrathoracic pressures can compress the vena cava, reducing venous return to the right side of the heart and potentially causing a drop in cardiac output and systemic blood pressure.

Modes of Mechanical Ventilation

Ventilator modes dictate how the machine interacts with the patient's own breathing efforts. Selecting the correct mode depends on the clinical objective, ranging from complete respiratory rest to independent breathing training.

  • Controlled Mandatory Ventilation (CMV): The ventilator delivers breaths at a set rate and volume regardless of patient effort. Rarely used today except in paralyzed or deeply sedated patients.
  • Assist-Control Ventilation (AC): Every breath is either machine-initiated or patient-triggered, but every breath delivers the full preset volume or pressure. This ensures a minimum minute ventilation.
  • Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV): Delivers preset mandatory breaths synchronized with the patient's inspiratory effort, but allows the patient to breathe spontaneously between these mandatory breaths.
  • Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV): Supports spontaneous breaths by delivering a constant pressure during inspiration, helping overcome the resistance of the endotracheal tube.

Ventilator Settings and Initial Setup

When a patient is first connected to a mechanical ventilator, specific initial parameters must be programmed based on ideal body weight, clinical diagnosis, and baseline arterial blood gas values.

  • Tidal Volume (Vt): Usually set between 6 to 8 mL/kg of ideal body weight to prevent volutrauma in conditions like ARDS.
  • Respiratory Rate (RR): Typically set between 12 to 20 breaths per minute to maintain a normal minute ventilation.
  • Fraction of Inspired Oxygen (FiO2): Initiated at 1.0 (100%) in emergency situations, then rapidly titrated down based on pulse oximetry and arterial blood gases to avoid oxygen toxicity.
  • Positive End-Expiratory Pressure (PEEP): Usually started at 5 cmH2O to prevent alveolar collapse at the end of expiration and improve functional residual capacity.

Patient Assessment and Monitoring

Continuous surveillance is mandatory for mechanically ventilated patients. The critical priority is ensuring immediate recognition of acute deterioration or equipment failure. Clinicians must monitor high-pressure and low-pressure ventilator alarms constantly, as these indicate kinks, secretions, circuit disconnection, or worsening bronchospasm.

Chest movement must be symmetrical, and breath sounds auscultated bilaterally to confirm proper endotracheal tube placement and rule out right mainstem bronchus intubation. Vital signs, pulse oximetry, and end-tidal carbon dioxide (capnography) provide real-time data regarding perfusion and ventilation efficacy. Regular arterial blood gas analysis remains the gold standard for assessing acid-base balance and oxygenation status.

Nursing Care of the Mechanically Ventilated Patient

Comprehensive nursing management minimizes complications and promotes recovery in ventilated individuals. Oral care utilizing chlorhexidine swabs is performed every two to four hours to prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). The head of the bed must remain elevated between 30 and 45 degrees at all times unless clinically contraindicated, significantly reducing aspiration risks.

Endotracheal suctioning is performed strictly as needed based on clinical findings—such as visible secretions, coughing, or sawtooth patterns on the flow scaler—rather than on a routine schedule. Saline instillation prior to suctioning is generally avoided as it pushes pathogens deeper into the lungs. Communication tools such as magic slates or picture boards are essential to reduce the profound anxiety associated with being unable to speak.

Complications and Troubleshooting in Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation carries significant risk for iatrogenic complications. Barotrauma and volutrauma occur when excessive pressures or volumes rupture alveoli, leading to pneumothorax or subcutaneous emphysema. Ventilator-associated pneumonia is another prevalent threat, caused by bacterial colonization migrating past the endotracheal tube cuff.

When troubleshooting sudden alarms or distress, clinicians apply the golden rule of emergency ventilation: if the cause cannot be identified immediately, disconnect the patient from the ventilator and manually ventilate with a bag-valve-mask resuscitator using 100% oxygen to ensure immediate patient safety.

Weaning Protocols and Extubation

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilatory support back to spontaneous breathing. The process begins once the underlying condition that necessitated ventilation has resolved and the patient demonstrates stable hemodynamics, adequate oxygenation, and an acceptable level of consciousness.

Before initiating a formal weaning trial, a Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT) is often conducted using minimal support, such as low pressure support or a T-piece. During the SBT, nurses closely monitor for signs of fatigue, tachypnea, tachycardia, or oxygen desaturation. If the patient tolerates the trial for 30 to 120 minutes, extubation is considered. Following tube removal, close observation for upper airway edema, stridor, and hoarseness remains vital.
