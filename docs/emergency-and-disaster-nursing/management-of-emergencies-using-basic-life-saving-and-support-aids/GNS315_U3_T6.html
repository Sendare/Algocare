Mechanical Ventilation

Introduction and Indications

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving intervention used to support or replace spontaneous breathing when a patient's respiratory system fails. It delivers warmed, humidified, and oxygenated air directly into the lungs using positive pressure via an artificial airway, such as an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy.

The primary goals are to maintain adequate gas exchange—delivering sufficient oxygen and removing carbon dioxide—and to reduce the physical work of breathing for an exhausted patient. Nurses must recognize the clinical triggers that mean a patient needs mechanical support before catastrophic arrest occurs.

  • Acute Respiratory Failure: Inability to maintain oxygenation or eliminate carbon dioxide, caused by conditions like severe pneumonia or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease flare-ups.
  • Airway Protection: Inability to protect the airway due to a severe head injury, drug overdose, or profound drop in level of consciousness.
  • Hemodynamic Instability: Severe shock or cardiac arrest where the body redirects oxygen away from respiratory muscles to save vital organs.
  • Prophylaxis: Anticipated prolonged surgery or severe facial trauma that threatens airway patency.

Modes of Ventilation

Ventilator modes describe how the machine interacts with the patient's breathing efforts. Selecting the right mode depends on how much respiratory drive and muscle strength the patient currently possesses.

  • Control Mode: The ventilator delivers breaths at a set rate and volume regardless of patient effort. This mode is used when the patient cannot breathe at all, such as during deep sedation or paralysis.
  • Assist-Control (AC): The ventilator delivers a set tidal volume whenever the patient initiates an inspiratory effort, but also guarantees a minimum backup rate if the patient fails to breathe.
  • Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV): The ventilator delivers a set number of mandatory breaths synchronized with patient effort, but allows the patient to breathe spontaneously between these mandatory breaths without machine assistance.
  • Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV): The ventilator provides a preset pressure boost to assist spontaneous breaths initiated entirely by the patient, overcoming the resistance of the artificial airway.

Settings and Initial Setup

Once a patient is connected to a mechanical ventilator, specific parameters must be programmed to match their physiological needs. Initial settings are usually calculated based on the patient's ideal body weight, not their actual weight, to prevent lung injury from overexpansion.

Patient Assessment and Monitoring

Continuous monitoring of a mechanically ventilated patient prevents life-threatening complications. Nurses must evaluate both the patient's clinical status and the mechanical function of the ventilator system simultaneously.

Chest movement must be symmetrical, and breath sounds should be auscultated bilaterally to confirm proper endotracheal tube placement. Nurses should routinely check the artificial airway insertion depth at the teeth or lips and ensure the securement tape or device is intact.

Alarm management is a critical nursing responsibility. High-pressure alarms typically indicate airway obstruction, secretions, coughing, or biting on the tube, while low-pressure alarms point to circuit disconnection or leaks. Never silence an alarm without first checking the patient's physical stability and connection to the machine.

Nursing Care and Maintenance

Daily nursing care for a ventilated patient focuses on infection prevention, comfort, and physical maintenance. Oral care using chlorhexidine gluconate swabs should be performed every two to four hours to reduce the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia.

Suctioning should be performed only when clinically indicated by coarse breath sounds, coughing, or visible secretions in the tube, rather than on a rigid schedule. Routine inline suctioning helps maintain a closed system and minimizes oxygen desaturation.

Maintaining the head of the bed elevated between 30 and 45 degrees prevents gastric contents from ascending and causing aspiration. Nurses must also coordinate regular sedation vacation protocols with the medical team to evaluate neurological function and readiness for weaning.

Complications and Troubleshooting

Mechanical ventilation, while life-saving, carries significant risks if not carefully managed. Ventilator-induced lung injury can occur from high pressures or excessive tidal volumes that overstretch lung tissue.

Oxygen toxicity is another concern when high FiO2 levels are maintained for prolonged periods, leading to cellular damage and free radical accumulation. Barotrauma can result in pneumothorax if high pressures rupture fragile alveoli, causing air to leak into the pleural space.

Cardiovascular compromise may also occur because positive pressure inside the chest increases intrathoracic pressure, reducing venous return to the heart and potentially causing hypotension. Nurses must monitor blood pressure closely when ventilator settings are increased.

Weaning Protocols and Extubation

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilator support back to spontaneous breathing. The process begins as soon as the underlying reason for mechanical ventilation starts to resolve.

A spontaneous breathing trial is commonly used to test the patient's readiness. During this trial, the patient breathes with minimal or no ventilator support for 30 to 120 minutes while nurses and respiratory therapists monitor for signs of distress.

  • Readiness Criteria: Stable vital signs, adequate oxygenation on low FiO2, stable mental status, and strong spontaneous cough reflex.
  • Signs of Failure: Rising heart rate, falling oxygen saturation, severe tachypnea, use of accessory breathing muscles, or diaphoresis.
  • Extubation: Once the trial is passed successfully, the artificial airway is removed, and supplemental oxygen is provided via a face mask or nasal cannula.
