Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Introduction and Principles of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation is an emergency procedure designed to manually preserve intact brain function until further measures are taken to restore spontaneous blood circulation and breathing in a person who is in cardiac arrest. It involves a combination of chest compressions and artificial ventilation. The core principle driving CPR is maintaining a critical flow of oxygenated blood to vital organs, specifically the brain and the myocardium, during the critical minutes when the heart has stopped beating effectively.

When cardiac arrest occurs, cellular hypoxia sets in rapidly, leading to irreversible brain damage within four to six minutes if perfusion is not restored. Immediate resuscitation buys time, transforming a biological death process into a reversible clinical state. Healthcare professionals in Nigerian hospitals frequently encounter delayed presentations where pre-hospital basic life support was absent; hence, initiating high-quality chest compressions upon arrival in the emergency ward remains the single most impactful intervention to alter patient survival outcomes.

Chain of Survival in Basic and Advanced Life Support

The chain of survival represents a series of critical events that, when enacted in sequence, maximize the probability of survival from cardiac arrest. Each link depends heavily on the success of the preceding one.

  • Early Recognition and Prevention: Identifying risk factors, calling for help, and preventing arrest before it occurs in vulnerable patients.
  • Immediate High-Quality CPR: Starting chest compressions instantly to generate immediate blood flow.
  • Rapid Defibrillation: Applying an automated external defibrillator or manual monitor quickly to shock shockable rhythms like ventricular fibrillation.
  • Advanced Life Support: Delivering specialized medical interventions including advanced airway placement and vasoactive medications.
  • Post-Cardiac Arrest Care: Managing targeted temperature control, hemodynamic stability, and neuro-prognostication in an intensive care setting.

Assessment of the Scene and Recognition of Cardiac Arrest

Before touching a collapsed individual, the rescuer must ensure the environment is safe from hazards such as live electrical wires, heavy traffic, or structural instability. Once safety is established, assess the victim by tapping their shoulders and shouting to check for responsiveness. Simultaneously, observe the chest for normal breathing movements for no longer than ten seconds.

A common pitfall for novice practitioners is misinterpreting agonal gasps—infrequent, abnormal, labored snorts or gasps—as normal breathing. Agonal gasps indicate cardiac arrest and demand immediate action. If the patient is unresponsive and not breathing normally, activate the emergency response system and fetch a defibrillator immediately.

High-Quality Chest Compressions Technique

Delivering effective chest compressions requires strict attention to biomechanics and physical parameters. The patient must lie flat on a firm, flat surface. Kneel beside the victim, placing the heel of one hand on the center of the lower half of the sternum, with the other hand interlocked on top. Keep your arms completely straight, positioning your shoulders directly above your hands to use your body weight.

Airway Management and Rescue Breathing

Opening the airway is essential to relieve upper airway obstruction caused by the tongue falling backward in an unconscious person. For medical patients, use the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver. If head or neck trauma is suspected, use the jaw-thrust maneuver instead to prevent cervical spine injury.

Rescue breaths provide necessary oxygen to the lungs. Using a barrier device or bag-valve-mask, deliver each breath over one second, ensuring visible chest rise. In standard adult CPR without an advanced airway, maintain a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 30 to 2. Over-ventilation must be avoided; excessive volume or rate increases intrathoracic pressure, decreases venous return to the heart, and severely compromises overall survival rates.

Application and Use of Automated External Defibrillators (AED)

An automated external defibrillator analyzes the cardiac rhythm and delivers an electrical shock to terminate malignant arrhythmias, specifically ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. As soon as an AED arrives, turn it on and follow the visual and voice prompts immediately.

Expose the victim's chest, wipe it dry if wet, and apply the adhesive pads according to the diagrams on the packaging—typically one pad on the upper right chest and the other on the lower left side. Ensure no one touches the patient while the device analyzes the rhythm or delivers a shock. Resume chest compressions immediately after the shock delivery, starting with compressions rather than checking for a pulse.

Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) Interventions and Pharmacology

Advanced life support expands on basic measures by introducing definitive airway control, continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, and intravenous or intraosseous drug therapy. Endotracheal intubation or supraglottic airway devices secure the airway, allowing continuous chest compressions without pauses for breaths at a rate of ten breaths per minute.

Pharmacology plays a central role in ACLS. Epinephrine, an alpha-adrenergic agonist, is administered every three to five minutes during cardiac arrest to vasoconstrict peripheral vessels and enhance coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure. For refractory ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia, antiarrhythmics like amiodarone or lidocaine are administered after defibrillation attempts fail.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Complications

Restoration of spontaneous circulation marks a major milestone, but patients enter a vulnerable post-cardiac arrest phase characterized by systemic ischemia-reperfusion injury. Comprehensive post-resuscitation care focuses on optimizing ventilation, maintaining mean arterial pressure, and identifying precipitating causes like myocardial infarction or electrolyte imbalances.

Common complications include rib fractures, sternal bruising, aspiration pneumonia, and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Targeted temperature management is often initiated to protect neurological function in comatose patients. Continuous hemodynamic monitoring is vital because post-arrest myocardial dysfunction is widespread.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Emergency Resuscitation

Resuscitation efforts carry profound ethical responsibilities. The fundamental legal and ethical default in emergency medicine is to presume consent for CPR unless explicit, valid documentation exists stating otherwise, such as a legally recognized do-not-resuscitate order.

Resuscitation is NOT indicated in certain scenarios, including obvious biological death, decapitation, rigor mortis, or when initiating CPR would place the rescuer's life in immediate physical danger. Nurses must understand institutional policies and local legal frameworks regarding end-of-life care, ensuring patient dignity and autonomy are respected when terminal illness makes further resuscitation futile.
