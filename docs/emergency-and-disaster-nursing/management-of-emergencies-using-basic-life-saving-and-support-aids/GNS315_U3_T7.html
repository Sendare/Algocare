Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Introduction and Principles of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR) is an essential emergency life-saving procedure performed when an individual's heart stops beating or they stop breathing effectively. The primary goal of CPR is to manually maintain a state of circulatory flow and oxygenation to vital organs, particularly the brain and heart, until advanced medical help arrives or spontaneous circulation is restored. In the context of nursing practice in Nigeria, mastering CPR is crucial for clinical competence across community health centers, primary healthcare clinics, and tertiary hospitals. The foundational principle underlying effective CPR is the restoration of artificial ventilation and chest compressions, often summarized as the ABCs of basic life support historically, though contemporary guidelines prioritize the CAB sequence (Chest compressions, Airway, Breathing) for adults to minimize delays in chest compressions. Nigerian nursing students must understand that time is of the essence; irreversible brain damage begins within four to six minutes of cardiac arrest, making prompt initiation of BLS critical for patient survival. CPR protocols require physical endurance, precise anatomical knowledge, and seamless teamwork among healthcare providers. Whether managing an in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) or an out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), nurses must act decisively, utilizing standardized algorithms taught by professional bodies such as the Resuscitation Council. Furthermore, understanding the physiology of cardiac arrest helps nursing students appreciate why high-quality compressions are non-negotiable for generating adequate cardiac output and perfusion pressure during an emergency scenario.

Chain of Survival in Basic and Advanced Life Support

The Chain of Survival represents a system of interconnected interventions designed to maximize the likelihood of survival from sudden cardiac arrest. For out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, the links include immediate recognition of cardiac arrest and activation of the emergency response system, early CPR with an emphasis on high-quality chest compressions, rapid defibrillation using an automated external defibrillator (AED), advanced life support (ALS), and integrated post-cardiac arrest care. In contrast, the in-hospital chain of survival incorporates surveillance, prevention, and treatment of at-risk patients by a rapid response team before arrest actually occurs. For Nigerian nursing students, understanding these links is vital because healthcare infrastructure can vary significantly between urban and rural settings. Early recognition and immediate community-level action form the bedrock of the chain, since response times for advanced emergency services may be prolonged in certain regions. Nurses often act as the critical link between basic life support and advanced interventions in hospital wards. Each link is dependent on the success of the preceding one; a failure in early CPR or rapid defibrillation drastically reduces overall survival rates, emphasizing that resuscitation is a team sport requiring rapid communication, resource mobilization, and strict adherence to established resuscitation algorithms to ensure optimal patient outcomes.

Assessment of the Scene and Recognition of Cardiac Arrest

Assessing the scene for safety is always the first mandatory step before approaching any potential victim of cardiac arrest. Nigerian nursing students must prioritize personal and team safety to avoid becoming secondary casualties from hazards such as live electrical wires, traffic, fire, or violent situations. Once the scene is deemed safe, the nurse evaluates the victim for responsiveness by tapping their shoulders and shouting loudly, 'Are you okay?' Concurrently, the nurse checks for normal breathing or gasping by observing the chest for movement, which should take no longer than 10 seconds. Agonal gasps are not normal breathing and must be recognized as a sign of cardiac arrest. If the victim is unresponsive and not breathing normally, the nurse must immediately shout for help, activate the emergency response system (such as calling the local hospital code team or emergency number), and retrieve an AED if one is nearby. Pulse checks should be performed simultaneously with breathing checks for healthcare providers, lasting no more than 10 seconds, typically palpating the carotid artery in adults and children, or the brachial artery in infants. Quick, accurate recognition prevents unnecessary delays in initiating life support interventions and ensures that resources are deployed efficiently within the clinical or community environment.

High-Quality Chest Compressions Technique

High-quality chest compressions are the most critical component of effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation. To perform high-quality compressions on an adult, the patient must be placed on a firm, flat surface. The rescuer kneels beside the victim, placing the heel of one hand in the center of the chest on the lower half of the sternum, with the other hand interlocked on top. The shoulders must be positioned directly over the hands with elbows locked straight, using the upper body weight to deliver effective force. For adults, the compression depth must be at least 2 inches (5 centimeters) but not greater than 2.4 inches (6 centimeters), at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Complete chest recoil must be allowed after each compression to allow the heart to refill with blood before the next stroke. Minimizing interruptions in chest compressions is paramount; pauses for airway management or pulse checks should ideally last less than 10 seconds. For nursing students practicing in Nigerian healthcare settings where mechanical devices may be scarce, physical stamina and proper biomechanics are vital to prevent rescuer fatigue, which can rapidly degrade compression depth and rate. Regular training and audit of compression technique ensure optimal coronary and cerebral perfusion during resuscitation attempts.

Airway Management and Rescue Breathing

Airway management and rescue breathing are integrated with chest compressions to maintain systemic oxygenation and eliminate carbon dioxide. Once 30 chest compressions are completed, the rescuer opens the airway using the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver, unless a cervical spine injury is suspected, in which case a jaw-thrust maneuver is indicated. During airway opening, any visible foreign bodies, vomit, or secretions should be cleared, though blind finger sweeps are strictly contraindicated as they may push obstructions deeper into the pharynx. Rescue breaths can be delivered via barrier devices, pocket masks, or a bag-valve-mask (BVM) apparatus connected to supplemental oxygen. Each breath should be delivered over approximately one second, ensuring visible chest rise. The compression-to-ventilation ratio for single rescuers in adult BLS is 30 compressions to 2 breaths. In intubated patients during advanced life support, compressions are delivered continuously at 100 to 120 per minute, while ventilations are given at a rate of one breath every 6 seconds (10 breaths per minute). Nursing students must master the seal of the BVM and avoid hyperventilation, as excessive ventilation increases intrathoracic pressure, decreases venous return to the heart, and ultimately reduces survival rates during cardiac arrest.

Application and Use of Automated External Defibrillators (AED)

An Automated External Defibrillator (AED) is a computerized medical device capable of analyzing a cardiac rhythm and delivering an electrical shock to terminate lethal arrhythmias such as ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT). AEDs are increasingly deployed in public spaces and clinical settings across Nigeria, making familiarity with their operation an essential nursing competency. As soon as an AED becomes available, the nurse should turn it on and follow the clear visual and voice prompts provided by the device. The steps involve exposing the patient's bare chest, wiping it dry if necessary, and applying adhesive electrode pads according to the diagrams on the pads (typically right upper chest and left lower lateral chest). While the AED analyzes the rhythm, the nurse must command everyone to stand clear and ensure no one is touching the patient. If a shock is advised, the nurse ensures all rescuers are clear and presses the shock button. Immediately after the shock is delivered, or if no shock is advised, high-quality chest compressions must be resumed instantly, beginning with the chest compression cycle. Defibrillation success drops rapidly with every minute of delay, highlighting the importance of rapid AED deployment alongside high-quality CPR.

Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) Interventions and Pharmacology

Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) builds upon basic life support by introducing advanced airway placement, invasive monitoring, electrical therapy, and targeted emergency pharmacology. In Nigerian hospital settings, ACLS is typically managed by an interprofessional team including doctors, specialized nurses, and anesthetists. Key pharmacological agents include Epinephrine, which is administered every 3 to 5 minutes during cardiac arrest to promote vasoconstriction via alpha-adrenergic effects, thereby increasing coronary and cerebral perfusion pressures. Antiarrhythmics such as Amiodarone or Lidocaine are utilized for refractory ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia after initial shocks and epinephrine. Reversible causes of cardiac arrest, known commonly as the 'H's and T's' (Hypovolemia, Hypoxia, Hydrogen ion/acidosis, Hypo-/Hyperkalemia, Hypothermia, Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade-cardiac, Toxins, Thrombosis-pulmonary, Thrombosis-coronary), must be systematically investigated and treated. Advanced airway management often involves endotracheal intubation or supraglottic airway insertion by trained practitioners, allowing continuous compressions paired with asynchronous ventilations. Nursing students must understand drug dosages, IV/IO access routes, and rhythm interpretation on cardiac monitors to effectively participate in code blue emergencies.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Complications

Post-resuscitation care begins immediately after the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) is achieved. The goals of post-resuscitation care are to optimize cardiopulmonary function, transport or admit the patient to an intensive care unit (ICU), identify and treat the precipitating cause of arrest, and manage targeted temperature management (TTM) to preserve neurological function. Nigerian nurses play a crucial role in monitoring vital signs, maintaining adequate blood pressure, ensuring normoxemia and normocapnia, and monitoring for common post-resuscitation complications. These complications include myocardial dysfunction, systemic ischemia/reperfusion injury, neurological deficits, acute kidney injury, and rib fractures or pneumothorax resulting from aggressive chest compressions. Hemodynamic instability frequently requires vasoactive infusions such as dopamine or norepinephrine. Continuous neurological assessment is imperative because anoxic-ischemic encephalopathy is a major cause of morbidity and mortality following cardiac arrest. Comprehensive nursing documentation, fluid balance management, and vigilant monitoring of laboratory parameters (such as electrolytes and arterial blood gases) are vital competencies for nurses working in post-resuscitation recovery areas.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Emergency Resuscitation

Emergency resuscitation involves complex ethical and legal considerations that every nursing student in Nigeria must navigate with professional judgment and adherence to local laws and professional codes of ethics. A fundamental ethical principle is the duty to rescue and preserve life, which obligates healthcare providers to initiate CPR unless valid, legally binding documentation such as a Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) order exists. In many Nigerian clinical environments, formal advance directives are rare, making communication with patients and families regarding prognosis critically important when applicable. Nurses must also recognize the concept of futility; resuscitation efforts are medically futile when there is no reasonable expectation of restoring meaningful life, such as in cases of rigor mortis, decapitation, or dependent lividity. Cultural and religious beliefs prevalent in Nigeria heavily influence perceptions of death, dying, and resuscitation, requiring nurses to demonstrate cultural sensitivity while fulfilling their professional duties. Legally, nurses are protected under Good Samaritan principles or professional scope of practice guidelines when acting in good faith during an emergency, provided they practice within their recognized level of training and competence. Understanding these boundaries ensures that patient dignity is maintained while upholding legal standards of care.
