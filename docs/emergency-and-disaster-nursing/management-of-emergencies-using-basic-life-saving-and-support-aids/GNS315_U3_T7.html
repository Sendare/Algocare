Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Introduction and Principles of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation is an emergency life-saving procedure performed when the heart stops beating effectively. Without immediate intervention, brain damage and death occur within minutes because oxygenated blood stops circulating. The core principle of CPR is to manually maintain a minimal flow of blood to the brain and heart until advanced medical help arrives or normal heart rhythm returns. High-quality chest compressions artificially pump blood through the body, acting as a substitute for the heart. Every second counts during a cardiac emergency, making prompt recognition and action the foundation of survival.

The Chain of Survival

The chain of survival represents a sequence of coordinated events that give a person in cardiac arrest the highest chance of survival. A break in any link of this chain reduces the likelihood of a positive outcome.

  • Early Recognition and Call for Help: Spotting danger and summoning emergency responders immediately.
  • Early CPR: Starting chest compressions quickly to buy vital time.
  • Early Defibrillation: Using an automated external defibrillator to shock a shockable heart rhythm.
  • Advanced Life Support: Providing specialized clinical care by trained medical personnel.
  • Post-Resuscitation Care: Managing the patient in a hospital setting to restore full neurological function.

Assessment of the Scene and Recognition of Cardiac Arrest

Before touching a fallen patient, the rescuer must ensure the environment is safe from hazards like fire, traffic, or live electrical wires. Once safety is confirmed, approach the patient and check for responsiveness by tapping their shoulder and shouting loudly. Simultaneously, look at the chest for normal breathing movements for no longer than ten seconds. Gasping or irregular, snorting breaths are not normal breathing; they indicate agonal respirations, which signify cardiac arrest. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally, activate the emergency response system right away.

High-Quality Chest Compressions Technique

Effective chest compressions are the most critical component of basic life support. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the patient's chest on the lower half of the sternum, placing your other hand on top with fingers interlocked. Position your shoulders directly over your hands and keep your elbows completely straight. Push hard and push fast, compressing at a depth of at least two inches for adults while allowing complete chest recoil between each push. Maintain a rate of one hundred to one hundred twenty compressions per minute and minimize interruptions to keep blood flowing.

Airway Management and Rescue Breathing

After delivering a set of chest compressions, open the airway using the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver. If head or neck trauma is suspected, use the jaw-thrust maneuver instead to protect the cervical spine. Deliver rescue breaths using a barrier device, bag-valve-mask, or mouth-to-mask technique. Each breath should be delivered over one second, just enough to make the chest visibly rise. Avoid giving too many breaths or blowing too hard, which can force air into the stomach and cause vomiting or aspiration.

Application and Use of Automated External Defibrillators (AED)

An automated external defibrillator analyzes the heart rhythm and delivers an electric shock to stop abnormal, chaotic rhythms like ventricular fibrillation, allowing the heart's natural pacemaker to resume normal electrical activity. As soon as the AED arrives, turn it on and follow its visual and voice prompts. Apply the adhesive pads to the bare chest according to the diagrams on the pads. Ensure no one is touching the patient while the device analyzes the rhythm and when the shock is delivered. Resume chest compressions immediately after the shock.

Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) Interventions

Advanced life support builds on basic life support by introducing medication administration, advanced airway placement, and rhythm monitoring. Emergency team members establish intravenous or intraosseous access to deliver drugs like epinephrine during cardiac arrest to vasoconstrict blood vessels and improve coronary perfusion pressure. Antiarrhythmic medications such as amiodarone are used for refractory ventricular fibrillation. Advanced airways, including endotracheal tubes or supraglottic devices, secure the airway and allow continuous chest compressions without pausing for breaths.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Complications

When spontaneous circulation returns, care shifts to stabilizing the patient and preventing neurological damage. The medical team monitors oxygen saturation, blood pressure, and blood glucose closely. Targeted temperature management is often initiated to cool the body and protect brain tissue from ischemic injury. Common complications after resuscitation include rib fractures from chest compressions, aspiration pneumonia, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, and recurring arrhythmias. Continuous intensive monitoring is vital to manage these complications.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Emergency Resuscitation

Healthcare providers must navigate complex ethical and legal boundaries during emergencies. Resuscitation should be initiated immediately unless there is a valid, legally binding advance directive or do-not-resuscitate order present. Nurses must respect patient autonomy and documented end-of-life wishes whenever available. In the absence of clear documentation, the legal default is always to initiate life-saving measures. Emotional support for grieving family members is also an essential part of emergency care when resuscitation efforts are unsuccessful.
