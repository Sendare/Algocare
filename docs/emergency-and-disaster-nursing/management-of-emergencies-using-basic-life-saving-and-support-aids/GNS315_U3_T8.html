Medical Evacuation

Introduction and Principles of Medical Evacuation

Medical evacuation, commonly abbreviated as Medevac, refers to the timely and monitored movement of critically ill or injured patients from a low-resource or hazardous setting to an appropriate receiving healthcare facility. In the Nigerian context, this often bridges the gap between rural primary health centres lacking specialist care and tertiary teaching hospitals located in urban centers. The primary goal is not merely transportation, but the continuous delivery of life-supporting interventions while in transit. Successful operations rely heavily on minimizing the time elapsed between injury onset and definitive surgical or medical management, frequently referred to as the golden hour of trauma care. Every movement carries inherent physiological risks caused by motion, altitude, and vibration, meaning the decision to transport must always weigh the potential benefits against the hazards of transit.

Indications and Patient Selection Criteria

Deciding who requires transfer demands sharp clinical judgment. Patients whose diagnostic and therapeutic needs exceed the capability of the current facility are prime candidates.

  • Unstable Airway or Breathing: Severe respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation that the local facility cannot sustain.
  • Refractory Shock: Hemodynamic instability unresponsive to initial fluid resuscitation and basic vasopressor therapy.
  • Neurosurgical Emergencies: Acute intracranial bleeding or expanding hematomas where immediate neurosurgical intervention is absent.
  • Severe Multi-system Trauma: Complex injuries requiring multidisciplinary surgical intervention.

A common pitfall in clinical settings is attempting transport before the patient is sufficiently stable, which frequently results in sudden cardiac arrest inside an ambulance or aircraft where advanced interventions are severely constrained.

Pre-Evacuation Stabilization and Triage

Before any vehicle moves, the patient must be optimized using the ABCDE framework (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure).

  1. Airway Management: Secure the airway definitively with an endotracheal tube if there is any risk of obstruction during movement.
  2. Circulatory Support: Establish reliable large-bore intravenous access and control active hemorrhage.
  3. Tube Thoracostomy: Decompress any pneumothorax, as altitude changes or ambient pressure variations during road travel can convert a simple pneumothorax into a life-threatening tension pneumothorax.
  4. Immobilization: Apply rigid cervical collars and spine boards for trauma cases to prevent secondary neurological injury.

Time spent stabilizing the patient on the ground is never wasted; rushing an unprepared patient into an ambulance usually shifts the location of the arrest from the ward to the roadside.

Modes of Transport: Ground versus Air

The choice of transport depends on distance, urgency, terrain, and weather conditions.

Transport ModePrimary AdvantageMain Limitation
Road AmbulanceAccessible to most locations; lower costVulnerable to traffic congestion and poor road networks
Fixed-Wing AircraftRapid over long interstate distancesRequires functional airfields at both ends
HelicopterDirect bedside-to-hospital access in remote areasHigh operational cost and weather dependent

In many parts of Nigeria, poor infrastructure makes road transport painfully slow, increasing transit times. Conversely, air transport provides unmatched speed but demands rigorous planning regarding cabin pressure changes and equipment compatibility with aircraft power supplies.

In-Transit Monitoring and Clinical Care

Once motion begins, the patient enters a dynamic and challenging environment. Ambient engine noise masks typical auditory cues from equipment alarms, making visual monitoring paramount. The nursing team must secure all lines, tubes, and drains to prevent accidental displacement caused by vehicle jolts. Continuous tracking of vital signs—including pulse oximetry, non-invasive blood pressure, and electrocardiography—helps detect subtle signs of deterioration early. Intravenous infusions must run on battery-powered infusion pumps rather than gravity sets, because vehicle movement and changing angles alter flow rates unpredictably. Anticipating physiological stressors like motion sickness, pain flare-ups, and hypothermia ensures the patient remains stable throughout the journey.

Communication and Team Coordination

Seamless coordination prevents dangerous delays and ensures the receiving team is fully prepared before arrival. Reliable communication channels must be maintained between the transport vehicle, the dispatch center, and the receiving trauma bay or intensive care unit. Utilizing a structured reporting format such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) provides a concise summary of the patient's condition, current interventions, and estimated time of arrival. Dead zones in mobile network coverage across rural highways require alternative communication methods, such as satellite phones or high-frequency radios, ensuring that unexpected changes in the patient's status are communicated instantly to the destination facility.

Handover and Documentation at the Receiving Facility

The mission concludes with a structured handover at the receiving hospital. The transferring nurse must provide a verbal face-to-face briefing to the receiving team while pointing out active lines, ongoing infusions, and recent physiological trends. Written documentation—including pre-transport baseline assessments, vital sign flowsheets, medication administration records, and exact fluid totals—is formally transferred. A thorough handover ensures that critical treatment details are not lost in transition, allowing the receiving multidisciplinary team to seamlessly assume ongoing care without interruption.
