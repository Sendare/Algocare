Artificial Respiration

Introduction to Artificial Respiration and Basic Concepts

Artificial respiration is a life-saving intervention used when a patient's natural breathing stops or becomes dangerously ineffective. The primary goal is to maintain adequate gas exchange by delivering oxygen to the lungs and removing carbon dioxide from the body. Without prompt intervention, brain tissue begins to suffer irreversible damage within minutes due to hypoxia.

  • Respiration vs Ventilation: Ventilation is the physical movement of air in and out of the lungs, while respiration refers to the actual exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide at the cellular level.
  • Spontaneous Breathing: The normal, automatic process where the diaphragm and intercostal muscles contract to create negative pressure, drawing air into the chest.
  • Positive Pressure Support: Artificial methods push air directly into the airway, overcoming natural lung resistance to inflate the alveoli.

Indications and Clinical Assessment for Respiratory Support

Nurses must quickly recognize when a patient is failing to breathe effectively. Waiting for complete respiratory arrest delays vital oxygenation and worsens patient outcomes. Assessment relies on observing the work of breathing, skin color, and mental status.

  1. Apnea: Total absence of breathing movements, requiring immediate rescue breaths.
  2. Severe Respiratory Distress: Using accessory muscles in the neck and chest, nasal flaring, and extreme fatigue from fighting to breathe.
  3. Altered Mental Status: Confusion, lethargy, or combativeness caused by a lack of oxygen or a dangerous buildup of carbon dioxide in the blood.
  4. Cyanosis: A bluish discoloration of the lips and nail beds, serving as a late sign of poor oxygen levels.

Manual Ventilation Techniques and Equipment

When a patient stops breathing or needs temporary assistance during emergencies, manual ventilation is the fastest intervention. The most common tool is the bag-valve-mask, commonly known as an Ambu bag.

To be effective, the rescuer must form a tight seal over the patient's nose and mouth using the C-E clamp technique. The mask is held with one hand while squeezing the bag with the other. Squeezing too fast or too forcefully can push air into the stomach instead of the lungs, leading to vomiting and aspiration. Nurses should deliver breaths smoothly over one second, watching for visible chest rise with each squeeze.

Advanced Airway Management in Emergency Settings

When basic manual ventilation is insufficient to maintain an open airway or protect the lungs from aspiration, clinicians turn to advanced airway devices.

  • Endotracheal Tube (ETT): A plastic tube inserted through the mouth or nose directly into the trachea, secured with a small inflated cuff.
  • Supraglottic Airways: Devices like the laryngeal mask airway that sit above the vocal cords, often used when intubation is difficult.
  • Confirmation: After insertion, nurses must assist in verifying placement by listening for bilateral breath sounds and observing chest movement, followed by a colorimetric carbon dioxide detector or chest X-ray.

A common misconception is that a tube secured at the teeth will never move. In reality, patient head movement can dislodge the tube, making regular position checks vital.

Mechanical Ventilation Principles and Nursing Care

Mechanical ventilators take over the work of breathing for patients over extended periods. The machine delivers air based on set parameters such as tidal volume, respiratory rate, and oxygen concentration.

Nursing care focuses on patient comfort, safety, and preventing complications associated with artificial breathing. Nurses must monitor ventilator alarms closely and never silence an alarm without investigating the cause. Oral care using chlorhexidine is performed regularly to prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia, a serious hospital-acquired infection. Additionally, keeping the head of the bed elevated between thirty and forty-five degrees reduces the risk of aspiration.

Monitoring, Complications, and Troubleshooting

Patients receiving artificial respiration require constant vigilance. Ventilator malfunctions, tube displacement, or mucus plugs can quickly turn fatal if not addressed.

If the cause of a ventilator alarm cannot be found immediately, disconnect the machine and ventilate the patient manually using an Ambu bag while calling for respiratory therapy support.

Weaning and Post-Resuscitation Nursing Responsibilities

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilator support back to spontaneous breathing. This process begins as soon as the underlying medical condition improves and the patient meets specific clinical criteria.

Nurses monitor the patient's tolerance during trial periods off the ventilator, watching for signs of distress such as sweating, rising heart rate, or falling oxygen levels. Once the breathing tube is removed, nurses continue close observation for upper airway swelling, hoarseness, or difficulty swallowing. Providing emotional reassurance is also vital, as waking up from prolonged artificial ventilation can cause severe anxiety and confusion.
