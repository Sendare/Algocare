Artificial Respiration

Introduction to Artificial Respiration and Basic Concepts

Artificial respiration encompasses methods used to replace or assist spontaneous breathing when a person's respiratory system fails to maintain adequate gas exchange. The primary goal is maintaining sufficient oxygenation of arterial blood and eliminating carbon dioxide to prevent cellular hypoxia and irreversible brain damage. In emergency settings across Nigerian healthcare facilities, basic maneuvers like the head-tilt/chin-lift or jaw-thrust are the immediate first line of defense before advanced equipment becomes available. Understanding these foundational principles allows nursing staff to quickly recognize respiratory arrest and initiate timely life-saving interventions.

Indications and Clinical Assessment for Respiratory Support

Identifying patients who require mechanical or manual respiratory support relies on both clinical observation and objective measurements. Nurses must monitor for signs of increased work of breathing, such as use of accessory muscles, nasal flaring, and tracheal tugging.

  • Hypoxemia: Inadequate blood oxygen levels evidenced by central cyanosis or low pulse oximetry readings.
  • Hypercapnia: Retention of carbon dioxide leading to altered mental status, lethargy, or combativeness.
  • Apnea: Complete cessation of breathing resulting from neurological trauma, severe poisoning, or cardiac arrest.
  • Exhaustion: Tachycardia and tachypnea giving way to bradypnea and irregular breathing patterns as respiratory muscles tire.

Manual Ventilation Techniques and Equipment

When mechanical ventilators are unavailable or during transport, manual ventilation using a bag-valve-mask (BVM) device is essential. Effective BVM ventilation requires establishing a secure seal over the patient's face using the C-E clamp technique. Squeezing the bag delivers tidal volumes that should make the chest rise visibly over one second.

Advanced Airway Management and Endotracheal Intubation

Advanced airway management secures the trachea directly, isolating the airway from the pharynx and digestive tract. Endotracheal intubation remains the gold standard for long-term artificial respiration. The nurse prepares the necessary equipment, often remembered by the mnemonic SHALL (Suction, Hook-up/oxygen, Airway equipment, Laryngoscope blade/handle, Local anesthetic/stylet). Following tube placement, verifying correct positioning is critical. Nurses must auscultate bilateral breath sounds and epigastric sounds, observe symmetrical chest expansion, and secure the tube at the correct lip line while noting the centimeter marking.

Mechanical Ventilation Principles and Nursing Care

Mechanical ventilators deliver preset volumes or pressures of oxygen-enriched air through an advanced airway. Understanding basic ventilator settings helps nurses manage alarms and maintain patient safety.

  • Tidal Volume: The volume of gas delivered with each breath, usually calculated based on ideal body weight.
  • Respiratory Rate: The number of breaths delivered per minute by the machine.
  • FiO2: The fraction of inspired oxygen, adjusted to maintain safe oxygen saturation levels.
  • PEEP (Positive End-Expiratory Pressure): Pressure maintained in the airways at the end of expiration to prevent alveolar collapse.

Routine nursing care includes maintaining closed-system suctioning to clear secretions, providing meticulous oral care to prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia, and ensuring continuous humidification of inspired gases.

Monitoring, Complications, and Troubleshooting

Patients receiving artificial respiration require constant vigilance. Ventilator alarms are safety features that demand immediate assessment rather than simple silencing. High-pressure alarms typically indicate airway obstruction, coughing, bronchospasm, or tube kinking. Low-pressure alarms suggest a dislodged circuit, tube disconnection, or an air leak in the cuff. Nurses must also monitor for ventilator-induced lung injury, barotrauma, and hemodynamic instability resulting from increased intrathoracic pressure which can reduce venous return to the heart.

Weaning and Post-Resuscitation Nursing Responsibilities

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilatory support back to spontaneous breathing. Successful weaning depends on the resolution of the underlying acute illness, stable hemodynamic status, and adequate nutritional reserves. Nurses monitor readiness parameters such as rapid shallow breathing index and spontaneous tidal volumes. During weaning trials, close observation for signs of distress—such as diaphoresis, tachypnea, tachycardia, or falling oxygen saturation—is required. Once the airway is successfully removed, post-extubation care involves monitoring for stridor, hoarseness, and maintaining adequate airway clearance.
