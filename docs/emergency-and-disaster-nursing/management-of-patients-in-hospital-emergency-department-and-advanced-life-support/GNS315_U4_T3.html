Automated External Defibrillator (AED)

Introduction to Automated External Defibrillators

An Automated External Defibrillator (AED) is a portable electronic device designed to diagnose life-threatening cardiac rhythms and deliver an electrical shock to restore a normal heart rhythm. During a sudden cardiac arrest, the heart stops pumping blood effectively, usually due to a chaotic electrical disturbance. Every minute that passes without defibrillation reduces the chance of survival by ten percent. AEDs are intentionally designed for public use, featuring voice prompts and visual diagrams so that bystanders or healthcare providers can act swiftly before emergency medical services arrive.

Indications for AED Use in Cardiac Arrest

An AED is indicated when a patient is in sudden cardiac arrest. The hallmark signs include unresponsiveness, absence of normal breathing, and no detectable pulse. If a person collapses suddenly, is unresponsive when shaken and shouted at, and only gasps irregularly, you must assume cardiac arrest and fetch an AED immediately. Do not delay pad placement to check vital signs endlessly; if the patient is unresponsive and not breathing normally, start the chain of survival.

Understanding Shockable Rhythms

An AED only delivers a shock when it detects specific abnormal heart rhythms. These are Ventricular Fibrillation (VF) and Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (VT).

  • Ventricular Fibrillation (VF): The ventricles quiver uselessly instead of pumping blood, showing a chaotic, irregular electrical pattern on the monitor.
  • Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (VT): The heart beats extremely fast in the ventricles, failing to fill with blood and maintain a cardiac output.

If the device detects asystole (a flat line) or pulseless electrical activity, it will advise against a shock and prompt rescuers to resume chest compressions.

Step-by-Step Operation and Placement of AED Pads

Using an AED is straightforward if you follow the automated prompts. First, turn the device on. Expose the patient's bare chest, wiping away moisture or excessive chest hair if it prevents the pads from sticking. Peel the adhesive pads and place them according to the diagrams on the pads: typically, the right upper chest below the collarbone, and the left lower ribcage along the side of the chest. Plug the connector into the machine if required, and clear everyone away while the device analyzes the rhythm.

Integrating AED Use with High-Quality CPR

An AED does not replace cardiopulmonary resuscitation; it complements it. Chest compressions circulate oxygenated blood to the brain and heart muscle, while the AED corrects the lethal rhythm. Minimize interruptions in chest compressions at all costs. When the AED prompts "analyzing rhythm" or advises a shock, immediately stop touching the patient. As soon as the shock is delivered, or if the device says "no shock advised," immediately resume chest compressions without checking for a pulse.

Special Considerations and Safety Precautions

Certain environmental and physical conditions require quick modifications during AED use.

  • Water and Metal: Move the patient away from standing water or puddles. If the patient is lying on a conductive metal surface like bleachers, ensure the pads do not touch the metal.
  • Medication Patches: Remove transdermal medication patches (such as nitroglycerin or nicotine) from the chest using gloved hands and wipe the skin clean before placing the AED pad over that area.
  • Implanted Devices: If a patient has an implanted pacemaker or defibrillator (visible as a hard lump under the skin), place the AED pad at least one inch away from the device to avoid interference.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Handover

Once spontaneous circulation returns and the patient shows signs of life, such as breathing normally and moving, leave the AED pads attached. The device continues to monitor the heart rhythm in case the patient relapses into cardiac arrest. Place the patient in the recovery position if they remain unconscious but breathing adequately. Provide a clear handover to arriving emergency personnel, detailing how many shocks were delivered, the total duration of CPR, and any interventions performed.

Maintenance, Troubleshooting, and Quality Assurance

An AED is only useful if it is functional when an emergency strikes. Regular maintenance includes checking the device's status indicator light, inspecting expiration dates on electrode pads, and ensuring spare batteries are available. Most modern AEDs perform automatic self-tests daily or weekly. If an error is detected or a battery is low, the device typically emits a chirping alert. Nurses and facility managers must document routine checks to ensure complete clinical readiness.
