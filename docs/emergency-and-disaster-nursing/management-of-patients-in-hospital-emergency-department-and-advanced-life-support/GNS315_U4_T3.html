Automated External Defibrillator (AED)

Introduction to Automated External Defibrillators

An **Automated External Defibrillator** is a portable electronic device designed to diagnose life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias of ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. By analyzing the heart rhythm through adhesive electrode pads, the device determines whether an electrical shock is required to re-establish an effective perfusion rhythm. Public access defibrillation programs have dramatically improved survival rates out-of-hospital by placing these units in airports, schools, and shopping complexes across Nigeria. Healthcare providers must recognize that an AED provides voice and visual prompts, guiding even untrained bystanders through the resuscitation sequence. The primary goal of early defibrillation is to halt chaotic electrical activity, allowing the natural pacemaker of the heart to resume coordinated contractions.

Indications for AED Use

An AED is indicated when a victim experiences sudden collapse, is unresponsive, and demonstrates abnormal or absent breathing, such as occasional gasping. When these criteria are met, the healthcare provider must immediately assume cardiac arrest. The device should never be applied to a patient who is breathing normally or has a palpable pulse, as analyzing a beating heart can disrupt its intrinsic rhythm. In busy Nigerian emergency wards, equipment should be mobilized instantly for any unexplained collapse, ensuring that basic life support protocols transition seamlessly into automated rhythm analysis.

Understanding Shockable Rhythms

- **Ventricular Fibrillation**: A chaotic, quivering heart rhythm where the ventricles fail to pump blood effectively. - **Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia**: A rapid, regular heart rhythm originating in the ventricles that fails to produce a cardiac output or pulse. Both rhythms represent electrical disorganization within the myocardial tissue. Without immediate electrical intervention, these rhythms rapidly deteriorate into asystole, where resuscitation success drops significantly.

Step-by-Step Operation and Pad Placement

1. **Power On**: Turn on the AED immediately by pressing the power button or opening the lid. 2. **Expose Chest**: Quickly tear or cut away clothing to expose the bare chest, drying any moisture if necessary. 3. **Apply Pads**: Peel the adhesive backing and place the pads firmly on the skin in the anterolateral position. 4. **Clear Patient**: Ensure no rescuer touches the victim while the device analyzes the rhythm. 5. **Deliver Shock**: If prompted, verify everyone is clear and press the shock button. Proper pad placement ensures the electrical vector passes directly through the heart muscle. In adult patients, place the upper right pad below the right clavicle and the lower left pad on the lower left ribcage.

Integrating AED Use with High-Quality CPR

An AED is a complementary tool that enhances, rather than replaces, cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Chest compressions must continue right up until the AED prompts for rhythm analysis and immediately resume after a shock is delivered. Minimizing interruptions in chest compressions preserves coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure. Rescuers should switch compressor roles every two minutes or when the AED pauses to reanalyze the heart rhythm, preventing fatigue-induced declines in compression depth.

Special Considerations and Safety Precautions

Certain clinical scenarios require minor adjustments during AED operation. If the patient's chest is heavily covered in hair, the adhesive pads may fail to make proper electrical contact; use the razor provided in the kit to quick-shave the area first. Wipe the chest dry if the patient is lying in water or sweating profusely, as moisture can create an electrical bridge and reduce the effectiveness of the shock. For patients with an implanted pacemaker or automatic implantable cardioverter-defibrillator, avoid placing pads directly over the metal device, positioning them at least one inch away to prevent equipment damage.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Handover

Once spontaneous circulation returns, the patient requires meticulous monitoring. Reassess airway, breathing, and circulation continually, keeping the AED pads attached in case the rhythm deteriorates again. Document the time of collapse, total number of shocks delivered, and response to interventions. During handover to the advanced medical transport team, provide a clear, concise verbal report detailing the resuscitation timeline and any medications administered.

Maintenance, Troubleshooting, and Quality Assurance

Reliability depends on routine equipment checks. Nursing staff must perform regular inspections of AED units, verifying battery expiration dates and ensuring adhesive pads remain sealed and within their shelf life. Most modern devices perform self-tests daily, indicated by a blinking status light or audio chirp. If a fault is detected, the unit must be removed from service and sent for biomedical servicing immediately. Establishing a standardized checklist schedule prevents equipment failure during critical emergencies.
