Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS)

Introduction and Chain of Survival in ACLS

Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) builds directly upon basic life support skills, adding advanced airway management, emergency medications, and electrical therapy. The adult Chain of Survival outlines the critical steps needed to give a person in cardiac arrest the highest chance of survival.

  • Early Recognition and Prevention: Spotting warning signs like chest pain or shortness of breath before the heart stops entirely.
  • Immediate High-Quality CPR: Pushing hard and fast on the chest to keep blood moving to the brain and heart.
  • Rapid Defibrillation: Delivering an electrical shock quickly to restart a heart stuck in a deadly rhythm.
  • Advanced Resuscitation: Providing medications and advanced breathing tubes by trained healthcare teams.
  • Post-Cardiac Arrest Care: Managing the patient in the hospital to protect brain function and heart recovery.
  • Recovery: Supporting the survivor through long-term physical and emotional rehabilitation.

Every link in this chain depends on the one before it. If bystanders fail to call for help or start chest compressions immediately, even the best hospital care later on will struggle to save the patient.

Basic Life Support Foundation and High-Quality CPR

Before any medications or advanced monitors arrive, high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) remains the single most important intervention in cardiac arrest. Chest compressions circulate oxygenated blood to vital organs, buying precious time until a defibrillator is ready.

  1. Compression Rate: Push at a speed of 100 to 120 compressions per minute.
  2. Compression Depth: Depress the adult chest at least 2 inches (5 centimeters), but not more than 2.4 inches.
  3. Chest Recoil: Allow the chest to spring completely back to its normal position after every compression to let blood refill the heart chambers.
  4. Minimizing Interruptions: Keep pauses in compressions under 10 seconds, especially when switching rescuers or placing airway devices.

A common mistake among student nurses is leaning on the chest between compressions. This prevents the heart from refilling with blood, which drastically cuts down the effectiveness of each push.

Cardiac Arrest Rhythms: Shockable Versus Non-Shockable

When the cardiac monitor is attached, the resuscitation team must quickly identify whether the arrest rhythm responds to an electrical shock.

FeatureShockable RhythmsNon-Shockable Rhythms
Rhythm TypesVentricular Fibrillation (VF), Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (pVT)Asystole, Pulseless Electrical Activity (PEA)
Electrical TherapyImmediate defibrillation requiredNo shock; focus on CPR and drugs
Primary MedicationsEpinephrine, AmiodaroneEpinephrine only

Ventricular Fibrillation looks like a chaotic, squiggly line where the ventricles quiver instead of pumping. Asystole is a flat line indicating no electrical activity at all. PEA shows an organized heart rhythm on the screen, but the heart muscle fails to squeeze or produce a pulse. For non-shockable rhythms, high-quality chest compressions and early epinephrine administration are your main tools.

Advanced Airway Management and Ventilation Strategies

Securing an open airway is a priority during prolonged resuscitation, but it should never interrupt chest compressions for long. Advanced airways include endotracheal tubes and supraglottic devices like laryngeal mask airways.

Once an advanced airway is in place, rescuers change their rhythm. Chest compressions continue uninterrupted at 100 to 120 per minute, while a separate provider delivers one breath every 6 seconds (10 breaths per minute). Avoid hyperventilation; pumping too many breaths into the lungs increases pressure in the chest, reduces blood returning to the heart, and lowers survival rates.

Pharmacological Agents and Emergency Vascular Access

Emergency medications support the heart's electrical system and blood pressure during resuscitation. Establishing rapid vascular access is essential for delivering these drugs.

  • Epinephrine: The primary vasoactive drug used in all cardiac arrests. It constricts blood vessels to boost blood flow to the brain and heart. Give 1 mg every 3 to 5 minutes.
  • Amiodarone: An antiarrhythmic drug used for shock-resistant ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia.
  • Vascular Routes: An intravenous (IV) line in the antecubital vein is preferred. If an IV is too difficult to place quickly, an intraosseous (IO) needle placed in the bone marrow is just as effective.

When giving emergency drugs through a peripheral IV, always follow the injection with a 20 mL saline flush and raise the arm for a few seconds to help push the medicine into central circulation.

Management of Symptomatic Bradycardia and Tachycardia

Patients with abnormal heart rates may still have a pulse, but if their heart beats too slowly or too fast, their blood pressure can drop dangerously. You must assess whether the patient is stable or unstable.

Symptomatic Bradycardia (heart rate below 60 beats per minute with signs of poor blood flow like confusion or chest pain) is treated first with atropine. If atropine fails, use transcutaneous pacing or an epinephrine infusion.

Unstable Tachycardia (fast heart rate causing shock or altered mental status) requires synchronized cardioversion immediately. If the patient has a fast rhythm with a normal blood pressure, medications like adenosine or beta-blockers are used instead.

Post-Cardiac Arrest Care and Targeted Temperature Management

Restoring a pulse does not mean the emergency is over. Many patients suffer from severe brain injury and heart dysfunction after surviving cardiac arrest.

  • Airway and Breathing: Maintain oxygen saturation between 92% and 98%, and keep carbon dioxide levels normal.
  • Hemodynamic Support: Use IV fluids and medications like norepinephrine to keep the blood pressure high enough to perfuse the brain.
  • Targeted Temperature Management (TTM): Cool unresponsive patients down to a constant temperature between 32°C and 36°C for at least 24 hours to protect brain tissue from long-term damage.

Fever is very harmful to the recovering brain after cardiac arrest. Nurses must monitor core body temperature closely and treat any fever aggressively.

Team Dynamics and Leadership in Resuscitation

Saving a life during cardiac arrest relies on clear communication and teamwork just as much as medical knowledge. Resuscitation teams use structured roles so everyone knows their exact duty.

  • Clear Roles: Assign roles like compressor, airway manager, medication nurse, and recorder before the code begins.
  • Closed-Loop Communication: When a team leader gives an instruction, the team member repeats it back clearly to confirm, and announces when the task is done.
  • Constructive Feedback: Team members should speak up immediately if they notice an error, such as a compression rate dropping too low.

A good team leader stands back to oversee the big picture rather than getting bogged down performing single tasks like chest compressions.
