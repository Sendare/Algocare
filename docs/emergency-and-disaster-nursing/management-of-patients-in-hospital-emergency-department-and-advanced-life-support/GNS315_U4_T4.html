Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS)

Introduction and Chain of Survival in ACLS

Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support builds directly upon basic resuscitation skills, integrating advanced airway management, definitive drug therapy, and organized team dynamics. The foundational concept guiding these interventions is the Chain of Survival, a sequence of standardized steps that drastically improves survival rates when executed without delay. For out-of-hospital arrests, the links emphasize early recognition, immediate high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation, rapid defibrillation, effective advanced life support, and comprehensive post-resuscitation care. In-hospital settings utilize a parallel chain that adds a preventative surveillance link to detect clinical deterioration prior to actual arrest. Recognizing that every second of delay exponentially drops myocardial and cerebral viability underscores the urgency required from nursing and medical personnel in Nigerian emergency units and beyond.

Basic Life Support (BLS) Foundation and High-Quality CPR

No advanced intervention compensates for inadequate foundational resuscitation. High-quality CPR remains the absolute core of both BLS and ACLS. Rescuers must ensure deep chest compressions—at least 5 centimeters in adults—coupled with a rapid rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Complete chest recoil between compressions is vital to allow venous filling of the heart chambers. Interruptions must be minimized to preserve coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure; any pause exceeding ten seconds drastically reduces the likelihood of return of spontaneous circulation. Ventilation should be delivered cautiously to avoid gastric inflation and increased intrathoracic pressure, which impedes venous return. Maintaining these strict physiological parameters requires rotating compressors every two minutes to prevent fatigue-induced shallow compressions.

Cardiac Arrest Algorithms: Shockable vs. Non-Shockable Rhythms

Rapid rhythm identification via monitor-defibrillator determines the branching path of the cardiac arrest algorithm. Rhythms are broadly categorized into two distinct operational groups:

  • Shockable Rhythms: Ventricular Fibrillation and pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia. These require immediate manual defibrillation or automated external defibrillator delivery, followed without pause by CPR.
  • Non-Shockable Rhythms: Asystole and Pulseless Electrical Activity. These do not respond to electrical shocks and require uninterrupted chest compressions paired with prompt epinephrine administration.

Failing to quickly differentiate these categories wastes precious minutes. When managing PEA or asystole, clinicians must simultaneously investigate reversible causes using the H's and T's mnemonic, checking for hypovolemia, hypoxia, hydrogen ion accumulation, hypokalemia or hyperkalemia, hypothermia, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, toxins, and thrombosis.

Advanced Airway Management and Ventilation Strategies

Securing a definitive airway—such as an endotracheal tube or supraglottic airway device—prevents aspiration and ensures reliable oxygen delivery. Once an advanced airway is in place, the delivery rhythm changes: chest compressions continue uninterrupted at 100 to 120 per minute, while ventilations are delivered at a rate of one breath every six seconds, equating to 10 breaths per minute. Over-ventilating is a dangerous pitfall that increases intrathoracic pressure, compromises venous return, and ultimately drops cardiac output. Tube placement must be confirmed rigorously using physical assessment—auscultating bilateral breath sounds and epigastric sounds—alongside quantitative waveform capnography to verify adequate carbon dioxide elimination and endotracheal placement.

Pharmacological Agents and Emergency Vascular Access

Pharmacotherapy during cardiac arrest supports perfusion and treats underlying electrical instability. Epinephrine remains the primary vasopressor, administered as 1 mg IV/IO every 3 to 5 minutes to stimulate alpha-adrenergic receptors, causing peripheral vasoconstriction and enhancing coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure. For shockable rhythms refractory to defibrillation and initial vasopressors, amiodarone is administered as an antiarrhythmic agent. Vascular access should be established via the intravenous route, preferably antecubital veins. When peripheral lines are unattainable due to peripheral vasoconstriction or collapse, the intraosseous route serves as a rapid, highly effective alternative for fluid and drug administration in emergency scenarios.

Management of Symptomatic Bradycardia and Tachycardia

Unstable bradycardia and tachycardia require immediate clinical intervention guided by the presence of signs of instability, such as acute altered mental status, ischemic chest discomfort, acute heart failure, or profound hypotension. For symptomatic bradycardia, atropine is the first-line pharmacological agent. If atropine fails, transcutaneous pacing or infusions of dopamine or epinephrine are initiated. Conversely, unstable tachyarrhythmias accompanied by compromised perfusion mandate immediate synchronized cardioversion. Stable tachycardias allow for more diagnostic depth, differentiating narrow-complex rhythms—which may respond to vagal maneuvers and adenosine—from wide-complex rhythms that require specialist antiarrhythmic consultations.

Post-Cardiac Arrest Care and Targeted Temperature Management

Survival following return of spontaneous circulation depends heavily on the quality of post-resuscitation care. The overarching goals are to optimize cardiopulmonary function, identify and treat acute coronary syndromes, control body temperature, and prevent multi-organ failure. Targeted Temperature Management is a cornerstone intervention where patients remaining comatose after resuscitation are actively maintained at a constant temperature between 32 and 36 degrees Celsius for at least 24 hours to mitigate neurological injury. Nursing care during this phase involves continuous hemodynamic monitoring, maintaining normoxia and normocapnia, managing blood glucose levels, and monitoring for secondary complications such as seizures, infection, and coagulopathies.

Team Dynamics and Leadership in Resuscitation

Advanced life support is rarely performed by a single clinician; it demands high-functioning multidisciplinary teamwork. Clear role assignment—designating compressor, airway manager, medication administrator, and team leader—prevents chaos and duplication of effort. Closed-loop communication is essential: the leader gives a directive to a specific team member, the member repeats the instruction back to confirm understanding, and reports back once completed. Constructive intervention is expected; if a team member notices an error in drug dosage or compression depth, they have the professional duty to voice it immediately. Effective leadership also requires maintaining situational awareness, stepping back from manual tasks to oversee the entire resuscitation flow.
