Tracheotomy

Introduction and Indications for Tracheotomy

A tracheotomy is a surgical procedure that creates a direct opening into the trachea through the neck. When an endotracheal tube cannot remain in place safely for long periods, this procedure provides a secure, alternative airway.

Common reasons nurses care for patients with a tracheostomy include:

  • Prolonged mechanical ventilation: Patients who need a ventilator for weeks often transition to a tracheostomy to reduce laryngeal damage and improve patient comfort.
  • Upper airway obstruction: Tumors, severe trauma, foreign bodies, or massive swelling of the face and neck can block airflow from above.
  • Inability to clear secretions: Patients with severe neurological impairment or weak cough reflexes require direct access to suction thick mucus from the lower airway.
  • Upper airway reconstruction: Surgeries involving the mouth, jaw, or throat may temporarily or permanently require bypassing the upper respiratory tract.

Anatomy of the Trachea and Surgical Landmarks

Understanding neck anatomy ensures safe bedside care and rapid identification of emergency landmarks. The trachea is a flexible tube made of horseshoe-shaped cartilage rings that keep the airway open against surrounding tissue pressure.

During an open surgical tracheotomy, the surgeon typically makes an incision between the second and third tracheal rings, just below the cricoid cartilage and the thyroid cartilage, commonly known as the Adam's apple. The thyroid isthmus, a small band of thyroid tissue, often overlies these rings and must be retracted or divided. Knowing these landmarks helps nurses quickly locate the tube insertion site during emergency re-insertions or skin assessments.

Types of Tracheostomy Tubes and Components

Tracheostomy tubes come in various designs, materials, and sizes to suit individual patient needs. Most standard tubes consist of an outer cannula, an inner cannula, and an obturator.

  • Outer Cannula: The main body of the tube that holds the tracheostomy open in the neck, secured by cloth ties.
  • Inner Cannula: A removable liner that slides inside the outer cannula. It can be cleaned or replaced to clear dried secretions without removing the entire outer tube.
  • Obturator: A rounded guide inserted into the outer cannula only during tube insertion to guide it smoothly through the stoma without scraping tissue; it is removed immediately once the tube is in place.
  • Cuffed vs. Uncuffed: Cuffed tubes have a small balloon at the tip that inflates to seal the airway, preventing aspiration and ensuring accurate mechanical ventilation. Uncuffed tubes allow air to flow around the tube up to the vocal cords, facilitating speech in patients who do not need a ventilator.

Routine Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Daily care of a tracheostomy patient requires systematic assessment to catch early signs of distress or infection. Nurses must inspect the stoma site every shift for redness, swelling, foul odor, or purulent drainage, which indicate infection or skin breakdown.

The skin beneath the tracheostomy ties must also be checked regularly to prevent pressure ulcers. Tube security is vital; ties should be snug enough to allow one finger to fit underneath. Nurses must always keep emergency bedside equipment readily available, including a spare tracheostomy tube of the same size, a smaller size, an obturator, and a working suction machine with catheters.

Suctioning Techniques and Airway Clearance

When a patient cannot clear pulmonary secretions independently, suctioning becomes necessary. However, suctioning can cause hypoxia, tissue trauma, and cardiac arrhythmias if done incorrectly.

  • Pre-oxygenation: Always hyperoxygenate the patient with 100% oxygen for a short period before suctioning to prevent dangerously low blood oxygen levels.
  • Aseptic Technique: Use sterile technique for open suction systems to prevent introducing bacteria directly into the lower respiratory tract.
  • Suction Pressure: Keep negative pressure within safe limits, usually between 80 to 120 mmHg for adults.
  • Duration: Limit each suction pass to 10 to 15 seconds, rotating the catheter as you withdraw it. Never apply suction while inserting the catheter.

Complications and Crisis Management

A tracheostomy can present sudden, life-threatening emergencies that require immediate nursing intervention. The two most critical crises are accidental decannulation and complete tube blockage from thick mucous plugs.

If a tracheostomy tube falls out unexpectedly in a newly formed stoma, it is a surgical emergency because the tract can close rapidly. The nurse must immediately attempt to reinsert the spare tube using the obturator. If reinsertion fails, call for emergency medical assistance and provide bag-valve-mask ventilation over the mouth and nose while sealing the stoma, or over the stoma depending on facility protocol. For a suspected mucous plug, try suctioning first; if the catheter cannot pass and the patient remains in distress, the inner cannula should be removed and cleaned or replaced immediately.

Weaning, Decannulation, and Patient Education

As a patient recovers and regains airway clearance and strength, the tracheostomy tube is gradually removed in a process called weaning and decannulation.

Steps toward removal include downsizing the tube to a smaller diameter or using a fenestrated tube that allows air to pass upward through the vocal cords. The nurse often places a cap over the outer opening to test if the patient can breathe comfortably through their upper airway while tolerating capped trials. Once the healthcare provider confirms the patient no longer needs the artificial airway, the tube is removed and the stoma is covered with an occlusive sterile dressing. The wound typically heals and closes on its own within several days without requiring surgical stitches.
