Tracheotomy

Introduction and Indications

A tracheotomy is a surgical procedure creating an opening into the trachea through the neck, establishing a direct airway. When this stoma is maintained with a tube, it is termed a tracheostomy. This intervention bypasses upper airway obstructions, facilitates long-term mechanical ventilation, and improves pulmonary hygiene in vulnerable clients. - **Upper Airway Obstruction**: Severe maxillofacial trauma, laryngeal tumors, or acute epiglottitis preventing normal airflow. - **Prolonged Mechanical Ventilation**: Neuromuscular diseases or chronic respiratory failure requiring ventilator support beyond safe endotracheal intubation limits. - **Pulmonary Hygiene**: Inability to clear copious lower respiratory secretions due to depressed level of consciousness or weak cough reflex.

Anatomy and Surgical Landmarks

Understanding neck anatomy is vital for safe airway management and complication prevention. The trachea is a fibrocartilaginous tube composed of C-shaped rings extending from the larynx to the bronchi. During an open surgical tracheotomy, the surgeon typically enters between the second and third tracheal rings. Key anatomical structures encountered or nearby include: - **Thyroid cartilage**: The superior landmark commonly known as the Adam's apple. - **Cricoid cartilage**: The only complete cartilaginous ring, serving as a critical landmark immediately inferior to the thyroid cartilage. - **Thyroid gland**: Lies across the upper tracheal rings; the isthmus may need to be retracted or divided during surgery. - **Jugular veins and carotid arteries**: Major lateral vascular structures that must be avoided during dissection.

Tracheostomy Tubes and Components

Tracheostomy tubes vary in material, design, and presence of a cuff. Selecting the correct tube depends on patient anatomy, ventilation requirements, and airway protection needs. | Component | Function | Clinical Consideration | | --- | --- | --- | | **Outer Cannula** | Main shaft maintaining stoma patency | Remains in place during routine care | | **Inner Cannula** | Fits inside outer cannula; removable for cleaning | Prevents dried secretions from occluding the airway | | **Cuff** | Creates a seal for mechanical ventilation | Must be monitored to prevent tracheal wall necrosis | | **Obturator** | Guides insertion and prevents mucosal trauma | Must be kept at the bedside at all times |

Routine Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Regular assessment prevents complications such as accidental decannulation, tube obstruction, and skin breakdown. Inspect the peristomal skin every shift for signs of infection, pressure injury, or excessive drainage. Clean the stoma site using sterile technique with normal saline, then apply a dry, split-gauze dressing underneath the faceplate. Check securing ties frequently; a snug fit allows only one finger to slip underneath the band. Monitor cuff pressure if the patient is cuffed, keeping it between 20 to 25 cmH2O to prevent mucosal ischemia while maintaining an adequate ventilatory seal.

Suctioning Techniques and Airway Clearance

Airway clearance is essential because artificial airways bypass the natural humidification and filtration functions of the upper respiratory tract. Secretions tend to become thick and dry without adequate intervention. 1. **Pre-oxygenate**: Administer 100% oxygen for 30 to 60 seconds prior to the procedure to prevent hypoxia. 2. **Insert catheter**: Advance the suction catheter gently without applying suction until resistance is met or the patient coughs. 3. **Apply suction intermittently**: Withdraw the catheter in a rotating motion for no longer than 10 to 15 seconds. 4. **Allow recovery**: Rest and re-oxygenate the patient between subsequent passes.

Complications and Crisis Management

Tracheostomy management involves recognizing and responding to acute emergencies. Accidental decannulation and tube obstruction are critical events requiring immediate nursing action. If a tube dislodges within the first 72 hours post-insertion, the tract has not matured, making reinsertion difficult and hazardous; call for emergency medical assistance immediately. For a mature tract, quickly insert a replacement tube using the obturator, remove the obturator, and confirm placement by auscultating breath sounds. Obstruction is typically managed by removing and cleaning the inner cannula or suctioning the airway. Keep a manual resuscitation bag and matched replacement tubes at the bedside.

Weaning, Decannulation, and Patient Education

Decannulation is the permanent removal of the tracheostomy tube once the underlying condition resolves and the patient can maintain ventilation, protect their airway, and clear secretions independently. The weaning process typically involves downsizing the tube, capping or using a speaking valve, and evaluating tolerance. Once the tube is removed, cover the stoma with a sterile occlusive dressing sealed on all four sides. The site usually closes spontaneously within several days without requiring surgical closure.
