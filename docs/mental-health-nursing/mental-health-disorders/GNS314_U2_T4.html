Causes of Mental Disorders

Introduction to Etiology in Mental Health-Psychiatric Nursing

Etiology refers to the study of the causes and origins of diseases or conditions. In psychiatric nursing, understanding why a mental disorder develops moves care beyond mere symptom suppression to targeted, comprehensive interventions. Unlike general medicine where a single pathogen often causes a specific disease, mental disorders rarely stem from a solitary source. They emerge from complex, intersecting pathways involving body, mind, and environment. Recognizing this multi-causal nature prevents the nurse from reducing a patient's suffering to a single flaw in character or biology.

The Biopsychosocial Framework

The biopsychosocial model serves as the foundational lens for modern psychiatric practice. It posits that biological, psychological, and social factors interact continuously to influence vulnerability, onset, and recovery.

  • Biological domain: Genetics, neurochemistry, and general physical health.
  • Psychological domain: Personality development, emotional regulation, and cognitive style.
  • Social domain: Family dynamics, socioeconomic status, and cultural background.

A disruption in one domain inevitably ripples through the others, shifting a person toward resilience or vulnerability.

Biological and Genetic Vulnerabilities

Genetics significantly shape an individual's susceptibility to psychiatric conditions. Family, twin, and adoption studies consistently demonstrate that disorders such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depression run in families. However, inheriting a genetic risk does not guarantee the development of a disorder; rather, it establishes a baseline vulnerability. This concept pairs with the diathesis-stress model, where genetic predisposition (diathesis) requires environmental pressure (stress) to trigger the actual manifestation of illness.

Neurochemical and Brain Circuit Alterations

At the microscopic level, mental disorders correlate with dysfunctions in neurotransmission and structural brain circuits. Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers that facilitate communication across synapses.

  • Serotonin: Regulates mood, sleep, and appetite; heavily implicated in depression and anxiety.
  • Dopamine: Influences pleasure, reward, motivation, and motor control; linked to psychosis and addiction.
  • Norepinephrine: Manages arousal, alertness, and the fight-or-flight response.
  • GABA: The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter, crucial for reducing neuronal excitability and anxiety.

Structural imaging studies also reveal volumetric differences in regions like the prefrontal cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala among individuals with chronic psychiatric conditions.

Psychological and Personality Dimensions

Individual psychological makeup influences how people interpret life events and cope with distress. Cognitive theories suggest that persistent negative thinking patterns—such as overgeneralization, catastrophic thinking, and personalization—maintain conditions like depression and anxiety. Furthermore, early childhood experiences shape personality development and attachment styles. Individuals who develop secure attachments generally build robust emotional resilience, whereas insecure or chaotic attachment patterns can impair a person's ability to regulate affect, predisposing them to personality disorders or emotional dysregulation later in life.

Sociocultural and Environmental Determinants

Mental health cannot be detached from the social and cultural matrix in which a person lives. Poverty, social isolation, community violence, and discrimination constitute severe environmental stressors that elevate the risk of psychological distress. In Nigeria, rapid urbanization, economic hardship, and communal shifts place distinct pressures on individuals and families. Cultural frameworks also dictate how distress is expressed and interpreted. For instance, somatic complaints—such as 'body heat,' crawling sensations in the head, or chest pains—frequently serve as culturally sanctioned idioms of distress for depression and anxiety, influencing whether a person seeks help from a psychiatric hospital, a prayer house, or a traditional healer.

Trauma and Life Stressors

Severe life events frequently act as precipitating triggers for mental health crises. Acute trauma—such as physical assault, road traffic accidents, loss of a loved one, or displacement—disrupts an individual's sense of safety and predictability. When traumatic stress is chronic or overwhelming, it can result in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, or anxiety disorders. In the Nigerian context, exposure to communal clashes, kidnapping threats, and economic displacement creates a pervasive background of chronic trauma that nurses must actively screen for during clinical assessments.

Substance Use and Dual Diagnosis

Substance use and mental disorders share a complex, bidirectional relationship known as dual diagnosis or co-occurring disorders. Psychoactive substances alter brain chemistry, frequently inducing psychiatric symptoms such as psychosis, depression, or severe anxiety. Conversely, individuals struggling with underlying psychological distress may use substances to self-medicate, temporarily masking symptoms while worsening the long-term prognosis. In Nigerian clinical practice, cannabis, alcohol, and pharmaceutical opioids like tramadol are frequently implicated in substance-induced psychiatric presentations.

Implications for Psychiatric Nursing Assessment

A comprehensive nursing assessment must evaluate all dimensions of the biopsychosocial framework rather than focusing exclusively on presenting symptoms. Nurses gather a detailed history that explores genetic background, previous trauma, substance use, coping styles, and sociocultural context. This holistic inquiry ensures that care plans address root causes and environmental contributors, rather than merely relying on pharmacological management. By understanding the multifaceted etiology of mental disorders, psychiatric nurses design individualized interventions that promote recovery, resilience, and reintegration.
