Causes of Mental Disorders

Introduction to Etiology and the Biopsychosocial Model

Understanding why mental disorders develop requires looking beyond a single cause. In psychiatric nursing, etiology is rarely traced to just one simple trigger. Instead, mental health conditions emerge from a complex web of interacting influences. To capture this complexity, nurses rely on the biopsychosocial model, a framework that groups causes into three major categories:

  • Biological factors: Genetic makeup, brain chemistry, physical health, and hormone levels.
  • Psychological factors: Personality traits, coping styles, emotional maturity, and past trauma.
  • Social factors: Family background, cultural environment, economic status, and life stressors.

This multi-layered approach prevents nurses from reducing a patient's suffering to 'just in their head' or purely a chemical imbalance. It guides holistic assessment, ensuring care plans address physical, mental, and social needs all at once.

Biological and Genetic Influences

Genetics play a powerful role in vulnerability to mental illness. Conditions like schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and major depression tend to run in families. However, inheriting a gene does not guarantee a person will develop the disorder; it only increases their genetic predisposition or susceptibility.

Beyond DNA, physical health conditions can directly mimic or trigger psychiatric symptoms. Thyroid imbalances, severe vitamin deficiencies, traumatic brain injuries, and chronic infections can alter mood, cognition, and behavior. A thorough psychiatric assessment always rules out underlying medical illness before assuming a purely psychological cause.

Neurochemical and Brain Circuit Alterations

Inside the brain, billions of neurons communicate using chemical messengers called neurotransmitters. When these chemical systems become unbalanced, thoughts, moods, and perception can change drastically. Several key neurotransmitters are closely linked to mental health:

  1. Serotonin: Regulates mood, sleep, appetite, and impulse control. Low levels are heavily linked to depression and anxiety.
  2. Dopamine: Controls pleasure, reward, motivation, and motor control. Excess dopamine activity is linked to hallucinations in schizophrenia, while too little in certain pathways contributes to depression.
  3. Norepinephrine: Influences alertness, energy, and the body's fight-or-flight response.
  4. GABA: The primary calming neurotransmitter that helps reduce anxiety and nervous system overactivity.

Structural brain changes also matter. Advanced neuroimaging shows that chronic stress or trauma can shrink parts of the brain like the hippocampus, affecting memory and emotional regulation.

Psychological and Personality Vulnerabilities

A person's internal psychological makeup shapes how they interpret life events. While some individuals bounce back easily from hardship, others possess personality traits or cognitive habits that make them more vulnerable to mental distress.

Maladaptive coping mechanisms—such as denial, avoidance, or emotional suppression—prevent healthy processing of stress. Additionally, negative thought patterns, such as constant catastrophic thinking or harsh self-blame, feed conditions like depression and anxiety. Early childhood experiences, including emotional neglect, chronic criticism, or attachment disruptions with primary caregivers, heavily shape these cognitive patterns and emotional resilience well into adulthood.

Social, Environmental, and Cultural Factors in Nigeria

Environment and culture exert a profound influence on mental health and illness presentation. In Nigeria, socio-economic pressures, poverty, unemployment, and community displacement create chronic environmental stress that strains psychological endurance.

Cultural beliefs also shape how mental illness is perceived, diagnosed, and treated. Many communities attribute psychiatric symptoms to spiritual attacks, generational curses, or destiny, which often leads families to seek help from traditional or religious healers before visiting a psychiatric hospital. This delay in orthodox care can worsen the illness. Furthermore, intense stigma surrounding mental health prevents individuals from speaking openly about their struggles or seeking timely professional help, leading to social isolation.

Life Stressors, Trauma, and the Diathesis-Stress Model

To understand why one person breaks under pressure while another remains strong, nurses use the diathesis-stress model. This model proposes that mental disorders develop from the interaction between a vulnerability (diathesis) and a trigger (stress).

Factor TypeExamplesRole in Illness
Diathesis (Vulnerability)Genetics, brain injury, childhood traumaSets up the underlying susceptibility
Stress (Trigger)Job loss, bereavement, financial crisis, abusePrecipitates the onset of symptoms

Severe life stressors and acute trauma—such as accidents, armed robbery, kidnapping, or domestic violence—can overwhelm a person's psychological defenses, leading to conditions like Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), depression, or acute psychotic episodes.

Substance Use and Dual Diagnosis

The relationship between substance use and mental illness is deep and complex. Many individuals struggling with mental disorders use alcohol, cannabis, stimulants, or other drugs to self-medicate their emotional pain, anxiety, or insomnia. Unfortunately, substance use often worsens psychiatric symptoms over time.

When a mental illness and a substance use disorder occur together, it is known as a dual diagnosis or co-occurring disorder. Psychoactive substances alter brain chemistry directly, sometimes triggering permanent psychotic states or severe depressive crashes. Effective nursing care requires treating both conditions simultaneously rather than addressing addiction while ignoring the underlying mental health disorder.

Implications for Psychiatric Nursing Assessment and Care

Recognizing that mental disorders have multiple causes changes how psychiatric nurses perform assessments and plan care. A comprehensive nursing assessment goes beyond checking current symptoms; it explores family medical history, childhood experiences, current life stressors, substance use, and cultural background.

Armed with this knowledge, nurses design individualized interventions that incorporate biological treatments (such as medication administration and monitoring), psychological support (therapeutic communication and crisis counseling), and social interventions (family education and connecting patients with community support groups). Understanding the root causes empowers nurses to advocate for their patients and reduce the heavy burden of stigma.
