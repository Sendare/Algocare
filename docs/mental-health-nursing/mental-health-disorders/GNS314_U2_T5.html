Anxiety Disorders

Introduction to Anxiety Disorders and Normal vs. Abnormal Anxiety

Anxiety is a universal human experience. It serves a vital evolutionary purpose by sharpening alertness, preparing the body to face danger, and driving performance. Normal anxiety is proportional to a real, identifiable threat and dissipates once the stressor resolves. Clinical anxiety, however, persists long after a threat passes, or arises entirely without provocation. When this physiological arousal begins to disrupt daily functioning, occupational duties, and interpersonal relationships, it crosses the threshold from an adaptive emotion into a psychiatric disorder. In Nigerian clinical settings, patients often somaticize psychological distress. Instead of reporting excessive worry, individuals frequently present with bodily complaints such as palpitations, heat in the body, excessive sweating, or a crawling sensation under the skin. Recognizing these cultural expressions prevents misdiagnosing anxiety as purely somatic pathology.

Neurobiology and Pathophysiology of Anxiety

The biological framework of anxiety centers on the brain's emotional circuitry, primarily involving the **limbic system**, **amygdala**, and **prefrontal cortex**. The amygdala acts as the brain's emotional sentinel, scanning the environment for threats and triggering the fight-or-flight response via the autonomic nervous system. In anxiety disorders, this alarm system becomes hypersensitive, misinterpreting neutral stimuli as dangerous. Neurotransmitter dysregulation heavily influences this hyperarousal: - **Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)**: The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. Deficits in GABA reduce the brain's ability to calm neural activity, leading to sustained agitation. - **Norepinephrine**: Often elevated in panic states, driving sympathetic nervous system overdrive. - **Serotonin**: Regulates mood, sleep, and impulse control; serotonergic dysfunction is heavily implicated in generalized and panic-related symptoms.

Classification and Major Types of Anxiety Disorders

Anxiety disorders comprise several distinct clinical entities, each characterized by specific triggers and behavioral patterns. - **Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)**: Characterized by chronic, excessive, and uncontrollable worry about everyday matters, lasting for at least six months. - **Panic Disorder**: Marked by recurrent, unexpected panic attacks accompanied by a persistent fear of having subsequent attacks or significant behavioral changes related to the attacks. - **Agoraphobia**: Marked fear or anxiety regarding being in situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable, such as public transportation or open spaces. - **Social Anxiety Disorder (Social Phobia)**: Intense fear of scrutiny, embarrassment, or humiliation in social or performance situations. - **Specific Phobias**: Marked, unreasonable fear triggered by a specific object or situation, such as heights, enclosed spaces, or flying.

Etiology and Predisposing Risk Factors

The development of an anxiety disorder rarely stems from a single isolated cause. Instead, a complex interplay of genetic vulnerability, environmental stressors, and psychological conditioning shapes an individual's susceptibility. Family history studies demonstrate a clear genetic component; individuals with first-degree relatives suffering from an anxiety disorder face a significantly higher risk. Environmental factors, particularly adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) such as trauma, neglect, or chronic unpredictable stress, can structurally alter the developing brain's stress-response pathways. Furthermore, cognitive theories suggest that maladaptive thinking patterns—such as catastrophic thinking and a persistent perception of personal helplessness—maintain and reinforce anxiety over time.

Clinical Manifestations and Behavioral Assessment

Anxiety expresses itself across multiple dimensions of human functioning. A thorough behavioral assessment captures both subjective internal distress and objective physical signs. | System | Clinical Manifestations | | :--- | :--- | | **Psychological** | Dread, apprehension, irritability, difficulty concentrating, feeling on edge | | **Cardiovascular** | Palpitations, tachycardia, chest tightness, elevated blood pressure | | **Respiratory** | Tachypnea, shortness of breath, smothering sensation | | **Neurological** | Tremors, dizziness, paresthesias, tension headaches | | **Gastrointestinal** | Dry mouth, nausea, abdominal cramping, loose stools | Behavioral indicators often include avoidance of triggering situations, restlessness, pacing, and hypervigilance. During severe anxiety or panic, cognitive capacity narrows drastically, impairing problem-solving and communication.

Psychiatric Nursing Assessment and Mental Status Examination

Conducting a mental status examination (MSE) on an anxious patient requires careful observation of both verbal and non-verbal cues. The nurse evaluates appearance, motor activity, speech, mood, affect, thought process, and perceptual disturbances. Anxious clients frequently present with tense posture, restless fidgeting, and darting eyes. Their speech may be rapid, pressured, or halting if panic interferes with thought articulation. It is vital to screen carefully for comorbid depression and substance abuse, as individuals frequently self-medicate with alcohol or local sedatives to manage unbearable internal tension. Assessing suicide risk is mandatory, particularly when anxiety is accompanied by profound hopelessness or agitation.

Pharmacological and Psychotherapeutic Management

Effective management of severe anxiety typically combines medication with evidence-based psychotherapy. - **Pharmacotherapy**: Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) such as sertraline or escitalopram serve as first-line chronic treatments, though they require several weeks to take full effect. Benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam, lorazepam) provide rapid relief for acute panic but carry a high risk of dependence and tolerance, making them unsuitable for long-term use. - **Psychotherapy**: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) is the gold standard. CBT helps clients identify and restructure distorted cognitive appraisals, while behavioral techniques like systematic desensitization gradually expose patients to feared stimuli in a controlled, safe environment.

Nursing Interventions and Crisis De-escalation Strategies

When caring for an individual experiencing acute anxiety or panic, the nurse must prioritize safety and physiological stabilization. The immediate environment should be quiet, low-stimulation, and secure. The nurse ought to remain with the patient, using a calm, reassuring voice and short, simple sentences, because cognitive overload prevents the processing of complex information. Practical de-escalation techniques include guiding the client through slow, deep breathing exercises to correct hyperventilation and engaging them in grounding activities—such as asking them to name objects in the room—to pull their focus away from internal panic and back to the external present.

Complications, Co-occurring Disorders, and Patient Education

Untreated anxiety disorders carry significant long-term complications. Chronic physiological arousal strains the cardiovascular system, elevates the risk of hypertension, and weakens immune function. Social isolation, clinical depression, and substance abuse frequently complicate the clinical picture, dramatically reducing overall quality of life. Patient and family education is central to recovery. Teaching clients to recognize early physiological warning signs allows them to implement coping strategies before anxiety escalates into full-blown panic. Involving supportive family members ensures that the patient has a reliable network at home, reinforcing healthy routines and medication adherence.
