MHPSS Interventions

Introduction to Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS)

Mental Health and Psychosocial Support is a composite term used to describe any type of local or outside support that aims to protect or promote psychological well-being and treat or prevent mental disorder. The framework recognizes that mental health and social experiences are inextricably linked. Psychological well-being relies heavily on the daily social structures, community bonds, and cultural practices surrounding an individual. In emergency contexts—ranging from insurgency displacements in northeastern Nigeria to global pandemics—the destruction of these social fabrics causes profound psychological distress that goes far beyond clinical psychiatric illness. MHPSS bridges the gap between purely biomedical psychiatric approaches and social community work, ensuring interventions respect local worldviews and leverage existing coping mechanisms rather than imposing foreign therapeutic models.

The IASC Multi-Layered Support Framework

The Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) guidelines provide a pyramid structure organizing MHPSS interventions into four distinct tiers based on intensity and target population needs.

  • Tier 1: Basic services and security: Ensuring that the emergency response promotes physical safety, basic physical needs, and community governance.
  • Tier 2: Community and family supports: Strengthening traditional social networks, supportive communal spaces, and activating local coping mechanisms.
  • Tier 3: Focused non-specialized supports: Targeted psychological first aid, basic counseling, and clinical management by trained health workers or general practitioners.
  • Tier 4: Specialized services: Clinical mental health care provided by psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, and clinical psychologists for individuals with severe, debilitating conditions.

Psychological First Aid (PFA)

Psychological First Aid is a humane, supportive response to a fellow human being who is suffering and who may need support. It involves assessing needs and concerns, listening actively, comforting people, helping them connect to basic needs, and protecting them from further harm. PFA is designed for use by both professional and non-professional helpers immediately following traumatic events. It is rooted in the principles of safety, dignity, and empowerment. Unlike formal psychotherapy, PFA does not ask people to analyze what happened or put time pressure on recounting details of trauma. Instead, it focuses on practical assistance, stabilization, and linkage to community resources. PFA can be summarized by its core action steps: Look, Listen, and Link.

Community-Based Psychosocial Interventions

Sustainable mental health care must be anchored within communities rather than isolated within large psychiatric institutions. Community-based interventions mobilize local assets, youth groups, religious leaders, and traditional institutions to foster environments where mental distress is met with empathy rather than stigma. In Nigeria, community dialogues, psychoeducation campaigns via local media, and peer support groups have proven effective in bridging treatment gaps. These interventions empower communities to identify early warning signs of emotional distress, promote healthy coping mechanisms, and facilitate smooth reintegration for individuals recovering from mental illness.

Specialized Mental Health Interventions

Specialized care forms the peak of the MHPSS continuum, addressing severe psychiatric disorders, complex trauma, and acute self-harm risks. Psychiatric nurses working at this level collaborate closely with psychiatrists and clinical psychologists to deliver evidence-based pharmacotherapy and structured psychotherapy such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT). Comprehensive psychiatric nursing assessments are vital here to monitor medication side effects, evaluate suicide risk, and manage behavioral crises safely. These interventions require robust referral pathways from community health posts to tertiary medical centers, ensuring seamless continuity of care for vulnerable patients.

Culturally Competent MHPSS Delivery in Nigeria

Nigeria is culturally diverse, with over 250 ethnic groups and distinct belief systems surrounding health, illness, and misfortune. Mental health symptoms are frequently expressed through somatic complaints like heat in the head, crawling sensations, or spiritual afflictions rather than standard Western diagnostic categories. Culturally competent nursing care requires acknowledging and respecting these explanatory models without dismissing them. Collaboration with traditional and faith-based healers—who are often the first point of contact for families seeking help—helps reduce harmful practices, such as shackling, while integrating biomedical care into a culturally acceptable framework.

Ethical Considerations and Self-Care for Nurses

Providing mental health support in high-stress environments exposes nurses to vicarious trauma, compassion fatigue, and burnout. Ethical practice demands strict adherence to patient confidentiality, informed consent, and the preservation of human dignity, especially when dealing with vulnerable or stigmatized populations. To sustain clinical effectiveness, nurses must prioritize personal self-care strategies, including regular clinical supervision, peer debriefing sessions, boundary setting, and adequate rest. Recognizing personal limits is not a sign of weakness, but a professional necessity to prevent clinical errors and ensure long-term resilience in psychiatric nursing practice.
