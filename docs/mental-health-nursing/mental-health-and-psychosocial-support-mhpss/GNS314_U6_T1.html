Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS)

Introduction to Mental Health and Psychosocial Support (MHPSS)

Mental Health and Psychosocial Support, commonly known as MHPSS, is a composite term used to describe any type of local or outside support that protects or promotes psychosocial well-being and treats or prevents mental disorder. In humanitarian crises, natural disasters, or community violence, people do not just suffer physical injuries; their social structures, livelihoods, and psychological safety are severely disrupted. MHPSS bridges the gap by addressing both the psychological wounds inside the mind and the social conditions surrounding the individual.

Traditionally, mental health interventions focused heavily on clinical pathology within hospital walls. However, modern global health recognizes that psychological distress is deeply intertwined with social relationships, cultural beliefs, and community resources. MHPSS unifies clinical mental health care with social interventions. A nurse practicing MHPSS looks beyond the isolated symptom of anxiety or depression to examine how family displacement, loss of income, or community stigma impacts healing.

The IASC MHPSS Pyramid Framework

The Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) guideline provides a tiered pyramid framework to organize mental health and psychosocial support in emergencies. This structure ensures that limited specialized resources are used efficiently while the wider population receives appropriate, scalable care.

  • Tier 1: Basic services and security: Ensuring that emergency responses satisfy basic physical needs like food, water, shelter, and safety, while restoring security and community networks.
  • Tier 2: Community and family supports: Strengthening traditional family support structures, supporting community-led healing, and providing Psychological First Aid (PFA) by frontline workers.
  • Tier 3: Focused, non-specialized supports: Targeted psychological and social interventions delivered by trained and supervised health workers or social workers, such as basic counseling.
  • Tier 4: Specialized services: Clinical mental health care provided by psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, and clinical psychologists for individuals with severe, disabling mental disorders.

Psychological First Aid (PFA) Principles and Practice

Psychological First Aid (PFA) is a humane, supportive response to a fellow human being who is suffering and who may need support. It is designed for use by frontline helpers—including nurses, community health extension workers, and volunteers—immediately after a distressing event.

Unlike traditional psychotherapy, PFA does not involve forcing survivors to analyze what happened or detail their trauma. Instead, PFA focuses on three core action principles:

  1. Look: Check for safety, check for people with urgent basic needs, and check for individuals with severe distress reactions.
  2. Listen: Approach people who may need support, ask about their needs and concerns, and listen patiently without pressuring them to talk.
  3. Link: Help people address basic needs, access services, deal with practical problems, connect with loved ones, and find social support.

Trauma, Stressors, and Vulnerabilities in the Nigerian Context

Mental health nurses in Nigeria operate within a complex psychosocial landscape shaped by diverse stressors. Beyond universal stressors like poverty and unemployment, Nigerian communities face localized challenges including insurgency, banditry, farmer-herder conflicts, kidnapping, and widespread displacement. These ongoing insecurities result in complex collective trauma.

Vulnerabilities are further compounded by cultural beliefs regarding mental illness. In many settings, psychological distress is attributed entirely to spiritual warfare, witchcraft, or generational curses, leading individuals to seek help from traditional or spiritual healing homes before visiting a health facility. Human rights violations, physical chaining, and severe delays in orthodox care are common consequences. Nurses must navigate these cultural realities respectfully while advocating for safe, evidence-based psychosocial support.

Community-Based Psychosocial Interventions

Moving mental health care out of isolated asylums into the community is vital for sustainable recovery. Community-based psychosocial interventions leverage local strengths, peer support groups, and primary health care networks to normalize mental health and reduce barriers to care.

Nurses working at the community level often facilitate psychoeducation workshops, support youth groups, and train local school teachers to recognize early signs of emotional distress in children. By engaging religious leaders, market unions, and village heads, nurses build bridges of trust. This grassroots approach destigmatizes psychological help-seeking and ensures that interventions respect local customs and social structures.

Role of the Psychiatric Nurse in MHPSS Delivery

The psychiatric nurse plays a pivotal, multi-faceted role across all levels of the IASC MHPSS framework. In clinical settings, the nurse conducts comprehensive mental status examinations, administers prescribed psychotropic medications, monitors for side effects, and provides individualized psychotherapy.

Beyond the clinic, psychiatric nurses act as trainers and supervisors for primary health care workers and community volunteers delivering Psychological First Aid. They advocate for patient human rights, lead mental health promotion campaigns in schools and workplaces, and coordinate referrals between grassroots community supports and specialized tertiary psychiatric facilities. Their clinical expertise combined with public health vision makes them central pillars of effective MHPSS programs.

Ethical Considerations, Stigma Reduction, and Self-Care

Delivering mental health support requires strict adherence to ethical principles. Nurses must fiercely protect patient confidentiality, obtain informed consent before interventions, and respect human dignity, especially when working with vulnerable or displaced populations. Stigma remains a massive barrier; nurses must actively challenge discriminatory language and practices in both community and clinical spaces.

At the same time, helpers are not immune to distress. Compassion fatigue, secondary traumatic stress, and burnout are occupational hazards for nurses working in high-stress MHPSS environments. Regular clinical supervision, peer support debriefing sessions, and intentional personal self-care strategies are mandatory professional requirements, not luxuries, to sustain long-term compassionate care.
