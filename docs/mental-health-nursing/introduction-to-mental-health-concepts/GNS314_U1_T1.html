Mental Health

Introduction to Mental Health and Mental Illness

Mental health is not merely the absence of a mental disorder. It is a state of well-being where an individual realizes their own abilities, can cope with normal life stresses, works productively, and contributes to their community. Mental illness, conversely, involves clinically significant disturbances in cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior that reflect dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning. These conditions often cause significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of life. A common misconception in clinical practice is viewing mental health and mental illness as a strict dichotomy. Instead, they exist on a continuous spectrum. An individual may experience high mental distress and physical symptoms yet still maintain high functional ability, or conversely, someone with a diagnosed chronic mental condition may experience periods of stable emotional balance and robust well-being.

Historical Perspectives and Evolution of Psychiatric Nursing

The care of individuals with mental illness has transformed dramatically over centuries. Historically, mental illness was frequently attributed to demonic possession, witchcraft, or divine punishment, leading to harsh treatments such as flogging, exorcism, and confinement in dark asylums. During the moral treatment era in the late 18th and 19th centuries, reformers like Philippe Pinel and Dorothea Dix advocated for humane conditions, kindness, and structured routines. The mid-20th century marked a revolutionary shift with the introduction of psychotropic medications, particularly chlorpromazine in the 1950s. This pharmacological breakthrough enabled deinstitutionalization, a movement that moved patients out of large, isolated asylums and back into community-based care settings. Psychiatric nursing evolved alongside these milestones, shifting its primary focus from custodial containment and physical restraint to therapeutic engagement, holistic assessment, and community rehabilitation.

Determinants of Mental Health and Psychosocial Factors

Mental well-being is shaped by a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors. Understanding these elements helps nurses identify vulnerability and plan comprehensive care.

  • Biological factors: Genetic predisposition, neurochemical imbalances (such as serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine dysregulation), and overall physical health.
  • Psychological factors: Individual temperament, emotional resilience, coping styles, and past traumatic experiences.
  • Social and environmental factors: Socioeconomic status, educational attainment, housing stability, cultural beliefs, exposure to violence, and social support networks.

Classification and Phenomenology of Mental Disorders

To provide structured, consistent care, psychiatric healthcare providers rely on standardized classification systems, primarily the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11). These manuals provide explicit criteria for diagnosing mental disorders based on observable symptoms, duration, and functional impairment. Phenomenology refers to the subjective experience of the patient—how they perceive, feel, and describe their symptoms. Recognizing phenomenological features allows nurses to understand the internal reality of a patient experiencing psychosis, severe depression, or overwhelming anxiety. Common diagnostic categories include mood disorders, anxiety disorders, psychotic disorders, and personality disorders. Accurate classification ensures clear interprofessional communication and guides appropriate evidence-based interventions.

The Nurse-Patient Relationship and Therapeutic Communication

The therapeutic nurse-patient relationship is the cornerstone of psychiatric nursing. Unlike a social relationship, this connection is goal-directed, professional, and centered entirely on the patient's health and growth needs. Peplau's theory outlines distinct phases of this relationship: the orientation phase (establishing trust, boundaries, and contracts), the working phase (exploring problems, developing coping skills, and implementing changes), and the termination phase (summarizing progress, addressing feelings of loss, and saying goodbye).

Effective therapeutic communication uses specific techniques to encourage expression while maintaining professional boundaries:

  • Active listening: Giving undivided attention to verbal and non-verbal cues.
  • Broad openings: Allowing the patient to take the initiative in setting the topic.
  • Reflecting: Directing questions or feelings back to the patient to help them explore their own thoughts.
  • Seeking clarification: Making vague statements explicit to ensure mutual understanding.

Mental Status Examination (MSE) and Psychiatric Assessment

The Mental Status Examination (MSE) is the psychiatric equivalent of the physical health examination. It is a structured way of observing and describing a patient's current psychological functioning across several key domains.

MSE DomainWhat is Assessed
Appearance & BehaviorGrooming, posture, eye contact, and psychomotor activity
SpeechRate, volume, tone, and spontaneity
Mood & AffectPatient's subjective emotional state versus observed emotional expression
Thought Process & ContentOrganization of thinking and presence of delusions or suicidal ideation
PerceptionPresence of hallucinations (auditory, visual) or illusions
CognitionLevel of alertness, orientation to time/place, and memory recall
Insight & JudgmentAwareness of illness and ability to make safe, rational decisions

Conducting an MSE requires careful observation during normal conversation, blending formal questioning with informal behavioral assessment.

Evidence-Based Nursing Management in Mental Health

Nursing management in psychiatric settings combines biological interventions with psychosocial therapies to promote recovery and stabilization. Pharmacotherapy management involves administering psychotropic medications—such as antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilizers, and anxiolytics—while closely monitoring for therapeutic effects and adverse reactions. Nurses play a crucial role in patient education regarding medication adherence. Psychosocial interventions include structured milieu therapy (maintaining a safe, therapeutic hospital environment), psychoeducation for patients and families, and supportive counseling. De-escalation techniques are essential when managing acute behavioral crises, prioritizing verbal reassurance, active listening, and reducing environmental stimuli before resorting to physical or chemical restraints, which are always used as absolute last resorts.

Legal, Ethical, and Cultural Considerations in Nigerian Psychiatric Care

Psychiatric nursing practice operates within strict legal and ethical boundaries designed to protect human rights while ensuring safety. Ethical principles such as autonomy (respecting the patient's right to make choices), beneficence (acting in the patient's best interest), non-maleficence (doing no harm), and justice (fair resource allocation) guide daily clinical decisions. In Nigeria, psychiatric care is influenced by both modern mental health legislation and deeply rooted cultural and traditional belief systems. Many families first seek help from traditional or spiritual healing homes before visiting orthodox hospitals, often due to cultural models attributing mental illness to spiritual attack or witchcraft. Psychiatric nurses must navigate these pluralistic care pathways with cultural sensitivity, avoiding judgment while educating communities and collaborating safely with families to bridge traditional practices with evidence-based psychiatric care.
