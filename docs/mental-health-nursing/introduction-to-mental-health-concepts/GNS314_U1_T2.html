Mental Illness

Introduction to Mental Illness in Nursing

Mental health and mental illness exist on a continuous spectrum rather than as rigid binary states. A person can function adequately day-to-day while experiencing psychological distress, or experience severe impairment during a mental health crisis. In professional nursing, mental illness refers to clinically significant behavioral or psychological syndromes associated with present distress, disability, or a significantly increased risk of suffering. Recognizing this spectrum prevents the common pitfall of viewing patients solely through the lens of their diagnosis, preserving their individual humanity and promoting holistic care approaches.

Classification and Major Categories of Mental Disorders

Psychiatric diagnosis relies on standardized classification systems like the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11). These manuals group disorders by observed symptom clusters to guide clinical judgment and treatment.

  • Mood Disorders: Conditions characterized by a persistent disturbance in emotional state, such as major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder.
  • Anxiety Disorders: Disorders featuring excessive, irrational fear and dread that impair daily functioning, including panic disorder and generalized anxiety disorder.
  • Psychotic Disorders: Severe conditions where individuals lose touch with reality, marked by hallucinations, delusions, and disorganized thinking, as seen in schizophrenia.
  • Personality Disorders: Enduring, inflexible patterns of behavior and inner experience that deviate markedly from cultural expectations and cause significant distress.

Etiology and Risk Factors: Biological, Psychological, and Sociocultural Influences

Mental illnesses rarely stem from a single isolated cause. Instead, the biopsychosocial model explains that psychiatric disorders emerge from a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors.

  • Biological factors: Genetic predispositions, neurotransmitter imbalances (such as serotonin, dopamine, and norepinephrine dysregulation), and structural brain abnormalities.
  • Psychological factors: Early childhood trauma, maladaptive cognitive patterns, ineffective coping mechanisms, and individual personality traits.
  • Sociocultural factors: Poverty, systemic discrimination, community violence, chronic stress, and cultural expectations surrounding emotional expression.

Stigma and Cultural Perceptions of Mental Illness in Nigeria

Cultural beliefs heavily shape how mental illness is perceived, diagnosed, and managed in Nigeria. Many communities attribute psychiatric symptoms to spiritual attacks, witchcraft, or ancestral displeasure rather than medical pathology. This deep-seated stigma frequently drives individuals toward traditional or religious healing homes before seeking formal psychiatric care, often leading to delayed interventions, human rights abuses, and severe physical restraint. Nurses practicing in this environment must navigate these cultural realities with empathy, providing education without invalidating the patient's cultural worldview.

Psychiatric-Mental Health Nursing Assessment and Clinical Interviewing

The psychiatric assessment differs from general medical assessment by relying heavily on observation, interpersonal connection, and the Mental State Examination (MSE). The MSE is a structured snapshot of a patient's current psychological functioning, covering appearance, behavior, speech, mood, affect, thought process, thought content, perceptual disturbances, cognition, and insight. Establishing psychological safety at the beginning of the interview is crucial. Nurses must observe non-verbal cues, listen actively, and avoid judgmental questioning to elicit accurate clinical data from distressed individuals.

Therapeutic Communication and Nurse-Patient Relationship

The therapeutic nurse-patient relationship forms the cornerstone of psychiatric nursing practice. Unlike social relationships, this professional bond focuses exclusively on the patient's needs, growth, and healing. Nurses use specific therapeutic communication techniques—such as active listening, broad openings, reflecting, and clarifying—to encourage patients to explore their feelings safely. Maintaining professional boundaries is essential to prevent countertransference, where the nurse projects personal emotions onto the patient, thereby compromising objective care.

Multidisciplinary Management and Treatment Modalities

Effective mental health care requires a collaborative, multidisciplinary team comprising psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, clinical psychologists, social workers, and occupational therapists. Treatment modalities span pharmacological interventions (such as antipsychotics, antidepressants, and mood stabilizers) and various psychotherapeutic approaches (including cognitive behavioral therapy and psychoeducation). Nurses play a pivotal role in administering medications, monitoring for adverse side effects, facilitating group therapy sessions, and coordinating ongoing community-based rehabilitation.

Legal, Ethical, and Human Rights Issues in Psychiatric Care

Psychiatric nursing operates within strict legal and ethical frameworks designed to protect vulnerable individuals. Key concepts include informed consent, confidentiality, and the principle of least restrictive alternative during crisis interventions. In emergency situations involving imminent danger to self or others, involuntary admission procedures may be legally initiated. However, coercive practices such as physical restraint or seclusion must be used strictly as absolute last resorts, adhering to established protocols to safeguard patient dignity and human rights.
