Mental Illness

Introduction and Historical Perspectives

Mental illness encompasses a wide range of conditions affecting mood, thinking, and behavior. Historically, care for individuals experiencing psychological distress moved from brutal confinement and superstition to moral treatment, and eventually to modern pharmacotherapy. In early Nigerian contexts, traditional healers and spiritual homes were often the primary points of care before the establishment of formal psychiatric institutions like Arochukwu. Recognizing this evolution helps modern nurses appreciate the shift from purely custodial care to holistic, patient-centered psychiatric nursing.

Classification and Major Categories of Mental Disorders

Clinical practice relies on standardized classification systems, primarily the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD). These manuals provide common terminology for multidisciplinary teams.

  • Mood Disorders: Conditions like major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder characterized by significant disturbances in emotional state.
  • Psychotic Disorders: Illnesses such as schizophrenia marked by delusions, hallucinations, and disorganized thinking.
  • Anxiety Disorders: Persistent, excessive fear and worry that impair daily functioning, including generalized anxiety and panic disorders.
  • Personality Disorders: Enduring, rigid patterns of behavior and inner experience that deviate markedly from cultural expectations.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Mental illnesses rarely stem from a single source. The biopsychosocial model explains that disorders arise from a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors.

  • Biological: Genetic predispositions, neurotransmitter imbalances (such as serotonin and dopamine dysregulation), and structural brain abnormalities.
  • Psychological: Early childhood trauma, maladaptive cognitive patterns, and poor coping mechanisms.
  • Sociocultural: Poverty, discrimination, community violence, and culturally specific stressors common in resource-limited settings.

Stigma and Cultural Perceptions in Nigeria

Stigma remains a formidable barrier to mental healthcare utilization in Nigeria. Many communities attribute psychiatric symptoms to supernatural causes, witchcraft, or moral failure rather than medical illness. Consequently, individuals often seek help from traditional or religious healers before visiting formal health facilities, frequently leading to delayed presentation, human rights abuses in prayer houses, and chronic disability. Nurses play a crucial role in community sensitization and patient advocacy to bridge this treatment gap.

Psychiatric Assessment and Clinical Interviewing

Assessment in psychiatric nursing relies heavily on observation, active listening, and the Mental State Examination (MSE). The MSE evaluates current cognitive and psychiatric functioning, covering appearance, behavior, speech, mood, affect, thought process, perception, and insight. Establishing rapport before diving into sensitive questioning ensures accurate data collection. Nurses must remain non-judgmental, observing both verbal cues and subtle non-verbal behaviors that might indicate underlying anxiety, hallucinations, or depression.

Therapeutic Communication

Therapeutic communication forms the bedrock of the nurse-patient relationship. Unlike social conversations, interactions with psychiatric patients are goal-directed and focused on the patient's needs. Techniques such as active listening, open-ended questioning, reflecting, and offering self help build trust and encourage emotional expression. Nurses must avoid non-therapeutic responses like giving advice, offering false reassurance, or asking "why" questions, which can make the patient defensive.

Multidisciplinary Management and Treatment Modalities

Comprehensive psychiatric care requires a collaborative team including psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, clinical psychologists, psychiatric social workers, and occupational therapists. Treatment modalities span pharmacotherapy (antipsychotics, antidepressants, mood stabilizers), psychotherapy (such as cognitive behavioral therapy), and psychosocial rehabilitation. Nurses administer medications, monitor for adverse side effects like extrapyramidal symptoms, and facilitate group therapy sessions to promote social reintegration.

Legal, Ethical, and Human Rights Issues

Psychiatric nursing operates within strict legal and ethical boundaries designed to protect vulnerable individuals. Key concepts include informed consent, confidentiality, and the least restrictive environment principle. In emergency situations involving imminent harm to self or others, involuntary admission may be legally instituted. Nurses must advocate fiercely against human rights abuses, such as the unlawful shackling or chaining of psychiatric patients in traditional or religious camps, ensuring dignity and autonomy are preserved.
