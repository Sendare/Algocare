Mental Illness

Introduction to Mental Illness in Nursing

Mental health and mental illness exist on a continuous spectrum rather than as rigid opposites. A person can experience periods of optimal well-being, moderate distress, or severe psychiatric impairment at different points in life. In nursing, understanding this fluid nature helps prevent framing patients strictly by their diagnosis. Mental illness refers to clinically significant disturbances in cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior that reflect dysfunction in the underlying psychological, biological, or developmental processes. Psychiatric-mental health nursing focuses on promoting mental health, preventing mental distress, and caring for individuals experiencing these alterations through holistic, evidence-based interventions.

Classification and Major Categories of Mental Disorders

To provide structured care and clear communication among healthcare teams, psychiatric professionals use standardized classification systems like the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR). Major categories include:

  • Mood disorders: Conditions characterized by severe disturbances in mood, such as major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder.
  • Anxiety disorders: Conditions featuring excessive fear, panic, and behavioral disturbances that interfere with daily functioning.
  • Psychotic disorders: Severe mental disorders, notably schizophrenia, marked by a break with reality, hallucinations, and delusions.
  • Personality disorders: Enduring patterns of inner experience and behavior that deviate markedly from cultural expectations and cause significant distress.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Mental illnesses rarely stem from a single isolated cause. Instead, the biopsychosocial model explains that psychiatric conditions develop from a complex interaction of biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors.

Stigma and Cultural Perceptions in Nigeria

Cultural beliefs heavily shape how communities perceive and respond to mental illness. In many Nigerian communities, mental health problems are frequently attributed to spiritual attack, generational curses, or moral failure rather than medical pathology. This deep-seated stigma causes families to hide affected relatives, delay orthodox medical care, or seek help from traditional and spiritual healing homes first. As nurses, recognizing these cultural frameworks is vital. Effective care requires bridging traditional beliefs with modern psychiatric nursing, avoiding judgment, and actively educating communities to view mental illness as a treatable health condition.

Psychiatric Assessment and Clinical Interviewing

The psychiatric interview relies heavily on observation, active listening, and systematic inquiry. A core component of this evaluation is the Mental Status Examination (MSE), which provides a snapshot of the patient's current psychological functioning. Key areas evaluated during an MSE include appearance, behavior, speech, mood, affect, thought process, thought content, perceptual disturbances, cognition, and insight. Unlike a physical exam that focuses on body systems, the MSE evaluates how a person looks, thinks, and feels at the exact moment of the interaction.

Therapeutic Communication and Nurse-Patient Relationship

The therapeutic nurse-patient relationship is the foundation of psychiatric nursing care. Establishing trust requires clear boundaries, empathy, and active listening. Nurses use specific therapeutic communication techniques to encourage patients to express their feelings without fear of judgment. Open-ended questions, restating, reflecting, and offering self are valuable tools. Conversely, non-therapeutic techniques—such as giving advice, offering false reassurance, or asking "why" questions—can shut down communication and make the patient feel dismissed or defensive.

Multidisciplinary Management and Treatment Modalities

Comprehensive psychiatric care requires a collaborative team including psychiatrists, psychiatric nurses, clinical psychologists, social workers, and occupational therapists. Treatment modalities span multiple approaches:

  • Pharmacotherapy: The use of psychotropic medications (antipsychotics, antidepressants, mood stabilizers, and anxiolytics) to manage chemical imbalances.
  • Psychotherapy: Individual or group talk therapy modalities like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) to reframe negative thought patterns.
  • Milieu therapy: Structuring the physical and social environment of the inpatient unit to provide a safe, therapeutic space for recovery.
  • Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT): A physical treatment option reserved for severe, treatment-resistant depression or catatonia.

Legal, Ethical, and Human Rights Issues

Caring for psychiatric patients requires a delicate balance between patient autonomy and safety. Nurses must understand legal frameworks regarding voluntary versus involuntary admission, patient rights, and confidentiality. In emergency situations where a patient poses an immediate danger to self or others, restraints or seclusion may be used as a last resort. However, these restrictive measures carry strict legal and ethical obligations: they must be ordered by a clinician, monitored continuously, and discontinued at the earliest possible moment to protect the patient's human dignity.
