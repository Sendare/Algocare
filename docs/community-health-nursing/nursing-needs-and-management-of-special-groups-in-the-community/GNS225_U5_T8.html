Aging and Care of the Elderly

Introduction to Aging and Gerontology

Gerontology is the scientific study of aging, spanning biological, psychological, and sociological domains. Geriatrics, by contrast, focuses specifically on the medical care and treatment of diseases in older adults. Chronological age, determined by the calendar, often differs significantly from physiological age, which reflects the actual wear and tear on body systems. In Nigeria, chronological benchmarks for older adulthood often align with retirement policies around sixty or sixty-five years, though individual functional capacity varies widely based on lifelong nutrition, labor, and access to healthcare. Understanding this distinction helps healthcare providers avoid treating every older person as frail or diseased, recognizing aging as a normal, diverse human developmental stage.

Physiological and Psychological Changes Associated with Aging

As the human body ages, progressive structural and functional declines occur across multiple organ systems. These changes are expected biological processes rather than pathologies, though they increase vulnerability to illness. - **Cardiovascular**: Decreased cardiac output, stiffening of the arterial walls, and a slower maximum heart rate. - **Respiratory**: Reduced chest wall compliance, weakened respiratory muscles, and diminished gas exchange efficiency. - **Musculoskeletal**: Gradual loss of muscle mass known as sarcopenia, decreased bone mineral density, and joint cartilage wear. - **Sensory**: Presbyopia, high-frequency hearing loss, and reduced olfactory and taste receptors. Psychologically, older adults navigate shifting social roles, retirement, and bereavement. While cognitive processing speed may slow slightly, crystallized intelligence and wisdom generally remain stable or improve throughout later life.

Common Health Conditions and Chronic Illnesses in the Elderly

Older adults frequently manage multiple chronic conditions simultaneously, a state known as multimorbidity. Hypertension remains one of the most prevalent cardiovascular issues, heavily contributing to stroke risks if poorly controlled. Type 2 diabetes mellitus often presents with subtle symptoms in seniors, making routine screening vital. Osteoarthritis causes chronic joint pain, directly impacting mobility and independence. Furthermore, neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease progressively impair cognitive function, memory, and daily self-care abilities. Managing these overlapping illnesses requires a shift from acute curative care to long-term chronic disease surveillance and symptom control.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment in Community Health

A Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment is a multidimensional, multidisciplinary diagnostic process designed to determine an older adult's medical, psychosocial, and functional capability. Unlike standard medical evaluations that focus strictly on pathology, this approach evaluates how health issues impact daily living. 1. **Functional Status**: Assessed via Activities of Daily Living and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living. 2. **Cognitive and Mental Health**: Screening for depression, delirium, and dementia using validated tools like the Mini-Mental State Examination. 3. **Social Circumstances**: Evaluating housing suitability, financial security, and availability of family caregivers. 4. **Environmental Safety**: Identifying domestic hazards that increase fall risks. This holistic evaluation allows community health nurses to formulate coordinated, long-term care plans tailored to the individual's specific needs.

Promoting Independence, Safety, and Mobility

Maintaining mobility is directly linked to preserving dignity, autonomy, and physical health in older adults. Sedentary behavior accelerates muscle wasting and joint stiffness. Nurses encourage regular, low-impact physical activity such as walking or seated stretching to maintain joint flexibility and cardiovascular health. Home safety assessments are equally critical; simple adjustments like removing loose rugs, ensuring adequate lighting, installing grab bars in bathrooms, and clearing cluttered walkways drastically reduce fall risks. When assistive devices like walking frames or canes are necessary, nursing staff must ensure proper sizing and patient education on their correct use.

Nutritional and Pharmacological Considerations in the Elderly

Aging alters both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, changing how drugs are absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted. Reduced renal clearance and hepatic blood flow mean older adults are highly susceptible to drug accumulation and toxicity. Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—greatly increases the risk of adverse drug reactions and dangerous drug interactions. Explicit criteria tools, such as the Beers criteria, help clinicians identify medications that should be avoided in older populations. Nutritionally, seniors face decreased caloric requirements due to lower basal metabolic rates, yet their need for high-quality protein, calcium, vitamin D, and fiber remains high. Dental issues, reduced sense of taste and smell, and social isolation can severely depress appetite, leading to malnutrition.

Family Caregiving and Community Support Systems in Nigeria

In traditional Nigerian society, caring for aging parents is deeply rooted in cultural expectations of filial piety and community solidarity. The extended family system has historically acted as the primary safety net for older adults. However, rapid urbanization, economic pressures, and the migration of younger generations to cities are straining these traditional support structures. Formal eldercare facilities remain scarce and often carry social stigma. Community health nurses play a vital role by supporting informal family caregivers, providing education on home nursing techniques, offering respite care guidance, and linking families with available local religious or community-based support networks.
