Aging and Care of the Elderly

Introduction to Aging and Gerontology

Aging is a universal biological process that affects every living organism, characterized by a progressive decline in functional capacity and an increased vulnerability to environmental stressors. In nursing, understanding this transition requires distinguishing between normal, age-related changes and disease pathology. Gerontology is the multidisciplinary scientific study of aging, covering physical, mental, and social aspects, while geriatrics focuses specifically on the medical care, treatment, and prevention of diseases in older adults.

As life expectancy gradually increases globally and within sub-Saharan Africa, the demographic landscape shifts toward an older population. This demographic transition brings unique health and social challenges, requiring nurses to adapt their approach from acute, short-term disease management to long-term chronic care and functional preservation. Recognizing older adults not merely as patients with failing bodies, but as individuals with rich life experiences, shapes the foundation of respectful, holistic nursing practice.

Physiological and Psychological Changes Associated with Aging

Body systems undergo gradual, cumulative structural and functional alterations over time. Recognizing these baseline changes prevents nurses from misinterpreting normal senescence as acute pathology.

  • Cardiovascular: Arterial walls thicken and lose elasticity, leading to increased systolic blood pressure and reduced maximum heart rate during physical exertion.
  • Respiratory: Chest wall compliance decreases due to calcification of costal cartilages, and residual volume increases while vital capacity drops.
  • Integumentary: The epidermis thins, sweat glands decrease output, and subcutaneous fat diminishes, increasing the risk of skin tears and pressure injuries.
  • Sensory: Presbyopia and presbycusis impair near vision and high-frequency hearing respectively, which directly impacts communication and social interaction.

Psychologically, older adults navigate transitions like retirement, bereavement, and shifts in family roles. While cognitive processing speed may slow slightly, core intelligence and wisdom remain stable. Depression is not a normal part of aging, though grief and social isolation frequently trigger mental health struggles.

Common Health Conditions and Chronic Illnesses

Older adults frequently live with multiple chronic conditions simultaneously, a state known as multimorbidity. Managing these conditions requires shifting priorities from curing acute infections to stabilizing chronic symptoms and maximizing functional independence.

  • Hypertension: A major risk factor for stroke and heart failure, requiring consistent blood pressure monitoring and salt reduction.
  • Osteoarthritis: Degenerative joint disease causing pain and stiffness, often managed through gentle exercise, weight management, and analgesics.
  • Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus: Characterized by insulin resistance, demanding careful blood glucose tracking to prevent peripheral neuropathy and foot ulcers.
  • Dementia: A progressive cognitive decline, most commonly caused by Alzheimer's disease, affecting memory, reasoning, and daily functioning.

A common clinical mistake is attributing new confusion or behavioral changes solely to dementia without ruling out delirium caused by a urinary tract infection or dehydration.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment

Evaluating an older adult goes beyond a standard medical checkup. A Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) is a multidimensional, multidisciplinary evaluation designed to assess an older person's functional ability, physical health, cognitive and mental health, and socio-environmental circumstances.

Nurses use standardized tools to measure specific domains:

  • Activities of Daily Living (ADLs): Measures basic self-care tasks like bathing, dressing, toileting, and feeding.
  • Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs): Evaluates more complex abilities needed for independent living, such as managing finances, cooking, and taking medications.
  • Cognitive Screening: Tools like the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) help detect early memory and thinking impairments.
  • Fall Risk Assessment: Identifies hazards and physical weaknesses that could lead to dangerous slips and fractures.

The primary goal of a CGA is to create a coordinated, long-term care plan that preserves independence and improves the overall quality of life.

Promoting Independence, Safety, and Mobility

Maintaining mobility is one of the strongest predictors of longevity and independence in older adults. When movement declines, the risk of pressure ulcers, muscle atrophy, deep vein thrombosis, and social isolation rises sharply. Nurses must encourage safe physical activity tailored to individual capacity, even for those requiring walking aids or wheelchairs.

Environmental safety is equally critical. Falls are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in the elderly. Prevention strategies include removing loose rugs, ensuring adequate lighting in hallways and bathrooms, installing grab bars near toilets, and encouraging the use of sturdy footwear. Restraints should be avoided, as they frequently increase agitation, muscle deconditioning, and the risk of severe injury from attempts to escape.

Nutritional and Pharmacological Considerations

Nutritional needs change with age, yet many older adults face malnutrition due to poor dentition, diminished taste and smell, chronic illness, or social isolation. Nurses should monitor weight changes, encourage nutrient-dense foods, and ensure adequate hydration, keeping in mind that the sensation of thirst declines with age, increasing dehydration risks.

Medication management in the elderly is especially complex due to pharmacokinetics—how the body absorbs, distributes, metabolizes, and excretes drugs. Reduced liver and kidney function means drugs stay in the system longer, raising toxicity risks. Polypharmacy, the concurrent use of multiple medications, frequently leads to adverse drug reactions and confusion. Nurses must regularly review medication charts, watch for interactions, and educate patients on correct dosing schedules.

Family Caregiving and Community Support Systems in Nigeria

In Nigeria and across much of West Africa, elder care has traditionally been a family and community responsibility rooted in deep cultural values of respect for elders. Extended family networks provide housing, emotional encouragement, and financial assistance. However, rapid urbanization, economic pressures, and the migration of younger family members to cities are straining these traditional support systems.

Family caregivers often experience high levels of physical fatigue, financial strain, and emotional exhaustion, known as caregiver burnout. Community health nurses play a vital role by supporting these families through home visits, health education, and connecting them with local support groups or available social welfare resources. Recognizing and relieving caregiver stress is just as important as treating the older adult patient.
