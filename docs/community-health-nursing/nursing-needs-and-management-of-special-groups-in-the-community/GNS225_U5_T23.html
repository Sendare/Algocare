Violence Against Women

Introduction and Scope of Gender-Based Violence

Violence against women, often referred to as gender-based violence, is a major public health and human rights crisis. It affects individuals across all social, economic, and educational backgrounds, though vulnerable populations face disproportionate risks. Nurses frequently encounter survivors in emergency rooms, maternity wards, and community health clinics. Understanding that this violence is rooted in unequal power dynamics helps clinicians look beyond physical injuries to address the full scope of patient safety and well-being.

Types and Manifestations of Abuse

Abuse takes many forms, often overlapping within a single relationship. Recognizing these distinct categories ensures nurses document and assess patient needs comprehensively.

  • Physical abuse: Acts inflicting bodily harm, ranging from slapping and shoving to severe beating and weapon use.
  • Emotional and psychological abuse: Constant belittling, threats, intimidation, and isolation from friends and family to break down self-esteem.
  • Sexual abuse: Any non-consensual sexual contact, coercion, or assault, regardless of whether it occurs inside or outside marriage.
  • Economic abuse: Controlling all financial resources, forbidding employment, or withholding money for basic needs to maintain dependence.

Health Consequences for Survivors

The toll of violence extends far beyond immediate trauma. Survivors often experience a complex mix of acute and chronic health issues.

CategoryCommon Manifestations
PhysicalFractures, lacerations, chronic pain, unwanted pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections
PsychologicalDepression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, suicidal ideation
SocialIsolation, loss of employment, housing instability, stigmatization

Identification and Clinical Assessment

Identifying survivors requires vigilance, as patients rarely disclose abuse spontaneously due to fear, shame, or threats from the abuser. Nurses should look for red flags such as injuries inconsistent with the explanation given, frequent emergency visits, or a partner who insists on answering all questions for the patient. Universal screening in safe, private settings encourages disclosure. Asking direct, non-judgmental questions builds trust and opens the door for survivors to share their experiences.

Nursing Interventions and First-Line Support

When a survivor discloses abuse, the nurse's immediate response shapes the healing journey. First-line support follows the principles of validation, safety, and empowerment. Nurses must listen without judgment, believe the patient's story, and reassure them that the abuse is not their fault. Prioritizing physical safety involves treating acute injuries and collaborating with social workers to develop a safety plan. Documenting injuries with objective descriptions and photographs, with patient consent, provides crucial evidence if legal action is pursued later.

Prevention and Community Advocacy

Healthcare professionals play a vital role beyond the bedside by engaging in community prevention strategies. Education campaigns challenge harmful gender norms and promote healthy relationship standards in schools and community centers. Nurses collaborate with multidisciplinary teams, including legal aid organizations, law enforcement, and shelter networks, to strengthen referral pathways. Advocating for protective policies ensures that survivors have access to justice, safe housing, and long-term economic empowerment resources.
