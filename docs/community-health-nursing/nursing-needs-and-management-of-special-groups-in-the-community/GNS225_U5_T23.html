Violence Against Women

Introduction to Violence Against Women in Public Health

Violence against women is recognized globally as a critical public health crisis and a severe violation of human rights. It transcends social, economic, and geographic boundaries, affecting individuals across all strata of society. Within the Nigerian healthcare context, encountering survivors of gender-based violence is a frequent reality in emergency departments, antenatal clinics, and primary healthcare centers. Understanding this issue requires looking beyond individual interpersonal conflicts to examine systemic gender inequality and imbalances in power dynamics. Public health frameworks treat this violence not merely as a private domestic matter, but as an epidemic contributing significantly to morbidity, mortality, and lost societal productivity. Healthcare providers occupy a unique frontline position; clinics often represent the only safe space where an abused woman can seek confidential help.

Types and Manifestations of Gender-Based Violence

Gender-based violence encompasses a wide spectrum of harmful acts directed against women based on their biological sex and socially constructed gender roles. Recognizing these diverse manifestations is essential for comprehensive clinical assessment.

  • Physical Violence: Acts resulting in bodily harm, pain, or trauma, including slapping, beating, burning, choking, and the use of weapons.
  • Sexual Violence: Non-consensual sexual contact, attempted or completed rape, forced prostitution, and reproductive coercion.
  • Psychological and Emotional Violence: Verbal insults, constant humiliation, isolation from family and friends, threats of harm, and extreme possessiveness or stalking.
  • Economic Violence: Denying access to financial resources, forbidding employment or educational pursuits, and sabotaging job retention.
  • Harmful Traditional Practices: Cultural or religious rituals deeply rooted in patriarchy, such as female genital mutilation, child marriage, and widowhood rites.

Root Causes and Socio-Cultural Risk Factors

The persistence of violence against women is sustained by complex structural and cultural foundations. Patriarchy structures societies in ways that grant men disproportionate authority over decision-making, resource allocation, and family discipline. Cultural norms in many communities perpetuate the notion that male dominance and female subordination are natural or even divinely ordained. Economic dependency often traps women within abusive environments; lacking independent income or property rights, leaving an aborer threatens absolute destitution for both the mother and her children. Furthermore, deeply ingrained stigmas surrounding divorce and sexual assault frequently force victims to endure abuse silently rather than face public shame or rejection by their extended families.

Physical, Psychological, and Social Health Consequences

The toll of gender-based violence extends far beyond acute injuries, manifesting as chronic, debilitating health conditions. Physically, survivors frequently present with unexplained chronic pain, gastrointestinal disorders, pelvic inflammatory disease, and traumatic brain injuries. Sexual violence introduces high risks of unwanted pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. Psychologically, victims experience profound trauma, presenting with major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and suicidal ideation. Socially, the stigma associated with abuse can lead to severe isolation, loss of employment, and disruption of family support systems. In worst-case scenarios, domestic violence culminates in homicide or suicide, representing a tragic and preventable loss of life.

Community Health Assessment and Identification of Survivors

Many survivors of violence never explicitly mention their abuse unless specifically and compassionately asked. Healthcare workers must maintain a high index of suspicion, looking out for clinical red flags such as frequent emergency room visits for vague injuries, delayed seeking of medical care, overly dominant partners who answer questions on the patient's behalf, and physical injuries inconsistent with the given explanation. Routine screening in antenatal and emergency departments normalizes talking about safety and relationships. Asking direct, non-judgmental screening questions in a private, confidential setting allows women the opportunity to disclose abuse when they feel psychologically safe to do so.

Nursing Interventions, First-Line Support, and Referral Pathways

When a survivor discloses violence, the immediate nursing response sets the tone for healing and safety. Nurses apply the principles of first-line support: listening empathetically without judgment, inquiring about immediate safety needs, validating the survivor's experience, and ensuring confidentiality. It is vital never to blame, pressure, or rush the patient into making decisions she is not ready for. Clinical management includes treating physical injuries, offering emergency contraception and prophylaxis for sexually transmitted infections when sexual assault has occurred, and documenting findings objectively in the medical record. Afterward, nurses connect survivors with multi-sectoral referral networks, including social workers, legal aid organizations, safe shelters, and psychological counseling services.

Legal Frameworks and Policies in Nigeria

Addressing violence against women requires robust legislative backing to protect victims and hold perpetrators accountable. In Nigeria, the landmark Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act, signed into law federally in 2015 and subsequently adopted by numerous states, criminalizes various forms of domestic abuse, harmful traditional practices, and sexual assault. Additionally, the Child Rights Act protects young girls from underage forced marriages. Despite these progressive laws, implementation remains uneven across different regions due to jurisdictional challenges, cultural resistance, and poor enforcement mechanisms. Nurses must be cognizant of existing legal protections to guide survivors toward legal aid services effectively.

Community Advocacy and Prevention Strategies

Clinical treatment alone cannot eradicate gender-based violence; primary prevention must address root cultural attitudes within communities. Nurses play a vital role in community advocacy by engaging local leaders, religious figures, and youth groups in open dialogues regarding gender equality and human rights. Educational programs in schools help challenge harmful stereotypes and promote respectful relationships from an early age. Mobilizing community watch networks and support groups creates a protective environment where abuse is actively discouraged rather than hidden in silence. Public health campaigns empower women by increasing awareness of their legal rights and available support services.
