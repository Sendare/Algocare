Violence Against Women

Introduction to Violence Against Women in Public Health

Violence against women (VAW) is widely recognized by global health authorities and nursing organizations as a major public health crisis and a profound violation of human rights. In Nigeria and across the globe, gender-based violence affects individuals across all socioeconomic, educational, and cultural strata. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice, understanding VAW is essential because healthcare facilities are frequently the first point of contact for survivors seeking medical attention, trauma care, or emotional support. Public health consequences extend far beyond immediate physical injuries, encompassing chronic mental health disorders, reproductive health complications, and increased mortality rates. As frontline healthcare professionals, nurses occupy a uniquely strategic position to identify hidden abuse, provide compassionate, non-judgmental care, and guide victims safely through multidisciplinary intervention pathways. Recognizing VAW as a public health issue shifts the clinical perspective from treating isolated trauma injuries to addressing the systemic determinants of health that perpetuate interpersonal violence. By integrating gender-sensitive care and evidence-based screening protocols into routine clinical assessments, nursing professionals can drastically reduce morbidity and mortality associated with domestic and community violence, ultimately fostering a safer and healthier society for women and children in Nigeria.

Types and Manifestations of Gender-Based Violence

Gender-based violence manifests in diverse and often overlapping ways that profoundly compromise a woman's health and well-being. Physical violence involves any act of bodily harm, ranging from slaps and bruising to severe trauma, fractures, and homicide. Sexual violence encompasses non-consensual sexual contact, attempted or completed rape, and coerced sexual acts, which carry immense risks for unwanted pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. Emotional and psychological violence involves continuous verbal abuse, humiliation, intimidation, isolation, and threats, which systematically erode self-esteem and create severe psychological distress. Furthermore, economic violence restricts a woman's access to financial resources, employment opportunities, and basic household necessities, thereby enforcing dependency and making it exceedingly difficult to escape abusive environments. Harmful traditional practices, such as female genital mutilation (FGM), early or forced child marriage, and widowhood rites, represent culturally institutionalized forms of gender-based violence prevalent in many parts of Nigeria. Recognizing these distinct categories is vital for nursing students, as survivors rarely present with just one type of abuse; instead, they often exhibit a complex web of overlapping physical, emotional, and social trauma that requires comprehensive assessment and individualized care planning.

Root Causes and Socio-Cultural Risk Factors

Understanding the root causes and socio-cultural risk factors of violence against women is critical for effective prevention and clinical intervention. Patriarchal social structures, deeply entrenched gender norms, and unequal power dynamics between men and women form the foundational bedrock upon which gender-based violence thrives. In many Nigerian societies, traditional expectations demand female subservience, silence, and obedience, which can normalize abusive behaviors and stigmatize survivors who attempt to speak out. Economic vulnerability, poverty, lack of educational opportunities, and financial dependence significantly heighten a woman's susceptibility to abuse while simultaneously creating barriers to leaving dangerous relationships. Cultural acceptance of corporal punishment within marital relationships, coupled with weak institutional enforcement of legal protections, creates an environment of impunity for perpetrators. Additionally, historical normalization of violence, substance abuse, and exposure to childhood trauma or domestic violence within the family cycle further perpetuate abusive behaviors across generations. For nursing students, analyzing these socio-cultural determinants prevents victim-blaming and ensures that care approaches account for the broader environmental pressures that trap women in cycles of abuse, guiding more holistic and empathetic clinical interactions.

Physical, Psychological, and Social Health Consequences

Violence against women inflicts profound physical, psychological, and social health consequences that often persist long after the acute abuse has ended. Physical injuries range from acute trauma, lacerations, and fractures to chronic pain syndromes, gastrointestinal disorders, traumatic brain injuries, and permanent disabilities. Reproductive health consequences are particularly devastating, including forced or unwanted pregnancies, unsafe abortions, high rates of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) such as HIV, pelvic inflammatory disease, and pregnancy complications like miscarriage or low birth weight. Psychologically, survivors frequently experience post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), severe clinical depression, anxiety disorders, chronic sleep disturbances, substance abuse, and suicidal ideation. Socially, the stigma surrounding violence often leads to social isolation, estrangement from support networks, loss of employment, and discrimination within the community. For nurses, recognizing these multifaceted health outcomes is essential, as women presenting with vague somatic complaints, chronic pelvic pain, or recurrent depressive symptoms may actually be concealing histories of ongoing domestic abuse. Comprehensive nursing care must therefore address both the immediate physical trauma and the invisible psychological scars through integrated, empathetic health service delivery.

Community Health Assessment and Identification of Survivors

Community health assessment and the identification of survivors require high levels of clinical vigilance, cultural competence, and interpersonal sensitivity from nursing professionals. Because survivors of violence often feel deep shame, fear, or self-blame, they rarely disclose abuse spontaneously during routine medical encounters. Nurses must therefore cultivate a safe, private, and non-judgmental clinical environment that encourages open communication and builds patient trust. Conducting routine, universal screening for gender-based violence during antenatal visits, emergency department admissions, and family planning consultations is a recommended public health strategy. Nurses should look for clinical red flags, such as injuries inconsistent with explanations provided, frequent unexplained hospital visits, partner controlling behaviors during the consultation, delay in seeking treatment, and somatic symptoms without clear organic pathology. Effective assessment also involves observing non-verbal cues like agitation, withdrawal, or hypervigilance. Utilizing validated screening tools and asking direct, compassionate questions in private ensures that hidden survivors are identified early, paving the way for timely medical evaluation, safety planning, and appropriate interprofessional referrals.

Nursing Interventions, First-Line Support, and Referral Pathways

When providing care to survivors of gender-based violence, nurses must prioritize immediate physical safety, psychological stabilization, and trauma-informed care principles. First-line support follows the LIVES acronym: Listen closely with empathy and without judgment, Inquire about needs and concerns, Validate the survivor's experiences, Enhance safety, and Support connection to additional services. Nurses must treat acute physical injuries, provide emergency contraception and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV prevention within the recommended window after sexual assault, and carefully document findings for potential forensic purposes with the patient's explicit consent. Establishing clear referral pathways is critical; nurses do not work in isolation but must connect survivors with social workers, legal aid organizations, psychological counselors, and specialized shelter services within the community. Maintaining strict patient confidentiality and ensuring informed consent before sharing any information are non-negotiable ethical duties that protect the survivor from further harm or retaliation. Through structured, compassionate nursing interventions, healthcare providers transform clinical spaces into sanctuaries of healing and empowerment for vulnerable women.

Legal Frameworks and Policies in Nigeria

A thorough understanding of legal frameworks and policies governing violence against women in Nigeria is essential for nursing advocacy and patient protection. Landmark legislation includes the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) (VAPP) Act, enacted at the federal level and adopted by numerous Nigerian states, which criminalizes various forms of physical, sexual, emotional, and economic abuse, as well as harmful traditional practices. Additionally, the Child Rights Act protects underage girls from early marriage and exploitation, while international frameworks such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) set global human rights standards. Despite these progressive laws, implementation and enforcement often face significant hurdles, including institutional corruption, societal normalization of abuse, and lack of awareness among law enforcement and healthcare workers. Nurses must understand their legal obligations regarding mandatory reporting, documentation of forensic evidence, and respecting patient autonomy regarding legal action. By knowing the legal rights of survivors and available statutory protections, nurses can effectively bridge the gap between clinical health services and the justice system, empowering women to seek legal recourse and protection.

Community Advocacy and Prevention Strategies

Community advocacy and prevention strategies are vital pillars in the public health approach to eradicating violence against women. Prevention efforts must operate at multiple levels: primary prevention aims to stop violence before it occurs by challenging harmful gender stereotypes, educating youth, and promoting gender equality through community and school-based programs. Secondary prevention focuses on early identification and prompt intervention for women experiencing abuse, while tertiary prevention involves rehabilitation and support for survivors to prevent recurrence and long-term disability. Nurses play a leading role in community advocacy by organizing health education workshops, engaging traditional and religious leaders who hold immense influence in Nigerian communities, and participating in public awareness campaigns during events like the 16 Days of Activism Against Gender-Based Violence. Effective advocacy also involves collaborating with non-governmental organizations (NGOs), law enforcement agencies, and social services to build robust community support networks. By transforming community mindsets and fostering environments that reject misogyny and violence, nursing professionals contribute actively to sustainable social change and the protection of women's rights across Nigeria.
