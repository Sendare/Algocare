Street Children

Defining Streetism: Categories and Terminology

The term **street children** describes minors for whom the street has become home, source of livelihood, or primary place of socialization. Distinctions exist in how these youth interact with their environment. UNICEF categorizes them into two main groups: - **Children of the street**: Those who live and sleep on the streets entirely detached from family support networks. - **Children on the street**: Those who work or beg on the streets during the day but return home to family dwellings at night. Understanding this distinction helps public health workers tailor interventions appropriately. A child sleeping under a flyover faces immediate environmental hazards that differ from one returning to a crowded urban slum every evening, though both experience severe deprivation.

Socioeconomic Drivers and Epidemiology in Nigeria

Streetism in Nigeria is heavily concentrated in rapidly growing urban centers like Lagos, Kano, Ibadan, and Onitsha. High rates of poverty, large family sizes, and inadequate social safety nets create conditions where households struggle to provide basic needs. Economic shocks, inflation, and the rising cost of living force dependent minors into the informal labor market. Traditional systems of extended family care, which historically absorbed orphaned or vulnerable youth, are increasingly strained by economic hardship, leaving fewer buffers against destitution.

Push and Pull Factors Leading to Streetism

The transition from home to the pavement is rarely random. **Push factors** drive children away from their households. These include domestic violence, physical abuse, parental substance abuse, neglect, the death of caregivers, and extreme household poverty. Conversely, **pull factors** draw minors toward the street environment. Urban peer groups, the allure of immediate cash from informal jobs or begging, the desire for autonomy, and the perceived excitement of city life create a strong magnetic effect. In certain northern Nigerian contexts, informal religious education systems have sometimes been exploited, where pupils sent away for Qur'anic study are left without adequate sustenance, pushing them into street begging.

Health Risks, Vulnerabilities, and Clinical Hazards

Life on the pavement exposes minors to severe physical health hazards. Malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies stunt physical growth. Environmental exposure leads to high rates of respiratory tract infections, malaria, and skin infestations. Because formal health facilities are often inaccessible due to financial constraints or social discrimination, minor injuries frequently progress to chronic ulcers or systemic infections. Substance abuse, particularly volatile solvent inhalation like 'solution' or fuel sniffing, causes permanent neurological and hepatic damage. Furthermore, street youth face high risks of physical violence, motor vehicle accidents, and sexual exploitation, increasing their vulnerability to sexually transmitted infections and human immunodeficiency virus.

Psychosocial and Developmental Sequelae

Chronic exposure to trauma shapes the cognitive and emotional architecture of street children. Prolonged survival stress triggers elevated cortisol levels, predisposing youth to anxiety, depression, and complex post-traumatic stress disorder. Trust is severely damaged, making interpersonal relationships volatile. Education is frequently disrupted, leading to functional illiteracy that limits future employment horizons. The absence of positive adult role models impairs moral and social development, fostering survival-driven behavioral patterns that society often misinterprets purely as criminality rather than adaptation to an adversarial environment.

Community Health Nursing Assessment and Identification

Community health nurses play a pivotal role in identifying and engaging street-connected children. Because these youth often avoid formal health institutions out of fear of arrest, harassment, or stigmatization, nurses must utilize outreach strategies, working through drop-in centers, mobile clinics, and trusted community allies. Assessment requires a non-judgmental, trauma-informed approach. Nurses must evaluate physical status, nutritional grading, immunization history, signs of substance toxicity, and psychological distress. Building rapport takes precedence over clinical speed; forcing intrusive questioning too early will drive the child away permanently.

Intervention Strategies, Rehabilitation, and Reintegration

Addressing streetism requires a multi-tiered continuum of care. **Primary interventions** focus on community-level poverty alleviation and family support to prevent initial displacement. **Secondary interventions** involve street-level outreach, provision of food, emergency shelter, and basic first aid through drop-in centers. **Tertiary interventions** encompass comprehensive rehabilitation: family tracing, mediation, vocational training, formal or non-formal education, and safe reintegration. Reintegration must be handled cautiously; returning a child to an abusive home without addressing the root push factors invariably leads to repeat desertion.

Policy Frameworks and Multi-Sectoral Stakeholder Roles

Tackling streetism transcends the capacity of the healthcare sector alone. It demands coordinated collaboration among government ministries, social welfare agencies, law enforcement, non-governmental organizations, and religious bodies. In Nigeria, the **Child Rights Act** provides a legal framework protecting minors from exploitation, abuse, and neglect, though enforcement varies across states. Public health nurses act as advocates, educators, and bridge-builders, linking vulnerable youth to social welfare programs and holding policymakers accountable for child protection implementation.
