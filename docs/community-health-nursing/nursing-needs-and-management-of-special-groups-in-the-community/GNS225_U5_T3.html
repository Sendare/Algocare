Street Children

Defining Street Children and Categories

Street children are individuals under the age of eighteen for whom the street has become home, source of livelihood, and primary environment. This population is rarely homogeneous, requiring nurses to distinguish between different levels of involvement to plan effective care.

  • Children of the street: Those who live, sleep, and work entirely on the streets, having broken most ties with their biological families.
  • Children on the street: Those who spend their days working or begging on the streets to support their families but return home to sleep at night.
  • Abandoned children: Those who have been completely rejected or left behind by family members with no intention of return, sliding quickly into the first category.

Socioeconomic Drivers in Nigeria

Streetism in Nigeria is deeply rooted in systemic economic challenges, poverty, and cultural dynamics. High rates of unemployment, inflation, and a lack of social security nets leave many households unable to feed their dependents. In some northern regions, the ancient Almajiri system—originally designed for Islamic scholarship—has been compromised by extreme poverty, turning young religious pupils into unsupervised street beggars. Family breakdown, domestic violence, and polygamous households lacking financial stability further compel children to seek survival independently in chaotic urban markets and motor parks.

Push and Pull Factors

The transition from a home environment to the street is driven by a dynamic interplay of forces that either force a child out or lure them in.

  • Push factors: Domestic abuse, harsh corporal punishment, step-parent neglect, parental substance abuse, and extreme rural poverty that make staying home unbearable.
  • Pull factors: The allure of city life, peer pressure from friends already on the street, the desire for quick money through hawking or scavenging, and a misplaced sense of independence.

Health Risks and Physical Vulnerabilities

Living exposed to the elements places street children at high risk for both acute and chronic health conditions. Environmental hazards include weather exposure, traffic accidents from roadside hawking, and physical violence from older gangs or law enforcement. Nutritional deficiencies lead to stunting and wasting. Furthermore, lack of clean water and sanitation promotes diarrheal diseases, skin infections, and parasitic infestations. Sexually active street youth face soaring rates of sexually transmitted infections and unintended pregnancies without access to confidential reproductive healthcare.

Psychosocial and Developmental Impacts

The psychological toll of streetism disrupts normal cognitive and emotional maturation. Constant survival mode fosters chronic hypervigilance, deep-seated mistrust, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress symptoms. Substance use—such as sniffing glue, smoking cannabis, or consuming crude alcohol—is common as a coping mechanism to numb hunger, cold, and emotional pain. This exposure often stunts social development, making it difficult for these children to form healthy interpersonal relationships or trust authority figures, including healthcare providers.

Community Health Nursing Assessment

Assessing street-connected children requires a non-judgmental, patient-centered approach. Nurses must conduct outreach directly in community hotspots like markets, motor parks, and under bridges where these children congregate. Establishing rapport is paramount because standard clinical interrogation fails with traumatized youth. Assessment tools must evaluate physical injuries, nutritional status, immunization gaps, and signs of substance abuse. Nurses should also screen for exploitation, human trafficking indicators, and commercial sexual exploitation while maintaining strict confidentiality.

Intervention, Rehabilitation, and Reintegration

Helping street children transition off the pavement involves a continuum of care starting with drop-in centers that provide basic needs like food, clean water, and wound care without demanding immediate behavioral changes. Long-term rehabilitation includes safe temporary shelter, formal education, or vocational training tailored to local job markets, such as carpentry, tailoring, or mechanics. Family tracing and reconciliation are critical steps, provided the home environment is safe and free from the original push factors that drove the child away.

Policy Frameworks and Multi-Sectoral Roles

Addressing streetism cannot be achieved by healthcare workers alone; it demands a coordinated multi-sectoral response. Policy instruments such as the Child Rights Act provide legal protections against child labor, abuse, and neglect, though enforcement varies across Nigerian states. Nurses collaborate with social welfare officers, law enforcement agencies, non-governmental organizations, and religious leaders to advocate for children's rights. Effective policy implementation requires strengthening social safety nets, providing free basic education, and holding exploitative employers or traffickers accountable.
