Breast Cancer

Introduction to Breast Cancer and Epidemiology in Nigeria

Breast cancer remains the most common malignancy affecting women globally, but the epidemiological pattern in Nigeria presents unique public health challenges. Unlike high-income countries where peak incidence occurs in older postmenopausal women, Nigerian patients are often diagnosed at a much younger age, frequently between thirty-five and fifty years old. Furthermore, presentation at health facilities is typically delayed. Many women arrive with advanced-stage disease due to structural barriers, lack of awareness, financial constraints, and heavy reliance on alternative therapies like traditional concoctions or spiritual healing. As a nursing professional, understanding these local epidemiological realities is crucial for effective patient engagement and overcoming delayed presentation barriers.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Breast

The female breast is a modified sweat gland situated over the pectoral muscles, extending from the second to the sixth ribs. Its primary functional components are arranged to produce milk during lactation.

  • Lobules: The milk-producing glands numbering fifteen to twenty lobes per breast.
  • Lactiferous Ducts: Tubes that transport milk from the lobules toward the nipple.
  • Areola and Nipple: The pigmented area surrounding the nipple, containing smooth muscle and sensory nerve endings.
  • Stroma and Fat: Supportive connective tissue and adipose tissue that determine the overall size and shape of the breast.
  • Lymphatic Drainage: A vast network of lymph vessels that primarily drain into the axillary lymph nodes, which is the main route for breast cancer metastasis.

Most breast cancers originate in the epithelial cells lining the terminal ductal-lobular units.

Risk Factors and Etiology of Breast Cancer

Breast cancer is a multifactorial disease influenced by genetic, hormonal, and environmental elements. Identifying these factors helps nurses target high-risk individuals for education and screening.

  • Gender and Age: Being female and advancing in age are the two most significant unmodifiable risk factors.
  • Family History: Inherited mutations in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes dramatically increase lifetime risk.
  • Hormonal Factors: Prolonged exposure to estrogen, such as early menarche before age twelve, late menopause after age fifty-five, and nulliparity, increases risk.
  • Lifestyle Factors: Regular alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, postmenopausal obesity, and smoking contribute to disease development.
  • Radiation Exposure: Previous therapeutic radiation to the chest wall during childhood or early adulthood elevates susceptibility.

Pathophysiology and Staging of Breast Cancer

Breast carcinogenesis begins with a genetic mutation that causes normal epithelial cells to multiply uncontrollably, forming a malignant neoplasm. These tumors can be non-invasive (carcinoma in situ), remaining confined within the milk ducts or lobules, or invasive, breaking through the basement membrane to invade surrounding tissue and metastasize to distant organs like the bones, lungs, liver, and brain.

Staging is determined using the TNM system, which evaluates Tumor size, Node involvement, and Metastasis. Clinical staging dictates the treatment plan and provides prognostic insight. Nurses must explain staging concepts simply, reassuring patients that knowing the exact stage helps the medical team choose the most effective therapies.

Clinical Manifestations and Early Warning Signs

Recognizing early warning signs saves lives. Most breast lumps are discovered by the patient herself. Nurses must teach women to watch for specific physical changes during self-examinations:

  • Painless Lump: A hard, firm, and irregular lump that is usually painless is the most common presentation.
  • Skin Changes: Dimpling, puckering, or an orange-peel appearance (peau d'orange) caused by lymphatic blockage.
  • Nipple Abnormalities: Recent inversion, retraction, or spontaneous bloody or clear nipple discharge.
  • Asymmetry: Unexplained change in the size or shape of one breast compared to the other.
  • Axillary Swelling: A noticeable lump or swelling in the armpit region.

Screening and Community Assessment Methods

Early detection drastically improves survival rates. Community health nurses play a vital role in promoting and conducting screening programs. The three primary screening methods include:

  1. Breast Self-Examination (BSE): Performed monthly by women from young adulthood onward to become familiar with their normal breast tissue.
  2. Clinical Breast Examination (CBE): Performed by a trained healthcare professional during routine clinic visits.
  3. Mammography: X-ray imaging of the breast capable of detecting microcalcifications and small tumors before they can be felt.

In resource-limited settings where mammography is scarce, regular BSE and routine CBE by nurses serve as critical life-saving interventions.

Medical, Surgical, and Nursing Management

Treatment for breast cancer is multimodal, combining surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, hormonal therapy, and targeted biological agents depending on the tumor's stage and receptor status.

  • Surgical Options: Procedures range from lumpectomy (breast-conserving surgery removing only the tumor) to modified radical mastectomy (removal of the entire breast and axillary lymph nodes).
  • Adjuvant Therapies: Chemotherapy destroys rapidly dividing cells, while hormonal therapies like tamoxifen block estrogen receptors in hormone-positive cancers.
  • Nursing Care: Nurses manage postoperative care, monitor surgical drains, assess for complications like lymphedema (arm swelling due to lymph node removal), and provide psychological support for body image disturbances.

Palliative Care and Rehabilitation

For patients with advanced, incurable breast cancer, the focus shifts to palliative care and quality of life. Palliative nursing relieves physical symptoms such as chronic bone pain, fatigue, and dyspnea while addressing psychological, social, and spiritual distress.

Rehabilitation involves physical therapy to restore arm movement after surgery, psychological counseling to cope with depression or anxiety, and peer support groups. Nurses coordinate this multidisciplinary care, ensuring patients and their families receive compassionate, dignified support throughout the disease trajectory.

Community Health Education and Prevention Strategies

Prevention and early education are the most powerful tools against breast cancer mortality. Community health nurses must lead grassroots sensitization campaigns to dispel harmful myths—such as the belief that breast cancer is caused by witchcraft or that touching a lump causes it to spread.

Education programs should teach women how to perform monthly breast self-examinations, encourage regular clinic visits, and promote healthy lifestyles including maintaining a healthy weight, exercising regularly, and reducing alcohol consumption. By building trust and engaging local community leaders, nurses can bridge the gap between healthcare facilities and the populations they serve.
