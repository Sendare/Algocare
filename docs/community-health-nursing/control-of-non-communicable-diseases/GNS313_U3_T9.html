Breast Cancer

Introduction and Epidemiology in Nigeria

Breast cancer is a malignant proliferation of epithelial cells lining the ducts or lobules of the breast. Globally, it remains the most common cancer among women. Within the Nigerian healthcare landscape, epidemiological patterns present unique and alarming challenges. Unlike high-income countries where peak incidence occurs in older postmenopausal women, Nigerian women are frequently diagnosed at a much younger age, often between thirty-five and fifty years. Compounding this age discrepancy is the late presentation phenomenon. Over seventy percent of patients in Nigerian teaching hospitals present at advanced stages, specifically stage III or IV. This delayed presentation stems from a complex web of cultural beliefs, financial constraints, reliance on traditional alternative therapy, and limited access to functional diagnostic infrastructure. Consequently, mortality-to-incidence ratios remain disproportionately high locally compared to Western nations.

Risk Factors and Etiology

The exact initiating event of breast cancer remains multifactorial, involving genetic, hormonal, and environmental triggers. Certain individuals carry inherited mutations in the **BRCA1** or **BRCA2** tumor suppressor genes, drastically increasing lifetime susceptibility. - **Hormonal factors**: Prolonged exposure to endogenous estrogen, such as early menarche (before age twelve) and late menopause (after age fifty-five), increases cellular division cycles in breast tissue. - **Reproductive history**: Nulliparity, or having a first full-term pregnancy after age thirty, elevates risk due to extended uninterrupted hormonal cycles. - **Lifestyle variables**: Excessive alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, and postmenopausal obesity contribute through metabolic and inflammatory pathways. - **Family history**: Having a first-degree relative, such as a mother or sister, diagnosed with the disease elevates personal baseline risk.

Pathophysiology and Staging

Breast cancer most commonly originates in the epithelial cells of the terminal duct lobular unit. It begins as **ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)**, where malignant cells multiply within the duct without breaching the basement membrane. When these cells break through the basement membrane and invade surrounding stromal tissue, it becomes **invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC)**. Metastasis occurs primarily via the lymphatic system, starting with the axillary lymph nodes, and through the hematogenous route to distant organs like the bones, lungs, liver, and brain. Clinicians utilize the **TNM staging system** to guide treatment: - **T (Tumor)**: Measures the physical size and local extent of the primary tumor. - **N (Node)**: Indicates the presence and location of cancer cells in regional lymph nodes. - **M (Metastasis)**: Determines whether distant spread has occurred to other organ systems.

Clinical Manifestations and Warning Signs

Early-stage breast malignancies are frequently asymptomatic, which highlights why reliance on symptoms alone delays diagnosis. As the tumor enlarges or disrupts local structures, distinct physical signs emerge. The classic presentation is a hard, painless, immobile lump with irregular borders. However, inflammatory breast cancer presents differently, characterized by rapid onset of diffuse skin redness, warmth, and **peau d'orange** (thickened skin resembling an orange peel caused by dermal lymphatic blockage). Other notable indicators include spontaneous bloody or serous nipple discharge, nipple retraction or inversion, localized skin dimpling, and palpable axillary lymphadenopathy. Pain is rarely an early symptom, leading many patients to ignore slow-growing masses until ulceration or fungation occurs.

Screening and Community Assessment

Early detection remains the most effective strategy to improve survival rates and reduce the need for radical surgeries. Screening protocols utilize three primary modalities: **Breast Self-Examination (BSE)**, **Clinical Breast Examination (CBE)**, and **Mammography**. BSE empowers individuals to recognize normal versus abnormal breast contours through monthly self-inspection. While its ability to reduce mortality as a standalone tool is debated, it fosters high body awareness. CBE is performed by trained healthcare providers during routine clinic visits. Mammography serves as the gold-standard radiological screening tool capable of detecting microcalcifications and non-palpable lesions years before they form a physical mass. In resource-limited settings where mammography machines are scarce or maldistributed, community health outreaches rely heavily on regular CBE and clinical palpation training.

Medical, Surgical, and Nursing Management

Therapeutic management requires a multidisciplinary approach tailored to tumor biology, stage, and patient preference. Treatment modalities combine local therapies like surgery and radiation with systemic therapies including chemotherapy, hormone therapy, and targeted biological agents. Surgical options range from **breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy)** coupled with postoperative radiation, to **modified radical mastectomy**, which involves total removal of the breast tissue, nipple-areola complex, and axillary lymph nodes. Systemic regimens target rapidly dividing cells or specific molecular receptors. For instance, **tamoxifen** or aromatase inhibitors are prescribed for estrogen receptor-positive tumors, while **trastuzumab** targets HER2-positive malignancies. Nursing care centers on managing treatment toxicities. Nurses monitor for chemotherapy-induced nausea, alopecia, myelosuppression, and lymphedema following axillary node clearance. Educating patients on arm exercises and infection prevention on the affected side is vital.

Palliative Care and Rehabilitation

When curative options are exhausted, the focus shifts to palliative care, which prioritizes maximizing the quality of life for patients with advanced or metastatic disease. Pain management forms the cornerstone of palliative nursing, utilizing the WHO analgesic ladder to control bone and visceral pain effectively. Rehabilitation addresses physical, psychological, and social dimensions. Psychological support is paramount, as body image distortion following mastectomy or hair loss triggers severe anxiety and depression. Support groups and counseling help patients and their families process grief and navigate terminal phases with dignity and comfort.

Community Education and Prevention

Community health education remains a powerful weapon against the high burden of advanced breast cancer presentations. Educational programs must dismantle cultural myths, such as the belief that cancer is contagious, spiritually induced, or exclusively treated by traditional herbalists. Nurses lead outreach campaigns in churches, mosques, markets, and schools to teach correct self-examination techniques and emphasize that early detection saves lives. Addressing structural barriers by advocating for decentralized diagnostic centers ensures that awareness campaigns translate into actual clinical action and improved survival statistics across all communities.
