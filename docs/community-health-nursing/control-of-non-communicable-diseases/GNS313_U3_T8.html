Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Sickle Cell Anaemia

Sickle cell anaemia is a hereditary blood disorder marked by the presence of an abnormal type of haemoglobin known as haemoglobin S (HbS). In a healthy individual, red blood cells contain haemoglobin A, which gives them a round, flexible, disc-like shape that slips easily through narrow blood vessels. When a person inherits the mutated gene from both parents, their red blood cells produce HbS, which changes shape under low oxygen conditions.

When these red blood cells release their oxygen into tissues, the abnormal haemoglobin molecules stick together to form stiff, rigid rods. This process deforms the red blood cell into a crescent or sickle shape.

  • Vaso-occlusion: Sickled cells are sticky and rigid, causing them to clump together and block small blood vessels, cutting off blood supply to tissues.
  • Haemolysis: Sickle cells are fragile and break down prematurely in the spleen, usually lasting only 10 to 20 days instead of the normal 120 days.

This early destruction of red blood cells leads to chronic anaemia, while the blocked vessels cause severe pain and tissue damage throughout the body.

Genetic Inheritance Patterns

Sickle cell anaemia follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. This means that a child must inherit one defective gene from the mother and one defective gene from the father to develop the disease.

If both parents carry the trait (AS), each pregnancy carries a specific mathematical probability:

  • 25% chance (1 in 4): The child inherits normal haemoglobin from both parents (AA).
  • 50% chance (2 in 4): The child inherits one normal gene and one sickle gene, becoming an asymptomatic carrier or having sickle cell trait (AS).
  • 25% chance (1 in 4): The child inherits the sickle gene from both parents, resulting in sickle cell anaemia (SS).

Carriers generally live normal, healthy lives without symptoms, but they can pass the gene to their offspring. Pre-marital genotype screening is a vital public health intervention in regions with high carrier prevalence to help couples make informed reproductive choices.

Clinical Manifestations Across the Lifespan

The clinical picture of sickle cell anaemia evolves as a person grows from infancy into adulthood. Infants rarely show symptoms during the first few months of life because high levels of fetal haemoglobin protect them. As fetal haemoglobin is replaced by adult haemoglobin S, symptoms begin to emerge.

In toddlers, one of the earliest signs is dactylitis, often called hand-foot syndrome, where painful swelling occurs in the hands and feet due to blocked blood flow in the small bones. Throughout childhood and adulthood, chronic anaemia causes baseline fatigue, pale conjunctiva, and mild jaundice from red blood cell breakdown.

Organs suffer progressive damage over time. The spleen repeatedly filters damaged cells until it fibrosis and stops working, leaving patients vulnerable to severe bacterial infections. Growth and puberty may be delayed, and chronic leg ulcers can develop around the ankles during adolescence. Regular monitoring helps catch these chronic changes early.

Acute Complications and Crisis Management

Acute crises are sudden, severe episodes that require immediate medical attention. The most common is the vaso-occlusive crisis, triggered when sickled cells clump together and obstruct microcirculation, producing intense, deep bone and joint pain.

Another life-threatening emergency is acute chest syndrome, characterized by chest pain, fever, and new lung infiltrates on an X-ray, mimicking pneumonia. Nurses must monitor oxygen saturation closely, administer intravenous fluids to reduce blood viscosity, and provide prescribed analgesics promptly.

Comprehensive Nursing Care and Pain Management

Managing a pain crisis demands a prompt, empathetic, and structured nursing approach. Nurses should never dismiss a patient's report of pain, as psychological stress and undertreatment can worsen outcomes. Pain management often requires prescribed opioids administered on a scheduled basis rather than strictly as needed during severe episodes.

Hydration is another cornerstone of nursing care. Intravenous or oral fluids help dilute the blood, lowering viscosity and helping red blood cells pass through narrow vessels. Nurses must also monitor for triggers such as cold exposure, infections, physical exhaustion, and emotional stress.

Oxygen therapy is provided only if the patient's oxygen saturation drops below normal levels; routine oxygen administration for non-hypoxic patients does not relieve pain and can suppress bone marrow function. Encouraging deep breathing exercises prevents atelectasis and chest complications during bed rest.

Community Health Education and Genetic Counseling

Public health initiatives and genetic counseling play an indispensable role in reducing the burden of sickle cell anaemia, particularly in high-prevalence regions like Nigeria. Community education focuses on dispelling myths, such as the belief that the condition is a spiritual curse or contagious disease.

Genetic counseling provides couples with accurate information about their hemoglobin genotypes before marriage or family planning. Nurses in primary healthcare centres are well-positioned to offer pre-marital screening counseling, explaining inheritance risks in simple, non-judgmental language. Empowering individuals with knowledge enables communities to make informed choices while supporting those already living with the condition to lead fulfilling lives.
