Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Sickle Cell Anaemia

Sickle cell anaemia is a severe hereditary blood disorder characterized by the presence of abnormal haemoglobin, known as Haemoglobin S (HbS). Under conditions of low oxygen tension, dehydration, or acidosis, this abnormal haemoglobin polymerizes, forming rigid rods that distort the flexible, biconcave red blood cells into a characteristic crescent or sickle shape. This genetic condition follows an **autosomal recessive** inheritance pattern. When both parents carry the sickle cell trait (HbAS), each pregnancy carries a 25% chance of producing a child with sickle cell anaemia (HbSS). The deformed, rigid cells cannot easily navigate narrow microvasculature. Instead, they cause microvascular occlusion, leading to tissue ischemia, severe pain episodes, and progressive, cumulative damage to reticuloendothelial organs such as the spleen, kidneys, and bones.

Clinical Manifestations Across the Lifespan

The clinical presentation of sickle cell anaemia evolves as a child grows. Infants rarely show symptoms immediately after birth because high levels of fetal haemoglobin (HbF) offer temporary protection. Once HbF declines around four to six months of age, classic manifestations begin to emerge. - **Dactylitis (Hand-Foot Syndrome)**: Painful, symmetric swelling of the hands and feet, often the earliest painful crisis seen in infants. - **Chronic Anaemia**: Persistent low haemoglobin levels leading to baseline fatigue, jaundice from accelerated haemolysis, and pallor. - **Splenic Sequestration**: Sudden, massive pooling of blood within the spleen, causing acute hypovolemic shock and profound left upper quadrant enlargement. - **Growth Retardation**: Delayed puberty and delayed linear growth due to chronic metabolic demands and chronic tissue hypoxia.

Diagnosis and Neonatal Screening in Nigerian Primary Healthcare

Early detection remains the cornerstone of reducing childhood mortality associated with sickle cell anaemia in resource-limited settings. In Nigerian primary healthcare facilities, screening relies heavily on cost-effective, rapid methods. - **Haemoglobin Electrophoresis**: The gold standard laboratory test that separates different haemoglobin types using an electrical field. - **Sickling Test (Sodium Metabisulphite)**: A quick bedside screening method that induces sickling in deoxygenated blood samples, though it cannot differentiate between the trait (HbAS) and the disease (HbSS). - **Point-of-Care Testing**: Newer immunochromatographic strip tests provide rapid results in rural clinics without electricity, though positive tests require confirmatory electrophoresis.

Acute Complications and Crisis Management

Acute crises are medical emergencies that require prompt recognition and structured intervention. Vaso-occlusive crises (VOC) cause severe, deep bone and joint pain triggered by infection, cold exposure, dehydration, or physical exhaustion. Another life-threatening event is **Acute Chest Syndrome**, characterized by fever, chest pain, and new pulmonary infiltrates on a chest radiograph; it can rapidly deteriorate into acute respiratory distress syndrome. Similarly, aplastic crises, often triggered by parvovirus B19 infection, cause a precipitous drop in reticulocyte count and profound worsening of baseline anaemia. Management focuses on adequate hydration, aggressive pain control using the WHO analgesic ladder, supplemental oxygen only if oxygen saturation drops, and prompt treatment of underlying triggers with broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Comprehensive Nursing Care and Pain Management

Pain is the hallmark symptom of sickle cell crises, yet it is frequently mismanaged or under-treated due to clinical skepticism. Effective nursing care requires validating the patient's subjective pain score without judgment. Intravenous access must be established early to administer prescribed analgesics and intravenous fluids at 1.5 times maintenance to reverse dehydration and reduce blood viscosity. Monitoring vital signs frequently helps detect early signs of sepsis or hypovolemic shock. Nurses also educate patients and family members on identifying early prodromal symptoms of a crisis, maintaining warm clothing during cold weather, drinking adequate water daily, and avoiding strenuous overexertion.

Community Health Education and Genetic Counseling

Public health initiatives in Nigeria emphasize primary prevention through community education and pre-marital genetic counseling. Because cultural stigma and misconceptions often surround sickle cell anaemia, nurses must deliver empathetic, non-judgmental education. Counseling sessions help couples understand their haemoglobin genotypes prior to marriage and childbearing. For example, an individual with genotype AS partnering with another AS carrier faces a significant risk of having a child with SS disease. Empowering young adults with accurate genetic knowledge allows them to make informed reproductive choices, reducing the societal and financial burden of the condition.
