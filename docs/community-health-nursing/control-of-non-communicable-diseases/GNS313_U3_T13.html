Poisoning

Introduction to Poisoning in Community Health

Poisoning remains a critical public health challenge across Nigerian communities, cutting across urban and rural settings alike. A poison is any substance—solid, liquid, or gas—that can disrupt normal body functions, cause structural damage, or lead to death when introduced into the body. In our local context, cases often range from accidental ingestion of kerosene and caustic soda by toddlers to agricultural pesticide exposure among farmers and traditional medicine misadventures. Community health nurses occupy the front line in recognizing, managing, and preventing toxicological emergencies. Understanding basic toxicology helps nurses swiftly differentiate between mild exposures and life-threatening crises, guiding prompt triage and community-level interventions.

Common Types and Sources of Poisons

Poisons originate from various domestic, agricultural, and industrial environments. In Nigerian homes, common culprits include stored petroleum products like kerosene kept in empty beverage bottles, locally brewed concoctions, herbal preparations of unknown dosage, and strong caustic agents used for soap making. Agricultural settings introduce organophosphate pesticides, herbicides, and rodenticides. Pharmaceuticals such as paracetamol, chloroquine, and iron tablets also account for numerous pediatric and adult overdoses. Recognizing these common sources allows nurses to anticipate specific clinical syndromes when patients present to local clinics or emergency departments.

Routes of Exposure and Mechanisms of Toxicity

The manner in which a poison enters the human body determines how fast it acts and how severe the damage becomes.

  • Ingestion: Swallowing toxic substances via the gastrointestinal tract; absorption speed depends on stomach contents.
  • Inhalation: Breathing in toxic gases, smoke, or fumes, leading to rapid entry into the bloodstream through the lungs.
  • Absorption: Direct contact with skin or mucous membranes, common with certain pesticides and industrial chemicals.
  • Injection: Direct introduction into tissue or blood vessels through insect stings, snakebites, or contaminated needles.

Once inside, toxins interfere with cellular metabolism, disrupt enzyme systems, or cause direct tissue destruction. For instance, organophosphates bind to the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, causing a dangerous buildup of acetylcholine that overstimulates the nervous system.

Assessment and Clinical Manifestations of Acute Poisoning

Assessing a poisoned patient requires systematic examination because toxidromes—groups of signs and symptoms associated with specific types of poisons—guide emergency care. Nurses must evaluate airway, breathing, and circulation first, followed by a targeted toxicological history from relatives or bystanders.

A common misconception is that all poisoned patients vomit; many caustic ingestions or tranquilizer overdoses present with little to no vomiting initially, requiring nurses to rely on other physical clues.

Emergency First Aid and Initial Management

Immediate management at the scene or clinic focuses on stabilization and preventing further toxin absorption. Nurses must ensure personal safety before handling any victim, particularly in chemical or gas exposures.

  1. Resuscitation: Secure airway, breathing, and circulation immediately. Administer oxygen if respiratory depression is present.
  2. Decontamination: Remove contaminated clothing and flush skin or eyes with copious amounts of clean water for at least fifteen minutes if exposed to chemicals.
  3. Limiting Absorption: Administer activated charcoal if ingestion occurred within a specific timeframe and the airway is protected.

Crucially, nurses must NEVER induce vomiting for petroleum products (like kerosene) or corrosive substances (like acid or caustic soda), because bringing the toxin back up causes chemical burns to the esophagus and increases the risk of aspiration pneumonia.

Community Prevention and Public Health Education

Prevention remains the most effective tool against poisoning. Community health nurses play an active role in educating households, market associations, and schools about safety hazards. Public health campaigns should focus on safe storage practices, emphasizing that dangerous chemicals must never be transferred into empty food or beverage containers. Nurses should teach parents to keep all medications out of the reach of children and to consult healthcare providers before administering traditional or herbal remedies. Organizing local town hall meetings and utilizing community radio broadcasts helps spread vital safety messages to remote areas.
