Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction and Definition of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic, metabolic disorder defined by the presence of hyperglycemia. This state of elevated blood glucose arises from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Insulin, an anabolic hormone produced by the beta cells of the pancreatic islets of Langerhans, normally facilitates the uptake of glucose into muscle and adipose tissue while suppressing hepatic glucose production. When this regulatory system fails, carbohydrates, fats, and proteins are improperly metabolized, leading to widespread systemic effects. Over time, chronic hyperglycemia causes endothelial damage, vascular dysfunction, and structural changes in various organ systems, most notably the eyes, kidneys, nerves, heart, and blood vessels. Recognizing the early systemic disruption of metabolic homeostasis is fundamental to clinical nursing assessment.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Concepts

The disruption of glucose homeostasis manifests differently depending on the underlying mechanism. In type 1 diabetes, an autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells leads to an absolute deficiency of insulin. Without endogenous insulin, glucose remains trapped in the bloodstream, forcing the body to break down stored fats for energy. This lipolysis releases free fatty acids, which the liver converts into ketone bodies, precipitating metabolic acidosis if left unchecked. Conversely, type 2 diabetes is characterized by peripheral insulin resistance paired with a progressive secretory defect. Target tissues like skeletal muscle and liver fail to respond effectively to normal circulating levels of insulin. To compensate, the pancreas initially secretes larger amounts of insulin. Eventually, beta cell exhaustion ensues, resulting in relative insulin deficiency and sustained high blood glucose levels.

Types and Classifications of Diabetes

Diabetes mellitus is categorized into distinct clinical types based on etiology and presentation:

  • Type 1 Diabetes: Characterized by beta cell destruction, usually leading to absolute insulin deficiency. Patients depend on exogenous insulin for survival.
  • Type 2 Diabetes: Encompasses a range from predominant insulin resistance with relative insulin deficiency to a predominantly secretory defect with insulin resistance.
  • Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM): Glucose intolerance onset or first recognition during pregnancy, driven by placental hormones that induce insulin resistance.
  • Other Specific Types: Includes diabetes due to genetic defects of beta cell function, exocrine pancreatic diseases like pancreatitis, or drug-induced states such as chronic corticosteroid therapy.

Risk Factors and Causes in the Nigerian Context

While genetic predisposition remains universal, environmental and lifestyle shifts heavily influence diabetes prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa. Urbanization in Nigeria has accelerated adoption of westernized diets high in refined carbohydrates and saturated fats, alongside a decline in physical activity due to sedentary occupations and motorized transport. Additional contributing factors include increasing rates of central obesity, chronic hypertension, and aging populations.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Criteria

Identifying diabetes relies on recognizing hallmark symptoms alongside confirmatory laboratory blood tests. Classic clinical indicators include polyuria, polydipsia, unexplained weight loss, and polyphagia. Vision blurring and slow-healing cutaneous wounds are also frequent complaints. Diagnostic confirmation typically involves assessing plasma glucose thresholds.

  1. Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG): A level of 7.0 mmol/L or higher after an overnight fast of at least eight hours.
  2. Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT): A 2-hour plasma glucose reading of 11.1 mmol/L or higher following a 75-gram anhydrous glucose load.
  3. Random Plasma Glucose: A reading of 11.1 mmol/L or higher in a patient exhibiting classic symptoms of hyperglycemia.
  4. Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c): A value of 6.5% or higher, reflecting average glycemia over the preceding three months.

Medical and Nursing Management

Comprehensive management aims to normalize blood glucose levels, prevent acute crises, and minimize long-term microvascular and macrovascular damage. Medical therapy pairs lifestyle interventions with pharmacological agents, including oral hypoglycemic drugs like metformin and sulfonylureas, or subcutaneous insulin injections.

Nursing care centers on continuous monitoring, medication administration, and empowering the patient through targeted education. Nurses must monitor blood glucose trends, assess injection sites for lipodystrophy, and inspect feet daily for early signs of neuropathy or ulceration. Furthermore, nursing staff play a vital role in teaching patients how to recognize and treat hypoglycemia promptly using fast-acting oral carbohydrates.

Community Health Education and Prevention Strategies

Preventing diabetes requires targeted public health interventions that address modifiable behavioral risk factors across populations. Community nursing programs should focus on promoting balanced nutrition, encouraging regular physical activity, and conducting routine blood glucose screening in primary healthcare clinics and marketplaces. Public outreach must dispel cultural myths surrounding diabetes while highlighting the importance of early detection. Empowering communities with knowledge helps reduce late presentations at tertiary hospitals, ultimately lowering the burden of preventable complications.

Acute and Chronic Complications

Uncontrolled diabetes leads to severe acute and chronic pathological consequences. Acute emergencies include Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA), common in type 1 diabetes, and Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar State (HHS), more frequent in type 2 diabetes. Both represent life-threatening metabolic derangements requiring urgent fluid resuscitation and electrolyte correction.

Chronic complications are divided into vascular categories:

  • Microvascular: Result from capillary basement membrane thickening, leading to retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy.
  • Macrovascular: Accelerated atherosclerosis affecting large vessels, increasing the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral arterial disease.
