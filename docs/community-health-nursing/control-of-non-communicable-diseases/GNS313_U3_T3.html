Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction and Definition of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic condition marked by elevated blood glucose levels resulting from defects in insulin production, insulin action, or both. Insulin is a hormone manufactured by the beta cells of the pancreas that allows glucose from digested food to enter body cells to be used for energy. When this system fails, glucose accumulates in the bloodstream, leading to chronic hyperglycemia.

Over time, unchecked high blood sugar damages delicate blood vessels and nerves, affecting major organs like the kidneys, eyes, heart, and lower limbs. For nursing students, understanding diabetes goes beyond memorizing lab values; it means recognizing how a single hormone deficiency triggers widespread systemic dysfunction across the human body.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Concepts

To grasp why patients with diabetes present with specific symptoms, you must look at cellular fuel supply. Normally, ingested carbohydrates break down into glucose, which enters the bloodstream. Pancreatic beta cells release insulin, acting like a key that unlocks cell membrane receptors to let glucose inside.

When insulin is absent or ineffective, glucose remains trapped in the blood while starving cells send distress signals. The liver responds to perceived starvation by releasing stored glycogen and manufacturing new glucose through gluconeogenesis, worsening the hyperglycemia. Meanwhile, the kidneys attempt to flush out excess sugar, pulling large amounts of water along with it and resulting in classic symptoms like extreme thirst and frequent urination.

Types and Classifications of Diabetes

Diabetes mellitus is broadly categorized into distinct types based on the underlying physiological failure:

  • Type 1 Diabetes: An autoimmune condition where the body's immune system mistakenly destroys pancreatic beta cells, resulting in absolute insulin deficiency. Patients require lifelong exogenous insulin replacement.
  • Type 2 Diabetes: Characterized by progressive insulin resistance combined with a relative secretory defect. Peripheral tissues fail to respond normally to insulin, and the pancreas eventually exhausts its ability to compensate.
  • Gestational Diabetes: Glucose intolerance recognized during pregnancy due to placental hormones that induce insulin resistance. It usually resolves after delivery but increases future risk for type 2 diabetes.
  • Other Specific Types: Includes diabetes caused by genetic defects, pancreatic disease, or certain medications like long-term corticosteroids.

Risk Factors and Causes in the Nigerian Context

In Nigeria, the burden of diabetes is rising rapidly, driven by shifting lifestyle patterns, urbanization, and dietary transitions. While genetic predisposition plays a role in all populations, local environmental factors heavily influence the surge in Type 2 diabetes.

Key risk factors observed in Nigerian healthcare settings include:

  1. Urbanization and Dietary Shifts: Increased consumption of processed, high-calorie foods and sugary drinks coupled with a decline in traditional high-fiber diets.
  2. Sedentary Lifestyle: Modern desk jobs and mechanized transport replacing active daily physical labor.
  3. Central Obesity: Accumulation of visceral fat around the abdomen, which significantly worsens insulin resistance.
  4. Aging Population: A growing life expectancy allowing chronic metabolic conditions more time to manifest.
  5. Family History: A strong genetic link among first-degree relatives with diabetes.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Criteria

Diagnosing diabetes requires objective laboratory testing rather than relying on symptoms alone, though clinical signs offer vital clues. Nurses must recognize the classic triad of manifestations: polyuria (excessive urination), polydipsia (excessive thirst), and polyphagia (excessive hunger), often accompanied by unexplained weight loss and fatigue.

Diagnostic tests commonly used in clinical practice include:

  • Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG): A value of 126 mg/dL or higher after an overnight fast.
  • Glycated Hemoglobin (HbA1c): A level of 6.5% or higher, reflecting average blood glucose over the past three months.
  • Random Plasma Glucose: A value of 200 mg/dL or higher in a patient with classic hyperglycemia symptoms.
  • Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT): A 2-hour plasma glucose reading of 200 mg/dL or higher following a standard glucose load.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management of diabetes centers on maintaining blood glucose levels within a safe target range to prevent acute crises and long-term organ damage. This requires a collaborative approach involving medication, medical nutrition therapy, regular exercise, and consistent blood glucose monitoring.

Pharmacological treatment depends on the classification of the disease. Patients with Type 1 diabetes require daily insulin therapy. Those with Type 2 diabetes may start with lifestyle modifications and oral hypoglycemic agents like metformin, though insulin may eventually be needed as beta-cell function declines.

Nurses play a frontline role in administration, patient instruction, and complication monitoring. Nursing responsibilities include teaching proper subcutaneous insulin injection techniques, monitoring for signs of hypoglycemia, assessing peripheral circulation in the lower extremities, and reinforcing dietary compliance.

Community Health Education and Prevention Strategies

Preventing diabetes and managing it at the population level requires robust community health initiatives. Nurses working in primary health care centers are uniquely positioned to spearhead screening programs, especially in rural and semi-urban Nigerian communities where undiagnosed diabetes remains high.

Education campaigns should focus on practical lifestyle modifications that fit local economic realities. Communities must be taught to substitute highly refined staples with whole grains, incorporate affordable physical activities like brisk walking into daily routines, and recognize early warning signs. Community outreach also helps dispel cultural myths about diabetes, encouraging affected individuals to seek orthodox medical care rather than relying solely on unverified alternative remedies.

Acute and Chronic Complications

Uncontrolled diabetes gives rise to serious complications that can drastically impair quality of life or prove fatal if untreated. Nurses must remain vigilant for both acute emergencies and long-term degenerative conditions.

  • Acute Complications:
  • *Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)*: Seen mostly in Type 1 diabetes, characterized by severe hyperglycemia, dehydration, acidosis, and ketone production.
  • *Hypoglycemia*: Low blood sugar caused by too much insulin, skipped meals, or intense exercise.
  • Chronic Complications:
  • *Microvascular Damage*: Diabetic retinopathy (leading to blindness), nephropathy (leading to kidney failure), and neuropathy (nerve damage).
  • *Macrovascular Damage*: Accelerated atherosclerosis increasing the risk of stroke, myocardial infarction, and peripheral vascular disease leading to diabetic foot ulcers.
