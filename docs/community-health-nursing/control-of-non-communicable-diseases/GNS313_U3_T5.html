Smoking

Introduction to Smoking and Nicotine Dependence

Tobacco smoking remains one of the leading preventable causes of death worldwide. The primary driver of this deadly habit is nicotine, a naturally occurring alkaloid found in the tobacco plant that possesses powerful addictive properties. When a person inhales cigarette smoke, nicotine enters the bloodstream and reaches the brain within seconds, triggering the release of neurotransmitters like dopamine that create pleasurable sensations and feelings of calm.

Over time, the brain adapts to the constant presence of nicotine, leading to tolerance—meaning the smoker needs more of the substance to get the same effect. When nicotine levels drop, the individual experiences uncomfortable withdrawal symptoms such as irritability, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, and intense cravings. This cycle of seeking relief from withdrawal drives compulsive tobacco use, making dependence a chronic relapsing brain disorder rather than merely a bad habit.

Epidemiology and Public Health Impact in Nigeria

In Nigeria, tobacco use patterns are shifting due to aggressive marketing by multinational tobacco companies targeting young populations in urban centers. While traditional forms of tobacco have historical roots in certain communities, manufactured cigarettes and newer products like shisha (waterpipe) are gaining popularity among adolescents and young adults.

The public health burden extends far beyond individual lung damage. Tobacco-related illnesses drain household finances, reduce national productivity through premature illness and death, and strain public health facilities that are already managing high rates of infectious diseases. Healthcare providers in Nigeria face a dual burden: treating traditional communicable diseases while simultaneously watching non-communicable, lifestyle-related conditions like cardiovascular disease and cancer rise steadily across urban and rural settings.

Pathophysiology and Health Consequences of Tobacco Use

Cigarette smoke contains a toxic mix of over 7,000 chemicals, including carcinogens, heavy metals, and oxidizing gases. When drawn into the respiratory tract, these substances damage the delicate epithelial lining of the airways. Cilia, the microscopic hair-like structures responsible for sweeping mucus and debris out of the lungs, become paralyzed and eventually destroyed, leading to chronic smoker's cough and increased vulnerability to infections.

Systemically, the health consequences affect nearly every organ system:

  • Cardiovascular system: Carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin more readily than oxygen, starving tissues while nicotine raises heart rate and blood pressure, accelerating atherosclerosis and the risk of myocardial infarction.
  • Respiratory system: Long-term irritation causes chronic bronchitis and destroys alveolar walls, resulting in irreversible emphysema (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease).
  • Oncology: Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons cause genetic mutations, heavily predisposing individuals to cancers of the lung, larynx, esophagus, and bladder.

Behavioral and Psychological Aspects of Addiction

Physical withdrawal is only one hurdle in quitting tobacco; the psychological and behavioral ties are equally strong. Smoking becomes deeply woven into daily routines. Lighting up with morning tea, taking a smoke break during work stress, or socializing with friends who smoke creates powerful conditioned triggers.

Over years of use, the brain associates these everyday cues with the rewarding feeling of nicotine. Consequently, simply encountering the cue can trigger intense psychological cravings even long after the physical chemicals have left the body. Nurses must recognize that helping a patient quit requires addressing these behavioral habits, teaching them to identify their personal triggers, and helping them build new coping mechanisms to handle stress without reaching for a cigarette.

Community Health Assessment and Screening for Tobacco Use

Routine screening is the foundation of effective tobacco control in clinical and community settings. Healthcare providers should assess tobacco use status at every patient encounter, treating tobacco dependence as a vital sign alongside temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and respiration.

A widely accepted framework for clinical intervention is the 5 As approach:

  1. Ask: Systematically identify and document tobacco use status for every patient at every visit.
  2. Advise: Urge every tobacco user to quit in a clear, strong, and personalized manner.
  3. Assess: Determine the patient's willingness and readiness to make a quit attempt at this time.
  4. Assist: Help the patient with a quit plan, provide practical counseling, and recommend pharmacotherapy.
  5. Arrange: Schedule follow-up contact to support the patient and prevent relapse.

Smoking Cessation Interventions and Counseling

Behavioral counseling significantly increases the success rates of individuals trying to quit smoking. Nurses play a frontline role in delivering brief interventions, often using the 5 Rs to motivate patients who are not yet ready to quit:

  • Relevance: Encourage the patient to explain why quitting is personally relevant to their life and health.
  • Risks: Highlight the negative consequences of continued smoking, linking them to the patient's specific health status.
  • Rewards: Outline the direct benefits of quitting, such as improved breathing, financial savings, and better stamina.
  • Roadmap (Roadblocks): Identify barriers the patient expects to face, including withdrawal symptoms, weight gain, or social pressure.
  • Repetition: Repeat motivational discussions regularly at future clinical visits without showing frustration.

For patients who are ready to quit, practical problem-solving counseling focuses on recognizing triggers, changing daily routines, and building a strong support system.

Pharmacotherapy in Tobacco Dependence Management

Combining behavioral counseling with pharmacotherapy doubles or triples a smoker's chances of quitting successfully. Medications help by reducing withdrawal symptoms and blocking the rewarding effects of nicotine.

Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT)Delivers controlled, lower doses of nicotine without toxic tar and carbon monoxide. Available as patches, gums, or lozenges.Gradual step-down dosing helps wean the brain safely off chemical dependence.

Nurses must educate patients on the correct usage of these products, correcting common misconceptions—such as fearing that NRT is just as dangerous as smoking cigarettes.

Community-Based Prevention and Public Health Policies

Preventing tobacco use requires broad community action and strong government policies. Individual counseling alone cannot combat the powerful influence of advertising, peer pressure, and cheap tobacco products. Effective public health measures include implementing heavy taxation on tobacco products, enforcing comprehensive bans on smoking in public indoor spaces, and prohibiting all forms of tobacco marketing and sponsorship.

Community health nurses play a vital role in prevention by conducting educational programs in schools, advocating for local smoke-free ordinances, and raising public awareness about the dangers of secondhand smoke. Protecting children and non-smokers from environmental tobacco smoke is a key nursing priority, ensuring healthier environments for future generations.
