Hypertension

Introduction and Classification of Hypertension

Hypertension, commonly referred to as high blood pressure, is a chronic medical condition in which the force of blood against the artery walls is persistently elevated. It is often called the "silent killer" because it rarely causes noticeable symptoms in its early stages, allowing individuals to walk around unaware until a major vascular event occurs.

Blood pressure is measured in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) and recorded as two numbers: systolic pressure (the pressure when the heart beats) over diastolic pressure (the pressure when the heart rests between beats). Clinical staging guides diagnosis and treatment decisions:

  • Normal: Less than 120/80 mmHg
  • Elevated: Systolic 120-129 AND diastolic less than 80 mmHg
  • Stage 1 Hypertension: Systolic 130-139 OR diastolic 80-89 mmHg
  • Stage 2 Hypertension: Systolic at least 140 OR diastolic at least 90 mmHg
  • Hypertensive Crisis: Systolic over 180 AND/OR diastolic over 120 mmHg, requiring immediate medical evaluation

Pathophysiology and Risk Factors

Blood pressure is determined by two main factors: cardiac output (the volume of blood the heart pumps) and systemic vascular resistance (the resistance of the blood vessels to blood flow). When arteries become narrowed, stiffened, or when blood volume increases, resistance rises, forcing the heart to work harder.

Most cases are classified as primary (essential) hypertension, meaning there is no single identifiable medical cause, though it is strongly linked to genetics, aging, and lifestyle. Secondary hypertension, by contrast, results from an underlying condition such as chronic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, or endocrine disorders like Cushing's syndrome.

  1. Age and Genetics: Arteries naturally lose elasticity over time, and a family history greatly increases personal risk.
  2. Dietary Factors: High sodium intake causes fluid retention, expanding blood volume and raising pressure.
  3. Lifestyle Habits: Physical inactivity, heavy alcohol consumption, and tobacco use contribute heavily to endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

While mild to moderate hypertension is typically asymptomatic, patients with severely elevated blood pressure or sudden spikes may experience specific warning signs. Common complaints include early morning occipital headaches, dizziness, palpitations, blurred vision, and easy fatigability.

Accurate measurement is critical for proper diagnosis. The patient should rest quietly for at least five minutes with feet flat on the floor and arm supported at heart level before inflating the cuff. Using an incorrect cuff size is a common pitfall: a cuff that is too small will falsely elevate the reading, while an oversized cuff may produce falsely low readings.

Target organ assessment is vital during clinical evaluation. Nurses must assess for signs of damage in key systems:

  • Cardiovascular: Listen for displaced apex beat or murmurs.
  • Neurological: Check for focal deficits or confusion.
  • Renal: Review serum creatinine and test for proteinuria.
  • Ophthalmological: Inspect retinal vessels for hemorrhages or exudates.

Non-Pharmacological and Lifestyle Management

Lifestyle modification is the cornerstone of preventing and managing hypertension. Even for patients who require medication, lifestyle changes enhance drug efficacy and reduce overall cardiovascular risk.

The DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) focuses on consuming ample fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy products while restricting saturated fats, red meats, and sugary sweets. Sodium restriction to less than 2,000 to 2,300 milligrams daily helps blunt the pressor effect of salt.

Regular aerobic exercise, such as brisk walking, swimming, or cycling for at least 30 minutes most days of the week, strengthens the cardiovascular system and lowers systemic vascular resistance. Weight reduction is equally powerful; losing even a modest amount of excess body weight significantly drops both systolic and diastolic numbers. Limiting alcohol intake and completely avoiding tobacco products protect endothelial function and prevent accelerated atherosclerosis.

Pharmacological Management and Nursing Care

When lifestyle adjustments fail to achieve target blood pressure goals, antihypertensive medications are prescribed. Therapy often begins with a single agent or a combination pill to improve adherence, as complex regimens discourage consistent use.

Common classes of antihypertensive drugs include:

  • Thiazide Diuretics: Promote sodium and water excretion by the kidneys (e.g., bendroflumethiazide).
  • ACE Inhibitors: Prevent the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, causing vasodilation (e.g., lisinopril; note that persistent dry cough is a common side effect).
  • Calcium Channel Blockers: Relax blood vessel smooth muscle (e.g., amlodipine).
  • Beta-Blockers: Reduce cardiac output and heart rate (e.g., atenolol).

Nurses play a vital role in monitoring therapy. They must check baseline blood pressure and pulse before administering medications, watch for adverse effects like orthostatic hypotension, and educate patients never to stop taking their drugs abruptly, as this can trigger dangerous rebound hypertension.

Complications and Crisis Intervention

Uncontrolled chronic hypertension exerts continuous mechanical stress on arterial walls, leading to microvascular and macrovascular damage throughout the body. Major long-term complications include stroke (cerebrovascular accident), myocardial infarction, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and peripheral arterial disease. Hypertensive retinopathy can also cause progressive visual impairment.

An acute medical emergency arises during a hypertensive crisis, which is split into two categories:

  1. Hypertensive Urgency: Blood pressure is severely elevated (usually >180/120 mmHg) with NO evidence of acute target organ damage. Treatment involves lowering blood pressure gradually over 24 to 48 hours using oral medications.
  2. Hypertensive Emergency: Blood pressure is severely elevated AND accompanied by acute, ongoing damage to vital organs (such as encephalopathy, acute stroke, myocardial infarction, or aortic dissection). This requires immediate admission to an intensive care unit and rapid reduction using intravenous medications.

Community Health Nursing and Patient Education

Community health nurses serve at the forefront of cardiovascular disease prevention. Public health campaigns focusing on blood pressure screening in markets, churches, and community centers help identify undiagnosed individuals early before irreversible organ damage sets in.

Patient education must be tailored to the local cultural context, language, and literacy level. Nurses should teach families how to reduce dietary salt in traditional dishes, encourage regular physical activity appropriate for age, and emphasize the importance of lifelong adherence to therapy even when the patient feels completely well. Empowering patients with home blood pressure monitors fosters active self-management and improves overall clinical outcomes.
