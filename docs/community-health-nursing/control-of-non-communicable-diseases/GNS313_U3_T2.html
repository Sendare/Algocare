Hypertension

Introduction and Definition of Hypertension

Hypertension, commonly referred to as high blood pressure, is a chronic medical condition where the systemic arterial blood pressure remains elevated. Clinically, it is diagnosed when the persistent reading equals or exceeds 140 mmHg systolic and/or 90 mmHg diastolic. The cardiovascular system relies on a delicate balance between cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance. When resistance within the arteriolar network increases chronically, the heart must work harder to propel blood forward, establishing the hypertensive state. Because early stages typically present without overt symptoms, the condition earned the designation of a silent killer. Regular measurement remains the only reliable method for identification, making routine clinical assessment a fundamental responsibility for healthcare providers.

Epidemiology and Public Health Significance in Nigeria

Non-communicable diseases present a growing burden across sub-Saharan Africa, with hypertension leading the surge. Within Nigeria, urban migration, dietary shifts toward processed foods containing high sodium, and rising stress levels contribute significantly to prevalence rates. Community surveys across various states show that millions of adults live with elevated blood pressure, and a large percentage remain unaware of their status. This undiagnosed pool creates a heavy public health crisis, as individuals frequently present to tertiary facilities only when end-organ damage such as stroke or chronic kidney disease has already occurred. Public health initiatives therefore focus heavily on decentralized screening programs to catch the disease early.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Risk Factors

The etiology of elevated blood pressure divides into two main categories. Essential or primary hypertension accounts for the vast majority of cases, possessing no single identifiable organic cause but arising from a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, renal salt handling abnormalities, and sympathetic nervous system overactivity. Secondary hypertension stems from an identifiable underlying pathology, such as chronic renal parenchymal disease, renal artery stenosis, or endocrine disorders like primary hyperaldosteronism. - **Age**: Arterial walls naturally lose elasticity and stiffen over time. - **Genetics**: A family history significantly raises individual risk profiles. - **Dietary Sodium**: Excessive salt intake expands extracellular fluid volume. - **Obesity**: Adipose tissue increases total vascular resistance and cardiac workload.

Classification and Blood Pressure Categories

Standardizing blood pressure categories helps clinicians assess cardiovascular risk and guide therapeutic decisions. Measurements require proper technique, including an appropriately sized cuff and a resting period of at least five minutes. | Category | Systolic (mmHg) | Diastolic (mmHg) | |---|---|---| | Normal | Less than 120 | Less than 80 | | Elevated | 120-129 | Less than 80 | | Stage 1 Hypertension | 130-139 | 80-89 | | Stage 2 Hypertension | 140 or higher | 90 or higher | An accurate classification cannot be made on a single reading unless severe crisis values are present; clinicians look for consistent elevations across multiple separate visits.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Techniques

While mild to moderate hypertension often remains asymptomatic, severe elevations or sudden surges can trigger specific clinical complaints. Patients may report occipital headaches occurring predominantly in the morning, dizziness, palpitations, blurred vision, or easy fatigability. Physical assessment involves thorough auscultation of heart sounds, noting any displaced apex beat indicating left ventricular hypertrophy, and palpating peripheral pulses. Proper technique during blood pressure measurement remains critical: ensure the arm is supported at heart level, select a cuff bladder width that covers at least 40 percent of the arm circumference, and inflate the cuff steadily while listening for Korotkoff sounds.

Community-Based Screening and Early Detection

Detecting elevated blood pressure early within communities prevents long-term disability and reduces hospital admissions. Mobile health outreaches in market squares, churches, mosques, and community town halls serve as vital access points for populations who rarely visit formal health facilities. Nurses leading these campaigns must ensure equipment is regularly calibrated and staff members follow standardized measurement protocols. Beyond simply recording numbers, screening events offer an ideal platform for lifestyle counseling, risk stratification, and immediate referral of individuals with dangerously high readings to appropriate medical centers.

Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Management

Managing hypertension requires a comprehensive strategy combining lifestyle modification with pharmacotherapy when indicated. Non-pharmacological interventions form the bedrock of care for all patients, focusing on dietary sodium restriction, weight management, regular aerobic exercise, moderation of alcohol intake, and smoking cessation. When blood pressure targets are not met through lifestyle changes alone, drug therapy is initiated. Common classes include thiazide diuretics, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers. Treatment plans must be individualized, considering potential side effects, cost, and patient adherence factors.

Complications and Crisis Intervention

Uncontrolled chronic hypertension inflicts continuous mechanical stress on arterial walls, accelerating atherosclerosis and damaging delicate microvasculature across major organs. Key target organs include the heart, leading to left ventricular hypertrophy and heart failure; the brain, resulting in transient ischemic attacks and cerebrovascular accidents; the kidneys, causing nephrosclerosis and chronic kidney failure; and the eyes, producing retinopathy. A hypertensive crisis demands immediate medical attention and splits into two distinct clinical scenarios: - **Hypertensive Urgency**: Blood pressure is severely elevated (typically >180/120 mmHg) without acute evidence of target organ damage. Management involves gradual reduction over 24 to 48 hours using oral agents. - **Hypertensive Emergency**: Severe elevation is accompanied by acute or progressive target organ damage (such as encephalopathy, intracranial hemorrhage, acute myocardial infarction, or pulmonary edema). This requires immediate intravenous intervention in an intensive care setting.

Role of the Community Health Nurse in Prevention and Patient Education

Community health nurses serve as the frontline bridge between healthcare institutions and the general population. Their responsibilities extend far beyond clinical measurements into active health promotion, disease prevention, and longitudinal patient support. Education efforts must be tailored to local cultural contexts, addressing misconceptions about herbal remedies versus orthodox medication. Nurses teach families how to monitor blood pressure at home, interpret dietary sodium labels, recognize early signs of complications, and emphasize the absolute necessity of lifelong adherence to prescribed regimens, even when symptoms are absent.
