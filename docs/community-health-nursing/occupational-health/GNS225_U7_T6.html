Recognition of Occupational Health Hazards

Introduction to Occupational Health Hazards

Work is central to daily survival and national development, yet every job carries some risk of harm. An occupational hazard is any factor within a workplace that can cause injury, illness, or death. When nurses assess workers, they look at the interaction between the worker, the tools used, and the working environment. Recognizing these hazards early prevents short-term injuries and long-term chronic illnesses that can devastate families and reduce workforce productivity.

Categories of Workplace Hazards

To systematically study and manage risks, occupational health specialists divide workplace dangers into distinct groups. Understanding these categories helps nurses perform thorough workplace inspections and design targeted interventions.

  • Physical Hazards: Environmental factors like excessive noise, extreme temperatures, radiation, and vibration.
  • Chemical Hazards: Liquids, gases, dusts, and fumes that can irritate skin, lungs, or enter the bloodstream.
  • Biological Hazards: Living microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites encountered on the job.
  • Ergonomic Hazards: Poor workstation design, heavy lifting, and repetitive movements that strain the musculoskeletal system.
  • Psychosocial Hazards: Workplace stress, long hours, bullying, and violence that impact mental health.

Physical and Chemical Hazards in Nigerian Workplaces

Across Nigeria, specific industries expose workers to severe physical and chemical dangers. In construction and manufacturing, heavy machinery generates deafening noise leading to occupational hearing loss. Artisans, welders, and miners face high levels of particulate dust, silica, and toxic metal fumes without adequate ventilation. Chemical exposure in local textile mills, tanneries, and agricultural sectors—such as handling organophosphate pesticides—frequently leads to acute poisoning or chronic skin and respiratory conditions.

Biological, Ergonomic, and Psychosocial Hazards

Healthcare workers, laboratory staff, and agricultural laborers face constant biological threats from bloodborne pathogens, tuberculosis, and Lassa fever. Alongside these infectious agents, ergonomic challenges are widespread; market traders, porters (locally called 'alagaru'), and factory line workers lift heavy loads repetitively in awkward postures. Furthermore, psychosocial pressures are rising. Shift work, job insecurity, and verbal abuse from customers or management create chronic mental fatigue, anxiety, and burnout.

Methods for Identifying Hazards in the Work Environment

Detecting workplace hazards requires a proactive, structured approach rather than waiting for accidents to happen. Nurses and occupational health officers use several practical methods:

  1. Workplace Walkthroughs: Visual inspections to observe daily operations, unsafe behaviors, and unguarded machinery.
  2. Job Safety Analysis (JSA): Breaking down a specific task step-by-step to spot hidden dangers at each stage.
  3. Incident and Injury Records: Reviewing past clinic logs, first-aid registers, and accident reports to find recurring patterns.
  4. Worker Interviews: Asking employees directly where they feel pain, discomfort, or fear while doing their jobs.

Worker Assessment and Occupational History Taking

Taking a detailed occupational history is just as important as taking a medical history. When a patient presents with a chronic cough, skin rash, or joint pain, the nurse must ask: "What do you do for a living?" and "What materials do you handle at work?" Workers may not connect their daily job tasks to their illnesses, especially if symptoms develop slowly over years. A thorough assessment uncovers exposures to dust, chemicals, or awkward postures that explain the root cause of the disease.

The Role of the Community Health Nurse in Hazard Recognition

Community health nurses serve as the primary link between vulnerable workers and health services. They educate local artisans, factory employees, and market workers on how to identify hazards and use personal protective equipment correctly. Furthermore, community nurses advocate for safer working conditions, conduct basic screenings, and refer sick workers to specialized occupational health clinics. Their presence in local communities ensures that even informal sector workers receive vital health guidance.

Reporting and Recording Occupational Incidents

Accurate reporting and recording of workplace accidents and illnesses are essential for preventing future occurrences. When an injury or near-miss happens, it must be documented immediately in the workplace incident register and reported to safety officers or regulatory bodies. Nurses often manage clinic injury logs, which serve as valuable data sources for spotting dangerous trends. Proper record-keeping helps employers improve safety policies and ensures that affected workers receive rightful compensation for occupational injuries.
