Classification of Occupational Health Hazards

Introduction to Occupational Health Hazards

An occupational hazard is any factor within the workplace that can cause physical or mental harm, illness, or injury to workers. In Nigeria, workplace safety standards are guided by the Factories Act, yet many workers in both formal and informal sectors face daily risks due to poor enforcement and lack of protective gear. Categorizing these hazards helps occupational health nurses identify risks, implement preventive measures, and advocate for safer working environments. Hazards are typically divided into distinct groups based on their nature: physical, chemical, biological, ergonomic, and psychosocial.

Physical Hazards

Physical hazards are environmental factors within the workplace that can harm the body without necessarily touching it directly. Common elements include noise, vibration, extreme temperatures, and radiation.

  • Noise: Excessive sound levels in factories or construction sites can cause permanent noise-induced hearing loss.
  • Vibration: Hand-arm vibration from using heavy machinery like jackhammers leads to vascular and neurological damage, known as hand-arm vibration syndrome.
  • Temperature extremes: Working under the hot sun in open markets or agricultural fields puts workers at risk for heat stroke and dehydration.
  • Radiation: Exposure to ionizing and non-ionizing radiation threatens healthcare workers handling X-ray equipment without lead aprons.

Chemical Hazards

Chemical hazards arise from the use of or exposure to dangerous substances in the workplace. These substances can exist in various states of matter including dusts, fumes, gases, liquids, and mists. Workers can absorb these chemicals through inhalation, skin contact, or accidental ingestion.

Industrial solvents, heavy metals like lead found in battery recycling sites, and strong acids can cause acute poisoning or chronic organ damage. For instance, chronic exposure to benzene vapor is linked to bone marrow depression and leukemia. Nurses must ensure Material Safety Data Sheets are accessible and that workers wear appropriate respirators and gloves.

Biological Hazards

Biological hazards involve exposure to living organisms that can cause disease, posing a major threat to healthcare workers, laboratory technicians, farmers, and waste handlers.

Pathogens include bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. In Nigerian hospitals, nurses frequently face needlestick injuries that transmit bloodborne pathogens such as Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and Human Immunodeficiency Virus. Agricultural workers may contract leptospirosis or tetanus through contact with contaminated soil or animal fluids. Standard precautions, proper waste segregation, and immunizations remain vital defense lines against biological agents.

Ergonomic Hazards

Ergonomic hazards occur when the type of work, body position, and working conditions put strain on the body. Unlike sudden injuries from falls, ergonomic injuries develop gradually over time due to repetitive tasks, forceful exertions, and awkward postures.

Desk workers, factory assembly line operators, and market traders carrying heavy loads on their heads frequently experience musculoskeletal disorders. Symptoms include lower back pain, tendonitis, and carpal tunnel syndrome. Workstation design must be adjusted to fit the worker, incorporating lifting techniques and regular rest breaks.

Psychosocial Hazards

Psychosocial hazards relate to the way work is designed, organized, and managed, as well as the economic and social contexts of the workplace. These factors affect mental health, emotional well-being, and physical health.

Key stressors include high job demands, low control, lack of social support, workplace bullying, job insecurity, and exposure to traumatic events. In healthcare settings, nurses often suffer from burnout, compassion fatigue, and depression due to chronic understaffing and heavy workloads. Left unmanaged, chronic psychosocial stress leads to cardiovascular disease, hypertension, and substance abuse.

Mechanical and Safety Hazards

Mechanical and safety hazards involve machinery, equipment, and physical workplace conditions that can cause immediate trauma, cuts, fractures, or amputations.

Unguarded moving parts of machines, slippery floors, unstable scaffolding, and electrical faults create immediate dangers. Construction sites and factories present high risks for slips, trips, falls, and machinery entanglements. Employers must install machine guards, ensure proper housekeeping to keep walkways clear, and enforce electrical safety protocols to protect workers.

Nursing Assessment and Hazard Identification

Occupational health nurses play a critical role in identifying workplace hazards through systematic assessment and environmental surveys. Nurses conduct workplace walkthroughs, review injury logs, and perform pre-placement and periodic health screenings for employees.

By evaluating the work environment, a nurse can recommend the hierarchy of controls: elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and lastly, personal protective equipment. Effective surveillance ensures early detection of occupational diseases, keeping the workforce productive and safe.
