Immunity

Introduction to Immunity and the Immune System

Immunity represents the physiological capability of the human body to recognize foreign substances, known as **antigens**, and neutralize or eliminate them before they cause cellular damage or disease. The immune system is not a single organ, but a dispersed network of specialized cells, tissues, and organs working in constant coordination. Primary lymphoid organs, namely the bone marrow and thymus, serve as the production and maturation sites for white blood cells. Secondary lymphoid organs, including the spleen, lymph nodes, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), trap pathogens and facilitate immune cell interaction. When a pathogen breaches structural barriers like the skin or mucous membranes, the body initiates immediate cellular and chemical defenses to maintain internal equilibrium.

Innate versus Adaptive Immunity

Human host defense relies on two complementary branches: the innate and adaptive immune systems. - **Innate Immunity**: Present from birth, this system provides rapid, non-specific protection against any foreign invader. It features physical barriers, chemical secretions, phagocytic cells like macrophages, and inflammatory mediators. It lacks immunological memory, responding identically to repeated exposures. - **Adaptive Immunity**: Developing over time through specific pathogen exposure, this system targets precise molecular signatures. It utilizes T-lymphocytes for cell-mediated immunity and B-lymphocytes for antibody production. Crucially, it possesses **immunological memory**, mounting a faster, more robust defense upon subsequent encounters with the same antigen.

Active Immunity: Natural and Artificial

Active immunity occurs when the host's own immune system synthesizes antibodies and activates T-cells following antigen exposure. This confers long-lasting, often lifelong protection due to the generation of memory cells. - **Natural Active Immunity**: Acquired when an individual contracts a live pathogen, experiences the clinical or subclinical infection, and recovers. For example, recovering from measles provides long-term immunity. - **Artificial Active Immunity**: Induced via vaccination. Administration of weakened, killed, or recombinant antigen preparations stimulates the immune system without causing full-blown disease pathology. Routine immunization schedules in Nigeria rely heavily on this mechanism to protect infants against poliomyelitis, tuberculosis, and tetanus.

Passive Immunity: Natural and Artificial

Passive immunity involves the direct transfer of pre-formed antibodies into a recipient host. Since the host's own immune system is not stimulated to produce these proteins, protection is immediate but temporary, typically fading within weeks or months as the exogenous antibodies degrade. - **Natural Passive Immunity**: The physiological transfer of maternal IgG antibodies across the placenta to the fetus during gestation, and the provision of secretory IgA through colostrum during breastfeeding. This protects the vulnerable neonate during early life. - **Artificial Passive Immunity**: The clinical administration of exogenous immunoglobulins or antiserum, such as tetanus antitoxin or rabies immunoglobulin, administered post-exposure when immediate neutralization of a lethal toxin is required before the patient's own immune system can respond.

Herd Immunity and Its Epidemiological Significance

Herd immunity, or community immunity, describes a form of indirect protection from infectious disease that occurs when a large percentage of a population has become immune—either through previous infections or vaccinations—thereby providing a measure of protection for individuals who are not immune. When herd immunity thresholds are reached, chains of transmission are interrupted, protecting vulnerable demographics such as neonates, immunocompromised patients, and individuals with medical contraindications to vaccines. Maintaining high immunization coverage is critical; if coverage dips below specific epidemiological thresholds tailored to a pathogen's contagiousness, outbreaks and community transmission rapidly re-emerge.

Factors Influencing Immune Responses in Populations

Immune competency varies significantly across individuals and communities due to a complex interplay of intrinsic and extrinsic variables. Nutritional status plays a profound role; protein-energy malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies, such as zinc or vitamin A shortages common in resource-limited settings, impair lymphocyte proliferation and antibody production. Age is another critical determinant, with the immature immune systems of neonates and the immunosenescence of older adults rendering both groups more susceptible to severe infections. Chronic illnesses, systemic infections like HIV, psychological stress, and environmental toxin exposure further modulate inflammatory pathways and reduce overall immune resilience.

Immunization Programs and Cold Chain Management in Nigeria

The success of immunization initiatives depends heavily on maintaining the **cold chain**—an uninterrupted series of temperature-controlled storage and distribution equipment. Vaccines are biological substances sensitive to heat, freezing, and light. If exposed to temperatures outside the recommended range (typically +2°C to +8°C for most refrigerated vaccines, and colder for oral polio or measles vaccines), their antigenic potency is irreversibly destroyed. In Nigeria, logistical challenges such as erratic electrical grids, remote terrain, and fuel shortages threaten cold chain integrity at primary health care levels. Healthcare workers utilize vaccine carriers with conditioned ice packs, solar-powered refrigerators, and temperature monitoring logs to safeguard vaccine efficacy from central depots down to rural immunization posts.

Community Health Nursing Roles in Vaccine Advocacy and Delivery

Community health nurses serve as vital links between health systems and local populations in promoting immunization coverage. Responsibilities extend beyond vaccine administration to active community mobilization, dispelling vaccine hesitancy, and addressing cultural myths through culturally sensitive health education. Nurses conduct home visits, track defaulters using immunisation registers, and organize outreach clinics to reach nomadic or hard-to-reach settlements. Furthermore, nurses monitor for adverse events following immunization (AEFI), providing prompt reassurance and clinical management to maintain public trust in public health interventions.
