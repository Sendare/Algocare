Immunity

Introduction to Immunity and the Immune System

Immunity is the body's physiological capability to ward off harmful foreign invaders like bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. The immune system operates as a complex network of cells, tissues, and organs working in absolute harmony. Primary defense structures include the bone marrow and thymus, where immune cells are born and mature. Secondary organs, such as the spleen and lymph nodes, serve as battlegrounds where immune responses are mounted against invading pathogens.

White blood cells, or leukocytes, drive this protective machinery. When a pathogen breaches physical barriers like the skin or mucous membranes, these cells recognize foreign molecular signatures known as antigens. Upon identification, the immune system orchestrates a targeted response to neutralize and eliminate the threat, while maintaining memory to respond faster during subsequent exposures.

Innate versus Adaptive Immunity

Defense mechanisms are broadly categorized into innate and adaptive systems. Innate immunity is the non-specific defense present from birth. It acts rapidly upon pathogen encounter using physical barriers like skin, chemical secretions, and phagocytic cells such as macrophages. It reacts identically regardless of how many times a person has met that specific pathogen before.

Conversely, adaptive immunity is specific, highly targeted, and develops over time following exposure to an antigen. Driven by T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes, this branch creates immunological memory. Once an adaptive response occurs, subsequent encounters with the same pathogen trigger a swift and powerful elimination phase, often preventing illness entirely.

Active Immunity: Natural and Artificial

Active immunity occurs when the body's own immune system produces antibodies in response to the presence of an antigen. This form of protection is typically long-lasting because memory cells are generated during the process.

  • Natural active immunity: Acquired when an individual contracts a live infection, fights it off, and subsequently recovers, leaving behind lasting protection.
  • Artificial active immunity: Induced through vaccination, where a weakened, killed, or fragment of a pathogen is introduced to stimulate an immune response without causing severe disease.

Passive Immunity: Natural and Artificial

Passive immunity provides immediate but temporary protection because the body receives pre-formed antibodies rather than manufacturing them independently. Because no memory cells are created, protection fades as the foreign antibodies degrade over weeks or months.

  • Natural passive immunity: Occurs when maternal IgG antibodies cross the placenta to the foetus, or when IgA antibodies are transferred through breast milk.
  • Artificial passive immunity: Delivered through the injection of human immune globulins or antitoxins, usually given after a known exposure to a dangerous toxin or disease like rabies or tetanus.

Herd Immunity and Its Epidemiological Significance

Herd immunity, also known as community immunity, occurs when a high percentage of a population becomes immune to a contagious disease. This high level of immunity makes the spread of disease from person to person unlikely. As a result, the entire community—including those who cannot be vaccinated, such as newborns or immunocompromised individuals—gains indirect protection.

The threshold required to achieve herd immunity depends on how contagious the specific infection is, often measured by the basic reproduction number. When vaccination rates drop below this critical threshold, dangerous outbreaks and epidemic resurgences of vaccine-preventable diseases quickly follow.

Factors Influencing Immune Responses in Populations

An individual's or community's ability to mount a robust immune response is shaped by multiple interconnected variables. Malnutrition remains a major barrier in resource-limited settings, as protein and micronutrient deficiencies impair antibody production and cellular immunity. Chronic illnesses like HIV directly attack immune cells, dampening overall defense capacity.

Age is another critical determinant; the very young have immature immune systems, while the elderly experience immunosenescence, a gradual deterioration of immune function. Furthermore, chronic psychological stress, environmental toxins, and inadequate access to clean water and sanitation further compromise physiological resilience against infections.

Immunization Programs and Cold Chain Management in Nigeria

Effective immunization programs rely heavily on maintaining a strict cold chain—a temperature-controlled supply chain that stores, manages, and transports vaccines from the point of manufacture to the point of administration. Most routine vaccines, such as polio, measles, and pentavalent vaccines, require strict storage temperatures between +2°C and +8°C.

In Nigeria, maintaining this unbroken chain presents unique logistical challenges due to erratic electrical power supply, harsh tropical climates, and remote rural terrains. Nurses and primary healthcare workers must monitor vaccine vial monitors, check refrigerator temperatures twice daily, and properly manage insulated vaccine carriers to ensure biological potency before administration.

Community Health Nursing Roles in Vaccine Advocacy and Delivery

Community health nurses serve as the primary link between healthcare systems and local populations in promoting immunization. Their responsibilities extend far beyond vaccine administration to active health education, community mobilization, and debunking dangerous myths or cultural misconceptions about vaccine safety.

Nurses map out hard-to-reach settlements, conduct home visits, track defaulters who miss scheduled doses, and collaborate with local community leaders to build trust. By ensuring culturally sensitive communication and consistent outreach, nurses bridge immunization gaps and safeguard public health across diverse Nigerian communities.
