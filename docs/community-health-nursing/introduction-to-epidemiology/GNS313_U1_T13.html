Communicable Disease

Introduction to Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

Communicable diseases represent a significant public health burden in Nigeria, contributing substantially to morbidity and mortality rates, especially among vulnerable populations such as children under five and pregnant women. These illnesses are caused by infectious agents—including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites—and can be transmitted from person to person, from animals to humans, or through environmental reservoirs. In the Nigerian context, endemic conditions like malaria, tuberculosis, cholera, Lassa fever, and vaccine-preventable diseases such as measles continue to challenge the healthcare delivery system. Factors such as poverty, overcrowded housing, inadequate sanitation, compromised clean water access, and gaps in routine immunization coverage further exacerbate transmission risks across both rural and urban settlements. For nursing students preparing for professional practice, understanding the epidemiology of these diseases is critical. Nurses serve on the frontline of community and clinical healthcare, playing indispensable roles in early detection, prompt notification, patient management, and public health education. Recognizing the socio-economic and environmental determinants of health unique to West Africa enables nurses to design culturally appropriate interventions, strengthen primary health care structures, and advocate for health policies that mitigate disease outbreaks. Mastery of these foundational concepts equips the nursing workforce to collaborate effectively with governmental and non-governmental agencies in reducing the incidence and impact of communicable diseases nationwide.

The Epidemiological Triangle and Chain of Infection

Understanding how infectious diseases spread requires an examination of the epidemiological triangle and the chain of infection. The epidemiological triangle consists of three interactive components: the agent, the host, and the environment. The agent is the microorganism responsible for the disease, while the host is the human or animal that harbors the infectious agent. The environment includes all external factors—such as climate, socioeconomic conditions, and sanitation—that influence the exposure, susceptibility, or response of a host to an agent. To cause illness, an infectious agent must also navigate the chain of infection, a continuous sequence of six links: the infectious agent, the reservoir, the portal of exit, the mode of transmission, the portal of entry, and the susceptible host. The reservoir is where the agent normally lives and multiplies, which could be a human, animal, or environmental source. The portal of exit is the path by which the pathogen leaves the reservoir, such as respiratory secretions, blood, or gastrointestinal tracts. Transmission can occur via direct contact, droplets, airborne particles, or vectors like mosquitoes. Once the pathogen enters a new susceptible host through a portal of entry, infection may or may not occur depending on the host's immune status. Breaking any single link in this chain stops the spread of infection, making this model an essential framework for nursing interventions and infection control protocols in Nigerian clinical and community settings.

Classification and Modes of Disease Transmission

Communicable diseases are classified in various ways, often by their causative agents, target organ systems, or predominant modes of transmission. Modes of transmission are broadly categorized into direct and indirect mechanisms. Direct transmission involves immediate, physical transfer of microorganisms from an infected or colonized individual to a susceptible host, occurring through touching, kissing, sexual intercourse, or direct droplet spread during sneezing or coughing within close proximity (usually within one meter). Conversely, indirect transmission involves the transfer of an infectious agent from a reservoir to a host via intermediaries. These intermediaries can be inanimate objects known as fomites—such as surgical instruments, door handles, bed linens, or medical equipment—or animate vectors like ticks, fleas, and mosquitoes that carry pathogens from one host to another. Airborne transmission is another critical indirect route, wherein droplet nuclei or dust particles containing infectious agents remain suspended in the air over time and distance, such as in the spread of Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Vector-borne transmission is especially prominent in tropical regions like Nigeria, where mosquitoes transmit Plasmodium species causing malaria or vectors transmit viruses like Yellow Fever and Dengue. Understanding these classifications is essential for nurses to institute appropriate isolation precautions, barrier nursing techniques, environmental decontamination, and vector control measures to safeguard patients, staff, and the wider community.

Stages of the Natural History of Disease

The natural history of disease refers to the progression of a disease process in an individual over time, in the absence of intervention. It is divided into two main phases: the pre-pathogenesis phase and the pathogenesis phase. The pre-pathogenesis phase occurs before the agent interacts with the host. During this stage, factors in the environment, agent, and host interact to create conditions that favor or hinder exposure, though the individual is not yet diseased. Primary prevention measures operate during this pre-pathogenesis phase. The pathogenesis phase begins with the actual entry and establishment of the agent in the host. This phase encompasses several sub-stages: the incubation period (the time between exposure and the appearance of clinical signs and symptoms), early pathogenesis where subclinical changes occur inside the body, fully manifested clinical disease, and finally, the outcomes which may include recovery, chronic disability, or death. Secondary prevention, such as screening and early diagnosis, is applied during the early pathogenesis stage to halt the disease process and prevent complications. Tertiary prevention occurs in the later clinical and recovery stages, focusing on rehabilitation and minimizing long-term impairment. For nursing students, mapping out the natural history of disease helps determine the exact timing for clinical assessments, diagnostic testing, medical treatments, and targeted health education campaigns.

Epidemiological Surveillance and Outbreak Investigation

Epidemiological surveillance is the ongoing, systematic collection, analysis, interpretation, and dissemination of health data essential for the planning, implementation, and evaluation of public health practice. In Nigeria, disease surveillance is coordinated through structures like the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC), tracking priority diseases, conditions, and public health events. Surveillance systems can be passive, where healthcare facilities routinely report diagnosed cases, or active, where public health workers actively search for cases in communities or health facilities. When cases of a communicable disease exceed normal expectancy within a specific geographical area or population, an outbreak is declared, triggering an immediate outbreak investigation. An outbreak investigation follows a systematic series of steps: confirming the existence of the outbreak, verifying the diagnosis, establishing a case definition, finding cases systematically and recording information, performing descriptive epidemiology (time, place, person analysis), developing and testing hypotheses, implementing control and prevention measures, and finally, communicating findings through a comprehensive report. Community health nurses play a pivotal role in surveillance by identifying unusual symptom clusters in patients, recording accurate data in facility registers, reporting notifiable diseases promptly to local health authorities, and participating in rapid response teams. Effective surveillance ensures timely resource allocation, prevents widespread epidemics, and safeguards community health.

Prevention and Control Strategies in Community Health

Prevention and control strategies in community health are categorized into three classic levels: primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention. Primary prevention aims to prevent the occurrence of disease altogether. In the context of communicable diseases in Nigeria, this includes routine childhood immunizations (such as the pentavalent vaccine, polio drops, and measles vaccines), health education on hand hygiene and food safety, vector control measures like distributing insecticide-treated mosquito nets (ITNs), and environmental sanitation programs. Secondary prevention focuses on early diagnosis and prompt treatment of individuals who are already infected, aiming to halt disease progression, shorten the duration of illness, and prevent transmission to others. Examples include voluntary counseling and testing (VCT) for HIV, malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs), sputum smear microscopy for tuberculosis, and contact tracing during outbreaks. Tertiary prevention involves managing established disease, reducing complications, preventing permanent disability, and restoring optimal functioning through rehabilitation and supportive care, such as physical therapy for polio survivors or adherence support for chronic infectious conditions. Community health nurses utilize these multifaceted strategies by designing health promotion campaigns, administering vaccines, conducting screening clinics, and coordinating referral networks across different tiers of the healthcare delivery system to protect communities from preventable morbidity and mortality.

Role of the Community Health Nurse in Disease Management

The community health nurse occupies a multifaceted and vital position in the prevention, management, and control of communicable diseases. Operating at the grassroots level, the nurse functions as a clinician, educator, advocate, epidemiologist, and coordinator of care. As a clinician, the nurse assesses clients, administers medications, provides wound care, and manages cases according to national health guidelines. As an educator, the nurse translates complex medical terminology into clear, culturally sensitive health messages for individuals, families, and communities, addressing myths, promoting hygiene practices, and emphasizing the importance of completing antibiotic or antiretroviral therapy regimens. In the realm of epidemiology, community health nurses are responsible for identifying notifiable diseases, tracking contact lists during outbreaks, collecting biological specimens safely, and maintaining accurate health registers. Furthermore, nurses act as advocates by mobilizing community leaders, engaging local stakeholders, and addressing the social determinants of health that perpetuate infectious disease cycles. By collaborating with multidisciplinary teams, including physicians, laboratory scientists, environmental health officers, and traditional birth attendants, community health nurses help bridge gaps in the healthcare system. Their leadership in primary health care settings ensures that preventive measures reach marginalized populations, ultimately strengthening national health security and improving overall health outcomes throughout Nigeria.
