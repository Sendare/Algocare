Communicable Disease

Introduction to Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

Communicable diseases remain a major public health challenge across Nigeria, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations, particularly children under five and rural communities. These illnesses are caused by infectious agents such as bacteria, viruses, parasites, and fungi that can spread directly or indirectly from one person to another. Endemic and epidemic-prone diseases—including malaria, tuberculosis, cholera, Lassa fever, and measles—place a heavy burden on local health facilities and economic development.

Environmental factors heavily influence disease transmission in this region. Inadequate access to potable drinking water, poor sanitation infrastructure, overcrowded housing, and seasonal variations, such as the rainy season breeding mosquitoes or the dry harmattan winds spreading cerebrospinal meningitis, all drive high infection rates. Recognizing these patterns helps nurses anticipate outbreaks and target health education effectively.

The Epidemiological Triangle and Chain of Infection

Understanding how infectious diseases spread requires examining two foundational epidemiological concepts: the epidemiological triangle and the chain of infection.

  • Epidemiological Triangle: Consists of three interactive elements: the agent (the microorganism), the host (the human who harbors the disease), and the environment (external factors allowing the agent to move to the host).
  • Chain of Infection: A sequential model showing how an infection moves. It begins with the infectious agent, moves to a reservoir (where the agent lives and multiplies), exits via a portal of exit, travels through a mode of transmission, enters through a portal of entry, and finally infects a susceptible host.

Breaking any single link in this chain stops the spread of the disease entirely, which is the primary goal of public health interventions.

Classification and Modes of Disease Transmission

Pathogens move from their sources to new hosts through various pathways, broadly classified as either direct or indirect transmission.

  • Direct Transmission: Involves immediate physical contact between an infected person and a susceptible host, such as touching, kissing, sexual intercourse, or direct droplet spread from sneezing or coughing within a close range (typically less than one meter).
  • Indirect Transmission: Involves an intermediate step. This can happen through vehicles (non-living items like contaminated water, food, or surgical instruments) or vectors (living organisms like mosquitoes, ticks, or flies that carry the pathogen).

Airborne transmission is another critical route where tiny infectious droplet nuclei remain suspended in the air over long distances, posing high risks for diseases like tuberculosis and measles.

Stages of the Natural History of Disease

Every communicable disease progresses through predictable stages within the human body, known as the natural history of disease.

  1. Susceptibility Stage: The period before disease occurs, where risk factors exist but no infection has happened yet.
  2. Pre-symptomatic (Incubation) Stage: The time between exposure to the pathogen and the appearance of the first clinical signs or symptoms. The patient feels completely normal, though the agent is multiplying inside.
  3. Clinical Stage: Symptoms clearly manifest, ranging from mild discomfort to severe organ failure. This is when individuals usually seek medical attention.
  4. Recovery, Disability, or Death Stage: The final outcome where the patient either clears the infection and heals, suffers long-term complications, or succumbs to the illness.

Epidemiological Surveillance and Outbreak Investigation

Epidemiological surveillance is the continuous, systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data essential for planning, implementing, and evaluating public health practice. In Nigeria, the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) coordinates disease surveillance across states through designated reporting systems.

When surveillance data reveals an unusual spike in disease incidence, an outbreak investigation is launched immediately. The standard steps of an outbreak investigation include:

  • Confirming the existence of an outbreak and verifying the diagnosis.
  • Establishing a case definition to identify who is affected.
  • Finding, counting, and mapping cases descriptively (by time, place, and person).
  • Formulating and testing hypotheses regarding the source of infection.
  • Implementing control and prevention measures, followed by evaluating their effectiveness.

Prevention and Control Strategies in Community Health

Public health programs rely on three levels of prevention to manage communicable diseases within populations:

Prevention LevelFocus / GoalConcrete Nursing Examples
PrimaryStop disease before it startsRoutine childhood immunization, health education on handwashing, vector control (bed nets)
SecondaryEarly detection and prompt treatmentScreening programs for tuberculosis, contact tracing, rapid diagnostic testing for malaria
TertiaryReduce complications and long-term disabilityPhysical rehabilitation for polio survivors, proper wound care, supportive chronic care

Control measures also involve quarantine (separating healthy people exposed to a disease) and isolation (separating sick patients from healthy ones) to contain the spread during epidemics.

Role of the Community Health Nurse in Disease Management

The community health nurse acts as a frontline defender in managing communicable diseases. Their responsibilities span clinical care, health advocacy, and community mobilization.

Nurses conduct home visits to assess living conditions, provide direct bedside care or administer medications (such as Directly Observed Therapy for tuberculosis), and ensure proper medical waste disposal. They also educate communities on hygiene practices, dispel harmful cultural myths surrounding disease transmission, and advocate for better local health infrastructure.

Collaboration is vital. Nurses work hand-in-hand with local leaders, environmental health officers, and government agencies to ensure that immunization campaigns reach remote areas and that suspected outbreaks are reported without delay.
