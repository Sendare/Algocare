Universal Precautions

Introduction to Universal Precautions and Standard Precautions

Universal Precautions, originally introduced by the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the mid-1980s, were developed in response to the human immunodeficiency virus epidemic. The fundamental premise is simple: treat all human blood and certain body fluids as if they are known to be infectious for bloodborne pathogens, regardless of the patient's diagnosed status or clinical background.

Over time, this strategy evolved into the broader concept known as Standard Precautions. While Universal Precautions focused exclusively on blood and bloodborne risks, Standard Precautions expand the safety net to include all body fluids, secretions, excretions (except sweat), non-intact skin, and mucous membranes. In busy Nigerian teaching hospitals and rural primary health centers alike, assuming infection risks are absent in patients who appear healthy is a dangerous clinical fallacy. Standard Precautions form the baseline tier of infection prevention and control, applied universally to every patient during every clinical interaction to break the chain of transmission.

Core Principles and Operational Context in Nigerian Healthcare

Applying infection control frameworks in resource-constrained environments presents unique operational challenges. Frequent stockouts of single-use gloves, intermittent municipal water supplies, and irregular electricity can severely disrupt standard protocols. Despite these systemic hurdles, the core tenets of infection prevention remain non-negotiable.

Clinicians must adapt without compromising safety. When running water fails, designated tippy-taps or stored chlorinated water systems serve as vital stopgaps for hand hygiene. Resource scarcity should never justify skipping barrier precautions during invasive procedures or blood collection. Healthcare institutions must foster a culture of safety where junior nursing staff feel empowered to halt procedures if basic protective supplies are missing. Pragmatic adaptation combined with an unwavering commitment to patient and staff safety defines effective infection control practice across diverse Nigerian clinical settings.

Personal Protective Equipment: Selection and Proper Usage

Personal Protective Equipment acts as a physical barrier between the healthcare worker and potential pathogens. Selecting the correct item depends entirely on the anticipated exposure.

  • Gloves: Wear when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin. Remove immediately after task completion and perform hand hygiene.
  • Gowns: Utilize during procedures likely to generate splashes or sprays of blood or bodily fluids to protect clothing and skin.
  • Masks and Eye Protection: Wear surgical masks combined with face shields or goggles whenever droplets, sprays, or splashes to the face are anticipated.
  • Respirators: Employ N95 or equivalent particulate respirators during aerosol-generating procedures on patients with suspected airborne infections like pulmonary tuberculosis.

Putting on and removing PPE in the correct sequence is just as critical as wearing it. Donning follows a logical progression from the cleanest items to the dirtier ones, typically starting with gowns, then masks, eye protection, and finally gloves. Removal, or doffing, follows a sequence designed to minimize self-contamination, prioritizing glove removal first because they carry the highest pathogen load.

Hand Hygiene Protocols and Clinical Best Practices

Hand hygiene is universally acknowledged as the single most effective intervention to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Microorganisms on hands are categorized as transient flora, acquired through direct patient contact or contaminated environments, and resident flora, which permanently colonize deeper skin layers.

Clinical staff must execute hand hygiene according to the World Health Organization's Five Moments for Hand Hygiene:

  1. Before touching a patient: Protects the patient against harmful germs carried on the nurse's hands.
  2. Before clean/aseptic procedures: Protects the patient against harmful germs, including the patient's own, from entering their body.
  3. After body fluid exposure risk: Protects the nurse and the healthcare environment from harmful patient germs.
  4. After touching a patient: Protects the nurse and the healthcare environment from harmful patient germs.
  5. After touching patient surroundings: Protects the nurse and the healthcare environment from harmful patient germs.

Handwashing with soap and water is mandatory when hands are visibly soiled, contaminated with blood or other body fluids, or after caring for patients with spore-forming pathogens like *Clostridioides difficile*. When hands are not visibly soiled, alcohol-based hand rub is preferred due to superior antimicrobial efficacy and skin tolerance.

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Injury Prevention

Sharps injuries represent one of the most hazardous occupational threats faced by nurses, exposing them to hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV. Safe injection practices are designed to protect both patients and healthcare providers from bloodborne pathogen transmission.

Never recap used needles using a two-handed technique. If recapping is absolutely unavoidable due to specific procedural constraints, employ a one-handed scoop technique or a mechanical device designed for needle shielding. Needles, syringes, scalpels, and other sharp instruments must be disposed of immediately after use into puncture-resistant, leak-proof sharps containers located at arm's reach within the workspace.

Overfilling sharps containers is a major occupational hazard frequently observed in busy wards; containers must be sealed and replaced when they reach the three-quarters full line. Furthermore, medications drawn into syringes must be administered immediately to the intended patient, and single-dose vials should never be pooled or used as multi-dose reservoirs for multiple patients.

Management of Healthcare Waste and Spills

Proper segregation of healthcare waste at the point of generation protects sanitation workers, the community, and the environment. Color-coding systems, though varying slightly by local guidelines, generally categorize waste into specific streams:

  • Black bins: Non-hazardous municipal waste, including paper, food wrappers, and administrative rubbish.
  • Yellow bins: Infectious anatomical and pathological waste, soaked dressings, and items contaminated with blood or body fluids destined for incineration.
  • Brown or Red bins: Highly hazardous pharmaceutical or chemical waste.
  • Yellow puncture-proof boxes: Sharps and needles.

When accidental spills of blood or body fluids occur on floors or hard surfaces, prompt action is required. Nurses should never clean up spills dry. First, don appropriate PPE including heavy utility gloves and a gown. Apply absorbent material to soak up excess fluid, clear the area, and disinfect the surface using a freshly prepared chlorine solution (typically a 0.5% sodium hypochlorite dilution for blood spills), allowing adequate contact time before wiping clean.

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis and Incident Management

Despite rigorous adherence to safety protocols, occupational exposures to blood and body fluids via needlestick injuries, cuts, or mucous membrane splashes still occur. Immediate first-line response following a percutaneous injury involves immediate physical care of the site.

  • For skin exposures: Wash the affected puncture or cut immediately with soap and running water. Do not scrub aggressively, and avoid squeezing or milking the wound, as this can increase pathogen absorption into local capillaries.
  • For mucous membrane splashes: Flush the affected area thoroughly with clean water, normal saline, or sterile eyewash.

Following initial first aid, the incident must be reported immediately to the infection control unit or occupational health officer. A rapid risk assessment evaluates the source patient's infection status and the nature of the exposure. If indicated, Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV must be initiated ideally within hours, and definitely within 72 hours of exposure, combined with baseline serological testing and appropriate counseling.

Community Health Nursing Strategies for Infection Control

Infection prevention extends far beyond hospital walls. Community health nurses play a pivotal role in translating universal precautions into home-based care and rural health outreaches where formal clinical infrastructure is absent. Family caregivers managing chronic illnesses or wound care at home face risks similar to hospital personnel.

Nurses must educate community members on basic hygiene, safe disposal of household healthcare waste—such as diabetic lancets—and improvising clean barriers when manufactured PPE is unavailable. During disease outbreaks, community health strategies focus on demystifying transmission routes, promoting water sanitation, and building local trust. Empowering communities with practical, culturally resonant infection control knowledge ensures sustainable health security from rural villages to urban slums.
