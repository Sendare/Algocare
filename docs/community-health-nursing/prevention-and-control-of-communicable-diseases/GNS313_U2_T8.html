Universal Precautions

Introduction to Universal Precautions and Standard Precautions

In busy clinical spaces, health workers face blood, body fluids, and non-intact skin daily. Universal Precautions, first introduced by the CDC in the late 1980s, were designed to protect staff from bloodborne pathogens like HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C by treating all human blood and certain body fluids as if known to be infectious.

Later, the system evolved into Standard Precautions. This expanded framework applies to all patients receiving care, regardless of their diagnosis or presumed infection status. Standard precautions go beyond blood to cover all body fluids, secretions, excretions (except sweat), non-intact skin, and mucous membranes. In modern Nigerian nursing practice, treating every patient with the same baseline level of caution prevents accidental cross-infection when medical histories are incomplete or unknown.

Core Principles and Rationale in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Resource-constrained environments present unique hurdles for infection prevention. Frequent utility failures, such as irregular water supply or unstable electricity, test the resilience of hospital hygiene systems. Despite these infrastructural gaps, the core principle remains unchanged: every patient interaction carries potential infection risks.

Adopting a default safety mindset protects both the nurse and the community. When supplies run short, improvising unsafe shortcuts—like reusing single-use syringes or skipping hand hygiene—dramatically increases nosocomial transmission rates. Recognizing local system constraints helps nurses advocate for basic safety commodities and adopt reliable work practices even under pressure.

Personal Protective Equipment: Selection and Proper Usage

Barrier items create a physical shield between the healthcare worker and potential infectious agents. Selecting the correct item depends on the anticipated type of exposure.

  • Gloves: Wear when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin. Change gloves between patients and never wash or reuse disposable pairs.
  • Gowns: Protect skin and clothing during procedures that are likely to generate splashes or sprays of blood or body fluids.
  • Masks and Eye Protection: Use surgical masks and face shields or goggles whenever splashes to the eyes, nose, or mouth are possible, such as during suctioning or wound irrigation.

A common misconception is that wearing gloves eliminates the need for hand hygiene. Gloves can develop microscopic holes or become contaminated during removal, making handwashing mandatory immediately after taking them off.

Hand Hygiene Protocols and Clinical Best Practices

Hand hygiene is widely recognized as the single most effective intervention for halting the spread of hospital-acquired infections. Nurses must understand when to use soap and water versus alcohol-based hand rub.

Use alcohol-based hand rub for routine decontamination when hands are not visibly soiled. It acts quickly and kills most transient micro-organisms effectively. When hands are visibly dirty, soiled with blood or body fluids, or after caring for patients with spore-forming organisms like *Clostridioides difficile*, traditional handwashing with soap and running water for at least 40 to 60 seconds is mandatory. Mechanical friction from washing physically removes dirt and spores that alcohol rubs cannot destroy.

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Injury Prevention

Needlesticks and sharp instrument injuries expose health workers to serious bloodborne pathogens. Preventing these injuries requires constant mindfulness and adherence to safety rules.

  • Never recap used needles using both hands.
  • If a needle must be recapped, use a safe one-handed scoop technique.
  • Never bend, break, or remove used needles from syringes by hand.
  • Immediately dispose of all used needles, lancets, and scalpels into a puncture-resistant safety box.
  • Never overfill sharps containers; close and replace them once they reach the three-quarters full mark.

A frequent error is placing a used needle on a bedside table or linen trolley instead of dropping it directly into the nearest sharps box, which creates an unnecessary hazard for housekeeping staff and colleagues.

Management of Healthcare Waste and Spills

Proper segregation of hospital waste protects the environment and the public. Color-coded bin systems are standard in most Nigerian healthcare facilities:

  • Black Bins: Non-hazardous domestic waste such as paper packaging, food remnants, and office trash.
  • Yellow Bins: Infectious clinical waste including soiled dressings, cotton swabs, and disposable items contaminated with body fluids (often incinerated).
  • Brown or Red Bins: Specialized or anatomical waste depending on local guidelines.

When blood or body fluid spills occur on floors or hard surfaces, staff must act quickly. Put on heavy-duty utility gloves, flood the area with a chlorine-based disinfectant solution (such as diluted sodium hypochlorite), allow proper contact time, wipe up the fluid with disposable absorbent materials, and discard everything into the infectious waste bin.

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis and Incident Management

Despite precautions, accidents happen. When a nurse suffers a sharps injury or mucous membrane splash, prompt action can prevent permanent infection.

  1. Immediate First Aid: Wash needle-pricked skin immediately with soap and running water. For splashes to the eyes or mouth, rinse thoroughly with clean water or normal saline.
  2. Report the Incident: Notify the infection control officer or supervisor immediately to document the event.
  3. Risk Assessment: Evaluate the source patient's infection status and the nature of the exposure.
  4. Intervention: If indicated, start Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV within hours of exposure, ideally under 72 hours, alongside baseline screening tests for Hepatitis B and C.

Community Health Nursing Strategies for Infection Control

Infection control extends far beyond hospital walls. Community health nurses play a vital role in educating families, traditional birth attendants, and local volunteers on basic hygiene and safe practices.

During home visits or outreach clinics, nurses teach proper handwashing techniques, safe drinking water storage, and how to manage sick family members without spreading illness. Engaging community leaders helps bridge the gap between formal medical knowledge and traditional practices. When rural communities understand how infections spread, adherence to hygiene improves, reducing the burden of preventable infectious diseases across the wider population.
