Transmission-Based Precautions

Foundation of Standard Precautions

Standard precautions form the core level of infection control applied to the care of all patients regardless of their diagnosed or presumed infection status. These measures assume that every body fluid, non-intact skin, and mucous membrane may harbor transmissible infectious agents. Core practices involve consistent hand hygiene, the strategic deployment of personal protective equipment when exposure is anticipated, safe injection practices, respiratory hygiene, and meticulous environmental cleaning. Implementing these baseline controls drastically reduces cross-contamination risks across clinical settings before any specific transmission route is even identified.

Contact Precautions

Contact precautions are implemented when patients are known or suspected to be infected with epidemiologically important pathogens spread by direct or indirect contact with the patient or their immediate environment.

  • Direct Contact: Transfer of microorganisms from an infected person to a susceptible host through physical touch.
  • Indirect Contact: Transfer of infectious agents via an intermediate object, such as contaminated hands, medical equipment, or high-touch environmental surfaces.

Required barriers typically include single-room placement and the donning of gloves and disposable gowns upon room entry. Common targets for these measures include multidrug-resistant organisms, Clostridioides difficile, and acute enteric infections.

Droplet Precautions

Droplet precautions guard against pathogens transmitted through large respiratory droplets, typically generated when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks, as well as during procedural interventions like suctioning. Because these heavy particles generally travel no further than one to two meters before settling, airborne suspension is limited.

Healthcare workers must wear a standard surgical mask upon entering the patient's room. Placing the patient in a private room or cohorting them with individuals infected with the exact same organism is standard practice. Transport outside the room should be strictly limited, and if movement is clinically necessary, the patient must wear a surgical mask to contain expelled droplets.

Airborne Precautions

Airborne precautions are mandatory when managing patients harboring infectious agents transmitted via small droplet nuclei that remain infectious over long distances and extended periods suspended in the air. Pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, varicella-zoster virus, and the measles virus require this elevated level of intervention.

Engineering controls are central to airborne containment. Patients must be placed in an Airborne Infection Isolation Room featuring negative pressure ventilation, which ensures air flows into the room rather than leaking out into clinical corridors. Air must be exhausted directly outside or filtered through high-efficiency particulate air filters. Healthcare workers entering these spaces must wear fit-tested particulate respirators, such as an N95 or higher-level device, rather than standard surgical masks.

PPE Donning and Doffing Sequence

Personal protective equipment acts as the final physical barrier between a clinician and infectious agents, yet improper removal frequently leads to self-contamination. Donning follows an intentional sequence designed to ensure full coverage before high-risk exposure occurs, typically beginning with hygiene, followed by the gown, mask or respirator, eye protection, and finally gloves.

Doffing follows the exact reverse logic or a protocol designed to minimize contact with contaminated exterior surfaces. Gloves are removed first, as they carry the highest microbial load, followed by eye protection, gown, and respiratory gear. Hand hygiene must be performed meticulously at strategic steps throughout the removal process to neutralize any accidental contact with pathogens.

Environmental Disinfection and Patient Transport

Maintaining safe care environments during transmission-based precautions extends far beyond individual barrier use. High-touch surfaces—including bedrails, call buttons, IV poles, and bedside tables—require rigorous, routine disinfection using agents proven effective against the specific circulating pathogen.

When patient transport outside an isolation room is unavoidable, the journey must be carefully planned. Receiving departments must be notified in advance so they can prepare their spaces and minimize waiting times in shared public areas. The patient must wear appropriate containment barriers, such as a surgical mask for droplet or airborne risks, and wounds must be securely covered with clean dressings to prevent environmental seeding along transport corridors.

Infection Control in Nigerian Clinical Settings

Applying transmission-based precautions in resource-constrained Nigerian healthcare facilities presents unique structural and logistical hurdles. Frequent power outages can disrupt centralized mechanical ventilation systems, making reliance on natural cross-ventilation crucial for ward designs managing airborne threats like tuberculosis. Shortages of single-use disposable PPE often necessitate innovative cohorting strategies and rigorous adherence to safe equipment reprocessing protocols where single-use is impossible. Nurse leaders must adapt standard infection prevention frameworks to local realities without compromising the core scientific principles of barrier nursing and patient safety.
