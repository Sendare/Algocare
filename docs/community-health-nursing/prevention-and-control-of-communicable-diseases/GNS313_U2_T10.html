Transmission-Based Precautions

Introduction to Transmission-Based Precautions

Transmission-based precautions are the second tier of basic infection control, designed for patients documented or suspected of being infected with highly transmissible pathogens. While standard precautions apply to all patients regardless of diagnosis, transmission-based precautions are tailored to the specific route of spread: contact, droplet, or airborne. Recognizing that standard precautions alone are insufficient to interrupt certain epidemiological chains of infection, healthcare facilities implement these extra measures immediately upon admission or symptom onset. Clinical staff must understand that these precautions do not replace standard practices; rather, they add targeted barriers to protect both healthcare personnel and other vulnerable patients in the ward.

Standard Precautions as the Foundation

Standard precautions form the bedrock of all hospital infection prevention programs. They are applied to every patient, every time, regardless of their infectious status. These practices recognize that any body fluid, non-intact skin, and mucous membrane may contain transmissible infectious agents.

  • Hand hygiene: The single most effective way to stop germ spread, using soap and water or alcohol rub.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: Wearing gloves, masks, eye protection, and gowns when splashes or sprays of blood or body fluids are likely.
  • Safe injection practices: Using sterile, single-use needles and syringes for every injection.
  • Environmental cleaning: Ensuring patient care equipment and room surfaces are properly cleaned and disinfected between users.

Contact Precautions: Indications and Implementation

Contact precautions are used when an infection can be spread directly through skin-to-skin contact or indirectly by touching contaminated environmental surfaces and patient care items. Common targets for these measures include multi-drug resistant organisms, enteric infections like Clostridioides difficile, and draining wounds.

Nurses must place the patient in a private room whenever possible or cohort them with someone having the same infection. Gloves must be worn upon entering the room, and a clean isolation gown must be put on if clothing is likely to touch the patient or environmental surfaces. A common clinical mistake is removing PPE inside the patient room; all disposable barriers must be removed and hand hygiene performed before exiting the space.

Droplet Precautions: Pathogens and Barrier Methods

Droplet precautions prevent the transmission of pathogens spread through close respiratory or mucous membrane contact, typically generated when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks. These larger droplets do not remain suspended in the air for long and generally travel only short distances, usually one meter or less.

Pathogens requiring droplet precautions include influenza, pertussis, and Neisseria meningitidis. Healthcare personnel entering the room must wear a medical or surgical mask. A private room is preferred, but if unavailable, maintaining spatial separation of at least one meter between beds and drawing privacy curtains can help. Unlike airborne infections, special ventilation is not required for droplet isolation.

Airborne Precautions: Engineering Controls and Respiratory Protection

Airborne precautions are necessary for tiny infectious droplet nuclei that remain suspended in the air for extended periods and can be dispersed widely by air currents. Diseases spread this way include pulmonary tuberculosis, measles, and varicella.

Managing airborne pathogens requires specialized environmental controls alongside personal gear:

  • Negative pressure rooms: Air flows into the room from the outside, preventing infectious particles from escaping into the hallway when doors open.
  • HEPA filtration: Exhaust air is filtered to trap microscopic pathogens before release.
  • Respiratory protection: Staff must wear fitted N95 or higher-level respirators rather than simple surgical masks.
  • Door management: The isolation room door must remain closed at all times except during entry and exit.

Proper Donning and Doffing of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Putting on and taking off personal protective equipment safely is critical to prevent self-contamination. Donning follows a specific order to ensure protection before exposure: gown first, then mask or respirator, eye protection, and finally gloves.

Doffing is even more hazardous because the outer surfaces of PPE are contaminated. The removal sequence must be executed carefully: gloves first, followed by eye protection, gown, and respirator last. Hand hygiene must be performed between steps if hands become contaminated. A frequent error in clinical settings is touching the front of a used mask or gown during removal, which transfers pathogens directly to the skin or scrubs.

Patient Placement, Transport, and Environmental Disinfection

Managing patient movement and room hygiene minimizes secondary transmission within health facilities. When a patient on transmission-based precautions must be transported outside their room for diagnostic tests, staff must notify the receiving department in advance. The patient wears a surgical mask if they have a respiratory infection, and draining wounds must be securely covered.

Environmental surfaces require rigorous cleaning using hospital-approved disinfectants. High-touch surfaces such as bed rails, call buttons, IV poles, and door handles need frequent disinfection. Terminal cleaning must occur immediately after a patient is discharged, ensuring the room is thoroughly safe for the next occupant.

Infection Control in Nigerian Community Health Settings

Implementing transmission-based precautions in resource-constrained Nigerian clinics and primary healthcare centers presents unique challenges. Erratic water supply, power outages, and limited stocks of disposable PPE require practical adaptations without compromising patient safety. Nurses must champion local solutions such as Veronica buckets for continuous handwashing and the use of locally available chlorine bleach solutions for surface disinfection when commercial disinfectants are scarce.

Staff training must emphasize improvisation and prioritization—focusing heavily on strict hand hygiene and safe waste segregation. Safe disposal of needles using locally made safety boxes prevents community needle-stick injuries, protecting both healthcare workers and the surrounding population from bloodborne infections.
