Ascariasis

Introduction and Definition of Ascariasis

Ascariasis is a prevalent intestinal helminthic infection caused by the giant roundworm, **Ascaris lumbricoides**. It remains one of the most common human parasitic infections globally, particularly affecting tropical and subtropical regions with warm climates and inadequate sanitation infrastructure. The adult worm resides in the lumen of the small intestine, where females can produce hundreds of thousands of eggs daily, which are then passed out through human faeces. While many infected individuals remain asymptomatic, heavy worm burdens can cause significant nutritional impairment, growth retardation in children, and acute surgical emergencies due to intestinal obstruction. Understanding this parasite is essential for healthcare providers working in endemic zones across sub-Saharan Africa.

Etiology and the Unique Life Cycle

The life cycle of **Ascaris lumbricoides** involves a complex migration through the human body before settling in the gastrointestinal tract. - **Ingestion**: Unembryonated eggs are passed in faeces and become infective in the soil after developing an embryo. - **Larval Migration**: Once ingested via contaminated food or water, the eggs hatch in the small intestine, releasing larvae that penetrate the intestinal wall. - **Pulmonary Phase**: Larvae enter the blood or lymph vessels, travel to the heart and lungs, break into the alveoli, ascend the bronchial tree, and are swallowed. - **Maturation**: Upon returning to the small intestine, the larvae mature into adult worms, completing a cycle that takes roughly two to three months.

Transmission Dynamics and Risk Factors in Nigerian Communities

Transmission occurs exclusively through the faecal-oral route via the ingestion of embryonated eggs. In many Nigerian communities, agricultural practices utilizing untreated human excreta as fertilizer, known as nightsoil, facilitate soil contamination. Geophagia, the intentional ingestion of soil, is frequently observed among young children and pregnant women, serving as a direct vehicle for parasite acquisition. Inadequate potable water supplies and limited access to sanitary latrines further compound these environmental risks. Consequently, school-aged children bear the highest burden of infection due to frequent outdoor play and suboptimal hand hygiene habits.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

The clinical presentation of ascariasis varies depending on the parasite burden and the migration stage. Light infections are frequently asymptomatic. During larval migration through the lungs, patients may develop **Löffler syndrome**, characterized by a dry cough, dyspnoea, wheezing, and transient eosinophilia. Intestinal stage manifestations range from vague, recurrent colicky abdominal pain and nausea to protein-energy malnutrition and growth stunting in children. Adult worms compete for host nutrients and can cause mechanical irritation of the bowel mucosa. In heavy infestations, tangled masses of worms can precipitate complete intestinal blockage or migrate aberrantly into the biliary tree.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough nursing assessment includes evaluating the patient's nutritional status, abdominal comfort, bowel habits, and recent travel or residency history in endemic rural areas. Nurses should actively screen children for signs of abdominal distension, failure to thrive, and respiratory complaints. - **Stool Microscopy**: The definitive diagnostic method is microscopic examination of stool samples to identify characteristic fertilized or unfertilized Ascaris eggs. - **Imaging Studies**: Abdominal plain radiographs may occasionally reveal a bolus of worms or show features of mechanical bowel obstruction. - **Laboratory Tests**: Complete blood count often reveals peripheral eosinophilia during the migration phase.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Treatment

Pharmacological therapy aims to eradicate the parasite safely and efficiently. **Albendazole** and **Mebendazole** are the first-line anthelmintic medications of choice due to their high efficacy and minimal side effects. They work by inhibiting microtubule formation in the parasite's cells, leading to glucose depletion and death of the worm. **Ivermectin** serves as an alternative therapeutic option. When treating a patient with a massive worm burden, clinicians must exercise caution because single-dose anthelmintics can stimulate erratic worm migration, potentially triggering intestinal obstruction or biliary colic as the dying worms thrash. Supportive care, including rehydration and nutritional supplementation, is initiated concurrently.

Complications of Severe Ascariasis

While mild infections cause few problems, heavy parasite loads can result in life-threatening complications requiring surgical or emergency intervention. Intestinal obstruction occurs when a dense bolus of worms physically blocks the bowel lumen, presenting with severe colicky pain, vomiting, and obstipation. Ectopic migration of adult worms can lead to biliary ascariasis, acute pancreatitis, appendicitis, or hepatic abscesses if worms enter the biliary or pancreatic ducts. In children, chronic malnutrition and malabsorption secondary to heavy infection severely compromise growth, cognitive development, and overall immune function.

Community Health Nursing Interventions, Prevention, and Control

Combating ascariasis requires a comprehensive public health approach centered on community education and environmental sanitation. Community health nurses play a pivotal role in organizing school-based mass drug administration campaigns in endemic areas. Health education initiatives must focus on promoting handwashing with soap before handling food, washing raw vegetables thoroughly, and preventing open defecation by constructing and utilizing sanitary latrines. Nurses also advocate for clean water access and safe waste disposal systems. Through sustained community engagement, recurrence rates can be drastically minimized, improving the overall health profile of vulnerable populations.
