Ascariasis

Introduction and Definition of Ascariasis

Ascariasis is a common intestinal infection caused by the giant roundworm, Ascaris lumbricoides. It remains a major global public health concern, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions with warm climates and inadequate sanitation infrastructure. The parasite lives in the small intestine of human hosts, where females can grow up to 35 centimeters in length and produce hundreds of thousands of eggs daily. These eggs are passed out through human feces into the environment. While many infected individuals remain asymptomatic, heavy worm burdens can cause significant nutritional deficiencies, physical growth retardation in children, and life-threatening surgical emergencies.

Life Cycle and Transmission Pathways

  • Ingestion: Humans become infected by accidentally swallowing infective Ascaris eggs, usually through contaminated food, water, or soil.
  • Larval Migration: Once swallowed, the eggs hatch into larvae in the small intestine, penetrate the intestinal wall, and enter the bloodstream.
  • Pulmonary Phase: The larvae travel through the heart to the lungs, break into the alveoli, ascend the bronchial tree, and are swallowed back down into the digestive tract.
  • Maturation: Upon returning to the small intestine, the larvae mature into adult worms, completing a cycle that takes about two to three months.

A common misconception is that person-to-person spread occurs directly; however, fresh feces are not immediately infectious because Ascaris eggs must mature in the soil for weeks to become infective.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Symptoms

The clinical picture of ascariasis depends heavily on the stage of the parasite and the worm burden. During the migratory larval phase through the lungs, patients often develop a dry cough, wheezing, low-grade fever, and transient pneumonitis resembling an asthma attack, a phenomenon known as Loeffler's syndrome. Once adult worms settle in the gut, mild infections cause vague abdominal discomfort or pass unnoticed. Heavy worm loads lead to intermittent colicky pain, loss of appetite, protein-energy malnutrition, and stunted growth in children. Because adult worms are sensitive to chemical changes, fever, or certain drugs, they can become disoriented and wander into unusual locations like the biliary tree, appendix, or pancreatic duct, causing acute surgical emergencies.

Severe Complications

  • Intestinal Obstruction: A tangled mass of adult worms can completely block the lumen of the small intestine, most commonly at the ileocecal valve in young children.
  • Biliary Ascariasis: Migration of worms into the bile ducts or gallbladder leads to biliary colic, cholecystitis, cholangitis, or acute pancreatitis.
  • Perforation: Weakened intestinal walls can rupture, leading to peritonitis and severe sepsis.

Intestinal obstruction presents with severe colicky pain, persistent vomiting, abdominal distension, and the inability to pass stool or flatus. Nurses must monitor for signs of bowel ischemia or shock, as these surgical emergencies require prompt medical intervention.

Nursing Assessment, Diagnosis, and Management

Nursing assessment involves a thorough history regarding sanitation habits, water sources, and travel to endemic areas, alongside physical checks for malnutrition, abdominal tenderness, and respiratory distress. The definitive diagnosis of intestinal ascariasis relies on stool microscopy to identify characteristic fertilized or unfertilized Ascaris eggs. In some cases, adult worms are spontaneously vomited or passed in the stool, which instantly confirms the diagnosis.

Medical management centers on anti-helminthic medications such as albendazole or mebendazole, which paralyze and kill the adult worms safely in the gut. For patients with complete intestinal obstruction, conservative management with nasogastric suction and intravenous fluids is attempted first, followed by anti-helminthic therapy or surgical removal if obstruction fails to resolve.

Community Health Nursing, Prevention, and Control

  • Sanitation Improvement: Constructing and properly maintaining sanitary latrines to prevent human waste from contaminating local soil and water sources.
  • Hygiene Education: Teaching communities proper handwashing techniques with soap and water before handling food and after using the toilet.
  • Food Safety: Emphasizing the washing of raw fruits and vegetables with clean, safe water, and cooking food thoroughly.
  • Mass Drug Administration (MDA): Implementing school-based deworming programs in high-prevalence areas to regularly treat children and reduce overall worm burdens in the community.

Community health nurses play a vital role in breaking the transmission cycle. Sustainable control relies heavily on changing long-term sanitation behaviors and ensuring clean drinking water access rather than relying on treatment alone.
