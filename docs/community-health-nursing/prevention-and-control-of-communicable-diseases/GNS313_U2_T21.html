Amoebiasis

Introduction and Definition of Amoebiasis

Amoebiasis is a parasitic infection of the intestines caused by a microscopic single-celled organism. It is a major public health concern, particularly in areas with crowded living conditions and poor sanitation. While many people infected with the parasite show no signs of illness, others develop severe intestinal distress or extra-intestinal disease. Understanding this condition requires looking closely at how the parasite enters the body and how it behaves once inside the host.

Causative Agent and Mode of Transmission

The causative organism is Entamoeba histolytica. This parasite exists in two forms during its life cycle:

  • Cyst: The hard, inactive shell that survives outside the body in soil or water.
  • Trophozoite: The active, feeding form that multiplies inside the human gut.

Transmission happens strictly through the fecal-oral route. Humans ingest mature cysts from contaminated food, water, or unwashed hands. Houseflies and food handlers play a major role in transferring cysts from faeces to what people eat and drink.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

Once swallowed, cysts travel to the small intestine where they hatch into active trophozoites. These trophozoites move down into the large intestine. They burrow into the mucosal lining of the colon, creating characteristic flask-shaped ulcers.

Symptoms vary widely. Intestinal amoebiasis presents with lower abdominal cramps, tenderness, and frequent watery or bloody stools known as amoebic dysentery. Some patients experience mild diarrhea alternating with constipation. If the parasite invades the bloodstream, it can travel to the liver and form an amoebic liver abscess, causing right upper quadrant pain and fever.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Confirming amoebiasis relies heavily on laboratory tests.

  1. Stool Microscopy: Examining fresh stool samples under a microscope to find cysts or red blood cells inside trophozoites. Multiple samples are often needed because shedding varies.
  2. Serology Tests: Blood tests detecting specific antibodies against the parasite, which is especially useful for diagnosing extra-intestinal infections like liver abscesses.
  3. Imaging Studies: Ultrasound or CT scans help locate and measure abscesses in the liver or other organs.
  4. Colonoscopy: Direct visual inspection of the bowel wall and tissue biopsy when stool results are unclear.

Medical and Nursing Management

Treating amoebiasis involves eliminating both the active tissue-invading parasites and the dormant cysts in the bowel lumen.

  • Tissue Amebicides: Medications like metronidazole or tinidazole kill active trophozoites in the gut wall and liver.
  • Luminal Agents: Drugs like paromomycin clear out remaining cysts from the intestinal lumen to prevent relapse.

Nursing care focuses on monitoring hydration status, recording stool frequency and consistency, administering prescribed anti-parasitic drugs with food to reduce nausea, and managing abdominal pain. Nurses also educate patients on strict hand hygiene to prevent spreading the infection to family members.

Complications of Amoebiasis

Untreated or delayed treatment can lead to life-threatening complications.

  • Intestinal Perforation: Deep ulcers can weaken the bowel wall until it tears, spilling stool contents into the sterile abdominal cavity and causing peritonitis.
  • Toxic Megacolon: Severe inflammation causes the large bowel to dilate dangerously, halting normal movement.
  • Amoebic Liver Abscess Rupture: An abscess can burst into the chest cavity, lung, or heart sac, causing respiratory distress or shock.

Nurses must monitor vital signs closely and report sudden spikes in fever, severe abdominal rigidity, or worsening pain immediately.

Community Prevention and Control

Controlling amoebiasis requires a strong focus on public health sanitation and individual hygiene habits.

  • Safe Water: Boiling drinking water or using reliable filtration systems.
  • Food Safety: Washing all raw fruits and vegetables thoroughly with clean water, and avoiding food from unregulated street vendors.
  • Hand Hygiene: Washing hands meticulously with soap and water after using the toilet and before handling food.
  • Sanitation: Proper disposal of human waste and protecting community water sources from sewage contamination.
