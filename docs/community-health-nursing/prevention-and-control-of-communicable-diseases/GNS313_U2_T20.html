Gastroenteritis

Understanding Gastroenteritis: Definition and Causes

Gastroenteritis is an inflammation of the mucous membrane lining the stomach and intestines, commonly referred to as stomach flu. In clinical practice, it presents primarily as sudden watery diarrhea and vomiting, often accompanied by abdominal cramps and low-grade fever.

In Nigeria, the burden of gastroenteritis is high due to seasonal variations, environmental factors, and water access challenges. The causative agents are diverse:

  • Rotavirus: The leading cause of severe, dehydrating diarrhea in infants and young children under two years old.
  • Escherichia coli (E. coli): Often linked to contaminated food and water, frequently causing traveller's diarrhea or bloody stools.
  • Vibrio cholerae: Responsible for epidemic cholera, characterized by profuse, painless watery stools resembling rice water.
  • Salmonella and Shigella species: Bacterial pathogens that invade the intestinal lining, frequently causing dysentery with blood and mucus in the stool.
  • Parasites: Organisms like *Giardia lamblia* and *Entamoeba histolytica* cause prolonged, persistent diarrheal illnesses.

Pathophysiology and Transmission Dynamics

The transmission of gastroenteritis typically occurs via the fecal-oral route. This happens when microscopic pathogens from infected stool find their way into the mouth of a healthy person through contaminated drinking water, unwashed raw foods, dirty eating utensils, or unwashed hands after toilet use.

Once ingested, these pathogens bypass the acidic barrier of the stomach and reach the intestines. Depending on the organism, they either produce enterotoxins that force the intestinal cells to dump massive amounts of water and electrolytes into the gut lumen, or they directly invade the mucosal cells, causing cellular destruction and bleeding. This massive fluid shift overwhelms the absorptive capacity of the colon, resulting in frequent watery evacuations and rapid depletion of body fluids and vital minerals like sodium and potassium.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Recognizing the clinical signs of gastroenteritis early allows nurses to intervene before severe complications set in. Patients usually present with a sudden onset of loose, watery stools and frequent vomiting. Abdominal pain, cramping, and low-grade fever are also very common.

The most critical part of nursing assessment is evaluating hydration status. A nurse must check for signs of fluid volume deficit:

  • Sunken eyeballs and a dry tongue or oral mucosa.
  • Poor skin turgor, where pinched skin on the abdomen or arm goes back very slowly.
  • Reduced urine output, or dark-concentrated urine.
  • Lethargy, restlessness, or altered mental status in severe cases, especially among infants and the elderly.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Most cases of mild gastroenteritis are diagnosed based on clinical history and physical examination alone, without the need for expensive laboratory tests. However, when symptoms are severe, persistent, or accompanied by blood in the stool, investigations become necessary.

  • Stool Microscopy, Culture, and Sensitivity: Helps identify specific bacterial or parasitic agents and guides targeted antibiotic therapy if needed.
  • Serum Electrolytes, Urea, and Creatinine (E/U/Cr): Essential for assessing electrolyte imbalances such as hypokalemia or hyponatremia, and checking kidney function secondary to dehydration.
  • Full Blood Count (FBC): Elevated white blood cells often point toward a severe bacterial infection.

Medical and Nursing Management

The cornerstone of managing gastroenteritis is fluid and electrolyte replacement. For mild to moderate cases, Oral Rehydration Salt (ORS) solution is the gold standard because its specific balance of glucose and sodium promotes rapid absorption in the small intestine.

  • Fluid Therapy: Administer ORS frequently in small sips. If severe dehydration or intractable vomiting occurs, intravenous (IV) fluids like Normal Saline or Ringer's Lactate are initiated promptly.
  • Dietary Management: Starving the gut is outdated practice. Feeding should resume with soft, bland foods (like rice, yam, bananas) as soon as the patient can tolerate it to maintain gut integrity.
  • Pharmacological Interventions: Antidiarrheal medications are generally avoided in children because they can trap toxins in the bowel. Antibiotics are only prescribed if a specific bacterial or parasitic cause is confirmed.

Complications and Preventive Strategies

If left untreated, severe gastroenteritis can lead to life-threatening complications. The most immediate danger is severe hypovolemic shock resulting from profound fluid loss. Other complications include acute kidney injury due to prolonged low blood flow to the kidneys, and severe electrolyte imbalances such as hypokalemia, which can cause dangerous cardiac arrhythmias.

Preventing gastroenteritis requires community-wide public health measures:

  • Promoting regular, thorough handwashing with soap and clean water after using the toilet and before handling food.
  • Ensuring access to safe, potable drinking water through boiling or chlorination.
  • Practicing proper food hygiene, including cooking food thoroughly and keeping surfaces clean.
  • Administering available rotavirus vaccines to infants as part of routine immunization schedules.
