Enteric Fevers

Introduction and Definition of Enteric Fevers

Enteric fevers are severe systemic infections characterized by prolonged fever, profound malaise, and gastrointestinal symptoms. The term primarily groups typhoid fever and paratyphoid fever together due to their similar clinical presentations and transmission routes. Typhoid fever is caused by Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi, while paratyphoid fever is caused by serotypes Paratyphi A, B, or C. These illnesses are major global health concerns, especially in regions with inadequate sanitation and limited access to clean drinking water.

Causative Organisms and Transmission Dynamics

The transmission of enteric fevers relies entirely on the fecal-oral route. Humans are the only natural reservoir and host for Salmonella Typhi, meaning the bacteria pass from person to person.

  • Contaminated Water: Drinking water treated improperly or sourced near sewage leaks.
  • Contaminated Food: Eating raw fruits washed in dirty water or food handled by infected individuals.
  • Poor Hygiene: Inadequate handwashing after defecation before food preparation.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

Once ingested, the bacteria survive gastric acid, invade the intestinal mucosa, and enter the lymphatic system and bloodstream, leading to bacteremia. From there, they colonize the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. Clinically, the disease unfolds gradually.

  • Week 1: Stepwise rising fever, severe headache, and relative bradycardia.
  • Week 2: Abdominal pain, high continuous fever, hepatosplenomegaly, and characteristic rose spots on the trunk.
  • Week 3: Complications such as intestinal bleeding or perforation can occur if untreated.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Accurately diagnosing enteric fever requires a combination of clinical suspicion and laboratory tests. Blood cultures are most sensitive during the first week of illness when bacteremia peaks. Stool and urine cultures become more reliable during the later weeks as the bacteria shed from the gallbladder and kidneys. The Widal test, measuring agglutinin antibodies, is widely used in resource-limited settings, though cross-reactivity and past infections can cause false positives.

Medical Management and Nursing Care

Effective management of enteric fever involves prompt antimicrobial therapy, supportive care, and diligent nursing monitoring. Antibiotics such as fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, or azithromycin are prescribed based on local resistance patterns.

  • Hydration: Maintain strict fluid balance through oral rehydration salts or intravenous fluids.
  • Temperature Control: Administer antipyretics and use tepid sponging to manage high fevers.
  • Nutritional Support: Provide a soft, high-calorie, low-fiber diet to minimize bowel irritation.

Potential Complications

Delaying treatment or failing to manage enteric fever properly can lead to life-threatening complications. The deep invasion of the intestinal lymphoid tissue (Peyer's patches) causes necrosis, which may result in intestinal hemorrhage or intestinal perforation, typically occurring in the third week. Other systemic complications include myocarditis, encephalopathy, and secondary infections like pneumonia.

Public Health Prevention and Control Strategies

Preventing enteric fever requires broad public health interventions centered on environmental sanitation and personal hygiene. Typhoid vaccines offer moderate protection and are recommended for travelers to endemic areas or populations at high risk.

  • Sanitation Infrastructure: Ensuring safe sewage disposal and clean municipal water supplies.
  • Food Safety: Enforcing strict hygiene standards for food vendors and handlers.
  • Screening Carriers: Identifying and treating chronic carriers, especially those working in food service.
