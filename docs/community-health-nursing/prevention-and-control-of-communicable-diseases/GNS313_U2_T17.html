Enteric Fevers

Introduction and Definition of Enteric Fevers

Enteric fevers comprise a group of systemic infections characterized by prolonged fever, profound malaise, and gastrointestinal disturbances. The term historically encompasses typhoid fever and paratyphoid fever. These illnesses remain a major public health concern in tropical regions, particularly across parts of Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, where municipal sanitation infrastructure is often compromised. Transmission occurs primarily through the ingestion of food or water contaminated by the faeces or urine of infected individuals or chronic carriers. In Nigerian clinical settings, diagnosing enteric fever requires careful clinical acumen because its presentation frequently overlaps with other prevalent febrile illnesses such as malaria.

Causative Organisms and Chain of Transmission

The disease is caused by specific human-adapted serotypes of Gram-negative bacilli belonging to the genus *Salmonella*.

  • Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi: The primary agent responsible for classical typhoid fever, exhibiting strict host restriction to humans.
  • Salmonella enterica serotype Paratyphi A, B, and C: Agents responsible for paratyphoid fever, which typically presents with a slightly milder clinical course than typhoid.
  • Human Carriers: Individuals who harbor the bacteria in their gallbladder or urinary tract long after clinical recovery, serving as critical reservoirs for ongoing community outbreaks.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Stages

Following ingestion, the bacteria survive gastric acid passage and invade the intestinal epithelium of the small intestine, particularly the Peyer's patches. They are phagocytosed by macrophages and transported via the lymphatic system to the mesenteric lymph nodes, subsequently entering the bloodstream to cause transient bacteremia. From the blood, the organisms seed the reticuloendothelial system of the liver, spleen, and bone marrow.

Clinically, the infection progresses in distinct weekly phases if untreated. The first week features a gradually climbing step-ladder fever accompanied by headache and malaise. During the second week, patients often develop abdominal distension, hepatosplenomegaly, and characteristic rose spots on the trunk. The third week brings severe systemic toxicity, delirium, and heightened vulnerability to life-threatening complications such as intestinal perforation and hemorrhage.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Accurate diagnosis requires correlating clinical findings with specific laboratory assays. Blood culture remains the gold standard, yielding the highest positivity rate during the first week of illness before circulating antibodies develop. Bone marrow culture has an even higher sensitivity and remains positive longer, though it is invasive. Serological tests, notably the Widal test, detect agglutinating antibodies against O and H antigens; however, a single elevated Widal titer can be misleading in endemic areas due to baseline population exposure. Modern rapid diagnostic tests detecting specific antibodies or circulating antigens provide quicker results at the bedside, though interpretation must account for the stage of infection.

Medical Management and Nursing Care

Management centers on effective antimicrobial therapy, fluid resuscitation, and rigorous supportive nursing care. Fluoroquinolones, third-generation cephalosporins, and azithromycin serve as primary therapeutic agents, though local resistance patterns must guide drug selection. Nurses play a vital role in monitoring vital signs, tracking fluid intake and output, and maintaining strict electrolyte balance during periods of high fever and diarrhea. Dietary management typically involves providing small, frequent, easily digestible meals that minimize mechanical irritation to inflamed intestinal walls. Rigorous personal hygiene and standard precautions protect healthcare personnel and prevent nosocomial transmission.

Potential Complications

Untreated or delayed management of enteric fevers can lead to catastrophic systemic complications. Intestinal hemorrhage arises from the erosion of blood vessels within the ulcerated Peyer's patches, while intestinal perforation typically occurs in the distal ileum during the third week, causing sudden severe abdominal pain, peritonitis, and rigid guarding. Other systemic involvements include typhoid encephalopathy, myocarditis, hepatitis, and cholecystitis. Recognizing early warning signs of acute abdomen is a critical competency for clinical nurses monitoring vulnerable patients.

Public Health Prevention and Control Strategies

Interrupting the transmission cycle requires comprehensive public health measures focused on environmental sanitation and personal hygiene. Access to safe, treated drinking water and effective sewage disposal systems form the foundation of primary prevention. Food safety education must emphasize thorough cooking, handwashing before meals, and safe food handling practices by commercial vendors. Immunization using available typhoid vaccines provides valuable individual and community-level protection, particularly for travelers and populations living in hyperendemic environments.
