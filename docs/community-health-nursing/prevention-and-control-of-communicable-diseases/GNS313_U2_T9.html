Standard Precautions

Introduction to Standard Precautions in Healthcare

Standard precautions form the foundation of infection prevention in any healthcare facility. These are a set of basic infection control practices designed to protect healthcare workers and patients from the spread of infections. Whether a patient carries a known diagnosis or not, every individual is treated as a potential source of bloodborne and body fluid pathogens. This includes blood, all body fluids, secretions, and excretions except sweat, regardless of whether they contain visible blood. Implementing these measures consistently protects you from unexpected exposure during routine tasks like wound dressing changes or bathing. In busy Nigerian wards and community clinics where diagnostic test results might be delayed, treating everyone with the same level of caution prevents accidental transmission of dangerous diseases like HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C.

Hand Hygiene Practices and Techniques

Hand hygiene is widely recognized as the single most effective way to stop healthcare-associated infections. Microorganisms are easily transferred from patient to patient via the hands of healthcare workers.

  • Alcohol-based hand rub: Use when hands are not visibly soiled. It is faster and highly effective against most common germs.
  • Soap and water: Use whenever hands are visibly dirty, contaminated with proteinaceous material, or after caring for patients with spore-forming organisms like *Clostridioides difficile*.
  • Friction and time: Vigorous rubbing covering all surfaces of the hands, including between fingers and under fingernails, must last for at least twenty to thirty seconds during washing.

Selection and Proper Use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical barrier between your body and infectious agents. Choosing the right item depends on the expected exposure. Gloves protect your hands from direct contact with blood, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes. Gowns or aprons protect your uniform and skin from splashes during procedures like draining infected wounds. Masks and eye protection shield your facial mucous membranes from respiratory droplets and splashes of body fluids.

A common misconception is that wearing gloves replaces hand hygiene; you must still wash your hands immediately after removing gloves because microscopic tears can occur during use. Always follow the correct sequence for putting on and taking off PPE to avoid contaminating your skin or clothing.

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Management

Unsafe injections put both patients and staff at severe risk of bloodborne viral infections. Needles and syringes are single-use devices and must never be reused for another patient, even if the needle is changed.

  1. Do not recap: Never recap used needles using both hands. If recapping is absolutely unavoidable due to clinical constraints, use a single-handed scoop technique or a mechanical device.
  2. Immediate disposal: Drop used sharps directly into a puncture-resistant safety box immediately after use.
  3. Overfilling hazard: Never force items into a safety box, and seal the box when it reaches the three-quarters full mark to prevent accidental needle-stick injuries among clinical and waste-management staff.

Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette

Respiratory secretions spread airborne and droplet infections rapidly through crowded clinical waiting rooms and wards. Patients, visitors, and staff must practice simple containment measures during coughing and sneezing. Cover your mouth and nose with a tissue when coughing or sneezing, then dispose of the used tissue immediately in a waste bin. If tissues are not available, cough or sneeze into your upper sleeve or inner elbow rather than your bare hands. Clean your hands with soap and water or an alcohol hand rub after contact with respiratory secretions. Providing tissues, trash cans, and alcohol rub stations near hospital entrances helps encourage visitors and patients to comply with these rules.

Environmental Cleaning and Decontamination

Hospital surfaces and patient care items can harbor pathogenic microorganisms for extended periods if left uncleaned. Routine cleaning removes dust, soil, and organic matter, while disinfection destroys specific disease-causing germs. High-touch surfaces—such as bed rails, bedside tables, door handles, and IV poles—require frequent cleaning and disinfecting using hospital-approved chemical solutions like diluted sodium hypochlorite. Cleaning must always proceed from cleanest areas to dirtiest areas to avoid spreading contamination across a room. Maintaining a clean physical environment directly reduces the transmission of resistant hospital strains.

Management of Occupational Exposures and Needle-Stick Injuries

Accidental exposure to blood and body fluids through needle sticks or mucous membrane splashes requires immediate, calm action.

  1. First aid: Wash needle-stick wounds immediately with soap and running water. Do not scrub or squeeze the wound aggressively, as this can force pathogens deeper into the tissues. Flush exposed eyes or mucous membranes with copious amounts of clean water or saline.
  2. Reporting: Report the incident to your supervisor or occupational health officer immediately.
  3. Evaluation: Rapid medical evaluation is essential to determine the source patient's infection status and start post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV or hepatitis B when indicated.
