Standard Precautions

Introduction and Core Principles of Standard Precautions

Standard precautions serve as the foundational tier of infection prevention and control designed to minimize the risk of transmission of bloodborne and other pathogens in healthcare settings. These measures apply to all patients receiving care, regardless of their diagnosed or presumed infection status. The system assumes that every person's body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes are potential sources of infectious agents. Core principles dictate that barriers must be utilized whenever contact with these substances is anticipated during routine nursing procedures. Implementation of these practices protects both healthcare workers and patients from cross-contamination within clinical environments. Key elements encompass proper hand hygiene, strategic utilization of personal protective equipment, maintaining respiratory etiquette, and executing safe environmental management protocols. Adhering consistently to these baseline rules drastically reduces nosocomial infection rates across diverse healthcare tiers.

Hand Hygiene Practices and Techniques

Hand hygiene stands as the single most critical intervention for preventing healthcare-associated infections. Microorganisms are readily transferred via transient flora on the hands of personnel during routine patient contact. Handwashing with plain or antimicrobial soap and water should be performed when hands are visibly soiled, following restroom use, or after caring for patients with spore-forming pathogens like Clostridioides difficile. Alcohol-based hand rubs represent the gold standard for routine decontamination when hands are not visibly dirty, offering superior microbial reduction and requiring less time. Clinical protocols dictate specific moments for cleansing: before touching a patient, before clean or aseptic procedures, after body fluid exposure risk, after touching a patient, and after touching patient surroundings. Adequate friction must cover all surfaces of the hands, including fingertips, thumbs, and web spaces, for at least twenty to thirty seconds.

Selection and Proper Use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical shield between the healthcare worker and potential infectious hazards. Selection depends entirely on the anticipated mode of transmission and the nature of the clinical interaction. - **Gloves**: Wear when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin; change between tasks on the same patient if moving from a contaminated to a clean body site. - **Gowns**: Protect skin and clothing during procedures likely to generate splashes or sprays of blood or bodily secretions. - **Masks and Eye Protection**: Shields, goggles, or surgical masks prevent mucous membrane exposure from respiratory droplets, splashes, or sprays. - **Respirators**: N95 or higher filtration devices are required during aerosol-generating procedures on patients with airborne infectious diseases. Correct donning and doffing sequences prevent self-contamination. Gowns and gloves are removed and discarded inside the patient's room, followed immediately by hand hygiene.

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Management

Aseptic techniques during parenteral medication administration prevent microbial contamination of injection equipment and protect against bloodborne transmission. Needles and syringes are single-use devices; they must never be reused for another patient, even if the needle is changed or the medication vial is shared. Multidose vials should be dedicated to a single patient whenever possible to prevent cross-contamination. Sharps management requires strict adherence to handling protocols. Used needles must never be recapped using two hands, bent, broken, or intentionally manipulated by hand. If recapping is unavoidable due to specific clinical procedural constraints, a safe one-handed scoop technique or mechanical device must be employed. Puncture-resistant sharps containers must be positioned close to the point of use at waist height and never filled beyond the designated fill line to prevent accidental percutaneous injuries.

Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette

Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette target the containment of respiratory pathogens released during coughing, sneezing, or talking. Educational strategies should target patients, visitors, and healthcare personnel alike upon entry into healthcare facilities. Source control measures include covering the mouth and nose with a tissue during coughing or sneezing, followed by immediate disposal of the used tissue into the nearest waste receptacle. When tissues are unavailable, individuals should cough or sneeze into their upper inner sleeve rather than their bare hands. Healthcare facilities must provide readily accessible resources at entrances, including facial tissues, no-touch waste bins, and alcohol-based hand rub dispensers. Surgical masks should be offered to coughing individuals visiting crowded waiting areas to limit droplet dispersion in semi-enclosed spaces.

Environmental Cleaning and Equipment Decontamination

Patient care environments accumulate infectious agents on frequently touched surfaces, serving as reservoirs for nosocomial outbreaks. Routine environmental cleaning involves the removal of organic soil followed by disinfection using appropriate chemical agents, such as sodium hypochlorite solutions, prepared fresh daily. High-touch surfaces—including bed rails, call buttons, IV poles, and door handles—require more frequent disinfection than floors or walls. Reusable medical equipment, ranging from stethoscopes to blood pressure cuffs, must be decontaminated between patients according to manufacturer guidelines. Decontamination processing follows Spaulding's classification system, categorizing items into critical, semi-critical, and non-critical based on the degree of risk associated with their intended clinical use. Non-critical items touching intact skin require low- to intermediate-level disinfection, whereas semi-critical items touching mucous membranes demand high-level disinfection or sterilization.

Management of Occupational Exposures

Accidental exposure to blood and body fluids through percutaneous injuries or mucous membrane contact demands immediate, structured intervention. First-aid measures must be initiated without delay: wash needle-stick injuries and cuts with soap and water, flush splashes to the nose or mouth with copious amounts of water, and flush eyes with clean water or saline. Do not scrub, squeeze, or bleed the puncture wound, as this may increase pathogen penetration. Following initial first aid, the incident must be reported immediately to the designated occupational health officer or supervisor. Post-exposure prophylaxis evaluations depend on the source patient's infection status and the nature of the exposure. Timely risk assessment for bloodborne pathogens like HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C is vital, as prophylactic interventions are most effective when initiated within hours of the exposure event.
