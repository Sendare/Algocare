Poliomyelitis

Introduction and Etiology of Poliomyelitis

Poliomyelitis, commonly called polio, is a highly contagious viral infectious disease that primarily affects young children. The causative agent is the poliovirus, an enterovirus belonging to the Picornaviridae family. It specifically targets the nervous system and can cause total paralysis in a matter of hours.

The virus enters the human body through the mouth and multiplies in the intestine. From there, it invades the bloodstream and can enter the central nervous system. Transmission occurs mainly through the fecal-oral route, typically by consuming water or food contaminated with infected faeces. Less frequently, it spreads through droplets from a sneeze or cough. Poor sanitation and crowded living conditions greatly speed up its spread in vulnerable communities.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

Once inside the body, poliovirus attacks the motor neurons located in the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord and brainstem. As these nerve cells become infected and destroyed, the muscles they control lose their nervous signals and stop working. This damage leads to flaccid paralysis, meaning the affected limbs become floppy, weak, and devoid of normal muscle tone.

The clinical picture varies widely among infected individuals:

  • Asymptomatic infection: About 90 percent of people infected show no visible symptoms at all, yet they can still shed the virus in their stool and spread it.
  • Abortive poliomyelitis: A minor illness causing mild fever, fatigue, headache, nausea, and sore throat that resolves quickly.
  • Non-paralytic aseptic meningitis: Patients develop stiffness in the neck, back, and legs along with severe headache and vomiting.
  • Paralytic poliomyelitis: Less than one percent of infections progress to this severe form, resulting in rapid, asymmetric muscle weakness and potential respiratory failure if the brainstem is involved.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Nurses play a critical role in identifying suspected cases of polio during routine clinical duties or community health outreaches. Assessment begins with a thorough history, checking immunization records and travel history to endemic regions. Nurses must perform detailed neurological assessments, carefully grading muscle strength, testing deep tendon reflexes, and noting any sudden, asymmetrical weakness in the limbs.

Diagnosis relies heavily on laboratory investigations rather than clinical signs alone, since other conditions can mimic flaccid paralysis:

  • Stool specimen collection: The gold standard for isolating the poliovirus. Two stool samples collected 24 to 48 hours apart are required.
  • Throat swabs: Can also yield the virus during the early acute phase of the illness.
  • Cerebrospinal fluid analysis: Shows elevated white blood cell counts and normal or slightly elevated protein levels during aseptic meningitis phases.

Medical Management and Supportive Care

There is currently no known cure for poliomyelitis once the virus has attacked the nervous system and caused paralysis. Medical management is entirely supportive, focusing on preventing complications, relieving discomfort, and maintaining vital functions.

Pain management is a priority for patients experiencing severe muscle spasms and limb aches; warm packs and mild analgesics provide relief. Nurses must ensure proper limb positioning using splints to prevent permanent deformities and contractures. For patients with bulbar polio affecting the muscles of swallowing and respiration, continuous monitoring of airway patency is vital. Mechanical ventilation may become necessary if breathing muscles fail. Nutritional support via nasogastric tubes or intravenous fluids prevents dehydration and wasting while the patient recovers or stabilizes.

Prevention, Immunization, and Cold Chain Maintenance

Prevention remains the only effective weapon against poliomyelitis. Two primary vaccines have successfully reduced global polio cases: the Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV) and the Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV). OPV is inexpensive, easy to administer drops by mouth, and provides excellent intestinal immunity, making it ideal for mass immunization campaigns. IPV is administered by injection and stimulates high levels of serum immunity without any risk of vaccine-associated paralytic polio.

Maintaining the cold chain is an absolute necessity in tropical settings like Nigeria. Vaccines are heat-sensitive biological products. If the temperature rises above recommended ranges during storage or transport, the vaccine loses its potency and fails to protect the child. Nurses must diligently monitor vaccine refrigerator temperatures, use vaccine carriers with ice packs during outreach, and discard any vials exposed to heat compromise.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Surveillance

Eradicating and keeping polio at bay requires robust community engagement and active disease surveillance. Community health nurses serve as the bridge between formal healthcare systems and local populations. They conduct health education sessions to dispel myths about routine immunization, address vaccine hesitancy, and encourage mothers to bring their infants for scheduled drops during immunisation drives.

Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP) surveillance is the cornerstone of global polio eradication efforts. Any child under fifteen years old who develops sudden, unexplained flaccid paralysis must be reported immediately as a public health emergency. Nurses must investigate AFP cases promptly, collect two stool specimens within fourteen days of paralysis onset, and ensure timely laboratory transport. Diligent surveillance ensures that any imported or lingering virus is detected instantly before it spreads further.
