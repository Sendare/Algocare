Poliomyelitis

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Poliomyelitis

Poliomyelitis is a highly contagious viral disease caused by the poliovirus, an enterovirus belonging to the picornavirus family. The infection primarily targets children under five years of age, though unvaccinated adults remain susceptible. Transmission occurs predominantly via the faecal-oral route through contaminated water or food, or less frequently through droplet infection from pharyngeal secretions.

Once inside the human host, the virus replicates in the oropharynx and gastrointestinal tract before invading regional lymph nodes. In a small fraction of cases, the virus enters the bloodstream and crosses the blood-brain barrier. It selectively attacks the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord and the brainstem motor nuclei. The destruction of these motor neurons leads to irreversible flaccid paralysis. Understanding this neurotropic mechanism is vital for recognizing why muscular weakness follows a systemic viral illness.

Clinical Manifestations and Infection Spectrum

The clinical presentation of poliovirus infection varies widely, ranging from silent asymptomatic infections to severe paralytic disease.

  • Asymptomatic Infection: Approximately 70 to 90 percent of all polio infections cause no outward symptoms. Infected individuals still shed the virus in their stool, contributing to community transmission.
  • Abortive Poliomyelitis: Represents about 4 to 8 percent of cases. Patients experience a minor, non-specific febrile illness characterized by fever, malaise, headache, sore throat, and anorexia, recovering completely within a few days.
  • Non-Paralytic Aseptic Meningitis: Occurs in 1 to 2 percent of cases. Manifests as stiffness of the neck and back, along with severe headache, while retaining full muscle strength.
  • Paralytic Poliomyelitis: Affects less than 1 percent of infected individuals. Features acute onset of flaccid muscle weakness that progresses rapidly over a few days, typically asymmetrical and more pronounced in the lower limbs.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Comprehensive nursing assessment for a suspected polio case involves a meticulous history of immunization status, recent travel, and sudden onset of weakness. The nurse performs serial neurological checks, closely monitoring muscle strength, deep tendon reflexes, and cranial nerve integrity, particularly in bulbar involvement where swallowing and breathing may fail.

Laboratory confirmation relies on isolating the virus from stool samples collected within two weeks of paralysis onset. Throat swabs can also yield the virus during the early acute phase. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis typically reveals elevated protein levels with normal or slightly elevated white blood cell counts. In resource-limited settings across Nigeria, surveillance officers coordinate stool sample collection and transport to accredited WHO laboratories to maintain acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) surveillance standards.

Medical Management and Supportive Care

There is no known curative antiviral treatment for poliomyelitis. Medical management is entirely supportive, focusing on preventing complications, relieving symptoms, and maximizing functional recovery. Pain management involves the use of warm compresses and mild analgesics for muscle spasms.

Respiratory support is paramount if intercostal muscles or the diaphragm become paralyzed. Patients require close monitoring in high-dependency units, and mechanical ventilation may be lifesaving during acute respiratory failure. Positioning the patient correctly helps prevent contractures, while proper nutritional support ensures adequate caloric intake despite swallowing difficulties.

Immunization and Cold Chain Maintenance

Prevention of poliomyelitis relies entirely on widespread vaccination using either the Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV) or the Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV). OPV induces mucosal immunity in the gut, making it exceptionally effective at halting community transmission, while IPV provides strong systemic immunity.

Maintaining the cold chain is critical, especially in tropical climates like Nigeria. OPV is extremely sensitive to heat and must be stored between +2°C and +8°C. Vaccine Vial Monitors (VVM) attached to vaccine labels change color as they are exposed to heat, serving as a reliable visual indicator of vaccine potency. If the inner square becomes darker than the outer circle, the vaccine must be discarded immediately.

Community Health Nursing and Surveillance

Community health nurses play a frontline role in disease eradication through routine immunization campaigns, health education, and Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP) surveillance. AFP surveillance is the gold standard for detecting circulating polioviruses. Every child under fifteen years presenting with sudden onset of flaccid paralysis must be reported immediately as a public health emergency.

Nurses collaborate with local traditional leaders, community health workers, and mothers' groups to dispel vaccine hesitancy and dispel myths. Active case searches in hospitals, traditional bone-setter clinics, and rural settlements ensure that no paralyzed child goes uninvestigated, keeping eradication efforts robust.
