Concept of Communicable Diseases

Introduction to Communicable Diseases

A communicable disease is an illness caused by a specific infectious agent or its toxic products that can be transmitted from one person, animal, or inanimate reservoir to a susceptible host, either directly or indirectly. Understanding these conditions forms the bedrock of community health nursing, especially in resource-limited settings where infectious pathologies remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality. Unlike non-communicable diseases such as hypertension or diabetes, communicable diseases possess the unique capability of triggering outbreaks, epidemics, or pandemics if proper public health measures are ignored.

Nursing care in this context requires shifting focus from treating just the individual patient to protecting the entire community. Pathogens responsible for these illnesses include bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. Recognizing how these microscopic organisms enter, multiply, and exit the human body allows nurses to break the cycle of infection before it overwhelms local healthcare facilities.

The Chain of Infection

The spread of any infectious disease follows a predictable sequence known as the chain of infection. If any single link in this chain is broken, the transmission stops completely. Community nurses use this model daily to plan targeted interventions.

  1. Infectious Agent: The pathogenic organism, such as the malaria parasite or Lassa virus.
  2. Reservoir: The natural habitat where the pathogen lives and multiplies, which can be human, animal, or environmental.
  3. Portal of Exit: The path by which the pathogen leaves the reservoir, such as respiratory secretions, blood, or stool.
  4. Mode of Transmission: How the pathogen travels from the source to a new host through direct contact, vectors, or airborne particles.
  5. Portal of Entry: The site through which the pathogen enters the new host, including broken skin, mucous membranes, or the respiratory tract.
  6. Susceptible Host: An individual who lacks immunity or resistance to ward off the invading pathogen.

Modes of Transmission

Pathogens move from their source to a new victim through various routes. Recognizing these pathways helps nurses advise families on home hygiene and quarantine protocols.

  • Direct Transmission: Involves immediate physical contact between an infected person and a susceptible host, such as touching skin lesions, kissing, or sexual intercourse.
  • Indirect Transmission: Occurs when pathogens are transferred via an intermediate object or substance, such as contaminated water, unwashed fomites, medical equipment, or shared utensils.
  • Airborne Transmission: Happens when tiny droplet nuclei remain suspended in the air over long distances and are inhaled, as seen with tuberculosis.
  • Vector-Borne Transmission: Relies on living carriers, usually arthropods like mosquitoes transmitting the malaria parasite or houseflies carrying enteric pathogens.

Stages of Disease Progression

Once a pathogen enters a susceptible host, a series of predictable clinical stages unfold. The incubation period is the silent phase between initial exposure and the appearance of the first symptoms, during which the microbe multiplies quietly. This is followed by the prodromal stage, marked by early, non-specific signs like mild fatigue, malaise, or low-grade fever.

The illness stage (or acute phase) brings out the classic, disease-specific signs and symptoms that allow for a definitive clinical diagnosis. Finally, the convalescent stage involves tissue repair and gradual return to baseline health, though some individuals may still shed pathogens during this recovery window.

Common Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

Nigeria carries a significant burden of communicable diseases influenced by climate, tropical geography, and socioeconomic factors. Malaria remains a perennial public health challenge, driven by warm weather and abundant mosquito breeding sites. Tuberculosis persists due to overcrowding and delayed diagnosis. Waterborne infections like cholera and typhoid frequently emerge during rainy seasons when municipal water supplies become compromised.

Viral hemorrhagic fevers, particularly Lassa fever, pose recurrent threats during the dry season, often linked to contact with rodent vectors. Vaccine-preventable conditions such as measles and cerebrospinal meningitis continue to cause periodic localized outbreaks, emphasizing the ongoing need for routine childhood immunization campaigns across all primary healthcare centers.

Epidemiological Surveillance and Reporting

Epidemiological surveillance is the continuous, systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data essential for planning and implementing public health practices. Nurses serve as frontline observers who detect unusual disease patterns in clinics and communities. When a notifiable disease—such as cholera, yellow fever, or measles—is identified, prompt reporting to local, state, and national health authorities is a legal and ethical duty.

Effective surveillance allows public health agencies to detect outbreaks early, deploy rapid response teams, and allocate medical supplies before infections spiral out of control. Under-reporting remains a major challenge, often caused by poor record-keeping or lack of diagnostic confirmation in remote rural clinics.

Prevention and Control Strategies

Community health nursing employs three levels of prevention to combat communicable diseases:

  • Primary Prevention: Measures to prevent the disease before it occurs, such as routine childhood immunizations, health education on hand hygiene, and insecticide-treated net distribution.
  • Secondary Prevention: Early diagnosis and prompt treatment to halt disease progression and prevent complications, including screening programs and contact tracing.
  • Tertiary Prevention: Rehabilitation and management of chronic complications to reduce disability and improve the quality of life for those recovering from severe infections.

Isolation and quarantine remain essential control measures during acute outbreaks. Isolation separates infected individuals from the healthy population, while quarantine restricts the movement of people who have been exposed to a contagious disease during its incubation period.
