Domiciliary Midwifery Practice

Introduction and Definition of Domiciliary Midwifery

Domiciliary midwifery refers to the provision of professional maternity care by certified midwives within a pregnant woman's private home, covering pregnancy, labor, delivery, and the postnatal period. In many resource-constrained settings across Nigeria, home births remain common due to cultural preferences, distance to health facilities, financial constraints, or fear of hospital protocols. Unlike institutional care, domiciliary practice places the midwife as a guest in the community, requiring high resourcefulness, acute clinical judgment, and absolute reliance on personal assessment skills rather than advanced electronic monitoring devices. The primary goal is safeguarding maternal and neonatal health while respecting the family's environment, ensuring that physiological processes are supported and deviations are recognized promptly before catastrophic deterioration occurs.

Historical Context and Policy Framework in Nigeria

Before the advent of modern orthodox medicine, childbirth in Nigeria was managed almost exclusively at home by traditional birth attendants (TBAs) and elderly female relatives. With the establishment of formal nursing and midwifery education, the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) was empowered to regulate professional practice, setting standards that govern registered midwives practicing in both hospital and community settings. National health policies emphasize the integration and regulation of community-based midwifery to reduce the country's high maternal mortality ratio. Midwives operating outside institutions must adhere strictly to the scope of practice defined by the regulatory body, ensuring accountability, proper record keeping, and formal linkages with the primary healthcare system.

Selection Criteria and Antenatal Risk Assessment for Home Births

Not every pregnant woman is a suitable candidate for a domiciliary birth. Comprehensive antenatal risk assessment is mandatory during pregnancy to screen out high-risk conditions that threaten maternal or fetal safety. Safe home birth candidates generally present with a confirmed low-risk, term, cephalic presentation, single pregnancy, and an uncomplicated medical and obstetric history.

  • Absolute Contraindications: Previous Caesarean section, severe pre-eclampsia, antepartum hemorrhage, multiple gestation, breech presentation, and severe medical disorders like cardiac disease.
  • Social Screening: Assessment of home cleanliness, availability of clean water, reliable transport logistics to a referral facility, and family consent.

Essential Equipment, Supplies, and Home Preparation

Conducting a delivery outside a hospital requires meticulous preparation and the transport of a self-contained emergency kit. The midwife must inspect the environment beforehand, ensuring adequate lighting, clean water supply, and a warm, draft-free room.

  • Monitoring Tools: Fetoscope or Doppler ultrasound, sphygmomanometer, stethoscope, and adult thermometer.
  • Delivery Kit: Sterile cord clamp, sterile surgical blades, cord ties, clean receiver, and clean towels for drying the newborn.
  • Emergency Supplies: Oxytocic drugs (such as oxytocin or misoprostol), intravenous cannulas, normal saline infusion sets, and a functional ambu bag with neonatal masks.

Intrapartum Care and Monitoring During Home Deliveries

Intrapartum care in the home setting demands vigilant observation and adherence to safe delivery protocols. The midwife utilizes the partograph to monitor the progress of labor, maternal vital signs, and fetal well-being. Regular auscultation of the fetal heart rate is performed using a fetoscope after contractions. Vaginal examinations are kept to a sterile minimum to reduce the risk of ascending infection. Pain relief strategies rely heavily on non-pharmacological methods, including continuous psychological support, massage, position changes, and hydrotherapy where available. The midwife must remain alert for any departure from normal labor progression, maintaining a low threshold for transferring the client to a higher-level facility.

Immediate Newborn Care and Resuscitation in Domiciliary Settings

The birth of the baby initiates immediate physiological adaptation from intrauterine to extrauterine life. The midwife receives the newborn in a clean, warm towel, dries the infant thoroughly, and assesses breathing, color, and tone using the Apgar scoring system at one and five minutes. Delayed cord clamping is encouraged for healthy term infants to improve iron stores. Vitamin K prophylaxis and eye care are administered according to national protocols. If the newborn fails to breathe spontaneously, the midwife must initiate prompt resuscitation steps—clearing the airway, providing tactile stimulation, and initiating positive pressure ventilation using a self-inflating bag and mask—while preparing for rapid neonatal transport if recovery is delayed.

Emergency Obstetric Complications and Referral Protocols

Despite careful antenatal screening, obstetric emergencies can occur without warning during home births. Recognizing these red flags early is the single most important skill for a domiciliary midwife. Common emergencies include postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), prolonged or obstructed labor, cord prolapse, retained placenta, and eclamptic seizures.

When complications arise, panic must be avoided in favor of swift, structured execution of a pre-arranged emergency transport plan. Life-stabilizing measures—such as establishing intravenous access, administering oxytocics, or applying manual uterine compression—are initiated concurrently while communicating with the receiving referral hospital to ensure the team is prepared for arrival.

Postnatal Care and Maternal Education at Home

The domiciliary midwife's responsibility extends well past delivery into the postpartum period, which carries significant maternal and neonatal vulnerability. Home visits during the first week are crucial to monitor uterine involution, lochia progression, perineal healing, and vital signs. Lactation support is provided to ensure successful exclusive breastfeeding and proper latching techniques. Education focuses on recognizing danger signs such as foul-smelling lochia, severe headache, fever, or excessive bleeding. The midwife also discusses postpartum family planning options and schedules subsequent immunization visits at the local primary health center.

Legal, Ethical, and Professional Challenges in Nigeria

Practicing domiciliary midwifery in Nigeria presents complex legal and ethical dilemmas. While the scope of practice is guided by the Nursing and Midwifery Council, midwives often encounter pressure from family members, traditional healers, or religious leaders whose beliefs conflict with orthodox care. Midwives bear full professional and legal liability for any negligence or failure to adhere to established standards of care during home deliveries. Navigating informed consent, maintaining confidential records outside a formal facility, and managing situations where clients refuse necessary hospital referrals require high diplomatic skill, cultural competence, and unwavering adherence to professional ethics.
