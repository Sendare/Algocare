Domiciliary Midwifery Practice

Introduction and Definition of Domiciliary Midwifery

Domiciliary midwifery refers to the practice of providing professional midwifery care to women during pregnancy, labor, childbirth, and the postnatal period within the home environment. In many parts of Nigeria, this practice bridges the gap between formal healthcare systems and community preferences, especially where cultural beliefs, transportation barriers, or financial constraints limit hospital access.

  • Autonomy: Midwives practicing at home must rely heavily on their clinical judgment without immediate access to multidisciplinary hospital teams.
  • Continuity of Care: Care is deeply personalized, fostering a strong bond between the midwife, the parturient woman, and her family.
  • Cultural Alignment: Home births respect local traditions regarding placenta disposal and family support structures.

Selection Criteria and Antenatal Risk Assessment for Home Births

Not every pregnant woman is a suitable candidate for a home delivery. Rigorous risk assessment during antenatal care visits helps identify women who are likely to experience normal, low-risk physiological labors.

  1. Low-Risk Status: Presence of a single fetus in cephalic presentation at term, with normal maternal vital signs and blood parameters.
  2. Exclusion Criteria: History of previous cesarean section, severe pre-eclampsia, multiple gestation, malpresentation, or significant antepartum hemorrhage.
  3. Informed Consent: The woman and her family must fully understand the risks and agree to a predetermined transfer plan if complications arise.

Essential Equipment, Supplies, and Home Preparation

Preparation is critical because emergency resources are not immediately at hand in a residential setting. The domiciliary midwife must pack a comprehensive birth kit and inspect the home environment before labor commences.

Intrapartum Care and Monitoring During Home Deliveries

During labor at home, the midwife acts as the primary monitor of progress and well-being. Using a partograph is mandatory to track cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, and maternal vital signs, ensuring timely detection of deviation from normal labor.

Monitoring involves regular auscultation of the fetal heart rate using a fetoscope or Doppler after contractions. Maternal hydration, emotional reassurance, and mobility are encouraged to support normal physiological progression. Frequent abdominal and vaginal examinations are kept to a strict minimum to reduce infection risks in an uncontrolled environment.

Immediate Newborn Care and Resuscitation in Domiciliary Settings

The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life requires prompt assessment and intervention. Immediately following birth, the newborn is dried, assessed for cry and tone, and placed skin-to-skin on the mother's chest.

  • Apgar Scoring: Assessed at 1 and 5 minutes of life.
  • Cord Care: Clean cord management using dry cord care principles.
  • Resuscitation: If the baby fails to breathe, initial steps include clearing the airway, stimulating, and initiating positive pressure ventilation with room air using a self-inflating bag.

Emergency Obstetric Complications and Referral Protocols

Despite careful selection, emergencies can develop rapidly during home births. Recognizing these danger signs early is the primary defense against maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Active management of the third stage of labor reduces risk, but severe bleeding requires uterotonics and rapid transfer.
  • Cord Prolapse: Immediate elevation of the presenting part and emergency transport in a knee-chest position.
  • Retained Placenta: Avoid aggressive cord traction; establish intravenous access and transfer.
  • Transportation Plan: Every home birth must have a functional vehicle, fuel, and a receiving secondary or tertiary health facility pre-arranged.

Postnatal Care and Maternal Education at Home

The midwife continues to visit the mother and baby during the puerperium to monitor recovery and prevent complications. Postnatal home visits typically occur within 24 hours, on day 3, day 7, and at six weeks post-delivery.

Care includes checking uterine involution, lochial discharge, perineal healing, and breast health. Midwives provide education on exclusive breastfeeding, infant immunization schedules, postnatal nutrition, and danger signs for both mother and child. Psychological support is also integrated to screen for postpartum blues or depression.

Legal, Ethical, and Professional Challenges in Nigeria

Domiciliary midwifery operates within a complex regulatory framework in Nigeria. Midwives must be duly registered with the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) and maintain active practicing licenses.

Ethical challenges often arise when family members pressure the midwife to perform outside her scope of practice or delay necessary referrals. Liability issues are significant because home birth mishaps can lead to legal scrutiny. Maintaining meticulous documentation of all assessments, interventions, and communications protects both the client and the professional midwife.
