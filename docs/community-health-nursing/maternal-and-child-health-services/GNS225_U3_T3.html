Domiciliary Midwifery Practice

Introduction and Definition of Domiciliary Midwifery

Domiciliary midwifery practice refers to the provision of skilled midwifery care to women during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period within the home environment. As an integral component of community-based healthcare, it bridges the gap between traditional birth attendants and institutional hospital care, particularly in resource-constrained settings. For Nigerian nursing and midwifery students, understanding domiciliary practice is crucial because a significant percentage of deliveries, especially in rural and semi-urban areas, still occur outside formal health facilities. The primary goal of domiciliary midwifery is to promote normal physiological childbirth while ensuring the safety of both mother and newborn through timely detection of complications. A domiciliary midwife acts as an autonomous practitioner who must possess sound clinical judgment, cultural competence, and the ability to manage emergencies independently until a referral can be executed. Practice in the home environment requires a high level of preparedness, as the midwife lacks immediate access to advanced medical technology, blood banks, and surgical theaters. Therefore, comprehensive antenatal risk scoring, meticulous planning, and clear communication channels are foundational pillars of safe domiciliary midwifery practice. By empowering women and their families through education and compassionate care, domiciliary midwives contribute significantly to reducing maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality across Nigerian communities.

Historical Context and Policy Framework in Nigeria

The historical context of domiciliary midwifery in Nigeria is deeply rooted in traditional community health structures and the evolution of professional nursing and midwifery regulated by the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN). Before the establishment of modern maternity hospitals and primary health care centers, childbearing was predominantly managed at home by family elders and traditional birth attendants. With the advent of Western medical training, formal midwifery education was introduced to standardize care and combat high maternal mortality rates. Policy frameworks such as the National Health Policy and the Midwives Service Scheme (MSS) have periodically sought to integrate skilled birth attendants into primary healthcare delivery, recognizing that home births remain a cultural and economic reality for many Nigerians. However, current regulatory guidelines emphasize that domiciliary practice must strictly adhere to professional standards set by the NMCN. Midwives practicing in the community must be duly registered, possess a current practicing license, and follow evidence-based protocols that dictate when a home birth is permissible. Policy frameworks also outline mandatory record-keeping, notification of births and deaths, and structured referral pathways to secondary and tertiary health facilities. Navigating these policies ensures that midwives operate within legal boundaries while delivering accessible, equitable, and safe maternity care to underserved populations.

Selection Criteria and Antenatal Risk Assessment for Home Births

Selection criteria and antenatal risk assessment are critical filters that determine whether a pregnant woman is a suitable candidate for a planned home birth. Not every woman is eligible for domiciliary delivery; safety is paramount, and high-risk pregnancies must be strictly excluded and referred for institutional management. During antenatal clinic visits, midwives conduct thorough risk scoring using parameters such as maternal age, parity, obstetric history, medical comorbidities, and current pregnancy complications. Ideal candidates for home births are generally multiparous women with uncomplicated singleton pregnancies in cephalic presentation, a normal pelvic architecture, a history of normal spontaneous vaginal deliveries, and no systemic diseases like hypertension, diabetes, or severe anemia. Conversely, absolute contraindications for domiciliary delivery include previous cesarean sections, malpresentations, multiple gestations, pre-eclampsia, antepartum hemorrhage, and prolonged infertility history. The risk assessment is not a one-time event; it is continuous throughout the antenatal period. Midwives must re-evaluate the mother's health status at every visit and during early labor. If any risk factor emerges, the birth plan must be immediately modified to an institutional delivery, ensuring that maternal and fetal safety is never compromised by rigid adherence to a home birth preference.

Essential Equipment, Supplies, and Home Preparation

Preparation of the home environment and gathering essential equipment are prerequisites for a safe domiciliary delivery. The midwife must conduct a home assessment visit prior to the expected date of confinement to evaluate lighting, ventilation, water supply, cleanliness, and ease of access for emergency transport. The family is guided on preparing a clean delivery kit, warm water, fresh linen, and adequate lighting sources in case of electrical power failures, which are common in many Nigerian communities. The midwife's emergency midwifery bag must be meticulously stocked with sterile instruments, suturing materials, oxytocic drugs (such as oxytocin and misoprostol), intravenous infusion sets, IV fluids, a functional fetoscope or Doppler, a neonatal resuscitation bag and mask, personal protective equipment, and appropriate waste disposal containers. Infection prevention protocols must be strictly maintained in the home setting, requiring the midwife to establish a clean working surface, practice meticulous hand hygiene, and properly manage sharps and infectious waste. Preparing the physical space also involves securing the psychological support of family members and identifying a reliable mode of transportation should an emergency transfer become necessary. Through thorough preparation, the midwife transforms a domestic space into a clinically safe and hygienic environment capable of supporting normal childbirth.

Intrapartum Care and Monitoring During Home Deliveries

Intrapartum care and monitoring during home deliveries require exceptional clinical vigilance, as the midwife operates without electronic fetal monitors and automated hospital alarms. Care must adhere strictly to evidence-based guidelines, utilizing the partograph to monitor the progress of labor, maternal vital signs, and fetal well-being. The midwife assesses uterine contractions for frequency, duration, and intensity, while performing regular abdominal and vaginal examinations (when indicated and under strict aseptic conditions) to monitor cervical dilation and descent of the presenting part. Fetal heart rate must be auscultated regularly using a fetoscope or Doppler, particularly immediately after a uterine contraction, to detect early signs of fetal distress. Maternal psychological support is equally vital; the midwife encourages mobilization, adequate hydration, bladder emptying, and pain relief through non-pharmacological comfort measures. Throughout the first, second, and third stages of labor, the midwife remains alert for deviation from normal physiological parameters. Active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL)—involving administration of a uterotonic agent, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage—is routinely performed to prevent postpartum hemorrhage, which remains a leading cause of maternal mortality in Nigeria. Accurate documentation on the partograph and immediate recognition of any prolonged labor or fetal compromise are essential for initiating prompt intervention or transfer.

Immediate Newborn Care and Resuscitation in Domiciliary Settings

Immediate newborn care and resuscitation in domiciliary settings demand rapid assessment and proficient skills because specialized neonatal intensive care units are absent. Upon delivery, the midwife immediately dries the newborn with a clean, warm towel while simultaneously assessing the baby's breathing, tone, and color, which forms the basis of the Apgar scoring system at 1 and 5 minutes. Skin-to-skin contact is initiated promptly to promote bonding and thermoregulation, and delayed cord clamping is practiced unless resuscitation is urgently required. Routine prophylactic measures, such as administering Vitamin K to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn and applying eye ointment (such as tetracycline) to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum, are administered within the appropriate golden hours. If a newborn fails to cry or breathe spontaneously, the midwife must initiate immediate neonatal resuscitation following standard protocols: clearing the airway, positioning the head in a neutral sniffing position, and providing positive pressure ventilation using a self-inflating bag and mask with room air or oxygen if available. The midwife must be certified in neonatal resuscitation and capable of recognizing when initial steps fail, necessitating an emergency referral to a higher-level facility with a special care baby unit. Providing meticulous newborn care at home safeguards the neonate from birth asphyxia, hypothermia, and infection during the critical transition to extrauterine life.

Emergency Obstetric Complications and Referral Protocols

Emergency obstetric complications and referral protocols represent the most critical aspect of domiciliary midwifery practice. Despite careful antenatal screening, unexpected emergencies can arise intrapartum or postpartum, including postpartum hemorrhage, obstructed labor, severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia, cord prolapse, retained placenta, and uterine rupture. When such complications occur, the midwife must remain calm, execute emergency first-aid measures (such as bimanual uterine compression for bleeding or IV access establishment), and activate the pre-arranged emergency transport and referral plan without delay. Communication with the receiving comprehensive emergency obstetric care (CEmOC) facility is vital so the medical team can prepare for the incoming patient. The midwife must accompany or send an accurate, detailed referral note containing the mother's history, labor progress, vital signs, and all interventions administered. In many Nigerian rural settings, delays in reaching a referral facility—often referred to as the second delay of the three delays model—contribute heavily to maternal mortality. Therefore, establishing reliable community transport networks, community mobilization, and maintaining strong inter-professional linkages with secondary and tertiary healthcare institutions are lifesaving responsibilities for every domiciliary midwife.

Postnatal Care and Maternal Education at Home

Postnatal care and maternal education at home are essential for ensuring the continued recovery of the mother and the optimal health of the newborn following a domiciliary delivery. The midwife must conduct scheduled postnatal home visits (typically within 24 hours, on day 3, day 7, and at 6 weeks postpartum) to monitor involution of the uterus, check lochial discharge, assess perineal wound healing, and screen for postpartum depression and puerperal sepsis. Education provided during these visits covers critical topics such as exclusive breastfeeding, neonatal thermal care, umbilical cord hygiene, danger signs in both mother and baby, and personal hygiene. The midwife also provides comprehensive family planning counseling and offers contraceptive options appropriate for the postpartum and lactating period. Empowering the family through health education ensures that they can recognize early warning signs—such as foul-smelling lochia, heavy vaginal bleeding, fever, or poor infant feeding—and seek prompt medical attention. In the Nigerian context, home-based postnatal care bridges cultural practices with evidence-based medicine, allowing midwives to provide culturally sensitive guidance while dispelling harmful traditional taboos regarding postpartum nutrition and infant care.

Legal, Ethical, and Professional Challenges in Nigeria

Legal, ethical, and professional challenges significantly impact domiciliary midwifery practice within the Nigerian healthcare system. Legally, midwives must practice strictly within the scope of practice defined by the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria; exceeding these boundaries by performing unauthorized medical procedures exposes the practitioner to litigation, loss of license, and criminal liability in cases of adverse outcomes. Ethically, midwives face dilemmas regarding patient autonomy, cultural beliefs favoring unassisted home births by traditional practitioners, and the pressure from family members to delay referrals during emergencies. Professional isolation is another major challenge, as community-based midwives often lack peer support, continuous professional development opportunities, and adequate institutional backing. Furthermore, infrastructural deficits such as poor road networks, lack of electricity, and telecommunication failures in rural areas hamper the execution of timely emergency referrals. Addressing these challenges requires strengthening regulatory enforcement, improving rural infrastructure, fostering inter-professional collaboration between traditional and orthodox healthcare providers, and ensuring that community midwives receive continuous training, supportive supervision, and legal protection while executing their duties.
