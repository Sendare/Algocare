Management of Obstetric Emergencies in the Community

Introduction to Obstetric Emergencies in Low-Resource Settings

Obstetric emergencies occur without warning, striking apparently healthy pregnant women during antepartum, intrapartum, or postpartum periods. In community settings across Nigeria, midwives and community health extension workers often operate far from comprehensive emergency obstetric care facilities. The delay in recognizing complications, the delay in deciding to seek care, and the delay in reaching a health facility—famously known as the three delays model—contribute heavily to maternal mortality rates. Understanding how to stabilize and manage acute crises using minimal equipment is a core competency for frontline health workers.

Identification and Triage of High-Risk Conditions

Rapid assessment saves lives. When an emergency strikes, the community midwife must quickly scan for immediate threats using the ABCD approach (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Danger signs). Common community red flags include severe vaginal bleeding, convulsions, high fever, altered mental state, and severe headache combined with blurred vision.

  • Danger Sign 1: Vaginal bleeding during any trimester or postpartum.
  • Danger Sign 2: Convulsions or loss of consciousness.
  • Danger Sign 3: Severe continuous abdominal pain out of proportion to normal labor.
  • Danger Sign 4: High fever and foul-smelling lochia indicating infection.

Immediate Stabilization and First Aid

Once an emergency is identified, immediate life-saving maneuvers take precedence over detailed history taking. Ensure the airway is patent, position the woman on her left lateral side to prevent aortocaval compression and improve placental perfusion, and establish reliable intravenous access using a large-bore cannula (16G or 18G). Administer normal saline or Ringer's lactate rapidly if hypovolemic shock is suspected. Monitoring vital signs every 15 minutes helps evaluate the response to initial fluid resuscitation.

Management of Severe Postpartum Hemorrhage

Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) remains a leading cause of maternal mortality across Nigeria. In the community, the most frequent cause is uterine atony.

  1. Call for Help: Alert available assistants and prepare emergency supplies.
  2. Uterine Massage: Perform firm, continuous bimanual uterine massage to stimulate contractions.
  3. Uterotonics: Administer oxytocin 10 IU intramuscularly or intravenously, or misoprostol 800 mcg sublingually if oxytocin is unavailable.
  4. Bladder Emptying: Insert an indwelling catheter to keep the bladder empty, as a full bladder prevents uterine contraction.
  5. Aortic Compression: Apply external aortic compression manually if bleeding persists while preparing for transfer.

Handling Eclampsia and Severe Preeclampsia

Eclamptic seizures present terrifying moments for family members and health workers alike. The immediate goal is to protect the patient from physical injury, maintain airway patency, and prevent recurrent seizures using magnesium sulfate.

  • Loading Dose: Administer 4g of 20% magnesium sulfate slowly IV over 5 minutes, followed by 5g of 50% IM deep into each buttock.
  • Maintenance Dose: Give 5g of 50% magnesium sulfate IM every 4 hours into alternate buttocks, provided patellar reflexes are present, respiratory rate is at least 16 breaths per minute, and urinary output is adequate.
  • Antidote: Always keep 10% calcium gluconate readily available at the bedside to treat magnesium toxicity.

Addressing Obstructed and Prolonged Labor

Labor that fails to progress normally can lead to uterine rupture, maternal exhaustion, obstetric fistula, and severe fetal asphyxia. In rural clinics, prolonged labor often results from cephalopelvic disproportion or malpresentation. Midwives must plot the partograph diligently. Once a patient crosses the alert or action line into prolonged labor, or shows signs of obstructed labor such as caput succedaneum, severe molding, and a rising contraction ring (Bandl's ring), no further oxytocin should be given. Immediate intravenous hydration and urgent emergency transfer for operative delivery are mandatory.

Emergency Referral and Transportation Protocols

A well-formulated referral plan bridges the gap between community stabilization and comprehensive hospital care. When transferring a compromised obstetric patient, communication is paramount. The receiving facility must be alerted ahead of time regarding the diagnosis, current condition, and treatments already administered. An escort nurse or trained birth attendant should accompany the woman, carrying emergency drugs, intravenous fluids, and documentation. Community transport schemes, such as emergency motorcycle riders or community vehicles, play a lifesaving role in overcoming geographic barriers.

Community Engagement and Emergency Preparedness Plans

Sustainable reduction in maternal mortality requires active participation from community members, traditional leaders, and transport unions. Midwives work with village health committees to establish birth preparedness and complication readiness (BPCR) plans for every pregnant woman. These plans identify a clean delivery site, a designated birth companion, a dedicated blood donor, and funds or transport arrangements for emergencies. Educating families to recognize early danger signs ensures that care is sought before complications become irreversible.
