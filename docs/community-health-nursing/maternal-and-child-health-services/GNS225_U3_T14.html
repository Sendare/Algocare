Role of the Midwife in the Community

Introduction to Community Midwifery in Nigeria

Community midwifery brings maternal and newborn healthcare directly to rural and underserved populations across Nigeria. Unlike hospital-based midwives who wait for patients to arrive, community midwives actively reach into villages, settlements, and primary health care centers. This grassroots approach bridges the severe gap between formal health institutions and remote communities where transportation barriers, financial constraints, and cultural preferences often keep pregnant women away from orthodox care. By embedding themselves within the community, midwives build essential trust, understand local health determinants, and deliver targeted interventions that directly reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality rates.

Core Responsibilities and Scope of Practice

The scope of a community midwife encompasses the entire continuum of maternal and child health. Their daily duties require a unique blend of clinical competence, public health tracking, and community mobilization.

  • Antenatal monitoring: Conducting routine physical checks, urinalysis, and screening for pregnancy-induced hypertension.
  • Intrapartum care: Managing normal labor and conducting safe deliveries at primary health clinics or homes when necessary.
  • Postnatal follow-up: Assessing uterine involution, checking lochia, and promoting exclusive breastfeeding.
  • Immunization delivery: Administering routine childhood vaccines and tracking community immunization schedules.
  • Record keeping: Maintaining accurate registers of births, maternal complications, and infant growth milestones.

Antenatal and Postnatal Care at the Grassroots Level

Grassroots antenatal care focuses on early booking, risk stratification, and preventive health education. Midwives use this period to administer intermittent preventive treatment for malaria during pregnancy, distribute insecticide-treated nets, and provide routine hematinic supplements to prevent anemia.

Postnatal care begins immediately after delivery and extends through the critical first six weeks of life. During home visits or clinic check-ins, the midwife inspects the perineal wound or cesarean site, evaluates emotional well-being to screen for postpartum depression, and observes breastfeeding attachment. Early identification of postpartum hemorrhage or neonatal sepsis during these visits saves lives before complications escalate.

Conducting Safe Deliveries in Resource-Limited Settings

Many primary health centers and home delivery sites in rural Nigeria lack reliable electricity, running water, and advanced surgical equipment. Community midwives must master aseptic techniques using minimal resources. Improvised clean delivery practices—such as using clean plastic sheets, boiled cord ties, and clean razor blades—drastically reduce neonatal tetanus and puerperal sepsis. Midwives must remain calm, rely on partograph plotting to monitor the progress of labor, and recognize the earliest signs of prolonged or obstructed labor long before uterine rupture occurs.

Health Education and Family Planning Advocacy

Education is one of the most powerful tools in a community midwife's arsenal. Midwives translate complex medical guidelines into simple, relatable local dialects during market square talks, church or mosque announcements, and antenatal group sessions. Topics cover nutrition, danger signs of pregnancy, and the critical importance of birth spacing. Family planning advocacy must be handled with cultural sensitivity, engaging both women and male community leaders. By explaining that modern contraception protects maternal health and reduces child mortality rather than restricting family size arbitrarily, midwives gradually dissolve long-held myths and increase uptake.

Identification and Emergency Referral of High-Risk Cases

A competent community midwife knows her clinical limitations and recognizes when a patient requires higher-level emergency obstetric care. Conditions such as severe pre-eclampsia, malpresentation, postpartum hemorrhage, and severe malaria demand immediate stabilization and transport. Midwives establish functional referral linkages, which include reliable transport arrangements, advance communication with secondary or tertiary facilities, and accompanying the patient when possible. A delay in making the decision to refer is a major contributor to maternal deaths; therefore, prompt action is non-negotiable.

Collaboration with Traditional Birth Attendants and Community Leaders

In many Nigerian communities, traditional birth attendants (TBAs) remain the first point of care due to cultural traditions and proximity. Rather than alienating TBAs through strict prohibition, progressive community midwives build bridges of collaboration. Midwives train TBAs to recognize pregnancy danger signs, stop harmful traditional practices like abdominal massage during obstructed labor, and promptly escort women to the health facility. Engaging village chiefs, religious leaders, and elders ensures community-wide backing for modern maternal health programs and transforms traditional birth attendants into active allies.

Challenges and Strategies for Effective Community Midwifery

Community midwifery in Nigeria faces persistent systemic hurdles. Midwives frequently contend with poor infrastructural development, insecure terrain, lack of essential drugs, irregular salary payments, and professional isolation. Many work alone in remote outposts without supportive supervision. To sustain motivation and improve service quality, strategies must include regular supportive supervision visits from healthcare coordinators, provision of solar-powered communication gadgets, security risk allowances, and continuous professional development training. Empowering the midwife ensures she remains resilient in her vital mission.
