Health Consequences of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation and Its Types

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving the partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. The procedure is traditionally classified into four distinct types by the World Health Organization to help healthcare providers document and understand the extent of anatomical damage:

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV: All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Practiced mostly in parts of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, FGM is driven by cultural, religious, and social pressures to control female sexuality and ensure chastity. Nurses must understand these classifications because the degree of internal and external scarring directly dictates the severity of physical and psychological complications a patient will face throughout her lifetime.

Immediate Physical and Surgical Complications

The acute phase following FGM carries severe physical dangers. Because the procedure is typically performed in non-sterile community settings without anesthesia, using traditional razors, pieces of glass, or knives, young girls face immediate trauma.

  1. Severe Pain: The sudden, extreme shock of cutting sensitive nerve endings without pain relief can cause neurogenic shock.
  2. Hemorrhage: Damage to the rich vascular supply of the clitoris and labia often leads to life-threatening bleeding.
  3. Acute Urinary Retention: Swelling, pain, and fear of passing urine over fresh wounds frequently prevent girls from voiding, leading to painful bladder distention.
  4. Injury to Adjacent Organs: Accidental cuts can easily damage the urethra, vagina, perineum, or rectum.

Initial management centers on stopping bleeding, managing acute pain, and relieving urinary obstruction. Nurses working in emergency settings must remain vigilant for signs of hypovolemic shock in young girls presenting with sudden lethargy, pallor, and a rapid pulse.

Long-Term Urological and Gynecological Sequelae

Long after the initial wounds heal, survivors deal with chronic physical conditions. Scar tissue formation, nerve entrapment, and anatomical distortion create persistent health challenges.

  • Chronic Urinary Tract Infections: Narrowed openings and trapped secretions promote bacterial growth, leading to recurrent infections.
  • Dysmenorrhea: Menstrual blood and tissue struggle to escape through narrowed scars, causing severe lower abdominal pain and hematocolpos.
  • Keloid Formation: Thick, raised scar tissue forms over the vulva, causing constant irritation, pain with walking, and dyspareunia (painful sexual intercourse).
  • Vaginal Fistulas: Unrecognized tissue damage during the cutting process can erode walls between the vagina and bladder or rectum.

Treating these chronic issues often requires specialized surgical intervention, such as defibulation to open scar tissue, combined with ongoing urological care. Nurses must provide a non-judgmental environment because many women feel profound shame when discussing these intimate, chronic symptoms.

Obstetric Complications and Risks During Childbirth

Childbirth for a woman with FGM, particularly Type III infibulation, carries immense risks for both mother and baby. The rigid scar tissue cannot stretch normally during the second stage of labor.

  • Prolonged and Obstructed Labor: The fibrous scar acts as a physical barrier, delaying fetal descent and increasing the risk of uterine rupture.
  • Fetal Distress and Asphyxia: Prolonged pressure on the baby's head within a rigid birth canal cuts off oxygen supply, leading to brain injury or stillbirth.
  • Severe Perineal Tears: Tissues tear unpredictably in multiple directions, often involving the anal sphincter.
  • Postpartum Hemorrhage: Uterine exhaustion from obstructed labor combined with extensive lacerations drastically raises the risk of maternal hemorrhage.

Midwives and obstetric nurses must anticipate the need for early episiotomies or defibulation during labor to allow safe delivery. Routine clinical protocols in areas with high FGM prevalence must include specialized training in managing scarred reproductive tracts during delivery.

Psychological and Psychosexual Impacts

The trauma of FGM extends far beyond physical scars, deeply affecting mental health and intimate relationships. Girls often experience the procedure as an act of betrayal by trusted family members and caregivers.

  • Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD): Flashbacks, severe anxiety, and panic attacks triggered by medical exams or reminders of the event.
  • Depression and Anxiety: Chronic feelings of worthlessness, low self-esteem, and grief over lost bodily integrity.
  • Sexual Dysfunction: Loss of clitoral sensitivity, fear of penetration, and dyspareunia lead to a lack of sexual desire and inability to achieve orgasm.

Many women suffer in silence due to cultural taboos surrounding female sexuality and mental health. Nurses play a crucial role by validating these emotional struggles and offering gentle, trauma-informed psychological referrals without judgment.

Infectious Risks Including HIV and Tetanus

Using unsterilized instruments across multiple girls during traditional FGM ceremonies creates an efficient pathway for blood-borne and environmental pathogens.

  • Tetanus: Clostridium tetani spores enter through unhygienic cutting instruments and dressings, leading to muscle spasms and high mortality rates.
  • Hepatitis B and C: Sharing blades among multiple initiates transmits viral infections that attack the liver.
  • Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV): If a contaminated blade has fresh blood from an infected person, the virus can pass directly into subsequent initiates.
  • Local Septicemia: Bacterial wound infections can rapidly spread into the bloodstream, causing systemic shock and organ failure.

Healthcare providers must ensure that young girls presenting with acute or healing wounds from FGM are evaluated for tetanus vaccination status and receive appropriate prophylactic screening for blood-borne viruses.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Nurses often encounter FGM survivors in emergency, pediatric, obstetric, and primary care settings. Identifying FGM requires gentle, respectful assessment skills because patients may feel profound embarrassment.

  • Create a Safe Environment: Ensure complete privacy and use a compassionate, non-blaming tone.
  • Conduct Sensitive Physical Exams: With the patient's explicit consent, inspect the external genitalia to document the type of FGM and any active complications.
  • Screen for Hidden Symptoms: Inquire about chronic pelvic pain, painful urination, difficult menstrual periods, and sexual discomfort.
  • Ask Direct, Gentle Questions: Use clear language without judgment, such as "Have you ever had traditional genital cutting done?"

Documenting findings accurately helps multidisciplinary teams plan appropriate surgical, urological, and psychological care. Never express shock or disgust during an exam, as this destroys trust and prevents the patient from seeking future care.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Prevention Strategies

Eradicating FGM requires community-level engagement rather than simple condemnation. Community health nurses work as educators and advocates to shift cultural norms from within.

  • Participatory Education: Collaborate with local leaders, elders, and traditional birth attendants to discuss the health harms using culturally sensitive language.
  • Empowering Women and Youth: Support economic and educational opportunities for girls, as educated communities are less likely to practice FGM.
  • Advocating for Policy: Support legal bans against FGM while ensuring that healthcare systems do not participate in 'medicalizing' the procedure (performing FGM in clinics to reduce physical harm, which still violates human rights).
  • Support Networks: Create safe spaces for survivors to share experiences and access holistic medical care.

Public health nurses must understand that FGM is upheld by deep social traditions; therefore, sustainable change comes from community dialogue and collective abandonment of the practice.
