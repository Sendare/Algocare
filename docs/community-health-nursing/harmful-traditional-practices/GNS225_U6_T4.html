Health Consequences of Female Genital Mutilation

Classification and Overview of Female Genital Mutilation

Female genital mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. The World Health Organization categorizes these procedures into four distinct types to standardize clinical documentation and research.

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and appositioning the labia minora and/or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV: All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

In many communities across Nigeria, these practices are deeply rooted in cultural norms, myths surrounding chastity, and social acceptance. Recognizing the specific type during a physical examination helps anticipate associated morbidities and guides sensitive, individualized clinical care.

Immediate Physical and Surgical Complications

The acute phase following the procedure carries severe physical risks that demand urgent clinical intervention. Because procedures are frequently performed in unsterile community settings by traditional practitioners using crude instruments like razor blades, knives, or broken glass, the immediate aftermath is fraught with danger.

Severe hemorrhage is a primary emergency, resulting from the transection of rich vascular networks supplying the clitoris and labia. Neurogenic shock can occur due to extreme localized pain during the procedure, especially when performed without anesthesia. Accidental injury to adjacent structures—including the urethra, urinary bladder, and anal sphincter—frequently leads to acute urinary retention and fecal contamination of fresh wounds. Edema and localized hematomas further complicate the early healing process, making prompt wound management and stabilization critical.

Long-Term Urological and Gynecological Sequelae

As acute wounds heal through secondary intention, extensive scar tissue, keloids, and fibrous bridges form across the vulvar region. This chronic scarring distorts normal anatomy and triggers a cascade of persistent urological and gynecological disorders.

Urological complications stem from mechanical obstruction of the urinary stream. Chronic straining to void leads to recurrent urinary tract infections, bladder calculi, and progressive ureteric reflux. Women with Type III infibulation often experience prolonged micturition, where urine pools behind the fused scar tissue before dribbling out.

Gynecological sequelae include chronic vulvar pain, dyspareunia, and hematocolpos. Menstrual blood and vaginal secretions become trapped behind scar tissue or the infibulation bridge, causing cyclical lower abdominal pain and premenstrual distention. Additionally, trapped secretions create an alkaline environment conducive to chronic bacterial vaginosis and localized dermatitis.

Obstetric Complications and Risks During Childbirth

Childbirth presents monumental challenges for women with a history of female genital mutilation, particularly Type III. Dense fibrous scar tissue lacks the elasticity required for normal physiological stretching during the second stage of labor.

Labor is frequently prolonged due to mechanical dystocia. The obstructed birth canal can cause severe fetal distress, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, and stillbirth. To facilitate delivery, healthcare providers often must perform a rigorous anterior or posterolateral episiotomy (defibulation or surgical opening). Failure to adequately manage the scarred tissue risks catastrophic perineal tearing that extends into the anal sphincter and rectovaginal septum.

Postpartum hemorrhage remains a significant threat, driven by uterine atony from prolonged labor or extensive surgical repair. Furthermore, the risk of puerperal sepsis escalates sharply due to the presence of chronic scar tissue and contaminated birth canal environments.

Psychological and Psychosexual Impacts

The trauma associated with female genital mutilation extends far beyond physical scars, profoundly altering psychological well-being and sexual health. The procedure is typically performed during childhood or adolescence without informed consent, representing a profound betrayal of trust by caregivers and community elders.

Survivors frequently report chronic anxiety, depressive disorders, and post-traumatic stress disorder triggered by memories of the event or subsequent physical pain. Flashbacks and panic attacks are well-documented clinical manifestations.

On the psychosexual front, the removal of erogenous zones, combined with painful scarring and psychological trauma, leads to profound sexual dysfunction. Dyspareunia is widespread, resulting in fear of sexual intercourse, loss of libido, and an inability to achieve orgasm. These challenges frequently strain marital relationships and contribute to social isolation or domestic distress.

Infectious Risks Including Blood-Borne Pathogens and Tetanus

The use of unsterilized, shared cutting instruments across multiple girls during traditional ceremonies creates a direct pathway for the transmission of infectious agents. This practice poses severe systemic risks that can be fatal within days or weeks of the procedure.

Bacterial infections are nearly universal in unhygienic environments, ranging from localized wound cellulitis and abscess formation to systemic sepsis. Tetanus is a particularly lethal complication; spores of *Clostridium tetani* enter through the open wound, leading to painful muscle spasms, autonomic instability, and respiratory failure.

Furthermore, the sharing of blades without disinfection facilitates the transmission of blood-borne viral pathogens, including human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and hepatitis B and C viruses. Community health initiatives must address these transmission routes when educating populations about the hidden dangers of traditional cutting practices.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Nurses play a pivotal role in identifying survivors of female genital mutilation, many of whom may not proactively discuss their history due to shame, fear of stigma, or cultural normalization of the practice. Comprehensive clinical assessment requires a trauma-informed, non-judgmental approach.

During routine gynecological, prenatal, or admission histories, nurses should ask sensitive, culturally competent screening questions. A physical examination must be performed with explicit informed consent, ensuring privacy and emotional reassurance. The nurse inspects the external genitalia to document the specific anatomical type, presence of keloids, signs of chronic infection, or incomplete healing.

Clinical documentation must be precise, objective, and supportive. Nurses should assess not only physical sequelae like urinary symptoms and dyspareunia but also screen for psychological distress, depression, and anxiety, ensuring appropriate multidisciplinary referrals.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Prevention Strategies

Eradicating female genital mutilation requires robust community health nursing interventions that engage grassroots populations without alienating cultural leaders. Nurses serve as educators, advocates, and agents of social change.

Interventions must be participatory, working closely with traditional birth attendants, religious leaders, and community elders to debunk myths linking the practice to hygiene, morality, or fertility. Health education programs should focus on communicating the physiological, psychological, and obstetric risks clearly and respectfully.

Furthermore, community health nurses support legislative advocacy against the practice, enforce professional codes prohibiting medicalized female genital mutilation (where healthcare workers perform the procedure), and establish support groups for survivors to access medical care, counseling, and peer support.
