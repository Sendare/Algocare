Health Consequences of Female Genital Mutilation

Introduction to Female Genital Mutilation and Types

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), also known as female genital cutting, comprises all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Internationally recognized and classified by the World Health Organization, FGM is divided into four major types to help healthcare providers understand the anatomical extent of the trauma. Type I involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce. Type II involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. Type III, commonly referred to as infibulation, is the most severe form and involves narrowing of the vaginal orifice with the creation of a covering seal by cutting and apposing the labia minora and/or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris. Type IV includes all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding these classifications is crucial because patients presenting in clinical settings may exhibit varying degrees of anatomical alteration, each carrying distinct physical, psychological, and obstetric implications. Recognizing these types allows nurses to provide culturally competent, trauma-informed care and accurately document findings in accordance with legal and professional standards.

Immediate Physical and Surgical Complications

The immediate aftermath of Female Genital Mutilation frequently brings severe physical and surgical complications that require urgent medical intervention. Because these procedures are often performed in non-sterile community environments by traditional practitioners without anesthesia, the risk for acute trauma is exceptionally high. Immediate complications include severe hemorrhage resulting from the laceration of highly vascular erectile and labial tissues, which can lead to hypovolemic shock if not managed promptly. Intense acute pain is universal, often causing neurogenic shock or profound psychological distress in young girls. Localized tissue swelling and severe inflammation immediately follow the cutting, potentially causing acute urinary retention due to mechanical obstruction or fear of pain during micturition. Additionally, accidental injury to adjacent anatomical structures such as the urethra, vagina, perineum, and rectum frequently occurs during the crude surgical manipulation. For nursing professionals practicing in Nigeria, recognizing these immediate surgical emergencies is vital for prompt stabilization, pain management, bleeding control, and referral to higher levels of surgical care when necessary.

Long-Term Urological and Gynecological Sequelae

Long-term urological and gynecological sequelae manifest months and years after the initial procedure, heavily impacting the quality of life and reproductive health of affected women. Scar tissue formation, fibrosis, and neuroma development are common chronic outcomes that cause persistent local pain and dyspareunia. Urological complications stem from anatomical distortion and urethral obstruction, predisposing women to chronic urinary tract infections, recurrent calculus formation in the residual infibulated tissue, and difficult, prolonged micturition. Gynecological consequences include chronic pelvic inflammatory disease, dysmenorrhea due to obstructed menstrual outflow (hematocolpos), and chronic vaginal discharge. For women with Type III FGM, sexual intercourse and pelvic examinations remain extremely difficult and painful due to scarring and narrowed introitus. Surgical intervention such as defibulation may become necessary to alleviate obstruction and pain. Nigerian nurses must be equipped to assess these chronic complaints sensitively, offering appropriate gynecological referrals, empathetic counseling, and patient education on managing recurrent urinary tract infections.

Obstetric Complications and Risks During Childbirth

Female Genital Mutilation poses profound obstetric risks and complications during pregnancy, labor, and childbirth, threatening the lives of both mother and newborn. Scar tissue from the procedure lacks elasticity, making normal dilation of the birth canal difficult and leading to prolonged and obstructed labor. This increases the likelihood of perineal tears, obstetric fistulas (vesicovaginal or rectovaginal), and severe postpartum hemorrhage. Furthermore, health care providers may need to perform emergency episiotomies or surgical opening (anterior episiotomy or defibulation) during labor to allow delivery, which requires careful postpartum resuturing. The risk of fetal distress, meconium aspiration, and stillbirth is significantly elevated due to prolonged second-stage labor and birth asphyxia. Neonatal resuscitation needs are higher in infants born to mothers who have undergone FGM. Midwives and obstetric nurses in Nigeria play a frontline role in managing these high-risk deliveries, necessitating vigilant intrapartum monitoring, timely surgical interventions, and specialized postpartum care to reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality.

Psychological and Psychosexual Impacts

Beyond the physical morbidity, Female Genital Mutilation inflicts deep psychological and psychosexual trauma that can endure throughout a woman's lifetime. The experience of the procedure—often performed on young girls via physical restraint, terror, and betrayal by trusted family members—frequently results in symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), chronic anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem. Trust issues and feelings of inadequacy or bodily mutilation are prevalent. Psychosexually, the removal of sensitive genital tissue, combined with severe scarring, pain during intercourse (dyspareunia), and lack of lubrication or arousal, can severely impair sexual function and intimacy. Many women experience reduced or absent sexual desire, difficulty achieving orgasm, and marital discord stemming from sexual dysfunction. Nurses must recognize that psychological trauma is just as debilitating as physical harm. Providing holistic nursing care requires creating a safe, non-judgmental environment, offering psychological support, and facilitating referrals to mental health professionals for counseling and trauma therapy.

Infectious Risks Including HIV and Tetanus

Infectious risks are exceptionally high both immediately following Female Genital Mutilation and as chronic conditions. Because FGM is routinely performed using non-sterile cutting tools—such as unsterilized razor blades, knives, or broken glass—shared across multiple girls in community settings, the transmission of blood-borne pathogens is a major public health threat. These include Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV). Additionally, the use of unsterile instruments and the application of traditional herbal mixtures, ashes, or animal dung to stop bleeding create an ideal entry point for Clostridium tetani spores, leading to fatal tetanus infections. Local bacterial infections, cellulitis, abscess formation, and sepsis are also frequent acute complications. Chronic infections can manifest as recurrent pelvic inflammatory disease and persistent urinary tract infections. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding these infection pathways highlights the importance of universal precautions, rigorous assessment for signs of systemic infection, prompt administration of tetanus toxoid and antibiotics, and advocacy against unsafe traditional practices.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Nursing assessment and clinical identification of Female Genital Mutilation require high levels of clinical vigilance, empathy, and cultural sensitivity. Nurses encounter survivors in various settings, including pediatric wards, emergency departments, antenatal clinics, and sexual health services. Assessment begins with building trust and establishing a safe, confidential environment where the patient feels respected and unjudged. During physical examinations, nurses must obtain informed consent and explain every step of the procedure. Clinical identification involves inspecting the external genitalia to document the absence, partial presence, or fusion of anatomical structures in accordance with WHO classifications. Nurses must document findings objectively without stigmatizing the patient. Furthermore, nurses should screen for associated complications such as chronic pain, urinary symptoms, psychological distress, and obstetric history. In Nigeria, nurses must also be aware of legal frameworks and mandatory reporting guidelines regarding minors at risk of undergoing FGM, balancing legal obligations with patient safety and therapeutic rapport.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Prevention Strategies

Community health nursing interventions and prevention strategies are essential in eradicating Female Genital Mutilation and supporting affected populations in Nigeria. Community health nurses serve as educators, advocates, and change agents at the grassroots level. Interventions include conducting culturally sensitive health education campaigns in schools, markets, and religious centers to raise awareness about the severe physical, psychological, and obstetric health consequences of FGM. Nurses must engage community leaders, traditional birth attendants (TBAs), elders, and men, as community buy-in is vital for behavioral change. Many programs successfully retrain traditional birth attendants to become advocates against FGM, leveraging their influence to protect young girls. Furthermore, community health nurses provide supportive care and rehabilitation for survivors, offering counseling, linking them with medical services, and advocating for protective legal policies. By addressing the root socio-cultural drivers of FGM through dialogue and empowerment, nurses play a pivotal role in protecting future generations of girls.
