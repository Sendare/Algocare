Types of Female Genital Mutilation

Introduction and Global Classification of FGM

Female Genital Mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. The World Health Organization categorizes these practices into four distinct types based on the anatomical extent of tissue removal or alteration. This standardized classification helps healthcare providers communicate clinical findings accurately, document cases properly, and plan targeted interventions. Recognizing the specific type aids in anticipating associated obstetric and gynecological complications during clinical assessments in Nigerian healthcare settings.

Type I FGM: Clitoridectomy

Type I involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce, which is the clitoral hood. When it is important to distinguish a subtype, sub-classification specifies whether only the prepuce was removed or the entire clitoris.

  • Type Ia: Removal of the clitoral hood or prepuce only.
  • Type Ib: Removal of the clitoris along with the prepuce.

While anatomically less extensive than other forms, this procedure destroys highly sensitive erectile tissue, leading to chronic pain, scarring, and psychological distress.

Type II FGM: Excision

Type II is characterized by the partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. Similar to Type I, sub-divisions exist to document the exact structures excised:

  • Type IIa: Removal of the labia minora only.
  • Type IIb: Partial or total removal of the clitoris and labia minora.
  • Type IIc: Partial or total removal of the clitoris, labia minora, and labia majora.

This form results in a more significant loss of external soft tissue, increasing the risk of severe hemorrhage, infection, and fibrous scar contractures around the introitus.

Type III FGM: Infibulation

Infibulation is widely recognized as the most severe form of FGM. The procedure involves narrowing the vaginal orifice through the creation of a covering seal. This seal is formed by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or labia majora, sometimes through stitching, with or without removal of the clitoris.

  • Type IIIa: Removal and apposition of the labia minora.
  • Type IIIb: Removal and apposition of the labia majora.

A small opening is left for the passage of urine and menstrual blood. Women with infibulation require careful defibulation prior to childbirth or gynecological examinations to prevent catastrophic tearing and obstructed labor.

Type IV FGM: Unclassified and Harmful Procedures

Type IV encompasses all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes. This category includes pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization of the genital area. Because these practices do not fit neatly into tissue excision or sealing categories, they are often overlooked during routine history taking. Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion when assessing patients with unexplained chronic pelvic pain, recurrent localized infections, or unusual scar patterns in the external genitalia.

Health Consequences Across FGM Types

The physical and psychological sequelae of FGM vary by type but share common pathophysiological roots. Immediate complications include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, shock, urine retention, and acute bacterial infections from unsterilized instruments. Long-term burdens feature chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia, keloid formation, neuromas, and psychological trauma such as post-traumatic stress disorder and depression. Obstetric complications are particularly prominent, including prolonged second-stage labor, obstetric fistula, and high rates of perinatal mortality due to obstructed delivery.

Nursing Role in Prevention, Care, and Advocacy

Community health nurses occupy a frontline position in combating FGM through education, early identification, and sensitive clinical management. Nurses must provide non-judgmental, trauma-informed care to survivors, addressing both physical complications and psychological distress. Advocacy involves engaging traditional rulers, religious leaders, and families to dismantle cultural myths supporting the practice. Educational campaigns should emphasize human rights, bodily autonomy, and the scientific realities of reproductive health risks to foster sustainable behavioral change.
