Types of Female Genital Mutilation

Introduction to Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Internationally recognized as a violation of the human rights, health, and integrity of women and girls, FGM is deeply rooted in cultural, religious, and social traditions, particularly within certain regions of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding FGM is critical because Nigeria has one of the highest absolute numbers of FGM cases globally. FGM is typically performed on young girls ranging from infancy to adolescence, often by traditional practitioners using unsterilized instruments without anesthesia, leading to severe physical and psychological trauma. Nurses must recognize that FGM is not a benign cultural practice, but a severe public health issue with life-threatening complications. Healthcare providers have an ethical obligation to understand the various classifications of FGM to effectively diagnose, treat, and counsel affected women and girls who present in clinical settings. Furthermore, nurses must be equipped with culturally sensitive communication skills to address the underlying misconceptions driving the practice while providing compassionate, non-judgmental care to survivors.

Overview of FGM Classification by the World Health Organization

To standardize research, clinical care, and advocacy, the World Health Organization (WHO) classifies Female Genital Mutilation into four distinct types based on the severity and anatomical extent of the procedure. Classification allows healthcare professionals, including nurses and midwives, to accurately document cases, predict associated health complications, and tailor appropriate clinical management plans. Type I involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce. Type II involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. Type III, often referred to as infibulation, is the most extensive form, involving narrowing of the vaginal orifice through the creation of a covering seal formed by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora. Type IV encompasses all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization. Understanding this structural categorization helps nursing professionals communicate effectively with multidisciplinary healthcare teams and provides a standardized language when assessing victims of FGM in emergency, obstetric, and gynecological settings across Nigeria.

Type I FGM: Clitoridectomy

Type I Female Genital Mutilation, commonly known as clitoridectomy, involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce, which is the clitoral hood. While it is sometimes perceived within practicing communities as a less severe form of FGM, it carries substantial immediate and long-term health risks for the young girl or woman. The clitoris is an extremely sensitive organ with a rich supply of nerve endings and blood vessels; consequently, its excision causes severe acute pain, profuse bleeding, and a high risk of localized infection. In addition to physical trauma, Type I FGM can lead to long-term psychological consequences, chronic pelvic pain, painful sexual intercourse, and reduced sexual sensation or responsiveness later in life. Nurses assessing patients may discover scarring or complete absence of the clitoral structure. When caring for women who have undergone Type I FGM, nurses must provide empathetic counseling, gentle physical examinations, and psychological support, recognizing that even the least extensive forms of FGM constitute a profound violation of bodily integrity and can trigger lasting trauma.

Type II FGM: Excision

Type II Female Genital Mutilation, also known as excision, is characterized by the partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. This procedure results in a greater loss of external genital tissue compared to Type I, increasing the magnitude of physical trauma and potential complications. During the acute phase, patients frequently experience severe hemorrhage due to injury to the dorsal clitoral artery and surrounding vascular networks, alongside intense pain and potential shock. The risk of ascending urogenital infections is significantly elevated because the protective folds of the labia minora and majora are compromised, exposing the urethral meatus and vaginal introitus. Long-term complications for survivors of Type II FGM include extensive scar tissue formation, keloids, painful sexual intercourse (dyspareunia), and psychological anxiety surrounding body image and sexual function. Nurses working in gynecological and obstetric units must be vigilant during physical assessments to identify scar tissue that may impede routine examinations, cervical smear collection, or normal progression during the second stage of labor, requiring specialized nursing interventions and patient reassurance.

Type III FGM: Infibulation

Type III Female Genital Mutilation, known as infibulation or pharaonic circumcision, is the most severe and medically hazardous form of FGM. It involves narrowing the vaginal orifice through the creation of a covering seal. This seal is formed by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or labia majora, sometimes through stitching, with or without removal of the clitoris. A small opening is intentionally left to allow for the passage of urine and menstrual blood. Infibulation causes devastating immediate complications, including severe hemorrhage, acute urinary retention, pelvic infection, and damage to neighboring organs such as the urethra and bladder. Long-term consequences are profound and affect every aspect of a woman's urogenital and reproductive health. Survivors frequently experience chronic urinary tract infections, painful menstruation (dysmenorrhea), hematocolpos due to trapped menstrual blood, and severe dyspareunia. During childbirth, women with Type III FGM almost universally require a surgical procedure known as defibulation—cutting the scar tissue open—to allow for delivery and prevent catastrophic tearing, followed by potential re-infibulation post-delivery, though nurses and midwives must advocate against re-infibulation due to its illegality and human rights violations.

Type IV FGM: Other Harmful Procedures

Type IV Female Genital Mutilation includes all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia performed for non-medical purposes. These practices vary widely across different cultural groups and communities and can include pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization of the clitoris and/or surrounding tissues. Although these procedures may appear less anatomically destructive than infibulation or excision, they still pose significant health risks to the individual. Immediate dangers include localized bleeding, acute pain, transmission of blood-borne infections such as HIV and tetanus due to unsterilized instruments, and severe bacterial infections. Long-term consequences can involve chronic pain, keloid formation, psychological trauma, and complications during future gynecological examinations or childbirth. Nursing students must understand Type IV FGM because patients may present with subtle injuries or scars that are easily overlooked if a thorough and sensitive physical assessment is not performed. Nurses play a crucial role in documenting these findings accurately, offering supportive counseling, and educating families and communities on the hidden dangers of all forms of genital modification.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Consequences Across Types

The health consequences of Female Genital Mutilation are extensive, spanning immediate trauma to lifelong physical, obstetrical, and psychological complications across all types. Immediate physical complications include severe pain, excessive bleeding (hemorrhage) which can lead to hypovolemic shock, swelling of the genital tissues, fever, and severe bacterial infections including tetanus. The use of crude, shared cutting instruments also elevates the risk of transmitting blood-borne viruses such as HIV and Hepatitis B and C. Long-term consequences affect multiple body systems; urological complications include chronic urinary tract infections, urinary incontinence, and painful urination. Reproductive health is severely impacted, manifesting as dyspareunia, infertility, hematocolpos, and complex obstetric labor complications such as prolonged labor, obstetric fistula, perineal tears, and higher rates of perinatal and maternal mortality. Psychologically, survivors often suffer from anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), flashbacks during sexual activity or childbirth, and chronic emotional distress. Nurses must maintain a holistic approach when assessing and managing patients with a history of FGM, addressing not only the visible physical scars but also providing mental health support and advocating for comprehensive interdisciplinary care.

The Role of Community Health Nurses in Prevention and Care

Community health nurses play a pivotal role in the prevention of Female Genital Mutilation and the comprehensive care of women and girls who have undergone the practice. Operating at the grassroots level, particularly in endemic regions of Nigeria, nurses serve as educators, advocates, and primary healthcare providers. Prevention efforts involve conducting culturally sensitive community outreach programs, engaging local leaders, traditional birth attendants, and parents to raise awareness about the physical, psychological, and legal consequences of FGM. Nurses must also empower young girls and women through health education regarding their reproductive rights and bodily autonomy. In clinical practice, nurses must provide trauma-informed, compassionate care, recognizing the signs of FGM during routine assessments, antenatal visits, and pediatric check-ups. Care includes managing acute complications, treating chronic infections, offering psychological counseling, and providing skilled intrapartum and postnatal nursing care for survivors. Furthermore, nurses are mandated to understand and uphold national legislation and anti-FGM laws, working collaboratively with social workers and governmental agencies to protect vulnerable children from undergoing these harmful traditional practices.
