Types of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation, commonly abbreviated as FGM, encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. This practice is rooted in cultural, religious, and social myths that attempt to control female sexuality, ensure virginity before marriage, or promote purity. It is internationally recognized as a severe violation of the human rights of women and girls, causing profound physical, psychological, and reproductive harm. Nurses encounter survivors in various settings, making an understanding of the procedure types and health impacts essential for providing compassionate, non-judgmental care.

WHO Classification of FGM Types

The World Health Organization classifies FGM into four distinct categories based on the severity of the tissue removed and the nature of the trauma. Categorization helps healthcare workers communicate clearly, assess clinical needs, and document cases accurately.

  • Type I: Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II: Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III: Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and apposing the labia minora and/or majora.
  • Type IV: All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as piercing, scraping, or cauterizing.

Type I FGM: Clitoridectomy

Type I, often called clitoridectomy, involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris, which is the sensitive erectile part of the female genitalia, and/or the clitoral prepuce, which is the fold of skin surrounding the clitoris. When only the prepuce or hood is removed, it is referred to specifically as a subtype, though complete removal of the visible glans clitoridis is more common. Because the clitoris contains thousands of sensory nerve endings, this procedure causes immediate sharp pain, bleeding, and long-term psychological and sexual distress for the survivor.

Type II FGM: Excision

Type II, known as excision, goes a step further than Type I. It involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, which are the inner folds of the vulva, with or without the excision of the labia majora, the outer folds. This results in a greater loss of external genital tissue. Survivors of Type II frequently experience prolonged healing times, extensive scar tissue formation, and chronic pain around the vulvar region due to the larger surgical wound left to heal without modern sterile techniques.

Type III FGM: Infibulation

Type III, or infibulation, is the most severe and extensive form of FGM. The procedure involves narrowing the vaginal orifice with the creation of a covering seal. This seal is formed by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or labia majora, often through stitching, with or without removal of the clitoris. A small pinhole is left behind to allow for the passage of menstrual blood and urine. When a woman with Type III FGM becomes pregnant or goes into labor, she often requires a surgical incision called a defibulation to allow for childbirth.

Type IV FGM: Other Harmful Procedures

Type IV includes all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia done for non-medical purposes. Examples include piercing, scraping, cauterization by burning, and incision of the tissue. While these may involve less tissue destruction than infibulation, they still pose significant health risks. Infection, bleeding, and severe psychological trauma remain common across all variations of Type IV interventions.

Health Consequences Across All Types

The physical and psychological fallout of FGM spans both immediate emergencies and lifelong chronic illnesses. Immediate complications include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, shock, urine retention, and acute infections due to unsterilized instruments. Long-term problems plague survivors for decades, featuring chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, painful intercourse, cysts, and difficult childbirths with high rates of infant and maternal morbidity. Psychological trauma, including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder, is also widely documented.

Nursing Role in Prevention and Care

Community health nurses hold a frontline position in both preventing FGM and caring for those who have already undergone the practice. Prevention efforts involve culturally sensitive health education, empowering women, and advocating against the practice within local communities. Clinical care requires trauma-informed approaches, gentle physical examinations, managing chronic infections, and providing psychological support without stigma. Nurses must approach every patient with deep empathy, acknowledging the cultural complexities while firmly advocating for health and human rights.
