Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Introduction and Definition of Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation comprises all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Internationally recognized by the World Health Organization, the practice is categorized into distinct types based on anatomical extent. In Nigeria, despite declining national prevalence rates, deep-rooted traditional beliefs continue to sustain the practice in specific regional belts, particularly among rural communities where traditional birth attendants still perform the procedure using crude instruments like unsterilized blades, broken glass, or sharp knives.

Types and Classifications of FGM

The World Health Organization classifies the procedure into four major types, guiding clinical documentation and nursing assessment: - **Type I (Clitoridectomy)**: Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce. - **Type II (Excision)**: Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. - **Type III (Infibulation)**: Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and apposing the labia minora and/or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris. - **Type IV (Unclassified)**: All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Cultural Drivers and Societal Perceptions in Nigeria

Cultural persistence relies on interconnected rationales. Communities often view the procedure as a rite of passage marking a girl's transition into adulthood, a mechanism to ensure chastity and premarital virginity, and a method to curb female sexual desire to preserve marital fidelity. Social conformity exerts immense pressure; uncircumcised women may face severe stigmatization, ostracization, or diminished marriage prospects. Recognizing these community pressures is vital for nurses designing effective grassroots educational campaigns that challenge social norms without alienating local leaders.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Complications

The physical consequences span across acute trauma and chronic morbidity. Immediate complications include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, neurogenic shock, acute urinary retention, and localized bacterial infections from unsterile cutting tools. Long-term morbidities frequently manifest during reproductive years. Chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, dysmenorrhea, scarring, keloid formation, and painful sexual intercourse are widespread. Obstetric complications are particularly severe; women with infibulation face obstructed labor, perineal tearing, postpartum hemorrhage, and higher rates of perinatal and maternal mortality.

Psychological and Psychosexual Impact on Women and Girls

Beyond physical trauma, survivors often endure profound psychological distress. Post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, chronic depression, and persistent feelings of betrayal or violation are common among affected women and girls. Psychosexual consequences stem directly from nerve damage and scar tissue formation. Dyspareunia, anorgasmia, and reduced sexual satisfaction frequently strain marital relationships. In conservative clinical encounters, patients may hesitate to voice these concerns due to cultural taboos surrounding female sexuality, necessitating a high index of suspicion and gentle inquiry by empathetic nurses.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of Survivors

Clinical assessment requires a trauma-informed approach, establishing trust, and maintaining strict confidentiality. Nurses must conduct sensitive physical examinations during antenatal visits, gynecological check-ups, or pediatric admissions. Key indicators include absent clitoral tissue, labial fusion, scarring, or reported chronic urological symptoms. Asking open-ended questions about menstrual health, pain during intercourse, or birth history allows patients to share their experiences safely. Avoiding judgmental language is critical to ensure the patient does not feel stigmatized or re-traumatized during clinical interactions.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Advocacy

Community health nurses serve at the forefront of eradication efforts through education, stakeholder engagement, and empowerment programs. Interventions involve collaborating with traditional rulers, religious leaders, and youth groups to reframe cultural narratives. Nurses organize sensitization workshops that address health risks openly and debunk myths linking the practice to hygiene or fertility. Providing alternative sources of income for traditional circumcisers who rely on the practice for livelihood has proven effective in several Nigerian rural communities.

Legal Frameworks and Policies Against FGM in Nigeria

Legal prohibition provides a structural foundation for protection and deterrence. At the federal level, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act criminalizes the practice, imposing penalties of imprisonment and fines on perpetrators and anyone who incites or aids the procedure. Furthermore, numerous Nigerian states have domesticated this legislation or enacted state-specific edicts. Despite these laws, enforcement remains challenging due to the clandestine nature of the practice within private homes. Nurses must understand these legal frameworks to support protective services and collaborate with social welfare agencies and law enforcement when safeguarding minors.
