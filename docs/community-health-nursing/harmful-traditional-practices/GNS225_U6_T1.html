Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Introduction and Definition of Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Female Genital Mutilation, commonly referred to as FGM, encompasses all procedures involving the partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. This practice is deeply rooted in cultural and traditional beliefs spanning various communities, particularly across parts of Africa and the Middle East. It has no health benefits whatsoever and permanently harms the bodies of women and girls. Recognizing FGM as a violation of human rights is essential for healthcare providers, especially nurses who stand on the frontline of community and hospital care. The procedure is typically performed on young girls between infancy and adolescence, leaving them with lifelong physical and emotional scars.

Types and Classifications of FGM

The World Health Organization classifies FGM into four distinct types to standardize documentation and research. Understanding these classifications helps nurses accurately document findings during physical examinations.

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV: All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, scraping, and cauterizing.

Cultural Drivers and Societal Perceptions in Nigeria

In many Nigerian communities, FGM persists due to deep-seated cultural norms, myths, and social pressures. Many believe the practice preserves virginity, ensures chastity, promotes marital fidelity, and enhances female hygiene or aesthetic appeal. In some settings, an unexcised woman faces severe stigmatization, social ostracization, and difficulty finding a marriage partner. Traditional birth attendants or local practitioners often perform the procedure because they hold respected positions in these societies. Addressing FGM requires nurses to engage with community elders, religious leaders, and families with cultural sensitivity rather than direct condemnation, fostering gradual behavioral change from within.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Complications

The physical consequences of FGM range from acute surgical emergencies to chronic, lifelong maladies. Immediate complications happen during or shortly after the procedure and include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, shock, urine retention, local infections, and injury to adjacent pelvic structures. Long-term complications develop over months and years, significantly affecting the survivor's quality of life.

  • Chronic Pain: Ongoing pelvic pain and painful scarring at the site.
  • Urological Issues: Recurrent urinary tract infections and difficulty urinating due to strictures.
  • Obstetric Complications: Prolonged labor, severe perineal tearing, obstetric fistula, and higher rates of infant resuscitation or stillbirth.
  • Menstrual Difficulties: Hematocolpos, where menstrual blood is trapped behind a scar or fused tissue.

Psychological and Psychosexual Impact on Women and Girls

Beyond physical trauma, FGM inflicts deep psychological wounds. Girls often experience intense fear, betrayal by trusted caregivers, and post-traumatic stress disorder following the procedure. As they grow into womanhood, survivors frequently struggle with anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem. The psychosexual impact is profound; removal of or damage to sensitive erectile tissue severely impairs sexual arousal and can make sexual intercourse painful or impossible. This often strains marital relationships and causes profound emotional distress for the survivor, who may feel inadequate or broken due to a practice forced upon her as a child.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of Survivors

Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion and clinical sensitivity when assessing female patients, particularly during obstetric and gynecological examinations. Conducting a thorough health history includes asking about urinary symptoms, painful periods, or difficulties during past deliveries without using judgmental language. During physical inspections, nurses look for anatomical changes such as missing clitoral structures, scar tissue, or fused labia. Creating a safe, confidential, and trauma-informed environment is paramount. Many survivors feel deep shame and will not volunteer this information unless the nurse demonstrates genuine empathy, patience, and professional respect.

Community Health Nursing Interventions and Advocacy

Community health nurses play a vital role in eradicating FGM through grassroots education, advocacy, and support groups. Nurses design and implement health promotion campaigns that debunk myths surrounding hygiene, fertility, and religion linked to the practice. Collaborating with local schools, youth groups, and women's associations helps empower young girls to speak out. Furthermore, nurses advocate for alternative rites of passage that celebrate a girl's transition into adulthood without causing physical harm. Empowering reformed traditional practitioners by offering them alternative sources of income also stops them from performing the procedure.

Legal Frameworks and Policies Against FGM in Nigeria

Nigeria established the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act in 2015, which prohibits all forms of female genital mutilation and imposes penalties of imprisonment and fines on offenders. Additionally, many individual states across Nigeria have domesticated this law or enacted their own specific edicts against FGM. Healthcare workers have a legal and ethical obligation to understand these laws, protect vulnerable minors, and report suspected cases or threats of FGM to appropriate child protection authorities. Knowing the legal framework empowers nurses to advocate firmly for patients and hold perpetrators accountable.
