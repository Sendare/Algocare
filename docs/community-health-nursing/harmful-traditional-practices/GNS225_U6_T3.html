Medicalisation of Female Genital Mutilation

Introduction and Definition of Medicalised FGM

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. **Medicalisation of FGM** occurs when these procedures are performed by any health-care provider—whether a doctor, nurse, midwife, or trained traditional birth attendant—either in public or private health facilities, at home, or in clinics. Health workers often adopt the practice under the mistaken belief that involving trained personnel makes the procedure safer by using sterile instruments and local anesthesia. International health bodies strictly condemn this practice, classifying it as a violation of fundamental human rights, bodily integrity, and medical ethics. Rather than eliminating harm, medicalisation legitimises a harmful cultural practice and creates a false sense of security regarding its safety.

Drivers and Rationales Behind Healthcare Provider Involvement

Understanding why qualified health professionals participate in FGM requires examining complex socio-cultural and economic pressures. In many communities where FGM is deeply entrenched, nurses and midwives face intense familial and community expectations to perform the procedure. Some practitioners engage in medicalised FGM out of misplaced empathy, believing that if they refuse, the family will turn to unregulated traditional practitioners who use unsterilized blades and lack anatomical knowledge. Financial incentives also drive the practice. In resource-constrained settings, performing FGM can serve as a lucrative side income for poorly paid health workers. Additionally, persistent myths regarding hygiene, aesthetics, and the control of female sexuality continue to influence clinical personnel who fail to separate cultural conditioning from professional bioethics.

Clinical Presentations and Types in Medical Settings

When FGM is performed in clinical environments, it generally mirrors the traditional classifications set by the World Health Organization, ranging from Type I (clitoridectomy) to Type III (infibulation). However, healthcare providers often perform modified or symbolic versions, such as pricking, scraping, or minor incisions of the clitoris, under the assumption that minimal tissue removal causes less damage. Patients presenting in clinical settings may exhibit immediate post-procedure trauma, or they may present years later during obstetric labor with severe scarring, keloid formation, or obstructed delivery. Nurses must recognize that even minor clinical alterations to the genitalia carry profound long-term physical and psychological consequences.

Complications and Health Implications

The physiological aftermath of FGM spans acute trauma and chronic morbidity. - **Immediate Complications**: Severe hemorrhage, acute pain, shock, urine retention, and localized bacterial infections from surgical trauma. - **Long-term Complications**: Chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, dyspareunia, dysmenorrhea, and formation of dermoid cysts or neuromas. - **Obstetric Complications**: Prolonged labor, perineal tearing, obstetric fistula, postpartum hemorrhage, and elevated rates of neonatal resuscitation or stillbirth.

Ethical, Legal, and Professional Accountability

Nursing and midwifery councils across jurisdictions uphold strict codes of professional conduct that forbid practitioners from performing, assisting, or promoting FGM. Participating in the procedure constitutes gross professional misconduct and breaches the foundational biomedical ethics principle of **non-maleficence**—the obligation to do no harm. Legally, many nations have enacted specific anti-FGM legislation that imposes severe criminal penalties, including imprisonment and revocation of practicing licenses, on any healthcare worker convicted of performing the act. Nurses carry a professional duty not only to abstain from the practice but also to report suspected cases or imminent threats of FGM to relevant child protection and legal authorities.

Community Health Nursing Strategies and Prevention

Community health nurses occupy a front-line position in eradicating medicalised FGM through education, advocacy, and culturally sensitive dialogue. Effective prevention relies on community engagement rather than outright condemnation, which often drives the practice underground. Nurses work alongside local leaders, traditional birth attendants, and women's groups to unpack the cultural myths sustaining the practice while promoting alternative rites of passage that celebrate transition into adulthood without physical harm. Clinical facilities must also serve as safe zones where women experiencing complications receive compassionate, non-judgmental care. By providing trauma-informed psychological support and managing physical morbidity, nurses demonstrate the healing role of healthcare while actively discouraging the perpetuation of the cycle.
