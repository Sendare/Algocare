Medicalisation of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding FGM and the Shift Toward Medicalisation

Female Genital Mutilation, commonly abbreviated as FGM, encompasses all procedures involving the partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Historically performed by traditional practitioners using unsterilized instruments under crude conditions, the practice has seen a concerning shift. Medicalisation of FGM occurs when these procedures are performed by any healthcare provider—such as doctors, nurses, midwives, or trained birth attendants—either within public or private health facilities or at home.

This shift creates a dangerous illusion of safety. Families and communities often turn to healthcare workers believing that clinical settings eliminate the severe health risks associated with traditional cutting. However, performing FGM in a hospital or clinic does not alter the fundamental violation of human rights, nor does it completely prevent physical and psychological complications. For the nursing profession, confronting this trend requires recognizing that clinical involvement directly betrays core ethical duties of healing and non-maleficence.

Drivers and Misconceptions Behind Medicalised FGM

The persistence of FGM, particularly its movement into clinical spaces, is fueled by deep-rooted cultural beliefs, social pressures, and persistent myths. Many communities view the practice as a necessary rite of passage, a marker of cultural identity, or a prerequisite for marriage and social standing. When healthcare providers participate, they inadvertently validate these harmful rationales.

  • Harm Reduction Myth: A primary driver is the belief that medical personnel can minimize harm through sterile tools, antiseptics, and local anesthetics.
  • Social Conformity: Families fear community ostracization, stigma, or the unmarriageability of unexcised daughters.
  • Misguided Professional Compliance: Some clinicians perform the procedure out of misplaced sympathy, believing that if they refuse, the family will resort to a dangerous traditional cutter.

None of these drivers justify the procedure. Professional bodies globally agree that harm reduction arguments are invalid because FGM has no health benefits under any circumstances.

Classification of FGM Procedures in Clinical Contexts

The World Health Organization classifies FGM into four distinct types, all of which may unfortunately be encountered in clinical or community practice settings.

  1. Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  2. Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  3. Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  4. Type IV (Other): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, scraping, and cauterization.

While medicalised procedures might sometimes involve minor nicks or symbolic pricks under the guise of reducing harm, clinicians have also been documented performing infibulation and excision. Every single category causes permanent anatomical and physiological damage.

Physical and Psychological Health Complications

Whether performed with a rusty blade by a traditional practitioner or a sterile scalpel by a trained nurse, FGM inflicts severe, lifelong health consequences. The human body is not designed to endure the intentional destruction of healthy, sensitive genital tissue.

  • Immediate Complications: Severe pain, excessive hemorrhage, shock, acute urine retention, and localized or systemic infections like tetanus.
  • Long-term Complications: Chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, painful sexual intercourse, scarring, and difficult childbirth with high risks of obstetric tearing and infant distress.
  • Psychological Trauma: Anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and deep-seated fears regarding sexual intimacy and bodily autonomy.

Sterile instruments prevent transmission of bloodborne pathogens like HIV or hepatitis, but they do nothing to stop the agonizing pain, scar tissue formation, or profound psychological distress.

Ethical, Legal, and Professional Frameworks for Nurses

Nurses occupy a trusted position in society, which places strict legal and ethical boundaries on their professional conduct. Participating in FGM directly violates the foundational nursing principle of non-maleficence—the obligation to do no harm. International codes of ethics from bodies like the International Council of Nurses explicitly forbid healthcare workers from performing any form of FGM.

Legally, many countries have enacted stringent legislation criminalizing FGM, with specific enhanced penalties for healthcare professionals who use their medical training to perform the practice. A nurse who cuts a patient cannot hide behind cultural defense arguments. Licensing boards will revoke nursing licenses, and criminal courts will prosecute offenders. Understanding these laws protects both vulnerable patients and the integrity of the nursing profession.

Community Health Nursing Strategies for Prevention and Advocacy

Nurses working in community health settings are uniquely positioned to lead the fight against FGM and its medicalisation. Effective prevention requires a culturally sensitive approach that engages local leaders, mothers, youth, and traditional practitioners without shaming or alienating the community.

Community health nurses should focus on health education regarding the true anatomical and physiological harms of the practice. Partnering with local women's groups helps create safe spaces for dialogue where alternative rites of passage—celebrating maturity without physical cutting—can be promoted. Nurses must also look for warning signs in clinical practice, such as young girls brought in for routine check-ups just before school holidays when cutting often occurs, allowing for early intervention and child protection referrals.
