Legal Aspects of Female Genital Mutilation

Introduction to Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) and Human Rights

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. As a profound violation of the fundamental human rights of women and girls, FGM breaches internationally recognized principles of bodily integrity, equality, and freedom from discrimination. For nursing and midwifery students in Nigeria, understanding FGM requires viewing the practice through a human rights lens rather than purely as a cultural tradition. The practice inflicts severe, lifelong immediate and long-term physical complications, including chronic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, obstetric fistula, severe psychological trauma, and even death. Human rights frameworks dictate that every female child has the inherent right to health, security, and physical well-being. When healthcare professionals encounter FGM, they must recognize that cultural relativism cannot justify human rights abuses. Nursing care must focus on compassionate support, clinical management of complications, and aggressive advocacy to eliminate a practice that disproportionately subjugates female health and autonomy. Recognizing FGM as a human rights violation empowers healthcare providers to stand firmly against the practice within local communities and institutional settings alike.

International Conventions and Global Legal Frameworks

Globally, numerous international treaties, conventions, and declarations explicitly protect women and children from harmful traditional practices like Female Genital Mutilation. Key international instruments include the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR), the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), and the Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC). Furthermore, regional frameworks such as the Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa, commonly known as the Maputo Protocol, obligate signatory states to commit to prohibiting and condemning all forms of FGM through legislative measures. These global frameworks establish clear standards that supersede conflicting local customs or traditions. For Nigerian nurses, awareness of these international agreements reinforces the global consensus against FGM and provides a legal backbone for advocacy. International human rights bodies hold member states accountable for failing to enact, implement, or enforce protective laws. Understanding these instruments enables healthcare professionals to align their clinical practices, community outreach, and advocacy efforts with global standards aimed at total eradication of gender-based violence and protection of vulnerable populations.

National Legislation and the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act in Nigeria

At the federal level in Nigeria, the primary legislative instrument criminalizing Female Genital Mutilation is the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) VAPP Act, signed into law in 2015. The VAPP Act was enacted to eliminate violence in private and public life, prohibit all forms of violence, and provide maximum protection and effective remedies for victims. Specifically, Section 6 of the VAPP Act explicitly prohibits FGM or circumcision, stipulating severe penalties including imprisonment and fines for anyone who performs FGM, attempts it, or aids, abets, or incites another person to carry it out. For nursing students and practicing clinicians, the VAPP Act serves as a definitive legal boundary. It removes any ambiguity surrounding the legality of the procedure, emphasizing that neither parental consent nor cultural justification can override federal law. Furthermore, the Act provides avenues for legal recourse, protection orders for vulnerable girls at risk, and support systems for survivors. Healthcare professionals operating in Nigeria must be thoroughly familiar with the VAPP Act to ensure that their clinical environments remain safe, legally compliant, and actively protective of women and children against gender-based violence.

State-Level Anti-FGM Laws and Enforcement Challenges

While the federal VAPP Act sets a strong national standard, Nigeria operates under a federal system where states must domesticate federal laws or enact their own state-level legislation. Consequently, the legal status and enforcement of anti-FGM laws vary widely across the 36 states. While numerous states have successfully domesticated the VAPP Act or passed dedicated anti-FGM laws, others lag behind, leaving gaps in legal protection. Even where laws exist, enforcement challenges remain a significant hurdle. These challenges include deep-seated cultural secrecy, community complicity, lack of awareness among law enforcement agencies, and the clandestine nature of procedures performed by traditional practitioners at home. Furthermore, medicalization of FGM—where healthcare workers perform the procedure under the guise of safety—poses a persistent threat. For community health nurses, understanding these state-level dynamics is crucial. Nurses must navigate local legal landscapes, collaborate with law enforcement and social welfare agencies, and remain vigilant against underground or medicalized practices within their local operational areas.

Legal Responsibilities and Professional Liability for Healthcare Workers

Healthcare workers, including nurses, midwives, and doctors, hold strict legal responsibilities and professional liability regarding Female Genital Mutilation. Participating in FGM in any form—whether performing the cut, nicking the tissue, or supervising the procedure—constitutes a grave criminal offense under Nigerian law and professional codes of conduct. Regulatory bodies such as the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) view participation in FGM as professional misconduct, punishable by the revocation of nursing licenses, heavy fines, and imprisonment. Furthermore, healthcare professionals can face civil lawsuits from victims for battery, negligence, and violation of fundamental rights. The ethical and legal stance is absolute: clinical medicalization of FGM is never permissible, regardless of patient or parental demands, reduction of harm arguments, or cultural traditions. Nurses have a professional duty of care to protect patients from harm, meaning any involvement in FGM directly violates nursing oaths and statutory laws. Healthcare workers must educate colleagues, refuse any pressure to participate, and report illicit practices immediately to protect themselves from severe criminal and professional liability.

Ethical Dilemmas in Clinical Practice and Mandatory Reporting

Clinical practice often presents complex ethical dilemmas for nurses, particularly when balancing patient confidentiality, cultural sensitivity, and mandatory reporting obligations regarding FGM. Nurses may encounter situations where a mother expresses intent to subject her newborn daughter to FGM during a routine postnatal visit, or an adult woman presents with acute complications from a recent procedure. While patient-nurse confidentiality is a cornerstone of ethical nursing practice, this duty is not absolute and must be breached when there is imminent harm or child abuse. Mandatory reporting laws require healthcare professionals to report suspected or confirmed cases of child abuse and FGM to appropriate child protection agencies or law enforcement authorities. Navigating these dilemmas requires balancing empathy with firm legal adherence. Nurses must prioritize the safety and welfare of the minor child over familial confidentiality or cultural appeasement. Clear institutional protocols, ethics committee consultations, and ongoing training help nursing students and practitioners resolve these conflicts, ensuring that legal duties to protect vulnerable minors are fulfilled without compromising ethical patient engagement.

Community Health Nursing Strategies for Legal Advocacy and Prevention

Community health nursing strategies play a pivotal role in legal advocacy, prevention, and the ultimate eradication of Female Genital Mutilation at the grassroots level. Community health nurses are uniquely positioned as trusted educators, health promoters, and advocates within Nigerian villages and urban settlements. Prevention strategies must be multi-pronged, combining culturally sensitive health education with active legal empowerment. Nurses can lead community dialogues that debunk myths surrounding FGM, explain the severe health complications, and educate community members, traditional leaders, and religious figures on the federal VAPP Act and state anti-FGM laws. Furthermore, nurses should collaborate with local women's groups, youth associations, legal aid organizations, and child protection services to establish community surveillance and support networks. Empowering local champions and alternative rite of passage programs helps communities abandon the practice without feeling culturally alienated. Through proactive outreach, health education, and staunch advocacy, community health nurses bridge the gap between national legislation and community behavior change, safeguarding the future of young girls across Nigeria.
