Legal Aspects of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation and Human Rights Violations

Female Genital Mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Classified by the World Health Organization into four distinct types ranging from clitoridectomy to infibulation, the practice carries zero health benefits. Instead, it triggers severe immediate complications like haemorrhage, acute pain, and shock, alongside long-term sequelae including chronic infections, psychological trauma, and severe obstetric fistulae during childbirth.

From a human rights perspective, FGM stands as an overt violation of fundamental liberties. International human rights law guarantees every individual the right to bodily integrity, freedom from torture, and the highest attainable standard of health. Subjecting non-consenting minors to irreversible genital cutting violates their rights to life, security of person, and non-discrimination. Healthcare professionals must recognize that cultural traditions do not supersede internationally recognized human rights instruments.

International Conventions and Global Legal Frameworks

Global efforts to eradicate FGM rely on a network of international treaties and human rights instruments. The Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women and the Convention on the Rights of the Child serve as foundational pillars. These treaties obligate ratifying states to take all appropriate measures, including legislation, to modify or abolish customs and practices that discriminate against women and harm children.

The Maputo Protocol—formally known as the Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa—marks a vital regional milestone. Article 5 of the protocol explicitly calls for the prohibition and condemnation of all forms of FGM through legislative sanctions, public awareness campaigns, and support for victims. Nigeria is a signatory to these international and regional frameworks, binding the country to domesticate and enforce protective laws.

The Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act and National Legislation in Nigeria

Nigeria enacted the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act in 2015 to provide a robust federal legal framework against harmful practices. Section 6 of the VAPP Act explicitly criminalizes Female Genital Mutilation, declaring the practice illegal and punishable by law. The legislation targets not only those who perform the procedure but also anyone who incites, aids, or abets another person to carry out genital mutilation.

The penalties outlined in the VAPP Act include imprisonment terms and financial fines, reflecting the gravity of the offense. While the Federal Capital Territory adopted the Act immediately, its jurisdiction initially extended only to federal territories due to Nigeria's federal system of government. Consequently, nationwide enforcement required individual state legislatures to domesticate the federal law within their respective domains.

State-Level Anti-FGM Laws and Enforcement Challenges

Because health and criminal legislation fall under concurrent legislative lists in Nigeria, federal laws like the VAPP Act require state domestication. Numerous states have successfully passed corresponding anti-FGM laws, yet implementation remains deeply inconsistent across the country. Rural communities often adhere to entrenched cultural norms, viewing traditional practices as superior to statutory laws enacted in urban political centers.

Enforcement faces significant roadblocks:

  • Conspiracy of Silence: Community solidarity shields practitioners and families from law enforcement officers.
  • Medicalization of FGM: Some health workers perform the procedure under the mistaken belief that clinical settings reduce harm.
  • Resource Deficits: Law enforcement agencies often lack the logistical capacity to investigate clandestine practices.
  • Lack of Awareness: Many remote populations remain entirely unaware that state laws forbid genital cutting.

Professional Liability and Legal Responsibilities for Healthcare Workers

Healthcare professionals occupy a position of public trust and legal accountability. Performing, assisting, or facilitating Female Genital Mutilation—a practice sometimes termed the "medicalization" of FGM—is a grave professional misconduct offense. Regulatory bodies such as the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria and the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria strictly prohibit practitioners from engaging in any form of genital cutting, regardless of parental consent or cultural pressure.

Participating in FGM exposes clinicians to severe legal consequences, including:

  • Criminal Prosecution: Immediate arrest and trial under state or federal anti-FGM statutes.
  • License Revocation: Permanent or temporary removal of professional registration, ending clinical practice.
  • Civil Litigation: Lawsuits for damages filed by victims or their guardians for breach of duty and battery.
  • Institutional Dismissal: Loss of employment from public health services or private health facilities.

Ethical Dilemmas in Clinical Practice and Mandatory Reporting

Clinical practice often intersects with complex ethical dilemmas, particularly when nurses encounter families determined to subject a child to FGM. The principle of beneficence commands nurses to act in the best interest of the patient, while non-maleficence demands doing no harm. These core bioethical principles completely override cultural relativism. A nurse cannot justify remaining passive on the grounds of respecting local customs when a child faces imminent, irreversible harm.

Mandatory reporting obligations require healthcare workers to act when a child's safety is compromised. While patient confidentiality is a cornerstone of nursing ethics, this duty is not absolute. When a minor is at risk of undergoing FGM, the legal and ethical duty to protect the child supersedes confidentiality. Nurses must report suspected cases to hospital social work departments, child protection agencies, or law enforcement authorities to prevent the procedure before it occurs.

Community Health Nursing Strategies for Legal Advocacy and Prevention

Eradicating Female Genital Mutilation requires community health nurses to look beyond the hospital walls and engage in active advocacy. Nurses serve as frontline educators who can dismantle myths surrounding hygiene, marriageability, and religious mandates tied to the practice. Collaborating with local traditional leaders, religious clerics, and women's associations helps shift community attitudes from within, fostering sustainable behavioral change.

Effective prevention strategies integrate legal education into routine community outreach. Nurses should clearly explain the legal penalties associated with FGM during antenatal classes, immunization clinics, and youth empowerment programs. By empowering women and equipping communities with accurate health and legal knowledge, nursing professionals play a pivotal role in protecting future generations from this harmful practice.
