Concept of Community Health Nursing

Introduction and Definition of Community Health Nursing

Community health nursing is a specialized field of nursing practice that merges public health science with nursing theory. The primary goal is to preserve, protect, promote, and maintain the health of entire populations rather than treating isolated individuals. Nurses working in this sector focus on groups, families, and communities to identify health needs, plan interventions, and evaluate outcomes within the local environment.

Unlike traditional ward nursing where patients come to the facility, community health nurses go directly to where people live, work, and school. This outreach approach allows nurses to observe environmental factors, cultural practices, and socioeconomic challenges that directly influence health status. Practice spans health promotion, disease prevention, management of chronic illnesses, and rehabilitation.

Core Principles and Philosophies

  • Client is the community: The population as a whole is viewed as the primary client, even when working with individuals or families.
  • Equality in service delivery: Healthcare services must be accessible to all community members without discrimination based on wealth or status.
  • Active community participation: Sustainable health programs require the involvement of local leaders and residents in planning and execution.
  • Use of available resources: Interventions must utilize local materials, manpower, and technology to ensure long-term continuity.
  • Intersectoral collaboration: Health cannot be achieved by the health sector alone; agriculture, education, and sanitation must work together.

Hospital-Based Nursing Versus Community Health Nursing

Nursing practice changes drastically when moving from the structured environment of a ward to the open-air realities of a settlement or village.

Roles and Functions of the Community Health Nurse

The community health nurse wears many hats to address diverse health needs. As a clinician, the nurse provides direct bedside care, immunizations, and wound dressings in clinics or homes. As an educator, the nurse teaches mothers about exclusive breastfeeding, nutrition, and hygiene during home visits or market outreaches.

In the role of an advocate, the nurse speaks up for vulnerable populations, such as malnourished children or marginalized rural women, ensuring they receive necessary social welfare. As a collaborator, the nurse works alongside community leaders, environmental health officers, and traditional birth attendants to bridge gaps in care. Finally, as a manager, the nurse plans clinic schedules, orders vaccine supplies, and supervises community health extension workers.

Levels of Prevention in Community Health

Preventive care is structured into three distinct tiers to interrupt the chain of disease causation.

  1. Primary Prevention: Measures to prevent disease before it even occurs. Examples include routine immunizations, health education on diet, and distribution of insecticide-treated mosquito nets.
  2. Secondary Prevention: Early diagnosis and prompt treatment to halt disease progression and prevent complications. Examples include blood pressure screenings, cervical smear tests, and contact tracing for infectious outbreaks.
  3. Tertiary Prevention: Rehabilitation and management of established chronic conditions to reduce disability and restore optimal functioning. Examples include physiotherapy for stroke survivors and support groups for individuals living with diabetes.

Primary Health Care as the Foundation

Primary Health Care serves as the bedrock of community health nursing. Established globally following the Alma-Ata Declaration of 1978, Primary Health Care brings essential health services as close as possible to where people live and work. It relies on full community participation and intersectoral cooperation.

In Nigeria, Primary Health Care is delivered primarily through primary health care centers at the local government level. The essential components include education concerning prevailing health problems, proper food supply and nutrition, adequate safe water and basic sanitation, maternal and child health care including family planning, immunization against major infectious diseases, prevention and control of locally endemic diseases, and appropriate treatment of common diseases and injuries.

Factors Influencing Community Health in Nigeria

The health status of communities in Nigeria is shaped by a complex web of social, economic, and environmental determinants. Poverty remains a major barrier, limiting access to nutritious food, clean water, and out-of-pocket payment for healthcare services. Environmental sanitation challenges, such as improper waste disposal and poor drainage, contribute to high burdens of malaria and waterborne diseases like cholera.

Cultural beliefs and traditional practices also influence health-seeking behaviors. Many community members patronize traditional birth attendants or herbalists before seeking orthodox care, sometimes leading to delayed presentation of obstetric emergencies. Furthermore, infrastructural deficits, including bad roads and erratic electricity supply, hinder the effective cold chain storage of vaccines and rapid emergency transport in rural areas.
