Concept of Community

Definition of Community

A community is a social group of people living within a defined geographic area, sharing common values, cultures, interests, or goals. In nursing, we look beyond just the physical boundary of a town or village. A community also includes groups of people who share a common bond or need, even if they live far apart, such as people living with diabetes or university students. Understanding this broad definition helps nurses see that health problems do not just belong to an individual patient, but often affect the entire surrounding social network.

Key Characteristics and Components of a Community

Every community relies on specific structural and functional components to maintain stability and health.

  • People: The community members, including their age distribution, sex, education levels, and ethnic background.
  • Place: The geographical location, climate, physical environment, and housing conditions.
  • Social Systems: The institutions that meet daily needs, such as healthcare facilities, schools, religious organizations, and local government.

A common misconception is that a community is healthy simply because it has modern hospitals. True community health depends heavily on how well its social systems work together to support everyday living, sanitation, and safety.

Types and Classifications of Communities

Communities take various forms depending on how members connect with one another. Recognizing these types helps public health nurses plan appropriate outreach programs.

  • Geopolitical Community: Defined by physical and natural boundaries, such as towns, cities, states, or school districts.
  • Phenomenological Community: Based on interpersonal connectedness, shared interests, or beliefs, such as church groups, professional associations, or ethnic enclaves.
  • Sustainable Community: Designed to meet current human needs while preserving the environment and resources for future generations.

In clinical practice, a nurse might work inside a geopolitical village clinic one morning, and run a support group for nursing mothers, which is a phenomenological community, in the afternoon.

Determinants of Community Health

The overall health of a community is shaped by a complex web of social, economic, and environmental factors known as the determinants of health.

Determinant CategoryExamplesImpact on Community
Economic StabilityEmployment rates, poverty, housingDetermines access to nutritious food and clean water
Physical EnvironmentSanitation, roads, pollutionAffects rates of respiratory and waterborne diseases
Education AccessLiteracy rates, school qualityInfluences health literacy and job opportunities
Health ServicesClinic availability, immunization drivesControls disease prevention and acute care response

When a community lacks clean water or stable employment, treating individual patients for infections at the clinic will never solve the root problem. True health promotion requires addressing these broader community-level determinants.

The Community as a Client in Nursing

When practicing community health nursing, the client is not just a single sick person or a family, but the entire population of the community. The goal shifts from curing an individual disease to protecting and promoting the health of the collective group.

In this role, the nurse partners with community leaders, teachers, and local chiefs. Instead of asking 'What is wrong with this patient?', the community health nurse asks 'What is causing illness across this population, and how can we build local capacity to fix it?' This requires strong communication, cultural awareness, and leadership skills.

Community Assessment and Diagnosis

Before any health program can begin, the nurse must conduct a thorough community assessment. This involves gathering data on population demographics, environmental safety, health habits, and available resources through windshield surveys, interviews, and statistical records.

Once the data is collected, the nurse analyzes it to write a community diagnosis. A proper community diagnosis identifies a specific health problem, the vulnerable population affected, and the contributing factors. For example, a diagnosis might state: *Risk of waterborne illness among residents of Ward B related to inadequate waste disposal and lack of borehole maintenance.* This clear statement guides the planning of effective nursing interventions.
