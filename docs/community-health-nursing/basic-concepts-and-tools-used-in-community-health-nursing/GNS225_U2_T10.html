Delivery Kit

Introduction to the Delivery Kit in Community Health

A clean delivery kit is a pre-packaged collection of essential items designed to promote hygienic childbirth practices, particularly in resource-constrained settings where access to modern hospital facilities is limited. In many rural Nigerian communities, women may deliver at home or in primary health care centres with intermittent running water and electricity. The kit serves as a portable barrier against contamination, significantly reducing the incidence of neonatal tetanus and puerperal sepsis. Community health extension workers and traditional birth attendants are frequently trained on its correct distribution and utilization to safeguard maternal and child health outcomes at the grassroots level.

Standard Components and Contents of a Delivery Kit

Standard World Health Organization guidelines specify several core items that must be included in every clean delivery kit to ensure safe intrapartum care. Variations exist based on local procurement, but the foundational contents remain consistent.

  • Plastic sheet: Provides a clean, waterproof surface for the parturient woman to lie on, preventing contamination from the floor or ground.
  • Soap: Used by the birth attendant to thoroughly wash hands prior to conducting any examinations or handling the newborn.
  • Cord tie: Two clean threads or ties used to securely ligate the umbilical cord before it is cut.
  • Blade: A single-use, sterile razor blade for cleanly severing the umbilical cord.
  • Gauze or cotton wool: Used for wiping the baby's eyes and cleaning the perineal area.

Aseptic Techniques and Infection Prevention

Maintaining strict asepsis during childbirth shields both mother and infant from opportunistic pathogens. Every healthcare provider must perform thorough hand hygiene using soap and clean water before opening the kit components. Sterile items must remain untouched on their inner packaging until the exact moment of use. Touching non-sterile surfaces such as unwashed clothing or surrounding furniture immediately after donning gloves contaminates the field and mandates a restart of the procedure. Universal precautions dictate treating all bodily fluids associated with childbirth as potentially infectious.

Preparation, Inspection, and Utilization During Childbirth

Before opening the kit, verify the integrity of the outer packaging and check the expiration date. A punctured or wet package compromises sterility. Once opened in a clean environment, layout the contents systematically in order of expected use.

  1. Environment preparation: Spread the plastic sheet on a flat, clean surface or bed.
  2. Hand hygiene: Wash hands and prepare the immediate perineal area.
  3. Delivery management: Allow spontaneous delivery of the infant, supporting the perineum as needed.
  4. Cord management: Wait for umbilical cord pulsation to cease, apply the two ties, and cut cleanly with the sterile blade.
  5. Placental delivery: Manage the third stage of labor gently without undue traction on the cord.

Immediate Postpartum Care and Newborn Management

Following the expulsion of the placenta, attention shifts immediately to stabilizing both mother and baby. Dry the newborn thoroughly with a clean cloth to prevent hypothermia, discarding any wet material. Assess the infant's breathing and color, assigning an Apgar score at one and five minutes post-delivery. Encourage early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life to promote uterine contraction and neonatal bonding. For the mother, monitor uterine tone regularly to detect early signs of postpartum hemorrhage, and inspect the perineum for lacerations requiring repair or referral.

Maintenance, Replenishment, and Disposal Guidelines

Safe management does not end with delivery; proper waste disposal protects the community from accidental sharps injuries and biological contamination. Single-use razor blades must be placed immediately into a puncture-resistant safety box or container. Soiled cotton, gauze, and plastic sheets contaminated with blood are disposed of according to local biomedical waste protocols, usually by incineration or deep burial in rural outposts. Restock kits immediately after use so they remain ready for the next emergency call, ensuring supplies never run out unexpectedly.
