History of Community Health Nursing

Origins of Care and Early Public Health

Long before modern hospitals existed, healthcare was primarily a domestic and communal responsibility. Families and community elders managed illnesses using traditional herbs, spiritual rites, and empirical knowledge passed down through generations. Ancient civilizations, including the Egyptians and Greeks, practiced early sanitation through basic drainage systems and personal hygiene rules, often tied to religious beliefs. Organized care began to take shape through religious bodies, where monks and nuns established the earliest almshouses and infirmaries to shelter the sick, poor, and dying. This era laid the foundational philosophy that caring for the vulnerable is a shared societal duty rather than merely an individual burden.

The Era of District Nursing and Pioneers

The Industrial Revolution brought rapid urbanization, factory overcrowding, and severe infectious disease outbreaks like cholera and tuberculosis. Hospitals alone could not cope with the rising tide of sick people living in squalor. This crisis prompted the birth of district nursing, where trained nurses visited patients directly in their homes.

  • William Rathbone: A wealthy English philanthropist who hired nurse Mary Robinson in Liverpool to care for sick poor people at home, eventually establishing the first district nursing association.
  • Florence Nightingale: Transformed nursing into a respected profession and emphasized environmental health, sanitation, and statistical record-keeping in community settings.
  • Lillian Wald: Founded the Henry Street Settlement in New York, cementing the role of the public health nurse in addressing social determinants of health like poverty and education.

Evolution of Community Health Nursing in Nigeria

The history of community health nursing in Nigeria is deeply intertwined with colonial administration and the control of tropical diseases like malaria, yellow fever, and sleeping sickness. Early Western healthcare focused primarily on protecting colonial officers and troops, but gradually expanded to local populations through dispensaries and missionary clinics.

The training of local personnel evolved from basic practical instruction to structured programs for midwives and community health workers. Programs such as the Basic Health Services Scheme in the 1970s attempted to decentralize care and bring services closer to rural dwellers. Community health nurses and extension workers became the backbone of immunization drives, antenatal care, and health education in remote villages.

Global Milestones and Primary Health Care

A major turning point in global health occurred in 1978 with the Alma-Ata Declaration, which formally established Primary Health Care (PHC) as the key strategy to achieve health for all. PHC shifted the focus away from expensive, hospital-centred curative medicine toward community-based preventive services.

Essential elements of PHC include:

  • Education concerning prevailing health problems and methods of preventing them.
  • Promotion of proper nutrition and safe water supply.
  • Maternal and child health care, including family planning.
  • Immunization against major infectious diseases.
  • Provision of essential drugs and treatment of common illnesses.

Community health nurses emerged as frontline coordinators, translating these global goals into local, grassroots actions.

Modern Trends and Future Outlook in Nigeria

Today, community health nursing in Nigeria faces a complex mix of persistent infectious diseases, emerging non-communicable illnesses like hypertension and diabetes, and infrastructural challenges. Nurses operate within primary health care centres, mobile clinics, and outreach programs. Modern practice integrates digital health tools, community stakeholder engagement, and interprofessional collaboration.

A persistent challenge is brain drain, where skilled nurses migrate abroad, leaving local facilities understaffed. Despite these hurdles, community health nurses continue to champion task-shifting policies, maternal mortality reduction, and health security at the grassroots level, ensuring that healthcare remains a fundamental right rather than a privilege.
