Model for Improvement

Introduction to Quality Improvement in Nursing

Quality improvement in healthcare is not about working harder or blaming individuals when errors happen. Instead, it focuses on systematically examining and redesigning daily care processes so that safe, effective care becomes the easiest thing to do. The Model for Improvement is a simple yet powerful framework developed by Associates in Process Improvement and widely used in healthcare settings worldwide. It helps clinical teams cut through the guesswork and make changes that actually lead to better patient outcomes. Rather than launching massive, disruptive changes all at once, this model encourages small, manageable tests of change right on the hospital floor or community clinic.

The Three Fundamental Questions

Before any change is tested or implemented on a ward, the Model for Improvement requires teams to answer three guiding questions. These questions keep improvement work focused and prevent teams from adopting random new ideas just because they sound trendy.

  1. What are we trying to accomplish? This defines the overall aim and direction of the improvement project.
  2. How will we know that a change is an improvement? This establishes specific measures to track progress over time.
  3. What change can we make that will result in an improvement? This identifies the specific ideas, interventions, or process redesigns to test.

Setting Clear and Measurable Aims

An aim statement is the compass of a quality improvement project. Vague goals like "we want to improve handwashing" or "patients will be happier" rarely succeed because nobody knows when the goal has been reached. A strong aim statement must be specific, measurable, time-bound, and relevant to the patient population.

A good nursing aim answers three basic questions: How much improvement? By when? For whom? For example, stating that "we will reduce catheter-associated urinary tract infections on Ward 4B by 50% within six months" gives the entire nursing team a concrete target to rally behind and measure against.

Establishing Nursing Measures

To know if your change is working, you must collect data. The Model for Improvement categorizes measures into three distinct types to ensure teams get a balanced picture of their clinical environment:

  • Outcome Measures: These assess the final impact on the patient, such as infection rates, fall occurrences, or patient satisfaction scores.
  • Process Measures: These track whether steps in the care delivery are happening as planned, such as the percentage of patients screened for pressure sore risk upon admission.
  • Balancing Measures: These look for unintended consequences in other parts of the system, such as whether a new fall prevention checklist increases nursing documentation time so much that medication administration is delayed.

Developing Change Ideas

Once the aim and measures are set, nurses must brainstorm ideas for what to change. Good change ideas do not always come from textbooks; often, they come directly from listening to patient frustrations and observing frontline workflows.

Teams can generate ideas by reviewing clinical evidence, studying current workflow bottlenecks, or using tools like process mapping. A common pitfall in clinical settings is jumping immediately to purchasing expensive equipment or rewriting complex policies when simpler solutions—such as reorganizing supply carts or redesigning shift handover boards—often yield faster, more sustainable improvements.

The Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) Cycle

The PDSA cycle is the testing vehicle of the Model for Improvement. It provides a structured, scientific way to try out a change idea on a small scale before committing the whole organization to it.

  • Plan: State the objective, make predictions about what will happen, and plan the test (who, what, when, where).
  • Do: Carry out the test, observe what happens, and document any unexpected problems or data.
  • Study: Analyze the data, compare the results against your initial predictions, and summarize what was learned.
  • Act: Decide whether to adopt the change, adapt it for another test, or abandon the idea completely.

Testing and Scaling Improvements

Improvement is a journey of scaling up. You start small—perhaps testing a new pain assessment tool with just one patient and one nurse on a single morning shift. If the test goes well, you expand it: try it with three nurses, then across the whole shift, then across the entire ward.

This principle of sequential testing reduces resistance to change and helps work out operational kinks before rollout. Once a change has been tested through multiple successful PDSA cycles and proven reliable, it is finally standardized into official policy, staff orientation, and daily routine.
