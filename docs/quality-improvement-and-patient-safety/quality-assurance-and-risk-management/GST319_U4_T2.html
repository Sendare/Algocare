Quality Assurance, Standard Setting and Nursing Process

Introduction to Quality Assurance in Nursing

Quality assurance in nursing refers to the systematic, ongoing efforts made by healthcare professionals to monitor, evaluate, and improve the care delivered to patients. It is not merely about inspecting errors after they occur; rather, it is a proactive approach designed to prevent mistakes and ensure that patient outcomes meet predetermined professional benchmarks. In clinical practice, quality assurance involves reviewing documentation, auditing infection control protocols, and assessing patient satisfaction.

Two closely related concepts often confused in this field are quality assurance and quality control. Quality control focuses on detecting defects or faults in a service or product after it has been delivered. Quality assurance, on the other hand, spans the entire process, focusing on the design and delivery of care to prevent those faults from happening in the first place. For student nurses, embracing quality assurance means recognizing that every clinical action contributes to the overall safety and reputation of the healthcare facility.

Understanding Healthcare Standards and Standard Setting

Healthcare standards are authoritative statements that describe the level of care expected in clinical practice, serving as a yardstick for measuring safe and competent performance. Without these established benchmarks, patient care would be chaotic, varying wildly from one practitioner or ward to another. Standards provide a clear, shared vision of what good nursing looks like.

The process of setting these standards involves looking at current scientific evidence, legal requirements, and patient needs. Standards generally fall into three main categories:

  • Structure standards: Focus on the physical environment, equipment, and staff qualifications of the healthcare facility.
  • Process standards: Describe the actual steps and activities performed by nurses during patient care, such as medication administration.
  • Outcome standards: Measure the final result of care, such as whether a surgical wound healed without infection or if a patient's pain was relieved.

Overview and Phases of the Nursing Process

The nursing process is a systematic, problem-solving framework that guides all clinical decision-making and patient care. It transforms vague nursing intentions into structured, individualized actions. Far from being just a textbook requirement, it is the practical heartbeat of professional nursing.

  1. Assessment: Gathering subjective and objective data about the patient through interviews, physical exams, and chart reviews.
  2. Diagnosis: Analyzing the collected data to identify actual or potential health problems that the nurse is licensed to treat.
  3. Planning: Setting measurable patient goals and selecting specific nursing interventions to achieve them.
  4. Implementation: Executing the planned nursing interventions directly at the bedside.
  5. Evaluation: Determining whether the patient goals were met and modifying the care plan if necessary.

Integrating Standardized Care into the Nursing Process

Integrating standards into the nursing process ensures that patient care is not only individualized but also safe and evidence-based. When nurses move through the five phases, they do not rely solely on personal habit; they use established clinical guidelines to inform their choices.

During the assessment phase, standards dictate what vital signs and screenings must be performed for every admission. In the planning phase, critical pathways and care plans incorporate standard interventions that have proven effective across populations. By linking professional standards directly to the nursing process, healthcare institutions reduce clinical errors and protect both patients and practitioners from substandard care.

Tools and Frameworks for Measuring Nursing Quality

To know whether quality nursing care is actually being delivered, hospitals use specific measurement tools and frameworks. These instruments help nurse managers spot trends, track patient safety indicators, and identify areas needing staff training or policy changes.

Common measurement tools include nursing audits, incident reports, patient satisfaction surveys, and clinical indicator tracking (such as rates of hospital-acquired pressure ulcers or falls). A popular conceptual model used in this context is the Donabedian model, which evaluates quality through three lenses: structure, process, and outcome. By examining these three areas together, a hospital gets a complete picture of its clinical performance.

Role of the Nursing and Midwifery Council in Standard Setting

In Nigeria, the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) is the statutory body charged with regulating the nursing profession and maintaining high standards of training and practice. The Council sets the curriculum for nursing schools, conducts professional examinations, and licenses qualified practitioners.

Beyond basic education, the NMCN establishes the scope of practice and code of ethics for Nigerian nurses. By enforcing these rules, the Council ensures that practitioners maintain competence throughout their careers, often through mandatory continuing professional development programs. Nurses found acting below the established professional standards face disciplinary action, protecting the public from unsafe practice.

Challenges and Strategies in Implementing Quality Assurance in Nigeria

Implementing effective quality assurance programs in Nigerian healthcare facilities comes with notable hurdles. Common challenges include severe shortages of nursing personnel, inadequate funding, poor infrastructural facilities, and a lack of consistent supply of basic medical equipment. These constraints often force nurses to overextend themselves, making it difficult to maintain rigorous documentation and audit routines.

Overcoming these barriers requires practical, localized strategies. Hospital management must foster a supportive, non-punitive reporting culture where staff feel safe reporting near-misses without fear of victimization. Continuous in-service training, better resource allocation, and supportive supervision by senior nursing leaders can greatly improve adherence to standards, even in resource-limited settings.
