Responding to Adverse Events, Errors and Harm

Defining Adverse Events, Medical Errors, and Patient Harm

In clinical practice, precision in terminology dictates how safety incidents are managed and investigated. An **adverse event** is an unintended injury or complication resulting from medical management rather than the underlying disease process of the patient. This includes events that prolong hospital stays or cause measurable disability. A **medical error**, by contrast, represents the failure of a planned action to be completed as intended, or the use of a wrong plan to achieve an aim. Errors can occur through acts of commission or omission. Crucially, not every medical error results in an adverse event, and not every adverse event stems from a medical error. - **Patient harm**: Physical, psychological, or social impairment resulting from or associated with healthcare management. - **Near miss**: An unplanned event that did not reach the patient and thus caused no harm, but possessed the potential to do so. - **Preventable adverse event**: An injury caused by medical management that could have been avoided given the current state of clinical care standards. Understanding these distinctions prevents misdirected blame during investigations. In Nigerian tertiary hospitals, distinguishing between systemic resource limitations and individual clinical lapses is vital for an accurate appraisal of incident data.

The Role of a Just Culture in Reporting and Responding

A **Just Culture** creates an environment where healthcare professionals feel safe reporting errors and near misses without fear of automatic retribution. Traditional punitive models drive mistakes underground, hiding valuable safety data from leadership. Instead of asking who made the mistake, a Just Culture asks what system vulnerabilities allowed the error to occur. Yet, accountability remains central. Human behavior is evaluated along a continuum ranging from innocent human error to negligent recklessness. - **Human error**: Inadvertently doing other than what should have been done; managed through process redesign, training, and console updates. - **At-risk behavior**: A behavioral choice where risk is not recognized or is mistakenly believed to be justified; managed by coaching and removing perverse incentives. - **Reckless behavior**: Conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk; managed through punitive disciplinary action. When Nigerian nursing units adopt this framework, incident reporting rates typically rise, reflecting transparency rather than declining standards of care.

Immediate Actions and Patient Safety Stabilization

When an adverse event unfolds, the immediate priority shifts away from administrative tracking toward clinical rescue and harm mitigation. The patient must be stabilized first. If a wrong medication is administered, vital signs require continuous monitoring while emergency antidotes or supportive therapies are initiated. Once the patient is stable, clinical staff must secure the environment. This involves preserving equipment, retaining medication vials, and quarantining any defective devices involved in the incident. Tampering with or discarding faulty apparatus obscures the investigative trail and compromises future safety analysis. Immediate actions also demand clear communication within the multidisciplinary team. The attending physician must be notified promptly, and nursing handover notes should reflect objective clinical observations without assigning premature subjective blame. Swift stabilization minimizes physiological deterioration and reassures the patient and family that competent care is actively managing the crisis.

The Root Cause Analysis (RCA) Process

A **Root Cause Analysis (RCA)** is a structured retrospective investigation technique used to identify the fundamental, underlying causes of a major adverse event. Rather than stopping at the surface mistake, an RCA digs deep into organizational structures, communication barriers, environmental stressors, and human factors. The investigation relies on multi-disciplinary teams including nurses, pharmacists, administrators, and frontline specialists. A common tool utilized within this process is the **"Five Whys" methodology**, which repeatedly questions the chain of events until the foundational systemic breakdown is uncovered. | Step in RCA | Primary Activity | |---|---| | Data Gathering | Collecting charts, interviewing witnesses, mapping timelines | | Identifying Contributory Factors | Examining communication, staffing, equipment, and environment | | Finding Root Causes | Determining underlying systemic flaws allowing the error | | Action Planning | Creating concrete, measurable interventions to prevent recurrence | For RCA findings to yield sustainable improvements across Nigerian healthcare facilities, recommendations must translate directly into modified workflows rather than sitting as unexecuted paperwork.

Open Disclosure and Communicating with Patients and Families

**Open disclosure** is the transparent, honest, and compassionate communication with patients and their families when an adverse event occurs during their care. Modern healthcare ethics and professional codes mandate that patients have a right to know what happened, why it happened, what is being done to manage the consequences, and how recurrence will be prevented. Initiating this conversation requires careful planning. Clinicians must choose a private, quiet setting, express sincere empathy without offering premature apologies that imply legal liability before facts are verified, and use clear, jargon-free language. Listening to the family's grief and anger is as vital as delivering the clinical facts. - Acknowledge the event promptly once the patient is stable. - Explain the known facts clearly and objectively. - Avoid speculation, defensiveness, or blaming colleagues. - Offer ongoing support and follow-up medical care. Transparent communication builds trust, respects human dignity, and paradoxically reduces the likelihood of hostile litigation compared to defensive stonewalling.

Documentation, Incident Reporting, and Legal Considerations

Accurate documentation following an adverse event bridges clinical care, internal quality improvement, and legal protection. Healthcare providers must record objective clinical assessments, patient status updates, and notifications given to physicians directly in the patient's official health record. Speculative remarks, personal opinions, or admissions of guilt have no place in clinical notes. Simultaneously, a separate **incident report** must be filed through the institution's designated reporting system. - **Medical record**: Clinical narrative intended for ongoing patient care; discoverable in legal proceedings. - **Incident report**: Administrative document for internal quality improvement; status regarding legal discovery varies by jurisdiction. Crucially, nurses must never document in the patient's chart that an "incident report was filed," as this creates a confusing cross-reference. Maintaining objective, factual clarity protects both the patient's legal rights and the integrity of the healthcare team.

Supporting the Second Victim: Healthcare Provider Care

When a severe medical error or adverse event occurs, the patient and family are the primary victims of direct harm. However, the clinicians involved—nurses, doctors, pharmacists—are frequently termed **"second victims."** These providers often experience intense emotional trauma, including profound guilt, anxiety, self-doubt, shame, and burnout. Traditional healthcare cultures often expect staff to "tough it out" and immediately return to high-acuity duties. This approach worsens psychological distress and increases the risk of secondary medical errors due to cognitive fatigue. Effective organizational support systems require: - Peer support networks trained in psychological first aid. - Confidential debriefing spaces free from administrative judgment. - Professional counseling access for traumatized staff. - Management validation that acknowledges human vulnerability in complex environments. When Nigerian hospitals actively care for their nursing workforce following traumatic clinical events, retention improves and psychological safety is restored across wards.

Implementing System Changes to Prevent Recurrence

Investigating errors and supporting staff are incomplete without tangible system redesign. The ultimate goal of incident response is transforming lessons learned into permanent safeguards. If an RCA reveals that similar drug packaging contributed to a medication administration error, the hospital must alter its formulary stock or introduce barcode scanning. System interventions should target reliability through forced functions, checklists, and automation rather than relying solely on human memory and vigilance. - **Hard stop**: A system barrier that prevents an action until a safety condition is met. * **Simplification**: Streamlining complex, multi-step workflows to reduce cognitive load. * **Standardization**: Implementing uniform protocols across all clinical units. Evaluating the effectiveness of these changes closes the quality loop. By tracking incident rates over time, nurse leaders ensure that the hard-won lessons from adverse events permanently elevate the safety baseline of the entire health system.
