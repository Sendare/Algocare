Responding to Adverse Events, Errors and Harm

Defining Adverse Events, Medical Errors, and Patient Harm

Understanding the precise definitions of adverse events, medical errors, and patient harm is foundational for nursing students practicing in Nigeria and globally. An adverse event is defined as an untoward incident, therapeutic misadventure, iatrogenic injury, or other adverse occurrence directly associated with care or services provided to a patient, whether or not it is preventable. For instance, a patient developing a severe allergic reaction to a newly administered antibiotic they have no documented history of can be classified as an adverse event. Conversely, a medical error involves the failure of a planned action to be completed as intended or the use of a wrong plan to achieve an aim, which can be an error of omission or commission. If a nurse administers the wrong dose of medication due to a miscalculation, this represents a medical error. Finally, patient harm refers to temporary or permanent impairment to the physical, emotional, or psychological structure or function of the body and/or resultant pain arising from or sustained during healthcare management. Distinguishing among these terms helps nurses accurately document incidents, participate meaningfully in quality improvement meetings, and contribute to safer healthcare environments in Nigerian hospitals and clinics where patient safety advocacy is rapidly growing.

The Role of a Just Culture in Reporting and Responding

A 'Just Culture' is an essential framework for healthcare organizations striving to improve patient safety. In a Just Culture, the environment balances individual accountability with systemic learning. Nurses and other healthcare professionals are encouraged to report medical errors, near misses, and adverse events without fear of unfair punishment. However, a Just Culture does not mean a blameless culture; rather, it differentiates between human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless behavior. Human error occurs when an individual inadvertently slips or lapses, such as accidentally selecting the wrong IV line due to fatigue. At-risk behavior involves a behavioral choice where the risk is not recognized or is mistakenly believed to be justified, like bypassing a double-check procedure because the ward is extremely busy. Reckless behavior, on the other hand, involves conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk, such as willfully sleeping on night duty while patients require critical monitoring. By understanding this distinction, nursing students and practicing clinicians in Nigerian health facilities can appreciate why honest reporting is rewarded, while willful violations are appropriately managed. Fostering this environment builds trust, enhances incident disclosure, and ultimately drives systematic safety improvements across clinical departments.

Immediate Actions and Patient Safety Stabilization

When an adverse event or medical error occurs, the immediate priority for the nurse and healthcare team must always be patient safety and clinical stabilization. Immediate actions involve assessing the patient's current clinical status, halting any ongoing harm, and providing immediate supportive interventions. For example, if a wrong blood transfusion is initiated, the nurse must immediately stop the transfusion, maintain IV access with normal saline using a new administration set, notify the attending physician, and closely monitor vital signs. Patient stabilization takes precedence over administrative tasks, documentation, or notifying management. Nigerian nursing practice emphasizes rapid clinical assessment using frameworks such as ABCDE (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure) to identify and treat life-threatening complications promptly. Once the patient is clinically stable, emergency interventions are documented, and ongoing monitoring is established to detect delayed adverse reactions, such as anaphylaxis or hemorrhage. By prioritizing rapid stabilization, nurses mitigate the severity of harm, uphold ethical obligations of beneficence and non-maleficence, and set the stage for subsequent investigation and transparent communication with the patient and multidisciplinary team.

The Root Cause Analysis (RCA) Process

Root Cause Analysis (RCA) is a structured, systematic problem-solving method used to identify the fundamental, underlying causes of an adverse event or medical error. Rather than simply blaming an individual nurse or doctor, RCA looks deep into the complex systems, processes, and environmental factors that contributed to the incident. In Nigerian healthcare institutions, implementing RCA helps shift the culture from punitive measures to systemic learning. The RCA process typically involves assembling a multidisciplinary team, gathering data through interviews and chart reviews, mapping out the chronological sequence of events, and identifying active failures (unsafe acts committed by frontline staff) and latent conditions (hidden organizational flaws, such as understaffing, poor equipment design, or ambiguous protocols). Tools like the 'Five Whys' or 'Fishbone Diagram' are frequently utilized to drill down to the root causes. Once the root causes are uncovered, the team develops and implements strong corrective action plans—such as redesigning medication storage, introducing barcoding, or revising handover policies—to ensure the same error does not happen again. Nursing students must understand RCA as a vital quality improvement tool rather than an interrogation.

Open Disclosure and Communicating with Patients and Families

Open disclosure is an honest, empathetic, and transparent communication process between healthcare providers and patients (and/or their families) following an adverse event or medical error resulting in harm. Practicing open disclosure is both an ethical duty and a vital component of patient-centered care in nursing. It involves acknowledging that an adverse event occurred, expressing regret and empathy for what happened, explaining what is known at the time, outlining steps being taken to investigate, and offering continuous support. When conducting open disclosure, nurses should use clear, non-technical language, avoid defensive posturing or assigning blame, and actively listen to the patient's concerns and questions. In the context of Nigerian healthcare, where patients and families may experience profound distress and loss of trust following an error, structured open disclosure helps rebuild trust, provides psychological closure, and can significantly reduce the likelihood of hostile litigation. Nursing students must learn how to participate in disclosure discussions sensitively, ensuring that communication is timely, compassionate, and documented appropriately in the patient's clinical record.

Documentation, Incident Reporting, and Legal Considerations

Accurate documentation, prompt incident reporting, and an understanding of legal considerations are critical responsibilities for professional nurses. Following an adverse event, the nurse must document the incident objectively in the patient’s clinical health record. Documentation must focus strictly on objective clinical facts, patient assessments, interventions performed, and physician notifications, avoiding subjective opinions, judgmental language, or pointing fingers at colleagues. Crucially, incident reports (sometimes called adverse event reports) are separate administrative documents used for quality improvement and risk management; they should never be referred to or placed inside the permanent patient medical record. In Nigerian legal and clinical contexts, proper documentation protects both the patient and the healthcare provider by providing a clear legal timeline of care. Nurses must be aware of statutory requirements, professional codes of conduct established by regulatory bodies like the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN), and legal principles such as negligence and liability. Maintaining high standards of integrity in documentation ensures transparency and accountability while safeguarding professional practice.

Supporting the Second Victim: Healthcare Provider Care

Healthcare providers who are involved in a serious adverse event, medical error, or unexpected patient death are frequently referred to as 'second victims.' These clinicians—including nurses, midwives, doctors, and pharmacists—often experience intense emotional turmoil, including guilt, anxiety, depression, shame, self-doubt, and fear of professional repercussions or loss of licensure. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where staffing shortages can lead to heavy workloads and emotional exhaustion, second victims often suffer in silence due to cultural stigmas surrounding professional mistakes. Recognizing and supporting the second victim is crucial for both personal well-being and patient safety; untreated emotional distress can lead to burnout, decreased clinical performance, and secondary errors. Supportive strategies include peer support programs, confidential counseling services, employee assistance programs, and a Just Culture environment that fosters psychological safety. Nurse leaders and educators must promote compassionate peer support, encouraging colleagues to talk about their experiences, validate their feelings, and access professional help when needed, reinforcing that making an error does not define one's worth as a compassionate healthcare professional.

Implementing System Changes to Prevent Recurrence

The ultimate goal of responding to adverse events and medical errors is to implement robust system changes that prevent recurrence. Identifying a root cause through RCA or incident reporting is ineffective unless actionable recommendations are translated into concrete policy, operational, and environmental modifications. In Nigerian hospitals and healthcare facilities, system changes can take many forms: redesigning medication administration workflows to incorporate mandatory double-checks, standardizing high-risk equipment across all wards, introducing electronic health record alerts, updating clinical protocols, and conducting ongoing simulation-based training for nursing staff. Furthermore, evaluating the effectiveness of implemented changes is vital to ensure that new safety measures actually reduce risk rather than creating unintended workarounds or new vulnerabilities. Nursing students and practicing nurses must actively participate in continuous quality improvement (CQI) initiatives and safety committees. By embracing systemic changes rather than relying solely on individual vigilance, the nursing profession contributes significantly to building safer, more reliable, and resilient healthcare delivery systems across Nigeria.
