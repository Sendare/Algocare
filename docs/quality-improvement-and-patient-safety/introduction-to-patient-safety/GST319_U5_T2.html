Adverse Events in Healthcare

Defining Adverse Events, Errors, and Near Misses

Understanding patient safety requires clear boundaries between distinct clinical mishaps. An **adverse event** is an unintended injury caused by medical management rather than the underlying disease state, which may or may not be preventable. A **medical error** involves a failure of a planned action to be completed as intended, or the use of a wrong plan to achieve an aim. Conversely, a **near miss** describes an unsafe situation that is intercepted before it reaches the patient, preventing actual harm through luck or timely intervention. In Nigerian hospital settings, distinguishing these categories helps nursing teams evaluate ward safety without immediately assigning personal blame, fostering a culture where every slip can be openly examined.

Classification and Types of Adverse Events

- **Diagnostic errors**: Delayed testing, misinterpretation of laboratory results, or failure to act on abnormal findings. - **Medication mishaps**: Incorrect dosage calculations, wrong route administration, or failing to check patient allergies. - **Surgical complications**: Retained foreign objects, wrong-site procedures, or anesthesia-related trauma. - **Healthcare-associated infections**: Catheter-associated urinary tract infections or surgical site contamination resulting from poor aseptic technique. Categorizing these events allows healthcare facilities to track recurring patterns and direct resources toward high-risk clinical areas.

Common Causes and Contributing Factors

Adverse events rarely stem from a single isolated mistake. Instead, they usually emerge when multiple system vulnerabilities align. **Human factors**, such as severe fatigue, cognitive overload, and poor shift handover communication, frequently compromise clinical judgment. **Environmental constraints**—including chronic understaffing, broken diagnostic equipment, and unreadable handwritten drug charts—further amplify clinical risk. In many resource-constrained public hospitals across West Africa, heavy workloads and intermittent power supplies create fertile ground for systemic breakdowns, making environmental and structural factors just as critical to evaluate as individual clinical actions.

Impact on Patients and Healthcare Systems

The fallout from an adverse event reaches far beyond immediate physical injury. For the patient, complications often mean prolonged hospital stays, permanent disability, financial ruin from out-of-pocket expenses, and profound psychological distress. For the healthcare institution, these incidents erode public trust, lead to costly litigation, and deplete scarce medical resources. Furthermore, healthcare professionals involved in severe mistakes frequently experience **second victim syndrome**, characterized by intense guilt, anxiety, depression, and loss of professional confidence if institutional support systems fail to help them process the trauma.

Reporting and Documentation Protocols

Transparent reporting forms the cornerstone of risk reduction. When a mistake or near miss occurs, institutional protocols demand prompt, factual **incident documentation** separate from the patient's official medical record. Nurses must record the precise sequence of events without using accusatory language or admitting legal liability. In many Nigerian clinical environments, fear of disciplinary action or public reprimand discourages staff from logging these events. Overcoming this barrier requires shifting toward a **just culture**, which separates reckless negligence from honest human slips, ensuring that incident reporting is viewed as a tool for system learning rather than punishment.

Root Cause Analysis and Quality Improvement Strategies

Fixing a safety breakdown requires looking past who made the mistake to examine why the system allowed it to happen. **Root Cause Analysis (RCA)** is a structured method used after an adverse event to trace the chain of failures back to underlying systemic weaknesses. Quality improvement teams use tools like the 'Five Whys' technique to drill down from the immediate error to policy gaps, workflow design flaws, or training deficiencies. Once the root causes are identified, hospitals implement targeted safety interventions, such as automated medication dispensing alerts, double-check policies for high-alert drugs, and standardized surgical safety checklists.

The Role of the Nurse in Prevention and Patient Safety

As the frontline provider maintaining continuous contact with patients, the nurse acts as the final and most vital safety barrier. Nurses operationalize safety through rigorous bedside practices: performing the **five rights** of medication administration, conducting thorough physical assessments, verifying surgical site markings before theater transfer, and encouraging patients to actively participate in their care. Furthermore, championing open communication during shift handovers and advocating for safe nurse-to-patient staffing levels directly prevents adverse events before they reach the patient.
