Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) Cycle

Introduction to Quality Improvement in Nursing

Quality improvement, often called QI, is a continuous, systematic approach used in healthcare to make patient care safer, more efficient, and more reliable. In nursing, we see daily routines where small errors or delays happen—like misplaced charts or delayed medication passes. Instead of accepting these issues as normal parts of a busy shift, QI gives nurses the tools to investigate and fix the root causes. It is not about blaming individual staff members; rather, it focuses on examining how systems and processes work. By actively engaging in quality improvement, nurses help shape better hospital policies, reduce waste, and directly improve patient health outcomes.

Overview and Structure of the PDSA Cycle

The Plan-Do-Study-Act cycle, widely known as the PDSA cycle, is a four-stage problem-solving model used for testing small-scale changes in clinical practice. Originating from industrial manufacturing and later adopted globally by healthcare organizations, it provides a structured framework for trying out a new idea and learning from the results before making a massive commitment. Rather than overhauling an entire hospital ward overnight, nurses use the PDSA cycle to test a small change with a few patients or on a single shift. If the change works, it can be expanded. If it fails, little time or money has been wasted, and the team can try a different approach.

Step 1: The Plan Phase

The Plan phase is the foundation of the entire improvement project. During this stage, the nursing team defines a specific goal, asks questions, and makes predictions about what will happen when a change is introduced.

  1. State the objective: Be very clear about what needs to be achieved, such as reducing patient wait times for pain medication by ten minutes.
  2. Formulate a theory: Decide on the intervention, like placing extra PRN analgesics in the treatment room.
  3. Plan the test: Decide who will do what, when it will happen, and what data needs to be collected to measure success.

Skipping this phase often leads to vague projects that fail to produce measurable results.

Step 2: The Do Phase

In the Do phase, the team puts the plan into action on a small scale. This is where theory meets reality. Nurses carry out the intervention exactly as discussed during the planning stage. While running this test, it is vital to document any problems, unexpected observations, or pleasant surprises that occur along the way. If nurses encounter severe practical barriers—such as a new documentation form taking twice as long to fill out—they must record these challenges. Maintaining careful notes during this phase ensures that the subsequent analysis is based on real-world experiences rather than guesswork.

Step 3: The Study Phase

During the Study phase, the nursing team steps back to analyze the data collected during the test. They compare the actual results against the predictions made back in the Plan phase. Did the new morning huddle reduce shift handover delays? Did patient satisfaction scores improve? If the results show no change or even negative outcomes, the team must explore why. This phase is about learning and reflection, not judgment. Even a failed test provides valuable insight into why a certain workflow does not fit the daily realities of the nursing unit.

Step 4: The Act Phase

The Act phase is where the team decides what actions to take based on the lessons learned in the Study phase. Nurses generally choose one of three paths, often referred to as the adopt, adapt, or abandon approach:

  • Adopt: The test was successful, and the change is ready to be implemented more broadly across the unit or hospital.
  • Adapt: The test showed partial success, but modifications are needed. The team loops back to the Plan phase with a revised strategy.
  • Abandon: The test failed completely and did not yield benefits, so the team discards the idea and tries a different approach entirely.

Once this decision is made, the cycle can begin again for further refinements.

Applying PDSA in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Applying the PDSA cycle in Nigerian healthcare requires a practical understanding of local resource realities, such as irregular power supply, staffing shortages, and high patient volumes. When nurses initiate quality improvement projects in environments like primary health clinics or tertiary teaching hospitals, large-scale technology overhauls often fail due to funding and infrastructure limits. Instead, PDSA thrives on low-cost, workflow-based changes. For example, a maternity ward nurse might use a PDSA cycle to rearrange how partographs are stored to speed up emergency obstetric reviews. By focusing on small steps that do not require massive budgets, Nigerian nurses can drive meaningful, sustainable improvements in patient safety.
