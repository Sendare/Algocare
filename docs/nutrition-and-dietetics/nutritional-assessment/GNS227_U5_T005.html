Biochemical Assessment

Introduction to Biochemical Assessment in Nutrition

Biochemical assessment involves measuring the concentration of nutrients or their metabolites in body fluids and tissues, primarily blood and urine. Unlike dietary surveys or anthropometric measurements, biochemical tests provide objective, quantitative data regarding a patient's nutritional status at a cellular level. This methodology identifies subclinical deficiencies long before physical signs or anthropometric changes manifest externally. In Nigerian clinical settings, where healthcare resources can sometimes be constrained, selecting the appropriate laboratory test is vital for cost-effective patient management. Routine biochemistry screening helps clinicians confirm suspected malnutrition, monitor the efficacy of nutritional support, and detect metabolic imbalances during severe acute illness. Integrating these tests with clinical histories ensures a comprehensive nutritional diagnosis.

Assessment of Protein-Energy Malnutrition: Albumin, Prealbumin, and Transferrin

Visceral proteins are traditional laboratory indicators used to evaluate protein-energy status, though their clinical utility requires careful physiological interpretation. - **Albumin**: The most abundant serum protein with a long half-life of approximately 20 days. Due to this slow turnover rate, serum albumin is a poor marker for acute protein depletion, reflecting chronic protein status and long-term morbidity risk instead. - **Prealbumin (Transthyretin)**: Features a much shorter half-life of roughly 2 to 3 days and a small body pool. This rapid turnover makes prealbumin highly sensitive to acute changes in nutritional intake and protein synthesis, making it useful for monitoring nutritional repletion. - **Transferrin**: An iron-transport protein with a half-life of 8 to 10 days. While useful for assessing visceral protein status, transferrin levels are heavily confounded by iron deficiency states, rising when iron stores are depleted regardless of protein intake.

Evaluation of Micronutrient Status: Vitamins and Minerals

Assessing vitamins and minerals presents distinct technical challenges because micronutrients often function intracellularly while tests are performed on extracellular serum. Serum levels may remain normal despite severe tissue depletion due to homeostatic mechanisms that mobilize stores to maintain blood concentrations. Fat-soluble vitamins such as Vitamin A and Vitamin D are routinely assessed via serum levels, though acute infections can transiently depress Vitamin A transport proteins. Water-soluble vitamins like folate and vitamin B12 require careful evaluation alongside red blood cell indices to differentiate true tissue deficiency from recent dietary intake fluctuations. Mineral assessment similarly relies on a combination of direct serum assays, such as serum iron and zinc, and functional tests that measure downstream enzymatic activity dependent on these micronutrients.

Hematological Tests for Anemia and Nutritional Deficiencies

Hematological assessment offers vital window into nutritional adequacy, particularly regarding iron, folate, and vitamin B12. Hemoglobin and hematocrit measurements screen for anemia, though they cannot specify the underlying nutritional etiology. Examining red blood cell indices provides deeper diagnostic clarity: - **Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV)**: Measures average red blood cell size. Microcytic indices point toward iron deficiency or thalassemia, while macrocytic indices suggest megaloblastic anemia caused by folate or vitamin B12 deficiency. - **Serum Ferritin**: Serves as the most sensitive indicator of total body iron stores. Low ferritin confirms iron deficiency anemia, but high ferritin must be interpreted cautiously because it is an acute-phase reactant. - **Total Iron-Binding Capacity (TIBC)**: Measures the blood's capacity to bind iron with transport proteins. TIBC increases in iron deficiency and decreases in chronic disease or inflammation.

Inflammatory Markers and Their Impact on Nutritional Biomarkers

Systemic inflammation dramatically alters biochemical nutritional markers, transforming how clinicians interpret laboratory data. During acute or chronic inflammatory states—such as malaria, sepsis, or trauma—the liver alters protein synthesis profiles. Positive acute-phase reactants like C-reactive protein (CRP) increase rapidly, while negative acute-phase reactants decrease. Proteins traditionally used to measure nutritional status, including albumin, prealbumin, and transferrin, are negative acute-phase reactants. Their concentrations drop during inflammation regardless of the patient's true nutritional intake. Consequently, evaluating nutritional proteins without checking an inflammatory marker like CRP leads to misdiagnosing inflammation-induced hypoproteinemia as severe protein-energy malnutrition.

Limitations and Confounding Factors in Biochemical Testing

Biochemical assessment is a powerful diagnostic tool, but test results are susceptible to multiple confounding factors that can obscure true nutritional status. Hydration status is a primary disturber: severe dehydration concentrates blood components, artificially raising laboratory values, while fluid overload hemodilutes blood constituents, producing falsely low readings. Renal and hepatic dysfunctions severely impair the metabolism, utilization, and excretion of nutrients and their transport proteins, independent of dietary intake. Furthermore, drug-nutrient interactions can alter laboratory values; for instance, certain medications interfere with vitamin absorption or accelerate micronutrient excretion. Recognizing these physiological variables prevents inappropriate nutritional interventions and ensures patient safety.
