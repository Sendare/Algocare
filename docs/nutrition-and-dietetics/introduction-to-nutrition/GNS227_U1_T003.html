Nutrients

Introduction to Nutrients in Nursing Care

Nutrients are chemical substances obtained from food that are essential for the survival, growth, development, tissue repair, and overall maintenance of the human body. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding the foundational principles of nutrition is vital because dietary management directly impacts patient recovery, immune function, and hospital length of stay. Nurses are uniquely positioned to assess nutritional status, educate patients on healthy eating habits, and collaborate with multidisciplinary healthcare teams to deliver holistic care. In many clinical settings across Nigeria, patients face vulnerabilities related to poverty, food insecurity, and chronic illness, making nutritional screening an indispensable component of daily nursing duties. By recognizing how proper nourishment accelerates wound healing and prevents complications, nurses can advocate effectively for their patients' dietary needs. Furthermore, understanding nutrients allows the nurse to interpret laboratory findings related to malnutrition, anemia, and electrolyte imbalances. As frontline healthcare providers, nurses must bridge the gap between scientific nutritional data and practical, culturally appropriate dietary practices that patients can implement at home. This foundational knowledge ultimately empowers nurses to promote health promotion and disease prevention across diverse community and hospital environments.

Macronutrients: Carbohydrates, Proteins, and Fats

Macronutrients are the primary dietary components that the human body requires in large quantities to provide energy, support structural growth, and regulate metabolic processes. They are categorized into three main classes: carbohydrates, proteins, and fats. Carbohydrates serve as the body's primary and most efficient source of energy, particularly for the brain and central nervous system. In the Nigerian context, staple carbohydrate sources include yam, cassava, maize, rice, and sorghum. Proteins are composed of amino acids, which are essential for tissue repair, cellular growth, hormone production, and immune defense. Complete proteins contain all essential amino acids and are found in animal products like meat, fish, eggs, and dairy, while plant-based sources like beans, cowpeas, and groundnuts provide complementary proteins. Fats, or lipids, supply a concentrated source of energy, protect vital internal organs, insulate the body, and facilitate the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K). Nursing students must understand the distinct roles of these macronutrients to effectively counsel patients on balanced diets, especially when managing metabolic disorders such as diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, and protein-energy malnutrition like kwashiorkor and marasmus.

Micronutrients: Vitamins and Minerals

Micronutrients encompass vitamins and minerals, which are required by the body in significantly smaller amounts compared to macronutrients, yet they are absolutely critical for health and physiological functioning. Vitamins are organic compounds classified into water-soluble (Vitamin C and the B-complex group) and fat-soluble (Vitamins A, D, E, and K) categories. They act as coenzymes, support immune health, facilitate vision, and protect cellular structures from oxidative damage. Minerals are inorganic elements divided into major minerals, such as calcium, potassium, and sodium, and trace minerals, such as iron, zinc, and iodine. Minerals maintain fluid balance, strengthen bones and teeth, facilitate nerve impulse transmission, and prevent conditions like iron-deficiency anemia and goiter. In Nigeria, micronutrient deficiencies remain a significant public health concern, particularly Vitamin A deficiency, iron-deficiency anemia in pregnant women, and iodine deficiency disorders. Nurses must educate communities on consuming diverse, nutrient-dense local foods such as green leafy vegetables, organ meats, and fortified staples, while also administering micronutrient supplementation programs when indicated to improve overall maternal and child health outcomes.

Water and Fluid Balance

Water is the most critical nutrient for human survival, constituting approximately 60% of an adult's total body weight. It serves as the primary medium for all cellular metabolic reactions, transports nutrients and waste products throughout the body, regulates internal body temperature through perspiration, and cushions joints and organs. Fluid balance is maintained through a delicate regulatory system involving intake, hormonal controls such as antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and aldosterone, and output via urine, feces, sweat, and respiration. In nursing practice, monitoring fluid balance is vital, particularly for patients experiencing dehydration, vomiting, diarrhea, burns, or renal failure. In tropical climates like Nigeria, high environmental temperatures increase fluid loss through sweat, elevating the risk of dehydration if fluid intake is inadequate. Nurses must accurately calculate and record fluid intake and output (I&O), observe clinical signs of fluid volume deficit or excess (such as edema, poor skin turgor, and altered vital signs), and educate patients on maintaining adequate hydration using safe drinking water and oral rehydration therapy when necessary.

Digestion, Absorption, and Metabolism of Nutrients

The journey of food from ingestion to cellular utilization involves three complex physiological processes: digestion, absorption, and metabolism. Digestion is the mechanical and chemical breakdown of large food molecules into smaller, absorbable units. It begins in the mouth with mastication and salivary amylase, continues in the stomach via gastric acid and pepsin, and is completed primarily in the small intestine through pancreatic enzymes and bile. Absorption is the process by which these smaller nutrient molecules pass through the intestinal mucosa into the bloodstream or lymphatic system. Most nutrient absorption occurs in the duodenum and jejunum of the small intestine, facilitated by specialized structures like villi and microvilli. Once absorbed, nutrients travel via the portal circulation to the liver and systemic circulation for metabolism. Metabolism encompasses all the biochemical reactions within the body cells, divided into anabolism (building up complex tissues and molecules) and catabolism (breaking down molecules to release energy). Nurses must understand these pathways to recognize how gastrointestinal pathologies, surgical resections, or malabsorption syndromes impair nutritional status, guiding effective nutritional support interventions.

Nutritional Assessment and Dietary Guidelines in Nigeria

Nutritional assessment is a systematic method used by nurses and healthcare teams to evaluate a patient's nutritional status, identify deficiencies or excesses, and develop tailored dietary care plans. This assessment utilizes the ABCDE approach: Anthropometric measurements (height, weight, body mass index, mid-upper arm circumference), Biochemical data (serum albumin, hemoglobin levels, electrolyte panels), Clinical examination (checking for signs of malnutrition in skin, hair, eyes, and nails), Dietary history (evaluating food intake patterns and cultural preferences), and Environmental factors (socioeconomic status and access to food). In Nigeria, dietary guidelines and food guides—often promoted by public health agencies and nutritionists—emphasize the use of locally available, affordable food items categorized into food groups that ensure balanced nutrition. Nurses must be familiar with these local guidelines to provide relevant counseling that respects cultural food habits and economic realities. For instance, encouraging the consumption of traditional grains, legumes, and locally grown vegetables ensures that dietary advice is practical, sustainable, and effective in preventing malnutrition across hospitalized patients and community populations alike.

Clinical Implications of Nutrient Deficiencies and Excesses

Imbalances in nutrient intake—whether through deficiency or excess—lead to significant pathological conditions that nurses encounter daily in clinical practice. Nutrient deficiencies can arise from inadequate intake, malabsorption, increased metabolic demands, or poverty. Classic examples include protein-energy malnutrition (such as kwashiorkor, characterized by edema and sparse hair, and marasmus, characterized by severe muscle wasting), iron-deficiency anemia resulting in fatigue and pallor, and scurvy resulting from Vitamin C deficiency. Conversely, nutrient excesses, particularly of calories, saturated fats, sodium, and refined sugars, contribute to the growing epidemic of non-communicable diseases in Nigeria, including obesity, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disorders. Nurses play a critical frontline role in identifying early warning signs of these deficiencies and excesses during routine health checks. By providing targeted nutritional education, monitoring laboratory values, and implementing multidisciplinary care plans, nurses help mitigate complications, promote healthy lifestyle modifications, and support long-term wellness for patients across diverse healthcare settings.
