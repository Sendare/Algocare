Nutrients

Introduction to Nutrients in Nursing Care

Nutrients are chemical substances found in food that the body needs to function, grow, repair itself, and produce energy. In clinical nursing, understanding nutrients goes beyond basic meal planning; it forms the foundation of patient recovery and health promotion. Every client admitted to a ward has unique nutritional requirements influenced by their age, medical diagnosis, metabolic state, and cultural background. Nurses are often the first healthcare professionals to notice signs of poor nutrition during admission assessments. Recognizing how the body utilizes food helps nurses educate patients, prevent hospital-associated malnutrition, and collaborate effectively with dietitians to improve clinical outcomes across all healthcare settings.

Macronutrients: Carbohydrates, Proteins, and Fats

Macronutrients are the three main classes of food required by the body in large amounts to supply energy and build structural tissue.

  • Carbohydrates: The primary and most efficient source of energy for the body, especially for the brain and red blood cells. They break down into glucose.
  • Proteins: Composed of amino acids, proteins are essential for tissue growth, immune function, fluid balance, and the synthesis of hormones and enzymes.
  • Fats (Lipids): Provide a concentrated source of energy, protect vital organs, support cell membrane integrity, and help absorb fat-soluble vitamins.

Micronutrients: Vitamins and Minerals

Micronutrients consist of vitamins and minerals. While the body only requires them in small quantities, they are vital for regulating physiological processes, supporting immune responses, and maintaining bone health.

  • Vitamins: Organic compounds divided into water-soluble (C and B-complex) and fat-soluble (A, D, E, and K). Water-soluble vitamins need regular daily intake because excess amounts are excreted in urine, whereas fat-soluble vitamins can accumulate in the liver and fatty tissues to toxic levels if consumed in extreme excess.
  • Minerals: Inorganic elements categorized as major minerals (such as calcium, potassium, and sodium) and trace minerals (such as iron, zinc, and iodine). They regulate fluid balance, nerve conduction, and muscle contraction.

Water and Fluid Balance

Water is the most critical nutrient for human survival, making up roughly 60 percent of total body weight in healthy adults. It acts as a universal solvent, regulates body temperature, cushions joints, and transports other nutrients and wastes through the bloodstream. Fluid balance is maintained when fluid intake roughly equals fluid output through urine, sweat, respiration, and stool. Nurses must monitor hydration status closely in vulnerable populations, such as infants and the elderly, who are at high risk for rapid dehydration or fluid overload.

Digestion, Absorption, and Metabolism of Nutrients

Digestion breaks down complex food particles into simple, absorbable units. It begins in the mouth with chewing and salivary enzymes, continues in the stomach with hydrochloric acid and pepsin, and finishes largely in the small intestine where pancreatic juices and bile complete chemical breakdown. Absorption occurs primarily across the mucosal lining of the small intestine into the bloodstream and lymphatic system. Once absorbed, metabolism takes over. Metabolism includes two processes: anabolism, which builds up new tissue, and catabolism, which breaks down molecules to release energy.

Nutritional Assessment and Dietary Guidelines in Nigeria

Nutritional assessment involves evaluating a patient's dietary history, anthropometric measurements (such as weight, height, and body mass index), clinical signs, and laboratory blood values. In the Nigerian context, dietary guidelines emphasize locally available staples to promote food security and cultural acceptability. Recommendations frequently center around energy-giving foods like cassava, yam, maize, and sorghum; body-building protein sources such as beans, cowpeas, fish, and lean meat; and protective foods rich in local vegetables and tropical fruits. Nurses use these context-specific guidelines to counsel patients on affordable, balanced meal planning.

Clinical Implications of Nutrient Deficiencies and Excesses

Imbalances in nutrient intake lead to distinct pathological states. Deficiencies can result from inadequate intake, poor absorption, or increased metabolic demand. Classic examples include iron-deficiency anemia, protein-energy malnutrition like kwashiorkor and marasmus, and vitamin deficiency diseases like scurvy or night blindness. Conversely, excessive intake of certain nutrients can be equally harmful. Overconsumption of calories and fats leads to obesity, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular disease, while megadoses of fat-soluble vitamins can cause systemic toxicity. Nurses must recognize both ends of the nutritional spectrum to provide targeted care and prevent long-term complications.
