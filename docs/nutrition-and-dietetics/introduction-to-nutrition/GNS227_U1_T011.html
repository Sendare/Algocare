Digestion

Introduction and Structural Organization of the Gastrointestinal Tract

Digestion is the physiological process of breaking down complex food substances into smaller, absorbable molecules that can be utilized for cellular energy, growth, and repair. The human gastrointestinal tract functions as a continuous muscular tube extending from the mouth to the anus, punctuated by specialized sphincters that regulate the forward passage of luminal contents. - **Mucosa**: The innermost layer surrounding the lumen, containing epithelial cells specialized for absorption and secretion, supported by the lamina propria and muscularis mucosae. - **Submucosa**: A thick layer of connective tissue containing blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and the submucosal nerve plexus, which regulates glandular secretion. - **Muscularis Externa**: Typically composed of an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer of smooth muscle responsible for peristalsis and segmentation. - **Serosa / Adventitia**: The outermost connective tissue covering that protects the tract and anchors it to surrounding abdominal structures. Throughout the Nigerian clinical landscape, understanding these structural layers is fundamental when managing pathologies ranging from simple enteritis to complex bowel resections.

Ingestion, Mastication, and Salivary Activity

Digestion begins in the oral cavity through ingestion, where food is physically processed by mastication and chemically altered by saliva. Teeth tear and grind large food boluses into smaller particles, exponentially increasing the surface area exposed to digestive enzymes. Salivary glands—including the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands—produce roughly 1 to 1.5 liters of saliva daily. Saliva is composed of water, electrolytes, mucins for lubrication, and two primary enzymes: 1. **Salivary Amylase (Ptyalin)**: Initiates the breakdown of complex carbohydrates into disaccharides and oligosaccharides. 2. **Lingual Lipase**: Secreted by glands on the tongue, remaining relatively inactive in the mouth but activated in the acidic environment of the stomach to digest triglycerides. In local clinical settings, patients presenting with painful parotitis or mumps frequently exhibit reduced salivary output, leading to early complaints of dysphagia and dry oral mucosa.

Deglutition and Esophageal Transit

Swallowing, or deglutition, transitions food from a voluntary oral action into a coordinated involuntary reflex. This process occurs in three distinct phases: the oral phase, the pharyngeal phase, and the esophageal phase. During the pharyngeal phase, the soft palate elevates to close off the nasopharynx, and the larynx moves upward and forward while the epiglottis folds downward to seal the glottis. This prevents aspiration into the respiratory tract. Once the food bolus enters the esophagus, the upper esophageal sphincter relaxes, and peristaltic waves sweep the material downward toward the stomach. The esophagus itself does not participate in significant digestion or nutrient absorption; its sole function is rapid transit. A lower esophageal sphincter guards the junction between the esophagus and stomach, preventing the retrograde flow of highly acidic gastric contents. Failure of this sphincter to tone properly results in gastroesophageal reflux disease, a common complaint characterized by retrosternal burning.

Gastric Secretions and Mechanical Breakdown

The stomach acts as a muscular mixing basin and temporary storage reservoir. Its mucosa features deep gastric pits leading to specialized exocrine and endocrine cells that produce gastric juice: - **Mucous neck cells**: Secrete alkaline mucus that coats the gastric lining, shielding it from autodigestion by acid and enzymes. - **Parietal cells**: Secretes hydrochloric acid, creating an optimal low pH for enzyme activation, and intrinsic factor, which is strictly required for vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum. - **Chief cells**: Release inactive pepsinogen, which is cleaved by hydrochloric acid into active pepsin to begin protein digestion. - **G cells**: Enteroendocrine cells that secrete the hormone gastrin directly into the bloodstream to stimulate acid secretion and gastric motility. Through vigorous muscular contractions, the stomach transforms solid food into a semi-liquid paste known as chyme, which is periodically released into the duodenum through the pyloric sphincter.

Small Intestine: Enzymatic Digestion and Nutrient Absorption

The small intestine—comprising the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum—is the primary site for chemical digestion and nutrient absorption. The internal surface area is maximized through circular folds, villi, and microscopic microvilli that form a brush border. Enzymes anchored to the brush border, alongside secretions from the pancreas, complete the breakdown of macromolecules. Carbohydrates are reduced to monosaccharides, proteins to amino acids and small peptides, and lipids to fatty acids and monoglycerides after being emulsified by bile salts. Absorption occurs via active transport, facilitated diffusion, or simple diffusion across the enterocytes. Water-soluble nutrients pass directly into the hepatic portal system for processing by the liver, whereas fat-soluble products are packaged into chylomicrons and enter the lymphatic lacteals.

Accessory Organs: Liver, Gallbladder, and Pancreas

Accessory organs provide essential chemical secretions that empty into the duodenum to support small intestinal digestion. | Organ | Primary Secretion / Product | Main Function in Digestion | |---|---|---|> | **Liver** | Bile | Emulsifies dietary fats and neutralizes acidic chyme | | **Gallbladder**| Concentrated Bile | Stores and concentrates bile between meals | | **Pancreas** | Pancreatic Juice | Secretes digestive enzymes and bicarbonate ions | Pancreatic juice contains a rich suite of enzymes, including pancreatic amylase, lipases, and proteolytic enzymes like trypsinogen and chymotrypsinogen, deployed in inactive forms to prevent autodigestion of the pancreas itself.

Large Intestine and Waste Elimination

The large intestine includes the cecum, colon, rectum, and anal canal. By the time chyme reaches this region, most nutrients have been absorbed, leaving primarily indigestible fiber, water, and inorganic salts. The colon reabsorbs significant amounts of water and electrolytes, transforming liquid chyme into semi-solid feces. Resident gut flora—the intestinal microbiome—ferment remaining carbohydrates, produce vitamin K and certain B vitamins, and protect against pathogenic colonization. Peristaltic movements are slower here, featuring mass movements that push fecal waste toward the rectum. Distension of the rectal wall triggers the defecation reflex, coordinating involuntary relaxation of the internal anal sphincter with voluntary control over the external anal sphincter to expel waste.

Nervous and Hormonal Regulation of Digestion

Digestion is meticulously controlled by the enteric nervous system—often called the second brain—alongside the autonomic nervous system and gastrointestinal hormones. The enteric nervous system contains local myenteric and submucosal plexuses that coordinate motility, secretion, and blood flow independently of the central nervous system, though parasympathetic signals (via the vagus nerve) generally enhance digestive activity while sympathetic signals inhibit it. Hormonal regulation is driven by key endocrine cells in the stomach and small intestine: - **Gastrin**: Stimulates gastric acid secretion and mucosal growth. - **Secretin**: Promotes pancreatic bicarbonate secretion to neutralize acidic chyme entering the duodenum. - **Cholecystokinin (CCK)**: Stimulates gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme release in response to dietary fats and proteins.
