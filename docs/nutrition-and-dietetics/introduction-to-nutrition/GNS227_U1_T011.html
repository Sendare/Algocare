Digestion

Introduction to Digestion and the Gastrointestinal Tract

Digestion is the complex process of breaking down large food molecules into smaller, water-soluble particles that the body can absorb and use for energy, growth, and cell repair. The human digestive system is organized around the gastrointestinal (GI) tract, a continuous muscular tube that stretches from the mouth to the anus, alongside several vital accessory organs.

The GI tract consists of four primary histological layers:

  • Mucosa: The innermost layer that secretes mucus, digestive enzymes, and hormones while absorbing nutrients.
  • Submucosa: A layer of connective tissue containing blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and nerve plexuses that nourish surrounding tissues.
  • Muscularis externa: Responsible for gut motility, featuring inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle layers.
  • Serosa: The outermost protective membrane composed of connective tissue and epithelium.

Food moves along this pathway through peristalsis, a series of wave-like muscle contractions that push contents forward, while segmentation sloshes food back and forth in the small intestine to mix it thoroughly with digestive juices.

Ingestion and the Role of the Oral Cavity

Digestion begins the moment food enters the oral cavity through ingestion. Teeth perform mastication, a mechanical process that tears and grinds food into smaller pieces, vastly increasing the surface area available for chemical attack by enzymes.

Simultaneously, the three pairs of salivary glands—parotid, submandibular, and sublingual—secrete saliva. Saliva is roughly 99% water, but it contains crucial active components:

  • Salivary amylase: An enzyme that initiates the chemical breakdown of complex carbohydrates into maltose.
  • Lingual lipase: An enzyme secreted by glands on the tongue that begins lipid digestion, though it remains mostly inactive until reaching the acidic environment of the stomach.
  • Mucus and electrolytes: Lubricate the chewed food mass, transforming it into a slippery, cohesive bolus that is safe and easy to swallow.

Swallowing and Esophageal Transit

Swallowing, or deglutition, is a coordinated reflex moving the bolus from the mouth through the pharynx and into the esophagus. It begins voluntarily as the tongue pushes the bolus backward, but once it hits the pharynx, swallowing becomes an involuntary reflex controlled by the swallowing center in the brainstem.

During this transition, the epiglottis folds downward to cover the larynx, preventing food from entering the trachea and lungs. The upper esophageal sphincter relaxes to let the bolus pass, then snaps shut to prevent backflow.

Once inside the esophagus, gravity and rhythmic waves of peristalsis propel the bolus downward toward the stomach. At the terminal end, the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxes briefly to allow entry into the stomach. A weak or persistently relaxed LES allows acidic gastric contents to splash upward, resulting in heartburn or gastroesophageal reflux disease.

Gastric Digestion and Secretions

The stomach acts as a muscular storage tank and mixing chamber. Its extra third layer of oblique smooth muscle allows it to churn, crush, and mix food vigorously with gastric juices, turning a solid bolus into a creamy, acidic paste called chyme.

Gastric glands located in the mucosal lining contain specialized cells responsible for powerful chemical secretions:

  • Mucous cells: Produce a thick, alkaline mucus coat that protects the stomach lining from self-digestion by acid and enzymes.
  • Parietal cells: Secrete hydrochloric acid (HCl), which creates a highly acidic environment (pH 1.5–3.5) to kill microbes and denature proteins, and produce intrinsic factor, which is essential for vitamin B12 absorption in the small intestine.
  • Chief cells: Secrete pepsinogen, an inactive enzyme precursor that is converted into active pepsin upon contact with HCl to begin protein digestion.
  • G cells: Enteroendocrine cells that secrete the hormone gastrin into the bloodstream to stimulate acid secretion and gastric motility.

Small Intestine: Enzymatic Digestion and Nutrient Absorption

The small intestine is the primary site for chemical digestion and nutrient absorption. It is divided into three sections: the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. The internal surface area is massively amplified—by circular folds, finger-like villi, and microscopic microvilli (the brush border)—creating a vast absorptive sheet.

Chyme entering the duodenum meets secretions from the pancreas and liver. Brush border enzymes anchored to the microvilli complete the breakdown of small peptides and double sugars:

  • Carbohydrates: Broken down into monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) and absorbed into intestinal capillaries.
  • Proteins: Broken down into single amino acids and small peptides, then transported into capillaries.
  • Fats: Emulsified by bile salts and digested by pancreatic lipase into fatty acids and monoglycerides, which are packaged into micelles, absorbed across the cell membrane, and reassembled into chylomicrons entering the lymphatic lacteals.

Accessory Organs: Liver, Gallbladder, and Pancreas

While food never passes directly through the accessory organs, their chemical contributions are critical to normal digestion.

  • Liver: The body's metabolic powerhouse, which continuously produces bile, a greenish-yellow fluid containing bile salts that emulsify large fat globules into tiny droplets, making them accessible to lipases.
  • Gallbladder: A small muscular sac nestled beneath the liver that stores, concentrates, and releases bile into the duodenum via the common bile duct in response to fatty meals and hormonal signals.
  • Pancreas: A dual-purpose gland with an exocrine function that produces powerful digestive enzymes—including pancreatic amylase, lipases, and proteolytic enzymes like trypsinogen—alongside bicarbonate ions to neutralize harsh stomach acid as chyme enters the small intestine.

Large Intestine: Water Absorption and Waste Formation

What remains after nutrient extraction moves through the ileocecal valve into the large intestine, comprising the cecum, colon, rectum, and anal canal. The primary tasks here are reclaiming remaining water and electrolytes, and compacting indigestible residue into solid feces.

The large intestine features distinctive pocket-like sacs called haustra, formed by muscle bands running its length. Slow, rhythmic contractions called haustral churning move contents leisurely along.

Trillions of resident bacteria known as the gut microbiome reside here. These beneficial microbes ferment remaining carbohydrates, synthesize valuable vitamins like vitamin K and biotin, protect against harmful pathogens, and train the local immune system. Once water is fully extracted, feces are stored in the rectum until defecation is triggered by neural stretch reflexes.

Regulation of Digestion: Nervous and Hormonal Control

Digestion is tightly coordinated by the nervous system and enteric hormones to ensure juices and motility match the type and timing of food intake.

The digestive tract has its own dedicated nervous network called the enteric nervous system (ENS), often nicknamed the 'second brain,' which controls local motility and secretion independently. The autonomic nervous system modulates this: parasympathetic stimulation (via the vagus nerve) ramps up digestive activity, while sympathetic stimulation shuts it down during stress or fight-or-flight responses.

Hormones released by the gut wall fine-tune systemic responses:

  • Gastrin: Stimulates gastric acid secretion and stomach motility.
  • Secretin: Stimulates the pancreas to release bicarbonate-rich fluid when acidic chyme reaches the duodenum.
  • Cholecystokinin (CCK): Stimulates the gallbladder to contract and release bile, and triggers the pancreas to release digestive enzymes in response to dietary fats and proteins.
