Metabolism

Introduction to Metabolism

Metabolism refers to all the chemical reactions that happen inside the body to keep us alive. These reactions are grouped into two main pathways: breaking things down to release energy, and building things up using energy. Every single cell in the human body relies on these continuous processes to maintain life, repair tissues, and respond to the environment. When nurses assess a patient, understanding these foundational cellular activities helps explain why vital signs change during illness, starvation, or stress.

Catabolism and Anabolism

Metabolic pathways are neatly divided into two opposing forces that balance each other out:

  • Catabolism: The breakdown phase. Large molecules like proteins, fats, and glycogen are split into smaller units. This process releases energy, trapping it as ATP (adenosine triphosphate) for the body to use.
  • Anabolism: The building phase. Small building blocks like amino acids and simple sugars are joined together to create complex tissues, hormones, and structural proteins. This phase requires an input of energy.

Think of catabolism as demolition that provides raw materials and power, while anabolism is construction using those supplies.

Role of Enzymes and Coenzymes

Chemical reactions in the body would happen much too slowly to support life without the help of catalysts. Enzymes are specialized proteins that speed up these biochemical reactions without being consumed in the process. Each enzyme has a specific shape designed to fit a specific reactant, known as a substrate. Coenzymes, which are often derived from vitamins, assist enzymes by carrying chemical groups or electrons from one reaction to another. Without adequate vitamin intake, coenzyme function drops, and overall metabolism slows down significantly.

Carbohydrate Metabolism

Carbohydrates are the body's preferred and most accessible source of energy. When a patient eats carbohydrates, the digestive system breaks them down into glucose, which enters the bloodstream. Cells take up glucose and process it through glycolysis, the Krebs cycle, and the electron transport chain to generate ATP. If blood glucose levels rise too high after a meal, the pancreas releases insulin to drive glucose into cells and into the liver for storage as glycogen. When fasting, the body breaks down stored glycogen back into glucose through glycogenolysis to keep blood sugar stable.

Lipid Metabolism

Lipids provide a dense and efficient reservoir of stored energy, yielding more than twice the calories per gram compared to carbohydrates or proteins. Dietary fats are broken down into fatty acids and glycerol, absorbed in the intestines, and transported through the bloodstream. When carbohydrate stores run low, the body taps into stored fats through lipolysis. In situations where glucose is completely absent—such as prolonged starvation or uncontrolled diabetes—the liver converts excess fatty acids into ketone bodies, which can serve as alternative fuel for the brain and heart but can also lower blood pH if they accumulate too much.

Protein Metabolism

Proteins are rarely used as a primary energy source because their main job is building structural tissues, enzymes, and antibodies. Dietary proteins are digested into individual amino acids, which travel to the liver and cells to build new human proteins. If a patient experiences severe calorie restriction or starvation, the body breaks down its own muscle tissue to harvest these amino acids for energy. During this breakdown, the amino group must be removed in a process called deamination, turning into toxic ammonia which the liver quickly converts into urea for the kidneys to excrete in urine.

Basal Metabolic Rate and Energy Balance

The Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) represents the minimum number of calories a person's body needs to maintain basic life functions—such as breathing, heartbeat, and brain activity—while resting in a warm room. BMR accounts for the largest chunk of daily energy expenditure. Total energy expenditure also includes physical activity and the energy used to digest food. Energy balance happens when calorie intake equals calorie output. If a patient takes in more calories than they burn, the extra energy is stored as fat. If intake falls short, the body burns stored fat and muscle to survive.

Common Metabolic Disorders and Nursing Implications

Metabolic disorders occur when the chemical pathways that process nutrients fail due to genetic defects, organ malfunction, or hormonal imbalances. Conditions like diabetes mellitus impair carbohydrate processing, while inherited disorders like phenylketonuria affect amino acid breakdown. Nurses caring for patients with metabolic conditions must monitor vital lab values, track strict intake and output, and educate patients on dietary restrictions. Recognizing early signs of metabolic crises—such as extreme fatigue, fruity breath odor, or sudden weight changes—can prevent life-threatening complications.
