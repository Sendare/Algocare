Role of the Nurse in Promoting Nutrition for Inpatients

Introduction to Inpatient Nutritional Care

Malnutrition among hospitalised patients remains a silent barrier to recovery, prolonging lengths of stay and increasing infection rates. When an individual is admitted to a ward, their metabolic demands often shift dramatically due to trauma, surgery, or underlying chronic disease. Meeting these nutritional needs is not an isolated task handled solely by the catering department; rather, it is a core clinical responsibility that dictates the pace of tissue healing and immune competence. Every clinical encounter provides an opportunity to evaluate a patient's nutritional baseline. In busy Nigerian tertiary health institutions, where family members often supplement hospital meals, coordinating these food sources safely is just as vital as managing prescribed hospital diets. The nurse serves as the primary bridge between the clinical prescription of nutrients and the actual biological intake of the patient.

Nutritional Screening and Assessment on Admission

Identifying patients at nutritional risk must happen within the first twenty-four hours of hospital admission. Screening tools like the Malnutritional Universal Screening Tool (MUST) or Subjective Global Assessment (SGA) guide this initial evaluation. - **Weight history**: Documenting recent unintentional weight loss over the past three to six months. - **Body Mass Index (BMI)**: Calculating height against weight, keeping in mind that fluid retention can falsely elevate readings. - **Dietary intake adequacy**: Evaluating whether the patient has experienced a recent drop in food consumption due to illness symptoms. - **Disease severity**: Factoring in how the underlying pathology increases metabolic stress and nutritional depletion. An abnormal finding on any of these parameters triggers a comprehensive nutritional assessment and alerts the multidisciplinary team to intervene early.

Collaborating with Dietitians and the Healthcare Team

Optimal nutritional support requires seamless communication between nurses, dietitians, physicians, and pharmacists. While the physician prescribes medical therapy and the dietitian calculates exact macro- and micronutrient prescriptions, the nurse provides continuous, ground-level feedback regarding tolerance, appetite fluctuations, and gastrointestinal symptoms. When a patient exhibits persistent nausea or refuses specific therapeutic diets, the nurse initiates a referral or direct discussion with the clinical dietitian. In settings where specific commercial supplements may be unavailable or culturally unfamiliar, nurses and dietitians collaborate to find acceptable local food alternatives that meet the targeted nutritional goals without violating medical restrictions.

Assisting with Feeding and Mealtime Management

Mealtime should be treated as an active clinical intervention rather than a routine housekeeping chore. Environmental preparation sets the tone: clearing bedside tables of emesis basins and urinals, ensuring dentures or glasses are within reach, and positioning the patient upright at a minimum of 45 degrees to prevent aspiration. For patients with cognitive impairments, physical weakness, or visual deficits, active feeding assistance is necessary. Feed patients slowly, offering small, manageable bites and allowing adequate time for chewing and swallowing. Never rush a vulnerable patient, as hurried feeding directly increases the risk of choking and subsequent aspiration pneumonia.

Monitoring Dietary Intake and Hydration Status

Accurate documentation of what a patient consumes transforms guesswork into actionable clinical data. Relying on vague estimates like 'ate some' or 'half the tray' introduces dangerous errors. Instead, nurses utilize visual percentage scales or calorie-count sheets to record meal fractions consumed. Hydration monitoring is equally critical. Intake and output (I&O) charts must capture all oral fluids, intravenous infusions, and liquid medications against urine output, emesis, and drainage losses. Daily weights remain the most sensitive indicator of acute fluid shifts, capturing subtle signs of dehydration or fluid overload long before visible edema appears.

Patient and Family Education on Nutrition

Nutritional education must begin early in the hospital stay and be tailored to the patient's literacy level, cultural background, and home resources. Providing a rigid diet sheet that mandates foods unavailable or unaffordable in local markets guarantees non-adherence after discharge. Incorporate family members or primary caregivers into teaching sessions, especially for patients returning home with physical limitations or specialized feeding tubes. Demonstrate preparation techniques, explain food safety principles, and provide clear written instructions on warning signs—such as persistent vomiting, tube displacement, or unexpected weight loss—that require immediate notification of the healthcare provider.

Evaluating Outcomes and Documenting Nutritional Care

The final stage of inpatient nutritional care involves evaluating the effectiveness of interventions against established baseline goals. Are wound edges closing? Is energy improving? Is weight stabilizing or recovering toward pre-illness levels? Comprehensive documentation captures these trajectory changes in the medical record. Clear notes ensure continuity of care across shift changes, providing legal evidence of diligent nursing oversight and guiding the ongoing refinement of the patient's nutritional care plan until safe discharge is achieved.
