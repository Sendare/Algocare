Communication Skills

Introduction to Communication in Nursing

Communication forms the bedrock of safe, effective nursing practice. Every clinical interaction relies on exchanging information, assessing emotional states, and building trust. Poor communication leads to medication errors, missed deterioration, and patient distress. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where nurse-to-patient ratios are frequently high, concise yet meaningful exchanges prevent misunderstandings and ensure continuity of care during shift handovers.

The Communication Process and Key Elements

The communication cycle involves a sender who encodes a message, a channel through which it travels, and a receiver who decodes it. Feedback closes the loop, confirming mutual understanding. Nurses must monitor for noise—anything that distorts the message, such as environmental sounds, pain, or cultural differences. Without active feedback, a verbal order or care plan instruction is easily misinterpreted.

Verbal and Non-Verbal Communication Techniques

Spoken words convey factual details, but non-verbal cues reveal true emotional states. Body posture, facial expressions, eye contact, and touch speak volumes. A nurse standing with crossed arms while telling a patient 'I have time to talk' creates a mixed message. Cultural norms dictate acceptable eye contact and personal space; ignoring these norms damages rapport immediately.

Therapeutic Communication vs. Social Communication

Social interactions focus on mutual pleasure and shared personal needs. Therapeutic communication, by contrast, is goal-directed, professional, and centered entirely on the patient's well-being. Nurses use specific techniques like open-ended questioning, reflection, and active listening to explore health concerns. Unhealthy boundary crossing occurs when therapeutic dialogue shifts into personal social chatter.

Barriers to Effective Communication in Healthcare

Numerous obstacles disrupt clinical conversations. Sensory deficits, language differences, acute pain, and emotional distress block clear message transmission. Environmental factors like bustling, noisy wards or lack of privacy also impede dialogue. Additionally, nurses must recognize personal biases and stereotypes that unconsciously color their listening and judgment.

Strategies for Overcoming Communication Barriers

- **Active Listening**: Give full attention without interrupting. - **Clarification**: Ask the patient to restate instructions in their own words (teach-back method). - **Language Support**: Utilize qualified interpreters or translated educational materials rather than relying on family members for complex medical translations. - **Environmental Adjustment**: Draw curtains or move to a quieter space to ensure confidentiality and reduce auditory distractions.

Special Considerations in Patient Communication

Communicating with vulnerable populations requires tailored approaches. Pediatric patients need age-appropriate language and play techniques to reduce fear. Older adults with cognitive impairment or sensory loss benefit from slow speech, adequate lighting for lip-reading, and patience. When caring for patients from diverse ethnic backgrounds in Nigeria, cultural humility ensures care is delivered with respect and without ethnocentric assumptions.
