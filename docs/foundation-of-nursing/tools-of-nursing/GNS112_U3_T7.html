Problem Solving Techniques

Introduction to Problem Solving in Nursing

Clinical practice rarely follows a straight line. Every shift brings unexpected challenges, from a sudden drop in a patient's blood pressure to a missing supply of intravenous infusion sets. Problem solving in nursing is the deliberate, cognitive process used to identify these clinical gaps, analyze underlying causes, and implement safe, effective interventions. Unlike everyday troubleshooting, clinical problem-solving carries direct consequences for human life and health.

At its core, this skill relies on bridging the gap between an undesirable current state and a desired patient outcome. When a patient admitted to a ward in Lagos develops acute restlessness, the nurse cannot simply guess the cause. They must recognize the discrepancy between normal oxygenation and the current distress, gather focused assessment data, and formulate a targeted action plan. Developing this capability separates a novice practitioner from an experienced clinician.

The Nursing Process as a Problem-Solving Framework

The nursing process is the foundational problem-solving model taught across Nigerian nursing institutions. It provides a systematic, cyclical approach to patient care, ensuring that interventions are neither random nor impulsive.

  • Assessment: Systematic collection, validation, and communication of patient data. Missing a subtle cue here distorts every subsequent step.
  • Diagnosis: Clinical judgment concerning human responses to health conditions or life processes. This names the actual or potential problem.
  • Planning: Setting measurable, patient-centered goals and determining specific nursing interventions to achieve them.
  • Implementation: Executing the planned interventions with clinical competence and safety.
  • Evaluation: Determining whether the patient goals were met, which loops back to reassessment if the problem persists.

Critical Thinking and Analytical Skills

A structured framework alone cannot save a patient if the clinician lacks critical thinking. This involves disciplined, self-directed mental habits that analyze information rather than accepting surface-level appearances.

Analytical skills require separating relevant clinical data from distracting noise. For instance, a patient post-appendectomy may report generalized abdominal discomfort. An uncritical nurse might assume standard surgical pain, while a critical thinker evaluates vital signs, checks surgical site dressings for hidden hemorrhage, assesses bowel sounds, and considers the timeline of analgesia administration. In busy clinical settings with high patient-to-nurse ratios, sharpening this mental discipline prevents subtle deteriorations from turning into full cardiac or respiratory arrests.

Structured Models and Root Cause Analysis

When recurring problems disrupt ward efficiency or patient safety—such as repeated medication administration errors or frequent delay in wound dressing supplies—individual clinical judgment is insufficient. Root Cause Analysis (RCA) and other structured quality improvement tools provide a systematic lens.

RCA asks "Why?" iteratively to trace a failure back to its fundamental systemic source rather than simply blaming the nearest person. In busy Nigerian hospital wards, a medication error often stems from illegible handwritten drug charts, verbal telephone orders during chaos, or interrupted preparation environments, rather than sheer carelessness by a single nurse. Fixing the system prevents recurrence.

Barriers to Effective Problem Solving in Healthcare

Even well-trained nurses struggle to solve problems when clinical environments are hostile or overwhelming. Recognizing these barriers is the first step toward overcoming them.

  • Fatigue and Burnout: Chronic staff shortages force double shifts and cognitive exhaustion, dulling analytical reflexes.
  • Communication Breakdowns: Hierarchical friction between multidisciplinary team members delays critical information sharing.
  • Confirmation Bias: Clinging to an initial impression of a patient and ignoring contradictory assessment data.
  • Resource Scarcity: Lack of basic diagnostic tools or medications forces nurses into frustrating makeshift solutions.

Reflective Practice and Continuous Improvement

Problem-solving capacity is not static; it grows through reflective practice. Reflection involves deliberately looking back at a clinical encounter to examine what went well, what failed, and why.

Gibbs' Reflective Cycle and similar frameworks guide nurses from raw description of an event through emotional appraisal, evaluation, analysis, and ultimate action planning. When a nurse reflects on a chaotic resuscitation attempt that lacked clear role delegation, they arrive for the next shift ready to advocate for structured role assignments during emergencies. This transforms clinical experience into lifelong professional wisdom.
