Selected Theories and Models Relevant to Nursing Practice

Introduction to Nursing Theories and Models

Nursing theories and models form the foundational framework of professional nursing practice, guiding how nurses think, act, and make clinical decisions. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding these concepts is crucial because they bridge the gap between abstract nursing philosophy and everyday bedside care. A nursing theory is a creative and rigorous structuring of ideas that project a tentative, purposeful, and systematic view of phenomena. Meanwhile, a nursing model provides a schematic representation or blueprint of these theories, offering practical ways to implement nursing care in diverse healthcare settings. By utilizing theories and models, student nurses learn to move beyond merely performing tasks to delivering individualized, holistic, and evidence-based patient care. These frameworks help define the unique scope of nursing, distinguishing it from other healthcare disciplines such as medicine and physiotherapy. Furthermore, studying historical and contemporary nursing theories fosters critical thinking, enhances clinical judgment, and empowers nurses to advocate effectively for patients in Nigerian hospitals and community health centers. Ultimately, these conceptual structures elevate nursing from a mere occupation to a recognized, respected scientific profession.

Florence Nightingale: Environmental Theory

Florence Nightingale's Environmental Theory, established in the 19th century, is considered the first formal nursing theory. Nightingale believed that nursing could be taught and that the primary focus of nursing practice should be on the patient's environment rather than solely on the disease process. Her theory emphasizes that a healthy environment is essential for healing and that nurses can manipulate environmental factors to enhance patient recovery. Key components of this environmental model include pure fresh air, pure water, efficient drainage, cleanliness, and light—especially direct sunlight. In the context of Nigerian healthcare settings, Nightingale's principles remain remarkably relevant. Environmental sanitation, proper waste management, ventilation in overcrowded hospital wards, and infection control are direct applications of her theory. When a nurse ensures that a patient's room is well-ventilated, free from foul odors, clean, and quiet, they are practicing Nightingale's environmental philosophy. She viewed the nurse's role as putting the patient in the best possible condition for nature to act upon them. Thus, managing the physical environment helps reduce physiological and psychological stress, promoting faster healing and preventing hospital-acquired complications.

Virginia Henderson: Definition of Nursing and the 14 Basic Needs

Virginia Henderson conceptualized nursing as assisting individuals, sick or well, in the performance of those activities contributing to health or its recovery (or to a peaceful death) that they would perform unaided if they had the necessary strength, will, or knowledge. Her definition highlights the independent function of nursing. Henderson identified 14 basic human needs that form the core of her nursing model, encompassing physiological, psychological, sociological, and spiritual dimensions. These needs include breathing normally, eating and drinking adequately, eliminating bodily wastes, moving and maintaining desirable postures, sleeping and resting, selecting suitable clothing, maintaining normal body temperature, keeping the body clean and well-groomed, avoiding environmental dangers, communicating with others, worshiping according to one's faith, working with a sense of accomplishment, playing or participating in recreation, and learning or discovering. For nursing students in Nigeria, Henderson's model provides a comprehensive guide for holistic patient assessment and care planning. When patients are admitted to a ward, the nurse assesses which of these 14 needs are compromised and designs nursing interventions to assist the patient. The ultimate goal is to help the patient regain independence as quickly as possible, empowering them to manage their own care.

Dorothea Orem: Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory

Dorothea Orem's Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory focuses on each individual's ability to perform self-care, defined as the practice of activities that individuals initiate and perform on their own behalf to maintain life, health, and well-being. Orem's theory is composed of three interrelated theories: the Theory of Self-Care, the Theory of Self-Care Deficit, and the Theory of Nursing Systems. The Theory of Self-Care emphasizes learned behaviors that meet human needs. The Self-Care Deficit theory occurs when there is a discrepancy between the demand for self-care and the agency of the individual, meaning the patient cannot meet their own needs and requires nursing intervention. To address this deficit, Orem outlines three nursing systems: Wholly Compensatory (the nurse accomplishes all care for the patient), Partly Compensatory (both nurse and patient perform care measures), and Supportive-Educative (the patient can perform or learn to perform self-care but needs assistance with decision-making or knowledge acquisition). In Nigerian clinical practice, Orem's model is especially useful in managing patients with chronic illnesses such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension. Nurses assess the patient's self-care capabilities, educate them on lifestyle modifications, and provide support, ensuring patients transition successfully from hospital care to self-management at home.

Application of Nursing Models in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Applying nursing models in Nigerian clinical practice presents both unique opportunities and practical challenges. Nigerian healthcare settings range from sophisticated tertiary teaching hospitals in urban centers like Lagos and Ibadan to primary health care centers in rural communities. Utilizing theories like those of Nightingale, Henderson, and Orem allows Nigerian nurses to deliver culturally congruent, holistic, and patient-centered care despite systemic resource constraints. For instance, in a rural primary health clinic, a community health nurse can apply Orem's theory by teaching mothers how to manage infant dehydration through oral rehydration therapy, fostering community self-reliance. Similarly, Henderson's 14 basic needs are routinely integrated into nursing care plans across Nigerian wards, ensuring that patients receive comprehensive physical hygiene, nutritional support, and psychological reassurance. However, application can be hindered by heavy workloads, high patient-to-nurse ratios, and resource shortages. Despite these hurdles, grounding clinical practice in established nursing models elevates the standard of care, promotes critical thinking among nursing students during clinical postings, and establishes professional autonomy. By consciously linking theory to bedside practice, Nigerian nurses demonstrate the scientific basis of their profession, ultimately improving patient outcomes and strengthening the national healthcare system.
