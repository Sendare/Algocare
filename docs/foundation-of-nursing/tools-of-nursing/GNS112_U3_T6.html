Selected Theories and Models Relevant to Nursing Practice

Introduction to Nursing Theories and Models

Nursing theories provide a structured framework that guides clinical practice, education, and research. Without these conceptual structures, nursing would merely be a collection of disconnected tasks rather than a distinct profession with a scientific base. A nursing theory explains, describes, or predicts nursing actions by defining key relationships between core concepts such as the patient, health, environment, and nursing itself. A nursing model offers a visual or symbolic representation of these ideas, helping nurses organize patient data and choose appropriate interventions. When students understand these foundational models, they move beyond simply following hospital routines to truly grasping why specific patient care decisions matter.

Florence Nightingale: Environmental Theory

Florence Nightingale proposed that the primary focus of nursing is altering the patient's environment to restore health. Her work during the Crimean War proved that sanitation, fresh air, pure water, efficient drainage, and light directly impacted recovery rates. In her view, nature heals the patient, and the nurse's job is simply to put the patient in the best possible condition for nature to act. While modern medical science relies heavily on pharmacology, Nightingale's core belief remains vital: a clean, quiet, well-ventilated ward prevents complications and speeds up healing.

Virginia Henderson: Definition of Nursing and the 14 Basic Needs

Virginia Henderson viewed the unique function of the nurse as assisting the individual, sick or well, in the performance of those activities contributing to health or its recovery that they would perform unaided if they had the necessary strength, will, or knowledge. Her framework breaks nursing down into 14 basic human needs:

  1. Breathe normally: Maintaining adequate oxygenation.
  2. Eat and drink adequately: Consuming nourishing food and fluids.
  3. Eliminate body wastes: Managing bowel and bladder functions.
  4. Move and maintain desirable postures: Changing positions and walking.
  5. Sleep and rest: Getting sufficient daily recovery time.
  6. Select suitable clothing: Dressing and undressing appropriately.
  7. Maintain body temperature: Keeping warmth within normal limits.
  8. Keep hygiene clean and well-groomed: Protecting the integumentary system.
  9. Avoid environmental dangers: Preventing accidental injury.
  10. Communicate with others: Expressing emotions, needs, and fears.
  11. Worship according to faith: Honoring spiritual beliefs.
  12. Work with a sense of accomplishment: Engaging in productive activity.
  13. Play or participate in recreation: Enjoying leisure pursuits.
  14. Learn, discover, or satisfy curiosity: Gaining health knowledge.

When a patient cannot meet one of these needs independently, the nurse steps in as a substitute, helper, or partner.

Dorothea Orem: Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory

Dorothea Orem's theory centers on the premise that all people need self-care to maintain life, health, and well-being. A self-care deficit occurs when a patient's demand for self-care is greater than their available capacity to perform it. Orem categorizes nursing systems into three types:

  • Fully compensatory: The nurse performs all the daily self-care activities for the patient.
  • Partially compensatory: Both the nurse and the patient share responsibility for meeting care needs.
  • Supportive-educative: The patient can perform self-care but needs guidance, support, and teaching from the nurse.

This model helps nurses evaluate how much independence a patient can safely maintain.

Application of Nursing Models in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Applying Western-born nursing theories in Nigerian healthcare settings requires cultural adaptation and practical resource awareness. Facilities often face challenges like power outages, staff shortages, and overcrowded wards. Yet, frameworks like Nightingale's and Henderson's remain remarkably practical. When running water is scarce, nurses innovate by organizing bucket systems for handwashing and wound cleaning, directly practicing environmental health control. Utilizing Henderson's 14 needs helps student nurses in busy public hospitals systematically prioritize patient assessments during shift handovers. Adapting these models ensures that patient-centered, holistic care survives even under constrained infrastructural conditions.
