Selected Theories and Models Relevant to Nursing Practice

Introduction to Nursing Theories and Models

Nursing theory provides a creative and rigorous structuring of professional practice, helping clinicians articulate what they do and why they do it. A nursing theory is a set of concepts, definitions, and assumptions that project a systematic view of phenomena. By defining specific boundaries, these frameworks distinguish nursing as a unique academic discipline separate from medicine and other allied health professions. At the core of almost all nursing models lie four central concepts known as the **metaparadigm**: person, environment, health, and nursing. The 'person' refers to the recipient of care, including individuals, families, and communities. 'Environment' encompasses all internal and external conditions affecting the patient. 'Health' represents the patient's state of well-being, which exists along a continuous spectrum. Finally, 'nursing' defines the attributes, actions, and goals of the professional providing care. Understanding how different theorists view these four pillars clarifies the philosophical basis behind bedside interventions.

Florence Nightingale: Environmental Theory

Published in 1859, Florence Nightingale's **Environmental Theory** shifted the focus of healing from the disease entity to the patient's surroundings. Nightingale observed that dirty conditions, foul air, and lack of light exacerbated suffering and mortality in military hospitals. Her framework posits that nursing is the act of utilizing the patient's environment to assist in their recovery. The theory outlines five essential components of a healthy physical environment: - **Pure air**: Maintaining adequate ventilation and freedom from noxious gases without chilling the patient. - **Pure water**: Ensuring clean hydration and washing facilities. - **Efficient drainage**: Preventing the accumulation of sewage and organic waste. - **Cleanliness**: Keeping skin, bed linens, and wards free of dust and filth. - **Light**: Providing direct sunlight, which Nightingale considered vital for psychological and physical well-being. Beyond the physical space, Nightingale emphasized quiet surroundings and proper nutritional management, noting that unnecessary noise and erratic meal delivery harm healing processes.

Virginia Henderson: Definition of Nursing and the 14 Basic Needs

Virginia Henderson conceptualized the unique function of the nurse as assisting the individual, sick or well, in the performance of those activities contributing to health or its recovery that they would perform unaided if they had the necessary strength, will, or knowledge. She viewed the patient as a composite whole with **14 fundamental human needs**, framing nursing assistance as a bridge toward ultimate independence. | Category | Focus Area | Example Need | |---|---|---| | Biological | Physiological survival | Breathe normally, eat and drink adequately | | Psychological | Emotional and mental balance | Sleep and rest, maintain body temperature | | Sociological | Social interaction and purpose | Communicate with others, work in a sense of accomplishment | When a patient exhibits a deficit in meeting any of these fourteen requirements—perhaps due to fatigue, lack of cognitive awareness, or physical immobility—the nurse steps in to supplement their capacity. The ultimate goal remains returning the patient to a state of self-sufficiency as rapidly as possible.

Dorothea Orem: Self-Care Deficit Nursing Theory

Dorothea Orem's theory centers on the premise that all people require self-care—the practice of activities that individuals initiate and perform on their own behalf to maintain life, health, and well-being. A **self-care deficit** occurs when there is an inequality between the demand for self-care and the agency of the individual to meet that demand. Nursing is required precisely when therapeutic self-care demand exceeds self-care agency. Orem structures her model around three interrelated theories: 1. **Theory of Self-Care**: Explains why and how people care for themselves. 2. **Theory of Self-Care Deficit**: Explains why a person can benefit from nursing intervention. 3. **Theory of Nursing Systems**: Describes how the nurse meets the self-care needs of the patient through three specific systems: - **Wholly compensatory**: The nurse accomplishes all self-care for the patient. - **Partially compensatory**: Both nurse and patient perform measures in meeting care demands. - **Supportive-educative**: The patient can perform or can learn to perform required self-care measures, but needs assistance with decision-making or behavior control.

Application of Nursing Models in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Integrating international nursing models into the Nigerian healthcare delivery system requires contextual adaptation due to prevailing socioeconomic realities, cultural beliefs, and resource constraints. While Western theories emphasize individual autonomy, Nigerian nursing practice frequently operates within a communal framework where extended family members play significant roles in patient care decisions and daily physical support. In busy federal and state teaching hospitals, as well as rural primary health care centers, nurses routinely utilize **Orem's Self-Care Deficit Theory** during antenatal health talks and chronic disease clinics, educating mothers and diabetic patients on lifestyle modifications. Similarly, **Henderson's 14 Basic Needs** form the bedrock of the nursing care plan taught in schools of nursing across Nigeria, guiding students during clinical rotations in medical-surgical wards. However, infrastructural challenges such as erratic electricity, water shortages, and staff shortages can complicate the strict execution of Nightingale's Environmental Theory. Nigerian nurses frequently display remarkable resilience by adapting these theoretical frameworks to local realities, ensuring holistic, culturally competent care despite systemic hurdles.
