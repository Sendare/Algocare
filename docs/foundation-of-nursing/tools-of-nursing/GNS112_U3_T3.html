Interviewing and Counselling

Introduction to Interviewing and Counselling in Nursing

Interviewing and counselling are foundational tools every nurse uses to deliver holistic care. While an interview is primarily structured to gather health data, assess symptoms, and understand patient needs, counselling offers guidance, emotional support, and helps patients cope with health challenges. Both processes rely heavily on deliberate, professional communication rather than casual conversation. Nurses use these skills during admission assessments, bedside handovers, and discharge planning. Mastering these techniques allows you to uncover hidden fears, clarify misconceptions, and empower patients to take an active role in their own healing process.

Core Communication Skills and Therapeutic Use of Self

Therapeutic communication requires conscious control of your verbal and non-verbal behaviors. The therapeutic use of self means purposefully using your personality, insights, and perceptions to establish relatedness and structure nursing interventions.

  • Active listening: Fully concentrating on what the patient says rather than just waiting for your turn to speak.
  • Non-verbal cues: Maintaining open posture, appropriate eye contact, and a calm facial expression.
  • Empathy: Understanding the patient's perspective without losing your professional objectivity.
  • Silence: Allowing strategic pauses for the patient to gather their thoughts and feel safe.

The Nursing Interview Process: Phases and Techniques

A successful patient interview follows a structured progression divided into three distinct phases:

  1. Orientation phase: Introduce yourself, explain the purpose of the interview, build initial rapport, and set a welcoming tone.
  2. Working phase: Gather the health history, ask relevant assessment questions, and actively listen to the patient's responses.
  3. Termination phase: Summarize key findings, ask if the patient has any final questions, and clearly explain the next steps in their care.

Questioning Techniques: Open vs. Closed Questions

The type of questions you ask dictates the quality and quantity of information you receive. Balancing open-ended and closed-ended questions ensures a thorough and efficient assessment.

Question TypePurposeExample
Open-endedEncourages narrative, exploration, and emotional expression"Can you tell me more about your pain?"
Closed-endedGathers specific, factual data quickly"Do you have chest pain right now?"

A common mistake among student nurses is relying too heavily on closed questions, which turns the interview into a rigid interrogation and misses vital patient context.

Establishing and Maintaining a Therapeutic Relationship

A therapeutic relationship differs from a social friendship because it is entirely focused on the patient's health needs, recovery, and well-being. It develops in stages, moving from initial distrust to mutual respect and collaboration. Maintaining professional boundaries is essential to prevent burnout and role confusion. Nurses must avoid crossing into over-involvement while remaining warm, approachable, and genuinely caring. Trust is fragile; it is built slowly through consistent reliability, confidentiality, and competence, but it can be destroyed instantly by broken promises or dismissive attitudes.

Overcoming Barriers to Effective Communication

Many obstacles can disrupt clear communication between a nurse and patient. Recognizing these barriers helps you adjust your approach and maintain connection.

  • Environmental noise: Busy wards, loud alarms, and lack of privacy hinder open dialogue.
  • Language differences: Inability to speak the same language requires professional medical interpretation services.
  • Sensory impairments: Hearing or visual loss calls for assistive devices, adequate lighting, and facing the patient directly.
  • Emotional distress: High anxiety, fear, or pain can drastically reduce a patient's ability to process verbal information.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Counselling

When conducting interviews and counselling sessions, nurses must strictly adhere to professional ethics and legal standards. Confidentiality is paramount; patients must know that their personal health information will be protected and only shared with the direct care team. The only exception to confidentiality is when a patient poses an immediate danger to themselves or others, or in cases of mandatory abuse reporting. Nurses must also respect patient autonomy, ensuring that any advice or counselling provided does not coerce the patient into making health decisions against their personal values.
