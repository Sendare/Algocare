Interviewing and Counselling

Foundations of Interviewing and Counselling in Nursing

Nursing practice extends far beyond physical tasks like wound dressing or medication administration. At its core, nursing relies heavily on human connection facilitated through structured conversation. Interviewing and counselling are distinct yet complementary clinical tools. Interviewing is primarily an information-gathering dialogue where the nurse systematically elicits health histories, symptom patterns, and psychosocial backgrounds. Counselling, conversely, is a supportive process designed to help patients explore emotional challenges, cope with illness, and make informed health-related decisions. Both processes require deliberate intent, active presence, and a deep respect for human dignity, particularly within diverse Nigerian healthcare environments where cultural nuances heavily influence health perceptions.

Core Communication Skills and Therapeutic Use of Self

Therapeutic communication transforms an ordinary conversation into a healing intervention. The therapeutic use of self involves harnessing one's own personality, insights, and experiences deliberately to structure nursing interactions and build trust.

  • Active Listening: Concentrating fully on verbal and non-verbal cues without premature interruption or formulating mental rebuttals.
  • Open-Ended Questioning: Encouraging narrative responses rather than brief, limiting 'yes' or 'no' answers.
  • Clarification and Reflection: Restating or asking for elaboration to validate mutual understanding of shared information.
  • Silence: Allowing deliberate pauses for the patient to process thoughts and muster emotional courage.

Phases of the Nursing Interview

A successful clinical interview follows a deliberate trajectory divided into three distinct stages. Rushing through these phases frequently yields incomplete data and alienates the patient.

  1. Orientation Phase: Introductions occur, roles are clarified, and the purpose of the interaction is established to reduce initial patient anxiety.
  2. Working Phase: The core exchange takes place. The nurse gathers the health history, explores symptoms, and identifies primary psychosocial needs using targeted techniques.
  3. Termination Phase: The interaction is gracefully summarized, key takeaways are reviewed, and plans for follow-up or subsequent care are clearly outlined.

Establishing and Maintaining the Therapeutic Relationship

The therapeutic nurse-patient relationship serves as the foundational vehicle for all clinical interventions. Unlike social friendships, this professional connection is time-limited, goal-directed, and strictly centered on the patient's well-being. Trust forms the bedrock of this bond; it is earned through consistency, reliability, confidentiality, and demonstrated clinical competence. Establishing rapport requires cultural sensitivity, particularly in multicultural settings where eye contact, posture, or gender dynamics carry specific social meanings. Maintaining professional boundaries prevents role blurring, ensuring the interaction remains exclusively focused on patient needs rather than the personal needs of the nurse.

Addressing Barriers to Effective Communication

Numerous obstacles can disrupt the flow of clinical communication, distorting data and increasing patient distress. Environmental barriers include excessive noise, lack of privacy, and uncomfortable room temperatures. Psychological barriers involve severe anxiety, fear, pain, or altered cognitive states. Cultural and language differences can easily lead to misinterpretations if the nurse relies on assumptions rather than clarification. Furthermore, non-therapeutic verbal responses by the nurse—such as giving premature advice, offering false reassurance ('Everything will be fine'), or using judgmental approval—shut down honest dialogue and alienate the patient.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Counselling

Counselling and interviewing in nursing operate within strict ethical and legal frameworks. Confidentiality is paramount; patients must feel secure that private health disclosures will not be shared indiscriminately. However, absolute confidentiality has legal limits, such as mandatory reporting requirements for child abuse, imminent harm to self or others, or certain infectious disease threats. Informed consent applies equally to psychological counselling interventions as it does to physical procedures: patients have the autonomous right to accept or decline counselling support without fearing compromised medical care. Upholding these standards safeguards patient autonomy and maintains professional integrity.
