Methods of Healthcare Waste Disposal

Introduction and Classification of Healthcare Waste

Healthcare waste includes all waste materials generated during medical diagnosis, treatment, immunization, or research involving humans and animals. Proper management is a vital duty for nurses because improper handling exposes patients, waste handlers, and the general public to serious infections and injuries.

Healthcare waste is broadly divided into two main groups:

  • General waste: Non-hazardous materials similar to domestic refuse, such as office paper, kitchen scraps, and packaging cartons. This group makes up about 75 to 80 percent of total hospital waste and requires no special handling.
  • Hazardous waste: Dangerous materials that pose infectious, chemical, radioactive, or physical risks. This includes used needles, contaminated gauze, expired drugs, and chemical disinfectants.

Color-Coding and Segregation Standards

Segregation means separating different types of waste at the exact point where they are generated. If a nurse waits until later to sort a mixed pile of trash, needle stick injuries happen. Standard international and national guidelines use specific colors to make segregation foolproof.

  • Black or Clear Bins: Reserved for general, non-hazardous municipal waste like food wrappers and office paper.
  • Yellow Bins or Bags: Used for infectious waste that requires incineration or autoclaving, such as soiled dressings, blood-soaked gauze, and anatomical parts.
  • Brown Bins: Designated for chemical and pharmaceutical waste, including expired syrups and heavy metal residues.
  • Puncture-Resistant Safety Boxes: Specifically built yellow or red containers with biohazard symbols strictly for needles, syringes, and surgical blades.

Safe Handling, Collection, and Storage

Collecting and moving waste within a healthcare facility requires strict adherence to safety protocols to prevent environmental contamination. Bags should never be packed completely full; three-quarters full is the maximum limit before they are securely tied or sealed. Overflowing bags invite tears and spills during transport.

Utility staff must use dedicated heavy-duty trolleys to move sealed waste bins from wards to the central storage area. Temporary storage rooms must be well-ventilated, secure from animals and scavengers, and clearly marked with biohazard warning signs. Waste should never be left sitting in public corridors or near food preparation zones.

Treatment Methods: Autoclaving and Chemical Disinfection

Before hazardous medical waste goes to a final dumpsite, it must be treated to destroy harmful pathogens.

An autoclave uses high-pressure steam to sterilize infectious waste. It is highly effective for laboratory cultures, surgical instruments, and soft infectious waste like cotton and bandages. Once autoclaved, the waste is rendered safe and can often be treated like general refuse.

Chemical disinfection uses liquid chemicals like sodium hypochlorite solutions to kill micro-organisms on items such as liquid waste, suction canisters, or reusable equipment. This method requires precise chemical concentrations and contact time to ensure complete sterilization.

Final Disposal: Incineration and Landfills

Once waste is treated or sorted, it reaches the final stage of disposal. Incineration is a high-temperature thermal process that burns waste down to ash. It is mandatory for anatomical waste, certain hazardous pharmaceuticals, and heavy infectious items that cannot be safely recycled or buried directly. Modern incinerators require air pollution control devices to capture toxic smoke and dioxins.

General waste and safely treated non-hazardous remnants go to sanitary landfills. A sanitary landfill is an engineered pit lined with thick plastic and clay to prevent toxic liquids, known as leachate, from seeping down into underground water tables.

Occupational Safety and Infection Control

Healthcare workers face daily risks of needle stick injuries, chemical splashes, and airborne infections during waste handling. Prevention starts with strict personal discipline. Nurses and waste handlers must always wear appropriate Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), including heavy utility gloves, aprons, and eye protection when managing dirty utility rooms.

If a needle stick injury occurs, the nurse must immediately wash the wound with soap and running water, encourage gentle bleeding, report the incident to the occupational health unit, and begin post-exposure evaluation protocols without delay.

Regulatory Compliance and Challenges in Nigeria

In Nigeria, healthcare waste management is guided by agencies such as the Federal Ministry of Environment and the National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency (NESREA), alongside state-level environmental protection bodies. These agencies set the rules for safe handling, transport permits, and approved disposal sites.

Despite these guidelines, Nigerian hospitals often face serious challenges, including erratic power supply that disrupts incinerators, lack of functional autoclaves, and poor funding for protective gear. Nurses must advocate for safe practices and use available local protocols to minimize environmental pollution and protect community health.
