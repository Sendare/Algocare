Pathophysiology of the Dying Patient

Cellular and Metabolic Changes at End of Life

As the human body approaches death, systemic failure originates at the microscopic cellular level. Oxygen delivery progressively declines due to failing circulation, forcing cells to abandon aerobic metabolism. Without sufficient oxygen, cells shift to anaerobic pathways, which yields minimal adenosine triphosphate and rapidly depletes intracellular energy reserves. This energy deficit causes the sodium-potassium pump to fail. Sodium and water flood into the cell, leading to cellular swelling, while potassium leaks into the extracellular space. Simultaneously, membrane-bound lysosomes rupture, releasing hydrolytic enzymes that autodigest the cell. Clinically, this widespread cellular destruction manifests as rising metabolic waste products in the blood, profound fatigue, and a total loss of metabolic homeostasis.

Cardiovascular and Circulatory Collapse

The cardiovascular system undergoes predictable deterioration as compensatory mechanisms exhaust. Peripheral vascular tone is lost due to vasomotor center failure and profound hypoxia, causing blood pressure to drift downward. To maintain perfusion to vital organs, the heart initially increases its rate, but this tachycardic response soon fails as myocardial ischemia sets in.

  • Peripheral Cyanosis: Blood pools in dependent areas as cardiac output drops, resulting in cold, mottled, and bluish extremities.
  • Diminished Pulses: Radial pulses disappear first, followed by femoral and carotid pulses as central blood pressure falls below critical thresholds.
  • Hypotension: Systolic blood pressure drops progressively, often becoming unmeasurable by standard cuff methods.

In Nigerian hospital wards, noting the gradual fading of peripheral pulses serves as a reliable bedside indicator that death is hours or days away, prompting preparation for family involvement.

Respiratory Dysfunction and Terminal Secretions

Respiratory changes during the dying phase reflect failing brainstem control and cumulative fluid shifts. Breathing patterns become irregular, characterized by alternating periods of apnea and hyperventilation, known as Cheyne-Stokes respirations. As the upper airway muscles relax and swallowing reflexes are lost, saliva and bronchial secretions pool in the hypopharynx and trachea. Air passing over these accumulated fluids creates a loud, rattling sound during inhalation and exhalation, commonly referred to as the death rattle. While distressing to family members, this sound indicates that the patient lacks the neuromuscular coordination to clear their airway rather than acute physical suffering.

Neurological and Cognitive Alterations

Cerebral hypoperfusion and toxic metabolic accumulation profoundly alter brain function. Patients frequently transition from alertness to confusion, lethargy, and eventual unresponsiveness. The cerebral cortex shuts down first, stripping away higher cognitive functions, while subcortical and brainstem structures maintain primitive reflexes for a short time longer.

Delirium is exceptionally common, manifesting as restlessness, picking at bedclothes, or disorientation. As blood flow to the reticular activating system drops further, the patient slips into a permanent coma. Examiners must recognize that unresponsiveness at this stage confirms severe global cerebral ischemia rather than sudden focal injury.

Gastrointestinal and Renal Decline

As systemic perfusion is centralized to the heart and brain, visceral organs experience severe ischemia. Gastrointestinal motility grinds to a halt due to splanchnic vasoconstriction, leading to paralytic ileus, nausea, and retained stool. Nutrient and drug absorption cease entirely. Simultaneously, renal blood flow drops precipitously, causing glomerular filtration to stall. Urine output declines steadily, leading to oliguria and eventual anuria. Nitrogenous waste products like urea and creatinine accumulate in the bloodstream, a state of uraemia that further suppresses neurological function and accelerates the dying process.

The Active Dying Phase and Clinical Signs

The active dying phase represents the final hours or days of life, characterized by a distinct constellation of physical signs. The body exhibits profound systemic failure, transitioning from chronic decline to acute cessation of life processes.

  • Facial Changes: The jaw may drop due to muscle relaxation, and the nose appears sharp and pinched with sunken eyes (facies hippocratica).
  • Skin Temperature: Coolness spreads upward from the feet and hands toward the trunk as blood is centralized.
  • Incontinence: Sphincter muscles lose tone, leading to involuntary release of urine and feces.
  • Breathing Irregularities: Periods of apnea lengthen significantly until breathing stops completely.

Nursing Assessment and Pathophysiological Correlation

Accurate nursing assessment during the end-of-life phase requires correlating outward signs with underlying pathophysiology rather than pursuing invasive diagnostics or frequent vital sign monitoring. Continuous blood pressure checks and blood draws cause unnecessary discomfort and provide no clinical benefit when death is imminent. Instead, nurses assess perfusion via skin color and temperature, airway patency via respiratory sounds, and hydration status through mucous membrane inspection. Recognizing that restlessness stems from bladder distension or hypoxia allows the nurse to implement targeted comfort measures, such as gentle repositioning or oral suctioning, rather than inappropriate pharmacological restraints.
