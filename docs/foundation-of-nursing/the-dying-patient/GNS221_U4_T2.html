Care of the Dying Patient

Introduction to Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Palliative and end-of-life care focuses on improving the quality of life for individuals facing life-limiting illnesses. While palliative care can be initiated at the time of diagnosis alongside curative treatments, end-of-life care specifically addresses the final stages of a terminal condition when curative options are no longer appropriate or desired. The core philosophy centers on holistic comfort, managing distressing symptoms, and honoring the personal preferences and cultural values of the patient. In many Nigerian healthcare settings, involving the extended family in care discussions is culturally vital, as medical decisions are often shared communally rather than made by the individual alone.

Physiological and Psychological Changes in the Dying Patient

As the body approaches the end of life, multiple physiological systems gradually decline. Recognizing these shifts helps prepare families and prevents unnecessary interventions. Psychological changes, such as terminal restlessness or withdrawal, often accompany physical decline.

  • Cardiovascular: Peripheral pulses weaken, blood pressure drops, and extremities become cool and cyanotic.
  • Respiratory: Breathing patterns change, frequently exhibiting Cheyne-Stokes respirations or noisy upper airway secretions known as the death rattle.
  • Gastrointestinal: Peristalsis slows significantly, leading to constipation, nausea, or a complete cessation of bowel sounds.
  • Sensory: Vision may blur, and hearing is widely considered the last sense to fade before death.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment at End of Life

Assessment at the end of life shifts away from diagnostic testing toward identifying comfort needs and monitoring the dying trajectory. Continuous evaluation focuses on pain intensity, respiratory status, skin integrity, and elimination patterns. Because verbal communication often fails in the final hours, non-verbal cues such as facial grimacing, restlessness, or clenched fists become critical indicators of untreated discomfort. Nurses must also assess the psychological and informational needs of the family, identifying coping mechanisms and cultural expectations that will guide ongoing support.

Symptom Management and Comfort Measures

Symptom relief is the cornerstone of terminal care. Pharmacological interventions are utilized alongside non-pharmacological strategies to alleviate distressing physical sensations.

  • Pain: Opioids such as morphine are titrated to relieve severe pain and dyspnea, without fear of hastening death when used correctly for symptom control.
  • Secretions: Anticholinergic medications help reduce noisy respiratory secretions, while regular repositioning prevents pooling.
  • Nausea and Dry Mouth: Frequent mouth care using swabs, ice chips, or petroleum jelly relieves mucosal dryness, and antiemetics control nausea.
  • Skin Care: Pressure redistribution mattresses and gentle turning safeguard fragile skin against breakdown.

Psychosocial and Spiritual Support for Patient and Family

Caring for the dying person encompasses emotional, psychological, and spiritual dimensions. Fear of the unknown, loss of control, and anxiety over leaving loved ones weigh heavily on the patient. Active listening and a calming presence provide immense reassurance. Spiritual distress often surfaces as individuals review their life's meaning, prompting requests for religious leaders or traditional healers, depending on their faith. Facilitating these connections is an essential nursing responsibility, honoring the cultural and spiritual framework that brings peace to the patient and their grieving family.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in End-of-Life Care

End-of-life care frequently involves navigating complex ethical dilemmas and legal boundaries. Principles such as autonomy, beneficence, and non-maleficence guide clinical decisions. Nurses must understand the distinctions between active euthanasia, physician-assisted suicide, and the withholding or withdrawal of extraordinary life-sustaining measures, which are generally accepted when treatment only prolongs the dying process. In environments where formal advance directives are uncommon, family consensus and cultural norms play a heavy role in decision-making, requiring the nurse to advocate for the patient's previously expressed wishes.

Post-Mortem Care and Supporting the Bereaved Family

Post-mortem care begins immediately after a patient is pronounced dead. The body is handled with dignity, respecting cultural and religious guidelines regarding washing, positioning, and immediate handling. Dentures are usually replaced, eyes closed, and the body aligned supine with arms folded or at the sides. Medical tubes and lines are removed unless an autopsy or coroner's case is pending. Following physical care, the nurse supports the bereaved family by providing a private space for grief, assisting with necessary documentation, and offering compassionate condolences that acknowledge their loss.
