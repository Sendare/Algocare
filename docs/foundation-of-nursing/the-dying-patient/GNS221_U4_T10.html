Last Offices

Introduction and Verification of Death

Last offices refer to the total care given to a deceased patient's body immediately after formal confirmation of death by a qualified medical practitioner. This sensitive duty preserves the dignity of the departed while protecting public health and supporting grieving relatives. Before any post-mortem care begins, a physician must legally verify death through the absence of spontaneous respiration, palpable pulses, and audible heart sounds via auscultation for at least one full minute. In Nigerian clinical settings, nurses must ensure this medical certification is fully documented in the case notes to prevent premature procedures, especially given local sensitivities surrounding sudden or unconfirmed demises.

Legal, Cultural, and Religious Considerations in Nigeria

Nigeria's multi-ethnic and multi-religious landscape requires nurses to navigate diverse post-mortem practices with cultural competence and strict adherence to the law. Certain faiths demand immediate burial within hours of passing, while others require specific bodily cleansings performed only by designated family members or religious leaders. Nurses must respect these traditions provided they do not compromise hospital policies or public health regulations. Furthermore, any sudden, unexplained, or suspicious death must be reported to the hospital administration and the coroner or police before last offices are initiated, as altering an uncertified crime scene or issuing improper certification carries severe legal penalties.

Standard Precautions and Equipment Preparation

Standard precautions remain paramount during last offices because post-mortem tissues can still transmit infectious pathogens such as HIV, hepatitis viruses, and tuberculosis. Nurses must don full personal protective equipment, including disposable gloves, aprons, and eye protection, particularly if body fluids are expected to purge.

  • Clean linen and shroud: Used to wrap the body respectfully after cleansing.
  • Identification tags: At least two waterproof tags bearing the patient's full name, hospital number, ward, and date of death.
  • Cotton wool and gauze: Used to pack natural orifices to prevent leakage of bodily fluids.
  • Basin of warm water and soap: For gentle cleansing of the skin surface.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Last Offices

Executing last offices requires a systematic approach to ensure dignity, safety, and proper body alignment before rigor mortis sets in. Privacy screens must be drawn around the bed to shield the deceased from other patients and visitors.

  1. Positioning: Place the body flat in a supine position with arms straight at the sides and a small pillow under the head to prevent pooling of blood in the face.
  2. Closing orifices and eyes: Gently close the eyelids by holding them down for a few seconds. Insert dentures if culturally or personally required, and close the jaw.
  3. Cleansing: Wash visible areas of the body, removing any blood, feces, or vomitus, and dress any wounds with clean dressings.
  4. Packing: Securely pack bodily orifices with cotton wool where leakage is anticipated.
  5. Tagging: Attach identification tags securely to the wrist or ankle before wrapping the body.
  6. Wrapping: Cover the body cleanly using a shroud or bed sheet, ensuring it is fully covered from head to toe.

Handling Patient Valuables and Personal Effects

Patient belongings require meticulous inventory control and secure handling to prevent loss, theft, or subsequent disputes between the hospital and grieving relatives. Wedding rings, money, phones, and religious items must be cataloged in the presence of another healthcare worker or a security officer. Each item is documented in a valuables register, signed by both the nurse and the witness, and usually handed over directly to the verified next of kin. If family members are absent, valuables are deposited securely in the hospital's safe or administrative vault following institutional policy, and the receipt number is clearly noted in the patient records.

Transfer to the Mortuary and Documentation

The final stage of last offices involves transporting the shrouded body to the hospital mortuary with discretion and reverence. The trolley used for transport should be covered neatly to maintain privacy and avoid distressing other patients and visitors in the corridors. Accompanying documentation must include the completed mortuary slip, identification details matching the body tags, and the medical certificate of cause of death. Upon arrival at the mortuary, the nurse or attendant hands over the body and obtains a formal signature in the transfer register, closing the physical aspect of the care cycle before completing the final nursing notes.
