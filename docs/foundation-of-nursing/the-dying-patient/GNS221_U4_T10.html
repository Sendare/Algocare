Last Offices

Introduction to Last Offices

Last offices refer to the care given to a patient's body immediately after death is pronounced. As nurses, performing this duty is one of our final acts of professional service, ensuring the deceased is treated with utmost dignity, respect, and cultural sensitivity. Beyond the physical steps involved, this process helps prepare the family for the initial grieving phase. Clinical experience shows that doing this calmly and professionally also provides great comfort to grieving relatives who might be watching from a distance.

Legal and Cultural Considerations in Nigeria

Nigeria is a culturally diverse nation with distinct religious and traditional beliefs surrounding death and the handling of the body. Nurses must always respect these wishes whenever possible. For instance, Islamic rites require prompt burial and specific washing rituals, often managed by family or religious leaders with nurse assistance. Conversely, Christian and traditional practices may involve different timelines and family gatherings. Legally, a formal certification of death must always precede any preparation or movement of the body.

Preparation and Standard Precautions

Before touching a deceased body, the nurse must gather all necessary supplies and wear appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE). Even after death, the body may still harbor infectious agents in body fluids.

  • Supplies needed: Basin of warm water, soap, clean linen, cotton wool, bandages, identification tags, and a body shroud or bag.
  • Infection control: Gloves, aprons, and masks protect the nurse from potential exposure to bloodborne pathogens or purging fluids.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Last Offices

Performing last offices requires a systematic approach to ensure the body is clean, aligned, and properly tagged.

  1. Privacy: Draw the curtains around the bed and ensure a quiet environment.
  2. Alignment: Place the body in a supine position with arms straight at the sides or crossed over the chest before rigor mortis sets in.
  3. Hygiene: Remove medical tubes, drains, and IV lines unless an autopsy or coroner's case is pending. Gently wash the body and comb the hair.
  4. Orifices: Close the eyes and mouth. Place cotton wool in natural orifices if there is a risk of purging.
  5. Identification: Secure identification tags to the wrist or ankle and the outside of the shroud.

Handling Patient Valuables and Personal Effects

Managing a patient's personal belongings requires strict honesty and adherence to hospital protocol. Rings, watches, money, and phones must be documented, signed for, and handed over to the family members present. If no family is available, these items are typically handed over to the hospital administration for safe custody, with a clear record entered in the patient's records. A common mistake is leaving valuables on the bedside table or inside the clothing sent to the mortuary, which frequently leads to loss and conflict.

Documentation and Post-Mortem Nursing Care

Accurate documentation concludes the nursing responsibilities after a patient passes away. The nurse must record the exact time death was pronounced, the name of the physician who certified it, notifications sent to family members, and the disposition of personal effects. Finally, nurses must process their own emotional experience. Caring for dying patients and managing death takes a psychological toll, and debriefing with colleagues helps maintain professional resilience.
