Total Care of the Very Ill Patient

Introduction to the Very Ill Patient

A very ill patient presents with multi-system instability, requiring continuous observation and complex clinical interventions. These individuals often experience a breakdown in normal homeostatic mechanisms, leaving them vulnerable to rapid physiological decline. In Nigerian tertiary hospitals, managing such patients demands resourcefulness, as clinical loads are high and equipment can sometimes be scarce. Recognising the trajectory of deterioration early prevents catastrophic arrest. Total care shifts the focus from managing a single isolated disease process to supporting the entire organism while medical therapies take effect. Every organ system requires vigilant tracking because failure in one quickly triggers collapse in another.

Comprehensive Assessment and Vital Sign Monitoring

Structured physical assessment forms the bedrock of safe nursing interventions for critical patients. Using systematic approaches like the ABCDE framework ensures life-threatening issues are addressed immediately before moving to secondary head-to-toe evaluations.

  • Airway and Breathing: Check for patency, respiratory rate, depth, and oxygen saturation.
  • Circulation: Monitor pulse strength, capillary refill time, blood pressure, and peripheral perfusion.
  • Disability: Assess level of consciousness using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) or AVPU scale.
  • Exposure: Inspect the skin for rashes, injuries, or temperature abnormalities while maintaining patient dignity.

Baseline trends matter more than single isolated readings. A slow, steady upward creep in heart rate can indicate internal bleeding or sepsis long before blood pressure actually crashes.

Airway, Respiration, and Hemodynamic Stability

Maintaining a patent airway is the absolute first priority in total patient care. Unconscious or heavily sedated patients lose pharyngeal muscle tone, causing the tongue to obstruct the upper airway. Suctioning secretions prevents aspiration and maintains alveolar gas exchange, though it must be performed with careful pre-oxygenation to avoid hypoxia.

Hemodynamic stability relies on matching fluid volume with vascular tone. In resource-limited settings, observing jugular venous pressure, peripheral warmth, and urine output serves as a practical proxy for central hemodynamic monitoring when advanced invasive lines are unavailable. Administering prescribed inotropes or IV fluids requires meticulous mathematical calculation to prevent fluid overload or persistent tissue hypoperfusion.

Nutrition, Hydration, and Elimination Care

Critical illness triggers a hypermetabolic catabolic state where the body breaks down its own muscle tissue for energy if exogenous nutrients are missing. Enteral nutrition via nasogastric or nasojejunal tubes should be initiated early to preserve gut mucosal integrity and prevent bacterial translocation. When the gastrointestinal tract is entirely non-functional, parenteral nutrition bypasses the gut via central venous access.

Fluid balance must be recorded with absolute precision. Every milliliter of fluid intake—oral, enteral, intravenous, and drug diluents—must be balanced against output from urine, drains, vomiting, and estimated insensible losses through skin and respiration. Indwelling urinary catheters allow accurate hourly urine output tracking, where anything falling below 0.5 mL/kg/hour signals impending renal hypoperfusion.

Hygiene, Skin Integrity, and Prevention of Complications

Immobility compromises multiple body systems simultaneously. Pressure injuries develop rapidly over bony prominences when constant external pressure cuts off capillary blood flow. Implementing strict two-hourly turning schedules, using pressure-relieving mattresses, and keeping skin clean and dry are non-negotiable standards of care.

Beyond the skin, passive range-of-motion exercises prevent permanent joint contractures. Pulmonary hygiene, including incentive spirometry and chest physiotherapy, wards off hypostatic pneumonia. Furthermore, prophylactic measures against deep vein thrombosis—such as compression stockings or early mobilization when permitted—protect the patient from life-threatening pulmonary embolisms.

Pain Management and Comfort Measures

Assessing pain in a very ill patient is complex, particularly when communication is impaired by mechanical ventilation or altered consciousness. Behavioral scales, such as the Behavioral Pain Scale or Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool, rely on observing facial expressions, muscle tension, and compliance with the ventilator.

Unmanaged pain triggers a stress response that increases myocardial oxygen demand, suppresses the immune system, and delays healing. Analgesia should be administered proactively rather than reactively. Alongside pharmacological options like opioids, non-pharmacological comfort measures—such as gentle repositioning, soothing oral care, reducing harsh ward lights, and easing tight linen folds—significantly lower physiological stress and promote restorative rest.

Psychosocial and Spiritual Support for Patient and Family

Critical illness affects the entire family unit, plunging relatives into acute anxiety, grief, and helplessness. Clear, compassionate communication helps demystify the intensive care environment and reduces fear caused by unfamiliar monitors and equipment. Allowing family members to participate in simple care activities, such as moistening lips or holding a hand, fosters a sense of involvement.

Patients who are conscious yet unable to speak experience profound isolation and terror. Providing communication boards, magic slates, or simply maintaining a calm, reassuring presence helps bridge this gap. Respecting cultural and spiritual beliefs, including accommodating prayer times or religious leaders in the ward, provides immense psychological comfort during times of severe crisis.

Ethical Considerations and End-of-Life Care

Providing total care also encompasses recognising when curative interventions no longer serve the patient's best interest. Nurses frequently navigate complex ethical dilemmas involving autonomy, beneficence, and the withholding or withdrawing of life-sustaining treatment. In such moments, palliative care takes precedence, shifting goals entirely toward symptom relief, dignity, and peaceful passing.

Pain control, alleviation of dyspnea, and meticulous mouth care become the core nursing duties. Supporting the bereaved family involves creating a quiet, private space, allowing time for cultural or religious death rituals, and offering compassionate bereavement guidance. Ensuring a dignified death is just as vital a nursing competency as preserving life.
