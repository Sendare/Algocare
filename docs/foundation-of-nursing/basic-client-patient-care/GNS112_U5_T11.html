Standard Precautions

Introduction and the Chain of Infection

Standard precautions form the absolute baseline of infection prevention in any healthcare setting. You must treat every patient's blood, body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes as if they are potentially infectious, regardless of their diagnosed condition or perceived health status. This universal approach protects both you and the people in your care from unexpected exposures.

The core rationale behind these practices is breaking the chain of infection. An infection requires six specific links to spread: an infectious agent, a reservoir, a portal of exit, a mode of transmission, a portal of entry, and a susceptible host. Standard precautions primarily block the mode of transmission and prevent the agent from reaching a new portal of entry.

Hand Hygiene Practices

  • Alcohol-based hand rub: Use this as the primary method for routine hand hygiene when hands are not visibly soiled. It is fast, highly effective against most common pathogens, and causes less skin dryness.
  • Soap and water: You must wash your hands with soap and water whenever they are visibly dirty, contaminated with proteinaceous material, or after caring for patients with known or suspected infectious diarrhea like *Clostridioides difficile*.
  • Friction and time: Rub all surfaces of your hands and fingers together for at least 20 to 30 seconds to mechanically remove transient microorganisms.

Personal Protective Equipment Selection

Selecting the correct Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) depends entirely on the type of clinical interaction and the anticipated exposure risk.

  1. Gloves: Wear them whenever you anticipate touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin. Remove them immediately after task completion and perform hand hygiene.
  2. Gowns: Put on a clean, fluid-resistant gown to protect your skin and clothing during procedures that are likely to generate splashes or sprays of body fluids.
  3. Masks and Eye Protection: Use surgical masks combined with face shields or goggles whenever splashes to the eyes, nose, or mouth are possible.

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Management

Needlesticks and sharp injuries pose a severe occupational hazard for nursing staff. You must never recap used needles using both hands, and ideally, avoid recapping altogether unless mandated by a specific clinical device. Always activate safety devices immediately after use.

Dispose of all needles, syringes, and scalpel blades directly into a puncture-resistant sharps container located close to the point of use. Never overfill these containers; once they reach the designated fill line, seal and replace them promptly to prevent accidental punctures.

Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette

Respiratory hygiene targets the source of respiratory pathogens, including influenza, SARS, and tuberculosis. Instruct patients, visitors, and staff to cover their mouths and noses with a tissue when coughing or sneezing, and to dispose of used tissues in the nearest waste receptacle immediately.

If tissues are unavailable, cough or sneeze into the inner elbow rather than bare hands. Provide conveniently located hand sanitizing stations, masks, and facial tissues at facility entry points to encourage compliance among all visitors.

Environmental Cleaning and Equipment Handling

Patient care equipment can easily transmit pathogens from one person to another if it is not reprocessed correctly. You must clean and disinfect reusable items, such as stethoscopes, blood pressure cuffs, and glucometers, between every single patient use.

Environmental surfaces like bed rails, bedside tables, and call buttons require regular cleaning with hospital-approved disinfectants. Always follow the manufacturer's instructions for the required contact time—the surface must remain visibly wet for the specified duration to ensure all targeted microorganisms are actually destroyed.

Management of Exposures and Spills

Despite strict adherence to safety protocols, accidental exposures and body fluid spills still occur. If you experience a percutaneous injury like a needle stick, immediately wash the affected skin thoroughly with soap and water. Encourage gentle bleeding if appropriate, but avoid scrubbing or using harsh chemicals on the wound.

Report the incident immediately to your supervisor or occupational health department to evaluate the need for post-exposure prophylaxis. For large body fluid spills on floors or furniture, use designated spill kits, put on heavy-duty gloves, contain the liquid with absorbent material, and disinfect the area thoroughly.
