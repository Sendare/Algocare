Providing Means of Elimination

Introduction to Elimination Needs

Elimination is a basic human need that must be met to maintain physiological balance and dignity. When patients are bedridden, weak, or recovering from surgery, they cannot walk to the toilet independently. Nurses step in to provide alternative means of elimination, such as bedpans, urinals, and absorbent pads. Assessing a patient's normal elimination pattern, mobility level, and cognitive state helps the nurse choose the right device and preserve the patient's self-esteem during vulnerable moments.

Using Bedpans Safely

A bedpan is used for elimination by patients who are confined to bed. There are two main types: the standard high-backed bedpan and the fracture pan, which has a shallow lower end.

  • Standard bedpan: Used for patients who can lift their hips slightly or be rolled onto it safely.
  • Fracture pan: Designed with a shallow front, making it ideal for patients with pelvic fractures, hip replacements, or severe spinal immobility.

To place a bedpan, raise the bed to a comfortable working height, lower the head of the bed if permitted, and ask the patient to bend their knees and lift their hips. If the patient cannot lift their hips, roll them onto their side, place the bedpan firmly against the buttocks, and gently roll them back onto it while supporting the device.

Managing Urinals for Male Patients

A urinal is a portable container used specifically for collecting male urine. It allows male patients to void while lying in bed or sitting on the edge of the mattress.

When offering a urinal, ensure the patient has privacy and can hold the device independently if their upper body strength allows. Place the penis completely inside the opening of the urinal to prevent spills on the bed linen or patient skin. After use, promptly remove the urinal, empty and measure the urine if required, rinse the container, and perform hand hygiene.

Absorbent Pads and Diapers

Adult diapers and incontinence pads provide a management solution for patients suffering from severe urinary or fecal incontinence, altered mental status, or immobility where bedpans are impractical. While they protect clothing and bedding, they require vigilant monitoring. Leaving a damp or soiled diaper on a patient for too long traps moisture and bacteria against the skin, rapidly causing skin breakdown and pressure injuries. Nurses must schedule regular checks and changes.

Perineal Skin Care

Perineal care involves cleaning the genital and anal areas. For patients using bedpans, diapers, or pads, this maintenance is critical to prevent infection and skin irritation. Always clean from the cleanest area to the dirtiest area—from the urethral meatus toward the rectum—to avoid spreading bacteria from the bowel to the urinary tract. Use mild soap and warm water, rinse thoroughly, and pat the skin completely dry with a soft towel.

Infection Control and Safety Precautions

Providing means of elimination puts healthcare workers and patients at risk for pathogen transmission if standard precautions are ignored. Always wear clean gloves when handling bedpans, urinals, soiled diapers, and emptying drainage devices. Perform hand hygiene before and after every patient interaction. Never place bedpans or urinals on overbed tables where food is served. Ensure call bells are always within the patient's reach so they can request assistance promptly.
