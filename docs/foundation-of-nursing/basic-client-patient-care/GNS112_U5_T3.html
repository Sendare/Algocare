Oral Hygiene

Introduction and Objectives of Oral Hygiene

Oral hygiene is the practice of keeping the mouth, teeth, and gums clean and free of disease-causing bacteria, food particles, and plaque. In clinical nursing, it forms a core component of basic nursing care that directly impacts a patient's systemic well-being, comfort, and recovery. Neglecting the mouth does more than cause bad breath or tooth decay; it opens a direct gateway for harmful pathogens to enter the bloodstream, especially in vulnerable hospitalised individuals. Nurses perform or assist with oral care not merely for aesthetic reasons, but to prevent dangerous complications like hospital-acquired pneumonia and systemic infections.

Anatomy of the Oral Cavity and Normal Flora

The oral cavity consists of the lips, cheeks, hard and soft palates, tongue, teeth, gingiva, and salivary glands. Saliva plays a vital protective role by washing away food debris, buffering acids produced by bacteria, and providing enzymes that begin digestion. Under normal conditions, the mouth hosts a balanced community of microorganisms known as the oral flora. When illness, dehydration, or poor nutrition disrupts this balance, harmful bacteria multiply rapidly, forming a sticky, colorless film called dental plaque that hardens into tartar.

Assessment of the Oral Cavity and Risk Factors

Comprehensive nursing assessment of the mouth involves inspecting the lips, mucosa, tongue, gums, and teeth using a penlight and tongue depressor.

  • Mucosa condition: Check for moisture, color, lesions, ulcerations, or bleeding.
  • Gingival health: Look for swelling, redness, or tenderness around the gum line.
  • Dental status: Note missing, broken, or decayed teeth, as well as the presence of dentures.
  • Risk factors: Identify conditions like zero-saliva dry mouth, immunosuppression, mechanical ventilation, or poor nutrition that increase vulnerability to oral disease.

Common Oral Pathologies in Hospitalised Patients

Hospitalised individuals frequently experience specific oral disorders due to illness, medication side effects, or poor hygiene.

  • Xerostomia: Chronic dry mouth caused by medications, dehydration, or mouth breathing, which drastically increases decay risk.
  • Stomatitis: Painful inflammation of the oral mucosa, often triggered by chemotherapy or radiation therapy.
  • Gingivitis and Periodontitis: Gum diseases marked by inflammation and bleeding that can eventually destroy jawbone support.
  • Oral Candidiasis (Thrush): A fungal infection characterised by creamy white, curd-like patches on the tongue and inner cheeks, common in immunocompromised patients.

Standard Oral Hygiene Procedures

Performing oral care requires adapting techniques to the patient's level of consciousness and physical capability. For conscious patients who are weak, nurses provide setup, adaptive toothbrush handles, and an emesis basin for rinsing.

For unconscious patients, the approach changes completely to ensure safety and prevent aspiration. The nurse must position the unconscious patient in a side-lying recovery position with the head turned toward the mattress. Suction equipment must be readily available at the bedside. Nurses use soft-sponge Toothette swabs moistened with water or mouthwash rather than a stiff toothbrush, gently cleaning all surfaces while suctioning excess fluid immediately to keep it from entering the airway.

Care of Dentures and Orthodontic Appliances

Removable dentures require careful daily cleaning to prevent odor, staining, and gum irritation. Nurses should line the sink with a soft towel or fill it with water to cushion the dentures if accidentally dropped. Clean them using a soft-bristle denture brush and non-abrasive cleaner, avoiding hot water which can warp the acrylic material. When not worn, dentures must be stored in a labeled cup filled with clean water or a specialized soaking solution to keep them moist. Orthodontic appliances like braces require special floss threaders and interdental brushes to remove trapped food particles around wires and brackets.

Patient Education and Lifelong Dental Health

Empowering patients with proper oral hygiene knowledge is a fundamental nursing responsibility. Education should cover the correct toothbrushing technique using a soft-bristled brush angled at 45 degrees to the gums, brushing for at least two minutes twice daily. Nurses should emphasize daily interdental cleaning with floss or interdental brushes to remove plaque where toothbrushes cannot reach. Dietary advice is equally crucial; limiting sugary snacks and drinks helps prevent dental caries. Finally, nurses should encourage routine dental check-ups every six months for professional cleaning and early detection of oral abnormalities.
