Hair Care

Anatomy and Physiology of Hair and Scalp

Hair is primarily made of a tough protein called keratin. Each strand grows from a living pocket in the skin called a hair follicle. Attached to this follicle is a tiny muscle called the arrector pili, which causes hair to stand up when we are cold or scared, producing goosebumps. The visible part of the hair above the skin is the shaft, while the part anchored below the surface is the root.

The scalp is rich in blood vessels and sebaceous glands. These glands secrete an oily substance called sebum, which travels up the hair shaft to keep the hair and skin naturally moisturized and waterproof. Hair growth happens in cycles, moving through active growth, resting, and shedding phases. Understanding these structures helps nurses recognize that changes in hair texture or strength often reflect deeper nutritional or systemic health issues.

Assessment of Hair and Scalp Health

A thorough nursing assessment of the hair and scalp involves both visual inspection and gentle palpation. Begin by parting the hair in multiple areas to check the underlying skin. Look for signs of dryness, excessive oiliness, scaling, redness, or swelling.

  • Texture and Strength: Note if the hair is coarse, fine, brittle, or dry. Check for breakage.
  • Scalp Integrity: Inspect for lesions, abrasions, flaking, or evidence of parasites like lice and nits.
  • Distribution and Loss: Observe patterns of hair thinning or sudden patches of hair loss, which can indicate stress, nutritional deficiencies, or illness.

Always ask the patient about their usual hair care routine, as harsh chemical treatments or tight hairstyles frequently cause contact dermatitis or traction alopecia.

Routine Hygiene and Shampooing Techniques

Regular hair cleansing removes dirt, sweat, dead skin cells, and excess sebum build-up. For hospitalized or immobile patients, bed shampoos are performed using a waterproof trough, basin, warm water, and gentle shampoo.

Before washing, gently comb out any tangles, working from the ends up to the roots to minimize pulling. Massage the scalp using the pads of your fingers—never your fingernails, as scratching can break the skin and introduce infection. Rinse thoroughly, because leftover soap residue can dry out the scalp, cause itching, and attract dirt. Dry the hair promptly with a clean towel, and use a hair dryer on a cool or warm setting only if necessary to prevent chilling.

Managing Special Hair Care Needs

Different hair textures, cultural practices, and medical conditions require tailored hair care approaches. Coarse, curly, or tightly coiled hair is naturally drier because the scalp's sebum struggles to travel down the bends of the hair shaft. These hair types often need less frequent washing and benefit from regular moisturizing oils or leave-in conditioners to prevent breakage.

For patients with highly tangled or matted hair—often seen in prolonged bed rest or severe depression—patience and a wide-toothed comb are essential. Apply a detangling spray or small amount of conditioner, and gently work through small sections at a time. Never cut a patient's hair without explicit consent, as hair holds deep personal and cultural significance.

Prevention and Management of Common Scalp Conditions

Nurses frequently encounter scalp conditions that require specific interventions.

  • Dandruff and Seborrheic Dermatitis: Characterized by flaky, itchy white or yellowish scales. Treatment involves medicated shampoos containing ingredients like zinc pyrithione, selenium sulfide, or ketoconazole.
  • Pediculosis Capitis (Head Lice): Parasitic insects that live on the scalp. Diagnosis involves finding live bugs or tiny eggs (nits) cemented near the hair shaft. Management requires pediculicide shampoos and meticulous combing with a fine-toothed nit comb, alongside washing bedding in hot water.
  • Fungal Infections (Tinea Capitis): Causes scaly, circular patches of hair loss. This condition requires prescription oral antifungal medications, as topical shampoos alone cannot cure the infection inside the hair follicle.

Documentation and Patient Education

Accurate nursing documentation ensures continuity of care regarding a patient's hygiene status. Record the condition of the hair and scalp, noting any lesions, parasites, flaking, or unusual hair loss. Document any specific products used during care, especially if medicated shampoos were applied.

Patient education empowers individuals to maintain healthy hair and scalp habits at home. Teach patients how to select gentle shampoos appropriate for their hair type, emphasize the importance of thorough rinsing, and caution against excessive heat styling or tight hairstyles that can damage roots. If treating a contagious condition like lice, provide clear, written instructions on environmental cleaning and checking other household members.
