Pressure Sore

Introduction to Pressure Sores

A pressure sore, medically known as a **pressure injury**, **decubitus ulcer**, or **bedsore**, represents localized damage to the skin and underlying soft tissue. Typically occurring over a bony prominence, this pathology arises from sustained pressure or shear forces combined with friction. When external pressure exceeds normal capillary pressure—roughly 32 mmHg—local blood flow halts. Without oxygen and essential nutrients, tissue ischemia sets in, leading rapidly to cellular death and tissue necrosis if the compressive force is not relieved.

Common Anatomical Pressure Points

Pressure sores develop wherever bony prominences press against a support surface like a mattress or wheelchair. Vulnerable sites shift depending on the patient's primary positioning: - **Supine position**: Occiput, scapulae, elbows, sacrum, heels, and toes. - **Side-lying position**: Ears, acromion process, ribs, greater trochanter, medial and lateral condyles, and malleoli. - **Prone position**: Ears, cheeks, breasts, male genitalia, anterior superior iliac spines, knees, and toes. - **Sitting position**: Ischial tuberosities, sacrum, scapulae, and feet.

Causes and Risk Factors

Multiple physiological and environmental variables increase vulnerability to tissue breakdown. Intrinsic factors involve the patient's internal health status, whereas extrinsic factors relate to external forces applied to the skin. - **Immobility and Inactivity**: Inability to shift weight independently. - **Sensory Perception Impairment**: Neurological deficits preventing the sensation of pain or discomfort from prolonged pressure. - **Moisture**: Incontinence, sweat, or wound drainage causing maceration and weakening the epidermal barrier. - **Nutrition and Hydration**: Protein-energy malnutrition and dehydration impairing tissue synthesis and repair. - **Friction and Shear**: Sliding down in a bed creates shear forces that tear internal blood vessels beneath the skin.

Stages and Assessment of Pressure Sores

Clinical staging categorizes pressure injuries based on the extent of anatomical tissue destruction. Accurate grading guides appropriate therapeutic interventions. - **Stage 1**: Intact skin with non-blanchable erythema of a localized area, usually over a bony prominence. - **Stage 2**: Partial-thickness loss of skin with exposed dermis. The wound bed is viable, pink or red, moist, and may present as an intact or ruptured serum-filled blister. - **Stage 3**: Full-thickness loss of skin, in which adipose fat is visible in the ulcer and granulation tissue and epibole are often present. Slough and eschar may be visible. - **Stage 4**: Full-thickness skin and tissue loss with exposed or directly palpable fascia, muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage, or bone. - **Unstageable**: Full-thickness skin and tissue loss in which the extent of damage cannot be confirmed because it is obscured by slough or eschar. - **Deep Tissue Pressure Injury (DTPI)**: Persistent non-blanchable deep red, maroon, or purple discoloration or epidermal separation revealing a dark wound bed or blood-filled blister.

Treatment and Wound Management

Managing established pressure sores requires a multidisciplinary approach focusing on pressure relief, wound bed preparation, infection control, and nutritional support. Debridement is often necessary to remove necrotic tissue, slough, or eschar that inhibits healing. Dressings are chosen based on the wound's moisture level and depth: hydrocolloids, foams, or alginates create an optimal moist healing environment. Regular assessment for signs of localized or systemic infection—such as purulent exudate, foul odor, or fever—remains critical. Furthermore, clinical outcomes improve significantly when dietary intake includes adequate protein, zinc, and vitamin C to support cellular repair.

Prevention Strategies in Nursing Care

Proactive prevention remains the cornerstone of quality nursing practice regarding pressure sores. Establishing a routine repositioning schedule—typically every two hours for bedbound patients and every hour for wheelchair users—drastically reduces sustained capillary compression. Using specialized support surfaces, alternating pressure mattresses, and heel-suspension devices redistributes body weight effectively. Maintaining skin hygiene, managing incontinence promptly with barrier creams, and conducting regular risk assessments using validated tools like the Braden Scale ensure that high-risk individuals receive targeted, timely interventions.
