Pressure Sore

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Pressure Sores

A pressure sore, also known as a bedsore or pressure ulcer, is localized injury to the skin and underlying tissue, usually over a bony prominence. It results from prolonged pressure, friction, or shear forces. When external pressure exceeds normal capillary pressure (around 32 mmHg), blood flow stops. Without oxygen and nutrients, the tissue becomes ischemic, leading to cell death and tissue necrosis. The outer skin might look intact at first, but deep muscle and fat layers closer to the bone suffer damage much faster because they are more sensitive to lack of blood supply. Understanding this mechanism helps nurses realize why changing a patient's position is not just for comfort, but a life-saving intervention for the tissue.

Common Anatomical Pressure Points

Pressure sores develop wherever bones press closely against a support surface like a bed or wheelchair. The specific sites depend entirely on the patient's usual position.

  • Supine position (lying on the back): Heels, sacrum, back of the head, elbows, and shoulder blades bear the most weight.
  • Side-lying position: Ears, shoulders, ribs, hips, knees, and ankles are at high risk.
  • Sitting in a wheelchair: Ischial tuberosities (sitting bones), tailbone, shoulder blades, and the back of the heels take the brunt of the pressure.

Novice nurses sometimes forget to check unusual spots, such as the bridge of the nose or ears from oxygen tubing and nasal prongs.

Causes and Risk Factors

Pressure is only part of the problem. Nurses must evaluate multiple contributing factors that speed up skin breakdown:

  • Shear: Occurs when the skin stays in one place while the underlying bone and muscle slide down, often when a patient is pulled up in bed or sits up too high. This twists and stretches local blood vessels.
  • Friction: Happens when skin rubs against bed sheets or rough surfaces, rubbing away the outer protective layer.
  • Moisture: Incontinence, sweat, or wound drainage softens the skin, making it fragile and prone to tearing.
  • Sensory loss: Patients with spinal cord injuries or stroke cannot feel pain, so they do not shift their weight naturally.
  • Nutrition and hydration: Low protein levels lower blood albumin, reducing tissue healing capacity and making skin weaker.

Stages and Assessment of Pressure Sores

Clinicians classify pressure ulcers into stages based on the depth of tissue destruction. Accurate staging guides the treatment plan.

  • Stage 1: Intact skin with non-blanching redness over a localized area. Pressing a finger on it does not turn the skin white.
  • Stage 2: Partial-thickness loss of dermis, presenting as a shallow open ulcer with a red-pink bed, or a ruptured fluid-filled blister.
  • Stage 3: Full-thickness tissue loss. Subcutaneous fat may be visible, but bone, tendon, or muscle are not exposed.
  • Stage 4: Full-thickness tissue loss with exposed bone, tendon, or muscle. Slough or eschar may be present.
  • Unstageable: Full-thickness loss where the base of the ulcer is covered by yellow, tan, gray, green, or brown slough or black eschar, hiding the true depth.
  • Deep Tissue Injury (DTI): Purple or maroon localized area of discolored intact skin or blood-filled blister due to damage of underlying soft tissue.

Treatment and Wound Management

Managing an existing pressure ulcer requires a multi-faceted approach. First, relieve all pressure from the affected site using specialized cushions or mattresses. Second, keep the wound clean with gentle saline irrigation, avoiding harsh antiseptics like hydrogen peroxide that damage newly forming granulation tissue. Debridement is often necessary to remove dead tissue, which can be done surgically, mechanically, or using autolytic dressings that use the body's own enzymes. Dressings must maintain a moist wound environment while absorbing excess drainage to prevent maceration around the wound edges. Nutritional support with high protein and vitamin supplementation is mandatory to fuel cellular repair.

Prevention Strategies in Nursing Care

Prevention remains the gold standard in clinical nursing because treating established pressure sores is difficult, costly, and painful for the patient. Nurses use validated tools like the Braden Scale to identify patients at risk upon admission.

Key nursing interventions include:

  • Repositioning: Turn bed-bound patients every two hours and encourage wheelchair-bound patients to shift weight every fifteen minutes.
  • Skin care: Keep skin clean and dry, use barrier creams for incontinence, and avoid vigorous rubbing during baths.
  • Support surfaces: Use pressure-relieving mattresses, gel cushions, and heel protectors.
  • Mobility support: Collaborate with physical therapists to get patients moving safely as soon as their medical condition allows.
