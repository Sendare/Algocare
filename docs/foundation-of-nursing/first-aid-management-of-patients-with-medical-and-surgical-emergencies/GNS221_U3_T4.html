Haemorrhage

Introduction and Classification of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage refers to the escape of blood from the vascular system due to vessel damage. It can arise from trauma, surgical complications, or underlying medical disorders such as coagulation defects. Clinicians classify bleeding based on its anatomical source and the specific type of blood vessel involved. - **Arterial bleeding**: Characterized by bright red blood that spurts rhythmically with the heartbeat under high pressure. - **Venous bleeding**: Features dark red blood flowing in a steady, continuous stream due to lower pressure. - **Capillary bleeding**: Involves a slow, oozing flow of dark or intermediate-colored blood, typically from minor superficial abrasions. Categorizing by source reveals whether blood escapes externally through a break in the skin or internally into body cavities and tissues, each presenting distinct clinical challenges.

Pathophysiology and Stages of Hypovolemic Shock

When acute blood loss reduces circulating volume, the body initiates compensatory mechanisms to maintain perfusion to vital organs like the brain and heart. Sympathetic nervous system activation triggers vasoconstriction, tachycardia, and increased myocardial contractility. As blood loss progresses, these compensatory reserves fail, leading to decompensated and eventually irreversible shock. The Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) classification divides haemorrhagic shock into four distinct classes based on percentage volume loss: 1. **Class I**: Loss of up to 15% (under 750 mL). Vital signs remain largely normal. 2. **Class II**: Loss of 15% to 30% (750–1500 mL). Tachycardia, tachypnoea, and narrowed pulse pressure appear. 3. **Class III**: Loss of 30% to 40% (1500–2000 mL). Marked hypotension, confusion, and delayed capillary refill set in. 4. **Class IV**: Loss exceeding 40% (over 2000 mL). Lethargy, unmeasurable blood pressure, and impending cardiac arrest.

Clinical Assessment and Recognition of Bleeding

Early recognition of haemorrhage relies on monitoring subtle trends in vital signs rather than waiting for a dramatic drop in blood pressure. Because compensatory vasoconstriction maintains systolic blood pressure initially, a normal reading can falsely reassure clinicians. Assessing peripheral perfusion provides a clearer picture. Cold, pale, and clammy skin, delayed capillary refill exceeding two seconds, and a rising heart rate point directly to occult blood loss. Mental status changes, such as mild anxiety, restlessness, or confusion, indicate diminished cerebral perfusion caused by falling cardiac output. In busy Nigerian emergency wards, tracking the shock index—dividing the heart rate by the systolic blood pressure—serves as a rapid bedside indicator of unrecognised hypovolemia.

Emergency Management of External Haemorrhage

Immediate control of external bleeding prevents rapid exsanguination. Direct pressure remains the gold standard initial intervention. Firm, continuous pressure applied directly over the wound using a sterile dressing compresses severed vessels against underlying bone. When direct pressure fails or the wound is located on an extremity, applying a commercially manufactured tourniquet proximal to the bleeding site is necessary. The tourniquet must be tightened until bright red pulsatile bleeding stops and distal pulses disappear. Documenting the exact time of application is vital to prevent ischemic nerve and muscle damage. Wound packing combined with a hemostatic gauze dressing is preferred for junctional areas like the groin or axilla where tourniquets cannot be secured.

Identification and Management of Internal Haemorrhage

Internal bleeding occurs within closed body cavities, such as the thorax, abdomen, or retroperitoneum, making visual detection impossible. Diagnosis relies on tracking clinical signs of shock alongside diagnostic imaging and focused assessment with sonography for trauma (FAST). Patients may present with abdominal rigidity, distension, absent bowel sounds, or bruising over specific anatomical landmarks like the flanks. Bleeding into the thoracic cavity compresses the lungs, resulting in respiratory distress and diminished breath sounds on the affected side. Management focuses on rapid fluid resuscitation and urgent surgical intervention to locate and ligate the bleeding source.

Nursing Interventions and Fluid Resuscitation

Managing hypovolemic shock requires securing large-bore peripheral intravenous access—typically 16-gauge or 18-gauge cannulae—to facilitate rapid volume replacement. For decades, aggressive crystalloid resuscitation was standard, but current protocols emphasize balanced resuscitation and damage control principles to avoid disrupting fragile blood clots. Blood component therapy replaces lost oxygen-carrying capacity and clotting factors. Group-specific or uncrossed O-negative blood is administered in massive transfusion protocols when waiting for crossmatching poses an unacceptable delay. Nurses must monitor for transfusion reactions, maintain patient normothermia, and correct acidosis and hypocalcemia, which together form the lethal triad of trauma.

Complications of Severe Haemorrhage

Uncontrolled blood loss and subsequent shock expose tissues to prolonged hypoxia, setting off secondary pathological cascades. The lethal triad of trauma—hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy—creates a destructive feedback loop where each factor worsens the others. - **Hypothermia**: Impairs enzymatic reactions required for the coagulation cascade. - **Acidosis**: Results from anaerobic cellular metabolism, further weakening myocardial performance and clotting efficiency. - **Coagulopathy**: Consumes available platelets and clotting factors, perpetuating bleeding. If left uncorrected, these metabolic imbalances culminate in multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), acute kidney injury, and ischemic brain damage.
