Haemorrhage

Introduction and Definition of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is the escape of blood from the vascular system, occurring when a blood vessel wall is compromised by injury, disease, or surgical incision. It can threaten life by rapidly depleting the circulating blood volume, which compromises tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery to vital organs. Understanding the origin and volume of blood loss is foundational for any nursing assessment. The body naturally attempts to halt bleeding through hemostasis, a complex physiological process involving vascular spasm, platelet aggregation, and the activation of the coagulation cascade. When this internal mechanism fails or is overwhelmed by a massive wound, external clinical intervention becomes mandatory to prevent the lethal triad of hypothermia, metabolic acidosis, and coagulopathy.

Classification of Haemorrhage: Types and Sources

Bleeding is categorized by the damaged vessel type and the anatomical location of the blood loss.

  • Arterial Haemorrhage: Bright red blood spurting in pulses synchronized with the heartbeat. This type is extremely dangerous because high pressure causes rapid volume loss.
  • Venous Haemorrhage: Dark red blood flowing in a steady, continuous stream. While under lower pressure, it can still cause severe blood loss if a major vein is lacerated.
  • Capillary Haemorrhage: Slow, oozing flow of dark or reddish blood typically seen in minor scrapes and superficial surface wounds.
  • Internal Haemorrhage: Blood loss confined within body cavities or tissues, making it invisible to direct observation.
  • External Haemorrhage: Blood visible escaping outside the body through a break in the skin or a natural body opening.

Pathophysiology and Stages of Hypovolemic Shock

When blood volume drops below critical thresholds, the body enters hypovolemic shock, a state of severe cellular oxygen deprivation. Compensatory mechanisms activate immediately to preserve perfusion to the brain and heart at the expense of the skin, gut, and kidneys.

  1. Compensated Shock: Blood loss is under 15 to 20 percent. Tachycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction maintain normal blood pressure, though the patient may feel anxious and look pale.
  2. Decompensated Shock: Blood loss reaches 20 to 40 percent. Compensatory mechanisms fail, blood pressure plummets, tachypnea worsens, and organs begin to suffer ischemic injury.
  3. Irreversible Shock: Prolonged lack of perfusion causes cellular death, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, and death despite later fluid resuscitation.

Clinical Assessment and Recognition of Bleeding

Rapid detection of hemorrhage relies on sharp nursing observation and serial vital sign measurements. Nurses must look past single numbers and track trends over time. Early signs of blood loss include resting tachycardia, tachypnea, cool and clammy skin, delayed capillary refill, and mild anxiety caused by sympathetic nervous system stimulation. As blood loss mounts, hypotension develops late—a dangerous diagnostic trap, because a young or fit patient can maintain a normal blood pressure despite losing nearly a third of their blood volume. Orthostatic hypotension, confusion, decreased urine output, and diaphoresis point unmistakably toward significant, ongoing blood loss requiring immediate intervention.

First Aid and Emergency Management of External Haemorrhage

Stopping external bleeding quickly prevents progression to deep shock. Standard trauma protocols prioritize direct, firm pressure over the bleeding site using a clean dressing or bandage. If bleeding continues through the first layer, do not remove it; simply add more dressings on top and maintain pressure. When direct pressure fails on an extremity, or during mass casualty situations, applying a tourniquet proximal to the wound is lifesaving. Elevating an injured limb above the level of the heart can assist when used alongside direct pressure. Hemostatic agents or gauze dressings infused with clotting agents may be packed deep into junctional wounds where tourniquets cannot be safely secured.

Identification and Management of Internal Haemorrhage

Internal bleeding occurs within closed body cavities such as the abdomen, thorax, cranium, or pelvic space, presenting a diagnostic challenge because no blood is visible externally. Common causes include blunt abdominal trauma, pelvic fractures, ruptured aneurysms, and bleeding peptic ulcers. Nurses must monitor for signs of internal pooling: abdominal distension, rigidity, pain, absent bowel sounds, or shortness of breath accompanied by dullness on chest percussion. Pelvic instability or unexplained tachycardia paired with falling hemoglobin levels demands urgent diagnostic imaging like a FAST ultrasound or CT scan. Management focuses on rapid fluid resuscitation, reversing any coagulopathy, and immediate surgical exploration to ligate the bleeding source.

Nursing Interventions and Fluid Resuscitation

Active resuscitation of a bleeding patient requires coordinated medical and nursing care to restore perfusion while avoiding further harm. Establishing large-bore peripheral intravenous lines—typically 16-gauge or 18-gauge—is a top priority for rapid volume administration. Crystalloid fluids like normal saline are used cautiously, as aggressive or excessive fluid infusion can dilute clotting factors, raise blood pressure too quickly and dislodge fragile clots, and worsen bleeding. Current protocols favor permissive hypotension in non-hemorrhagic brain injury trauma until surgical control is achieved. For massive blood loss, damage control resuscitation prioritizes early administration of blood products—packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets—in a balanced 1:1:1 ratio to mimic whole blood and correct coagulopathy.

Complications of Severe Haemorrhage

Severe hemorrhage triggers systemic complications that extend far beyond initial blood loss. The lethal triad of trauma—hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy—creates a vicious cycle where each factor worsens the others. Hypothermia impairs enzyme systems necessary for blood clotting. Acidosis from anaerobic metabolism similarly inhibits the coagulation cascade. Once coagulopathy sets in, bleeding accelerates, worsening shock and tissue hypoxia. Other risks include acute kidney injury from prolonged renal hypoperfusion, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome, and transfusion-related complications such as volume overload or allergic reactions. Rigorous nursing monitoring of core temperature, acid-base balance, and coagulation profiles remains vital throughout recovery.
