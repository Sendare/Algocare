Haemorrhage

Introduction and Definition of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is defined as the escape of blood from the vascular system, resulting from the rupture, laceration, or erosion of blood vessels such as arteries, veins, or capillaries. In clinical nursing practice, understanding haemorrhage is foundational because uncontrolled blood loss is a leading cause of preventable mortality globally and within Nigerian healthcare facilities. Blood loss can compromise tissue perfusion, limit oxygen delivery to vital organs, and rapidly precipitate cellular hypoxia if the homeostatic mechanisms fail. Nurses must recognize that haemorrhage is not merely a local tissue injury but a systemic emergency that threatens cardiovascular stability. When an injury occurs, the body immediately attempts to halt the bleeding through primary and secondary haemostasis, which involves vascular spasm, platelet aggregation, and the activation of the coagulation cascade. However, when the volume of blood lost exceeds the body's compensatory mechanisms, the patient transitions from normal physiological states into acute hypovolemic crisis. For nursing students preparing for professional licensing examinations, it is vital to master the terminology associated with blood loss, including acute versus chronic bleeding, and to appreciate the speed at which clinical deterioration can occur. Prompt identification of risk factors, such as trauma, postpartum complications, or gastrointestinal ulcers, allows nurses to anticipate bleeding events and initiate rapid interventions. By grasping the fundamental definition and systemic impact of haemorrhage, nurses lay the groundwork for effective critical care, accurate patient assessment, and swift life-saving interventions in diverse clinical settings.

Classification of Haemorrhage: Types and Sources

Haemorrhage is classified based on the source of the bleeding vessel and its anatomical location relative to the body. Based on the source, bleeding can be arterial, venous, or capillary. Arterial haemorrhage is characterized by bright red blood that spurts synchronously with the heartbeat, reflecting high pressure within the arterial system; this type of bleeding is notoriously rapid and life-threatening. Venous haemorrhage features dark red or maroon blood that flows in a steady, continuous stream due to lower pressure within the veins. Capillary haemorrhage involves a slow, oozing flow of blood, typically from minor cuts or abrasions, and is usually easy to control through standard clotting mechanisms. Based on anatomical location, haemorrhage is categorized into external and internal bleeding. External haemorrhage is visible, with blood escaping directly through a break in the skin or body orifices. Internal haemorrhage occurs within body cavities, tissues, or organs, such as the cranial vault, thorax, abdomen, or pelvic cavity, where it is hidden from immediate view. Internal bleeding presents a unique diagnostic challenge for nursing students and clinicians because external signs may be absent initially, while massive volumes of blood accumulate internally. Recognizing these distinct types and sources is critical for determining the severity of blood loss and choosing the correct immediate intervention strategy during emergency triage in clinical practice.

Pathophysiology and Stages of Hypovolemic Shock

Pathophysiologically, severe haemorrhage leads to hypovolemic shock, a life-threatening condition characterized by inadequate cellular oxygenation and tissue perfusion. The body responds to acute blood loss through four distinct stages of hypovolemic shock, determined by the percentage of circulating blood volume lost. Stage I involves a blood loss of up to 15% (approximately 750 mL in an average adult). At this stage, compensatory mechanisms like tachycardia and mild vasoconstriction are activated, and vital signs often remain within normal limits. Stage II involves a blood loss of 15% to 30% (750-1500 mL). Here, sympathetic nervous system activation becomes pronounced, resulting in tachycardia, tachypnea, narrowed pulse pressure, and cool, clamky skin as blood is shunted away from the skin and kidneys to vital organs. Stage III entails a blood loss of 30% to 40% (1500-2000 mL), where compensatory mechanisms begin to fail. The patient exhibits marked hypotension, severe tachycardia, confusion, and delayed capillary refill, indicating impending cardiovascular collapse. Stage IV involves a blood loss exceeding 40% (>2000 mL), representing a pre-terminal state characterized by profound hypotension, lethargy or coma, absent peripheral pulses, and negligible urine output. Nursing students must understand these stages to evaluate the severity of bleeding accurately, recognize early signs of decompensation, and advocate for prompt, aggressive fluid and blood product resuscitation before the patient reaches irreversible shock.

Clinical Assessment and Recognition of Bleeding

Clinical assessment and recognition of bleeding require a systematic approach using structured assessment tools, such as the Primary Survey (ABCDE approach), combined with continuous vital sign monitoring. Nurses must be vigilant for both subtle and overt signs of acute blood loss. Initial clinical indicators often include restlessness, anxiety, and apprehension, which occur because cerebral hypoxia stimulates the central nervous system. As blood volume diminishes, cardiovascular changes become apparent: tachycardia is typically one of the earliest signs of haemorrhage, acting as the body's compensatory attempt to maintain cardiac output. Blood pressure may initially remain normal due to intense vasoconstriction (masked shock), but as blood loss progresses, systolic blood pressure drops and pulse pressure narrows. Tachypnea (increased respiratory rate) develops as the body attempts to compensate for metabolic acidosis caused by poor tissue perfusion and anaerobic metabolism. Skin changes such as pallor, diaphoresis, and delayed capillary refill time (>2 seconds) provide crucial clues regarding peripheral perfusion status. Furthermore, monitoring urine output via an indwelling urinary catheter is an essential nursing duty; oliguria (urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h) serves as a sensitive indicator of renal hypoperfusion. By integrating these assessment findings, nurses can rapidly identify active bleeding, estimate the volume lost, and alert the medical team for immediate intervention.

First Aid and Emergency Management of External Haemorrhage

First aid and emergency management of external haemorrhage focus on stopping the flow of blood rapidly to prevent exsanguination and hypovolemic shock. The immediate primary action in managing external bleeding is the application of direct, firm pressure over the bleeding site using a clean dressing, sterile gauze, or cloth. If direct pressure is insufficient or if bleeding is profuse from an extremity, the application of a commercial tourniquet proximal to the wound is a life-saving intervention. Nurses and emergency responders must apply tourniquets tightly enough to eliminate distal pulses and stop arterial blood flow, documenting the exact time of application. Elevation of the bleeding extremity above the level of the heart can be used as an adjunct to direct pressure, provided there are no suspected fractures or musculoskeletal injuries that could be worsened by movement. If blood soaks through a dressing, the nurse should not remove the original dressing; instead, additional layers should be applied directly on top to preserve any fragile clots that have begun forming. Wound packing with hemostatic gauze is another advanced first aid technique used for junctional wounds in areas like the groin or axilla where tourniquets cannot be placed. Throughout these emergency procedures, the nurse must maintain universal precautions to protect against blood-borne pathogens while ensuring rapid airway, breathing, and circulation management.

Identification and Management of Internal Haemorrhage

Identification and management of internal haemorrhage present a significant challenge because bleeding occurs within closed body cavities, leaving no external evidence of blood loss. Common sites for massive internal haemorrhage include the abdominal cavity (from splenic rupture or liver laceration), the thoracic cavity (from rib fractures or hemothorax), the pelvic cavity, and the retroperitoneum. Patients suffering from internal bleeding often present with signs of hypovolemic shock—such as tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, and diaphoresis—accompanied by region-specific symptoms. For example, abdominal distension, rigidity, and tenderness suggest intra-abdominal bleeding, while absent breath sounds and dullness to percussion on one side of the chest indicate a haemothorax. Pelvic fractures can conceal liters of blood within the pelvic ring, presenting with localized instability and severe pain. Diagnostic modalities play a critical role in confirming internal bleeding; nurses must prepare patients for and assist with Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) exams, diagnostic peritoneal lavage, computed tomography (CT) scans, and upright chest or abdominal X-rays. Management requires urgent multidisciplinary collaboration, often necessitating emergency surgical exploration (such as exploratory laparotomy), damage control surgery, or interventional radiology procedures like transcatheter arterial embolisation. Continuous hemodynamic monitoring, frequent neurological checks, and strict preparation for rapid blood transfusion are fundamental nursing responsibilities in these high-acuity scenarios.

Nursing Interventions and Fluid Resuscitation

Nursing interventions and fluid resuscitation for haemorrhagic shock focus on restoring circulating volume, ensuring adequate tissue oxygenation, and correcting coagulopathy. Upon identifying acute haemorrhage, the nurse must immediately secure large-bore peripheral intravenous lines (typically 16-gauge or 18-gauge) to facilitate rapid fluid and blood product administration. Current resuscitation guidelines emphasize damage control resuscitation, shifting away from aggressive crystalloid loading (such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate) toward early administration of blood and blood products. Excessive crystalloid infusion can dilute clotting factors, disrupt fragile clots, cause hypothermia, and worsen acidosis—a lethal triad in trauma care. Therefore, protocols often recommend balanced transfusion ratios using packed red blood cells (PRBCs), fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and platelets to mimic whole blood. The nurse must strictly adhere to blood transfusion safety protocols, including dual-person patient identification, baseline vital sign recording, checking for compatibility, and monitoring for transfusion reactions. Furthermore, nurses should implement warming measures, such as fluid warmers and warm blankets, to prevent hypothermia, which impairs the coagulation cascade. Continuous monitoring of hemodynamic parameters, urine output, and serial laboratory values (hemoglobin, hematocrit, coagulation profiles, and arterial blood gases) allows the nurse to evaluate the efficacy of fluid resuscitation and make timely adjustments in collaboration with the medical team.

Complications of Severe Haemorrhage

Severe haemorrhage and the resulting hypovolemic shock can precipitate numerous life-threatening complications that require vigilant nursing monitoring and intervention. One of the most critical complications is the lethal triad of trauma: hypothermia, metabolic acidosis, and coagulopathy. Hypothermia impairs coagulation enzymes; acidosis (resulting from anaerobic metabolism and poor tissue perfusion) further inhibits the clotting cascade; and coagulopathy prevents effective haemostasis, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of worsening bleeding. Another major complication is Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), a systemic pathological condition where widespread microvascular clotting consumes platelets and clotting factors, paradoxically leading to severe, uncontrolled haemorrhage from multiple sites. Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) frequently occurs secondary to prolonged renal hypoperfusion during shock, manifesting as oliguria or anuria and requiring careful fluid management or dialysis. Furthermore, prolonged tissue hypoxia can cause Multiple Organ Dysfunction Syndrome (MODS), where two or more organ systems fail simultaneously, severely threatening patient survival. Nurses play a pivotal role in preventing and managing these complications by maintaining meticulous intake and output records, performing serial physical assessments, administering blood products and medications as prescribed, and maintaining close communication with the intensive care team to ensure optimal patient outcomes.
