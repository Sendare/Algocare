Fracture

Definition and Types of Fractures

A fracture is a break or disruption in the continuity of a bone. When the physical force applied to the bone is stronger than the bone can absorb, it cracks or completely snaps. Fractures are broadly categorized based on how the bone breaks and the skin condition over the site.

  • Closed (Simple) fracture: The bone is broken, but the overlying skin remains intact with no open wound.
  • Open (Compound) fracture: The broken bone punctures the skin, exposing the bone to the outside environment and a high risk of infection.
  • Complete fracture: The break goes completely through the bone, dividing it into two or more distinct pieces.
  • Incomplete fracture: The bone is cracked or buckled, but the break does not go all the way through.
  • Greenstick fracture: A common pediatric incomplete fracture where one side of the bone bends and breaks while the other side just bends.
  • Comminuted fracture: The bone is shattered or broken into three or more pieces at the fracture site.

Causes and Risk Factors

Bones are surprisingly resilient, yet certain forces and conditions strip them of their strength. Most fractures occur due to sudden, high-energy impact like motor vehicle accidents, physical assaults, or falls from a significant height. These are acute traumatic injuries.

However, not every break needs a major accident. Pathologic fractures happen in bones already weakened by an underlying disease process, such as osteoporosis, bone cancer, or severe infection. A person with advanced osteoporosis might break a hip simply from twisting awkwardly while standing.

Key risk factors include older age, postmenopausal hormone changes, poor dietary intake of calcium and Vitamin D, sedentary lifestyles, and chronic use of corticosteroid medications which gradually erode bone density.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

When a bone breaks, the body reacts swiftly, producing clear warning signs that nurses must recognize during assessment.

  • Pain: Immediate, sharp, and severe pain localized over the injury site, aggravated by movement.
  • Loss of function: The limb or body part cannot be used normally because the supportive skeletal framework is gone.
  • Deformity: Abnormal positioning, angulation, or a visible shortening of the injured limb compared to the healthy side.
  • Swelling and bruising: Localized bleeding into the soft tissues causes rapid edema and discoloration.
  • Crepitus: A crunching or grating sound and feeling when the broken bone ends rub against each other during movement. Nurses should avoid intentionally eliciting crepitus as it causes unnecessary pain and further tissue damage.
  • Neurovascular changes: Numbness, tingling, pale skin, or a weak pulse distal to the break indicate nerve or blood vessel compression.

Emergency First Aid Management

Immediate care at the scene of an injury prevents further damage to surrounding muscles, nerves, and blood vessels. The golden rule of fracture first aid is to immobilize the injury in the position it was found before moving the patient.

If the injury is an open fracture with exposed bone, do not attempt to push the bone back inside the wound. Cover the area loosely with a clean, sterile dressing to block dirt and bacteria. Control any active external bleeding by applying gentle direct pressure around the wound, taking care not to press directly over the protruding bone.

Splint the joints above and below the fracture site to keep the broken bone stable during transport to a health facility. Always recheck the distal pulse, movement, and sensation after applying any splint or bandage to ensure it is not tied too tightly.

Medical and Surgical Treatment Interventions

Healing a fracture requires restoring the normal alignment and length of the bone, a process called reduction, followed by immobilization to let the bone regrow.

  • Closed reduction: The doctor manually manipulates and pushes the bone fragments back into proper alignment using hands and traction, usually under local or general anesthesia. A plaster cast or fiberglass splint is then applied.
  • Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF): A surgical incision is made to directly view the fracture site, align the pieces, and secure them using metal plates, screws, rods, or pins.
  • External fixation: A surgical frame with metal pins inserted through the skin directly into the bone fragments above and below the break. This holds unstable or open fractures securely from the outside.
  • Traction: The application of a pulling force to align and immobilize a body part, though used less frequently today due to advanced surgical options.

Potential Complications

While many fractures heal without incident, serious local and systemic complications can arise. Early detection by vigilant nursing staff can save a limb or a life.

  • Compartment syndrome: Increased pressure within a confined muscle compartment restricts blood flow, causing severe, unremitting pain that is not relieved by strong pain medications. Classic signs include the 5 Ps: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, and paralysis. This is a surgical emergency requiring an immediate fasciotomy.
  • Fat embolism syndrome: Fat globules from the yellow marrow of a fractured long bone enter the bloodstream and travel to the lungs and brain, causing respiratory distress, petechiae on the chest, and confusion.
  • DVT and Pulmonary Embolism: Immobility promotes blood clot formation in the deep veins of the legs, which can break loose and travel to the lungs.
  • Malunion and Nonunion: The bone heals in an incorrect position (malunion) or fails to heal entirely after many months (nonunion).

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Comprehensive nursing care focuses on promoting bone healing, managing pain, preventing complications of immobility, and restoring independence.

For patients in plaster casts, nurses must monitor the cast edges for roughness, keep the cast dry, and never insert objects underneath the cast to scratch an itch, as this can break the skin and cause severe infection. Support wet casts with the palms of your hands, not your fingertips, to prevent indentations that can press against the skin and cause pressure ulcers.

Encourage deep breathing exercises and incentive spirometry to prevent pneumonia from bedrest. Promote early mobilization as prescribed, teach safe crutch or walker ambulation, and ensure adequate nutrition rich in protein, calcium, and Vitamin C to support tissue repair and bone remodeling.
