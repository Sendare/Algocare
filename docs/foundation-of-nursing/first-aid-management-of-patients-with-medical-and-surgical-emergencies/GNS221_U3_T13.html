Fracture

Definition and Classification of Fractures

A fracture represents a complete or partial disruption in the structural continuity of bone tissue. When mechanical force applied to the bone exceeds its tensile strength, a break occurs. Fractures are broadly classified based on the integrity of the surrounding skin, the pattern of the break, and the degree of displacement.

  • Closed (Simple) Fracture: The bone is broken, but the overlying skin remains intact, minimizing the immediate risk of external bacterial contamination.
  • Open (Compound) Fracture: The bone breaks through the skin or a wound extends down to the fracture site, carrying a high risk of osteomyelitis.
  • Transverse Fracture: The break occurs straight across the bone shaft at a right angle to the long axis.
  • Oblique Fracture: The break runs diagonally across the bone at an angle.
  • Spiral Fracture: The break twists around the bone shaft, frequently resulting from a rotational or torsional injury.
  • Comminuted Fracture: The bone fragments into three or more pieces at the injury site.

Pathophysiology of Bone Healing

Bone healing is a dynamic, continuous cellular process rather than simple scar formation. Unlike soft tissues that heal with fibrous scar tissue, bone regenerates back to its original structural architecture. The healing sequence follows five distinct overlapping stages:

  1. Hematoma Formation: Immediately following the break, blood vessels rupture, bleeding into the injury site and forming a localized blood clot within the first 24 to 72 hours.
  2. Inflammatory Phase: Granulation tissue invades the hematoma. Neutrophils and macrophages clean up cellular debris, releasing growth factors that stimulate angiogenesis.
  3. Soft Callus Formation: Fibroblasts and osteoblasts migrate to the area. Chondrocytes secrete a cartilaginous matrix, forming a fibrocartilaginous soft callus that stabilizes the bone ends.
  4. Hard Callus Formation: Osteoblasts deposit calcium and mineralize the soft matrix, converting it into woven bone over several weeks. This hard callus bridges the fracture gap.
  5. Remodeling: Osteoclasts resorb excess bony tissue while osteoblasts lay down new compact bone along lines of mechanical stress, eventually restoring the original bone contour.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Fractures stem from diverse mechanical forces and systemic vulnerabilities. High-impact trauma, such as motor vehicle collisions or falls from significant heights, easily overcomes the strength of healthy bone. In contrast, pathological fractures occur when underlying diseases weaken the bone structure, causing a break from minimal force or normal daily activities.

Metabolic bone disorders, particularly osteoporosis, stand as the leading predisposing factor among older adults, rendering trabecular bone porous and fragile. Nutritional deficiencies involving calcium and vitamin D impair bone density development during youth and maintenance in later life. Endocrine imbalances, long-term corticosteroid therapy, and localized bone malignancies further increase skeletal susceptibility. Recognizing these risk factors guides targeted health education and preventative nursing interventions in vulnerable populations.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Identifying a fracture requires a systematic physical assessment correlated with diagnostic imaging. Classic clinical indicators point directly to structural failure of the skeletal system.

  • Pain and Tenderness: Immediate, severe pain localized at the injury site, exacerbated by any attempted movement.
  • Deformity: Abnormal angulation, rotation, or shortening of the affected limb compared to the uninjured side.
  • Loss of Function: Inability to bear weight or effectively use the affected body part due to pain and mechanical instability.
  • Edema and Ecchymosis: Rapid accumulation of fluid and extravasation of blood into soft tissues surrounding the break.
  • Crepitus: A palpable or audible crunching sensation produced by bone ends rubbing together during movement.

Thorough neurovascular assessment—evaluating pulse, pallor, polar temperature, paresthesia, and paralysis—must accompany every orthopedic examination to rule out vascular or nerve compromise.

Emergency First Aid Management

Immediate pre-hospital care for a fracture aims to prevent further injury, minimize pain, and protect surrounding soft tissues, nerves, and blood vessels. The golden rule of emergency fracture care is to splint the injury 'as it lies' unless distal pulses are absent.

Rescuers should never attempt to manually push protruding bone ends back beneath the skin in open fractures, as this drives surface bacteria deep into the medullary cavity. Instead, cover open wounds with clean or sterile dressings to control bleeding and block contamination. Apply rigid or soft splints that immobilize the joints immediately above and below the fracture site. Elevate the extremity if possible and apply cold packs to limit local edema. Continuous monitoring of distal circulation remains mandatory until definitive medical care is established.

Medical and Surgical Treatment Interventions

Definitive fracture management centers on reduction—restoring bone fragments into anatomical alignment—followed by immobilization until healing occurs. Reduction can be closed through manual manipulation under analgesia or open through surgical exposure.

  • External Fixation: Pins or screws are placed through the skin into the bone above and below the fracture, connected to an external metal frame. Frequently used for open fractures with severe soft tissue damage.
  • Internal Fixation (ORIF): Open Reduction Internal Fixation utilizes surgical plates, screws, intramedullary nails, or wires to secure bone fragments directly.
  • Traction: The application of a pulling force to align and immobilize a body part, though largely replaced by modern surgical techniques in many settings.

Following successful reduction, casting or bracing maintains alignment while physiological bone healing proceeds undisturbed.

Potential Complications

While many fractures heal without incident, local and systemic complications can develop, altering recovery trajectories and threatening life or limb. Prompt recognition by nursing staff is vital for preventing permanent damage.

  • Compartment Syndrome: Increased pressure within a confined muscle fascial compartment restricts blood flow, causing severe, unrelenting pain unresponsive to opioids, diminished pulses, and potential nerve ischemia.
  • Fat Embolism Syndrome: Yellow bone marrow fat globules enter the bloodstream after long bone fractures, traveling to the lungs and brain, presenting with petechiae, respiratory distress, and altered mental status.
  • Osteomyelitis: Severe bone infection resulting from open fractures or surgical contamination, characterized by localized inflammation, fever, and purulent drainage.
  • Delayed Union or Nonunion: Failure of the fracture to heal within the expected timeframe or complete cessation of bone repair processes.

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Comprehensive nursing care extends from acute stabilization through long-term physical rehabilitation. Nurses monitor casted and surgically repaired extremities vigilantly, performing frequent neurovascular checks and assessing cast integrity. Edema reduction strategies, including limb elevation and cold therapy, prevent secondary tissue damage.

Pain management combines pharmacological interventions with non-pharmacological comfort measures. Nurses educate patients on safe crutch or walker mobility, isometric muscle-contracting exercises to prevent muscle atrophy, and signs of complications to report immediately. Nutritional support emphasizing protein, calcium, and vitamin D accelerates tissue repair, helping patients regain optimal functional independence.
