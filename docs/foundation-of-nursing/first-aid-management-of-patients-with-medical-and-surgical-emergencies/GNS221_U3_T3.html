Fever

Normal Body Temperature Regulation

Body temperature is tightly controlled by the hypothalamus, which acts as the body's internal thermostat. This area of the brain balances heat production with heat loss to keep core body temperature within a safe, narrow range, typically around 36.5°C to 37.5°C. Heat is generated primarily through cellular metabolism, muscle activity, and shivering. Conversely, the body loses excess heat through radiation, conduction, convection, and evaporation of sweat. When external or internal conditions shift, the hypothalamus adjusts blood flow to the skin and triggers sweating or shivering to restore balance.

Definition and Pathophysiology of Fever

A fever, clinically known as pyrexia, occurs when the hypothalamic set point is elevated in response to an immune challenge. Unlike hyperthermia, which is an uncontrolled rise in body temperature due to environmental heat or metabolic failure, fever is a regulated physiological defense mechanism.

  1. Pyrogen release: Pathogens, damaged tissues, or immune cells release substances called exogenous pyrogens.
  2. Prostaglandin production: These pyrogens stimulate the release of endogenous pyrogens, leading the hypothalamus to synthesize prostaglandin E2.
  3. Set point elevation: Prostaglandin E2 raises the hypothalamic thermal set point, making the body feel cold and triggering chills to generate more heat.
  4. Resolution: Once the immune threat diminishes, pyrogens decrease, the set point drops back to normal, and the body initiates heat loss through sweating.

Clinical Assessment and Temperature Measurement Techniques

Accurate temperature measurement is essential for diagnosing and monitoring fever. Different routes provide varying estimates of core body temperature.

RouteClinical NoteSafety Considerations
OralConvenient, reflects core temperature wellUnsuitable for unconscious or confused patients
TympanicFast, measures infrared energy from the eardrumMust position the ear canal correctly; earwax can alter readings
AxillarySafe, non-invasive, good for routine screeningGenerally lower than core temperature; takes longer
RectalMost accurate reflection of true core temperatureInvasive; risk of mucosal trauma if done improperly

During assessment, nurses also look for accompanying signs such as tachycardia, flushed skin, sweating, and chills.

Pharmacological Interventions in Fever Control

Managing fever with medications involves using antipyretics, which work primarily by inhibiting the enzyme cyclooxygenase (COX) in the central nervous system. This blockage prevents the synthesis of prostaglandin E2, allowing the hypothalamus to reset its temperature target back to normal.

  • Paracetamol (Acetaminophen): Widely used due to its strong safety profile and minimal gastric irritation when dosed correctly.
  • Nonsteroidal Anti-inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs): Medications like ibuprofen provide effective fever and pain relief by reducing inflammation, but they should be used cautiously in patients with kidney problems or stomach ulcers.

Nurses must verify weight-based dosages in children and monitor total daily intake to prevent toxicity, particularly liver damage from paracetamol overdose.

Potential Complications and Febrile Seizures

While fever is a helpful defense mechanism, high temperatures increase metabolic demand. The body consumes more oxygen, heart rate accelerates, and fluid losses through respiration and sweating rise significantly. Dehydration is a common risk, especially in young children and older adults.

In children between the ages of six months and five years, a rapid spike in temperature can sometimes trigger a febrile seizure. These short, generalized seizures frighten parents, but simple febrile seizures typically last less than fifteen minutes and cause no permanent brain damage. Nursing care during a seizure focuses on maintaining a clear airway, protecting the child from physical injury, and observing the exact duration and characteristics of the event.

Patient Education and Home Care Guidance

Empowering patients and caregivers with accurate home care information prevents panic and promotes safe recovery. Nurses should instruct families to focus on comfort rather than aggressively forcing the temperature down to normal at all costs. Adequate hydration with clean water, oral rehydration salts, or clear broths is essential to prevent dehydration from increased sweating.

Caregivers should also learn about warning signs that require immediate medical evaluation. These red flags include persistent vomiting, difficulty breathing, a stiff neck, extreme lethargy, a non-blanching rash, or a fever that fails to respond to antipyretics and lasts for more than a few days.
