Bites

Introduction and Classification of Bite Injuries

Bite injuries present a unique challenge in clinical practice because they combine mechanical tissue destruction with heavy microbial contamination. In Nigerian emergency departments, mammalian bites—particularly from domestic dogs and humans—are exceedingly common, alongside various venomous and non-venomous snakebites in rural agricultural belts. Understanding the source of the injury dictates the initial treatment strategy, as human, canine, feline, and reptilian bites carry vastly different pathogen profiles and tissue damage potentials. Feline bites, for instance, create deep puncture wounds that easily trap bacteria, whereas human bites feature a high density of oral flora capable of causing severe soft tissue necrosis.

Pathophysiology and Microbiology of Bite Wounds

The pathological impact of a bite stems from two distinct mechanisms: physical trauma and microbial inoculation. - **Crushing and Puncturing**: Teeth inflict lacerations, avulsions, or deep punctures that compromise vascular supply and create ideal anaerobic pockets for bacterial proliferation. - **Polymicrobial Contamination**: Wound cultures typically yield a mix of aerobic and anaerobic organisms derived from the biter's oral cavity and the victim's skin. - **Specific Pathogens**: *Pasteurella* species are frequently isolated from dog and cat bites, while human bites routinely harbor *Eikenella corrodens* and *Staphylococcus aureus*. Without prompt mechanical cleansing, these microorganisms rapidly multiply within the damaged matrix, progressing from localized cellulitis to deep space infections, tenosynovitis, or systemic sepsis.

Assessment, Triage, and History Taking

Initial evaluation requires a systematic approach to gauge both immediate life threats and the extent of local tissue damage. Nurses must quickly determine the exact time of the incident, the animal species or human involved, and the immunization status of the patient—focusing specifically on tetanus. During physical examination, assess neurovascular status distal to the wound. Check for capillary refill, sensory deficits, and active motor function to rule out tendon or nerve laceration. Document the depth of the wound meticulously, noting whether periarticular structures or joint spaces have been breached, as puncture wounds over the knuckles from clenched-fist injuries demand high surgical suspicion.

First Aid and Immediate Pre-Hospital Management

Immediate first aid dramatically reduces the bacterial load and lowers the subsequent risk of infection. 1. **Irrigation**: Flush the wound vigorously with large volumes of sterile normal saline or clean running water under moderate pressure using a syringe. 2. **Debridement**: Remove any visible foreign debris or devitalized tissue if trained and equipped to do so safely. 3. **Antisepsis**: Apply a mild antiseptic solution, avoiding caustic agents like concentrated hydrogen peroxide or iodine that can cause further cellular damage. 4. **Elevation**: Elevate the affected limb to minimize local edema accumulation. Avoid immediate primary closure of puncture wounds or high-risk human bites in the field, as trapping bacteria inside closed tissues invites severe abscess formation.

Medical and Surgical Interventions in the Hospital

Hospital-based care moves beyond basic cleansing into targeted antimicrobial therapy and definitive surgical repair. Physicians typically prescribe prophylactic or therapeutic broad-spectrum antibiotics, such as amoxicillin-clavulanate, to cover the mixed flora typical of animal and human bites. Surgical intervention becomes necessary for deep wounds, joint involvement, or established abscesses. Operating theatres facilitate thorough surgical debridement, washout of tendon sheaths, and delayed primary closure once the infection risk subsides. For suspected rabid animal exposures, prompt administration of rabies immunoglobulin and the multi-dose vaccine series is initiated according to national public health protocols.

Infection Control and Prevention of Complications

Monitoring for secondary complications is a core nursing responsibility throughout the recovery phase. Nurse-led surveillance includes tracking local inflammatory markers such as spreading erythema, increasing pain, purulent discharge, and localized warmth. Systemic complications like sepsis, osteomyelitis, and tenosynovitis require vigilant observation, especially in vulnerable populations such as the elderly, diabetic individuals, or immunosuppressed patients. Strict adherence to aseptic technique during dressing changes prevents nosocomial superinfections, while timely administration of tetanus toxoid boosters ensures long-term humoral protection against *Clostridium tetani*.

Patient Education and Discharge Planning

Discharge preparation ensures continuity of care once the patient leaves the clinical facility. Nurses must instruct patients on proper wound hygiene, emphasizing gentle daily cleansing and dry dressing changes as prescribed. Education must also highlight warning signs that mandate immediate return to the hospital, such as red streaks tracking up the limb, fever, or pus drainage. Emphasize the absolute necessity of completing the full course of prescribed antibiotics rather than stopping when symptoms improve. Finally, provide guidance on animal safety and rabies follow-up schedules where relevant.
