Bites

Introduction to Bites in Emergency Care

Bite injuries present a common and potentially complex challenge in emergency care settings across Nigeria, ranging from domestic animal encounters to venomous snakebites and human altercations. For nursing students, understanding the acute presentation of bites is vital because delayed or mismanaged injuries can rapidly escalate into severe systemic infections, tissue necrosis, or life-threatening systemic toxicity. Animal bites, particularly from dogs and cats, carry a high risk of bacterial contamination due to the oral flora of the biter, while human bites are often considered the most contaminated due to the dense concentration of anaerobic and aerobic microorganisms in the human mouth. Furthermore, venomous snakebites pose a significant public health burden in rural agricultural regions of Nigeria, requiring rapid clinical identification and the timely administration of antivenom. Emergency nurses must approach every bite incident with a high index of suspicion, assessing not only the visible local trauma but also the potential for hidden deep tissue damage, neurovascular compromise, and systemic complications. Prompt recognition, systematic physical assessment, and adherence to established emergency protocols form the bedrock of reducing morbidity and mortality associated with bite injuries in clinical practice.

Pathophysiology and Types of Bites

The pathophysiology of bite injuries depends heavily on the mechanism of injury and the specific organism involved. Animal bites typically create puncture wounds, lacerations, or crush injuries. Puncture wounds, common from cat teeth, drive bacteria deep into fascial planes, tendons, and joints while appearing deceptively minor on the surface, frequently resulting in deep-space infections such as tenosynovitis or osteomyelitis. Dog bites, possessing greater jaw strength, cause extensive tissue tearing and crush injuries that compromise local tissue perfusion and create ideal anaerobic environments for bacterial proliferation. Common pathogens introduced include Pasteurella species, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus species in animal bites, whereas human bites frequently involve Eikenella corrodens alongside oral anaerobes. Venomous bites, particularly from snakes, introduce complex enzymatic toxins that disrupt blood coagulation, destroy endothelial linings, cause local tissue destruction, and induce neurotoxicity or systemic shock. Understanding these pathophysiological mechanisms allows the nurse to anticipate complications, recognize early signs of localized spreading infection, and correlate physical findings with the specific organisms most likely to be cultured from the wound.

Assessment and Triage of Bite Injuries

Assessment and triage of a patient presenting with a bite injury require a structured, systematic approach beginning with the standard primary survey (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) to stabilize any life-threatening systemic collapse, especially in cases of anaphylaxis, severe envenomation, or massive hemorrhage. Once the patient is stabilized, the secondary survey focuses on a detailed history and physical examination of the bite site. Nurses must ascertain the time of the bite, the type of animal or individual involved, the vaccination status of the patient (particularly tetanus and rabies history), and whether the animal can be quarantined or observed. During the physical assessment, the nurse should document the exact location, dimensions, and depth of the wound, assess for foreign bodies like broken teeth, and meticulously check distal neurovascular status, including capillary refill, motor function, and sensory integrity. Systemic signs of infection such as fever, tachycardia, or spreading erythema must be documented. Triage prioritization should elevate patients exhibiting signs of airway compromise, systemic envenomation, compartment syndrome, or rapidly spreading cellulitis to high-acuity treatment areas for immediate medical intervention.

First Aid and Immediate Pre-Hospital Management

First aid and pre-hospital management can significantly alter the clinical trajectory of a bite injury by minimizing bacterial load and local tissue destruction. Immediate actions include copious irrigation of the wound using large volumes of clean water or sterile normal saline under moderate pressure to mechanically wash away debris and bacteria. Harsh antiseptics or strong chemical agents should be avoided as they may further damage delicate tissues and exacerbate local necrosis. For bleeding control, direct pressure should be applied using clean dressings; tourniquets are generally discouraged in routine animal bites unless there is life-threatening arterial hemorrhage that cannot be otherwise controlled, and they are strictly contraindicated as a standard first aid measure for elapid snakebites due to the risk of severe localized tissue ischemia and gangrene. In the case of venomous snakebites, traditional harmful practices such as making cuts over the bite mark, attempting oral suction, or applying tight ligatures must be strongly discouraged through community health education. The affected limb should be kept immobilized in a neutral, dependent or slightly elevated position depending on guidelines, and the patient must be transported calmly and swiftly to the nearest healthcare facility for definitive care.

Medical and Surgical Interventions in the Hospital

Upon arrival at the hospital, medical and surgical interventions for bite injuries are tailored to the severity and nature of the wound. Physician and nursing teams collaborate to administer appropriate pharmacotherapy, which typically includes prophylactic or therapeutic broad-spectrum antibiotics (such as amoxicillin-clavulanate) to cover common oral pathogens. Tetanus toxoid and, when indicated, tetanus immunoglobulin must be administered promptly based on the patient's immunization history. For patients with confirmed venomous snakebites exhibiting systemic coagulopathy or neurotoxicity, weight-based administration of polyvalent or monovalent antivenom is initiated under close monitoring for potential anaphylactic reactions. Surgically, wounds are evaluated for debridement; heavily contaminated, crushed, or high-risk human bites are often left open (healing by secondary intention) to prevent trapping bacteria in deep tissue spaces. Primary closure may be considered for select low-risk facial dog bites executed within a strict time window, provided thorough cleansing and debridement have been performed. Nurses play an essential role in monitoring vital signs, tracking intake and output, managing intravenous access, and administering pain relief and emergency medications as prescribed.

Prevention of Complications and Infection Control

Preventing complications and maintaining strict infection control are paramount nursing responsibilities throughout the continuum of care for bite victims. Nurses must implement standard precautions during all wound dressings and debridement procedures, utilizing gloves, gowns, and eye protection to prevent occupational exposure to bloodborne pathogens and infectious exudate. Continuous monitoring for local signs of infection—such as increasing erythema, localized warmth, purulent discharge, edema, and worsening pain—allows for early escalation of antibiotic therapy before sepsis develops. Furthermore, healthcare providers must evaluate the need for rabies post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) following exposures from potentially rabid animals like dogs, bats, or monkeys, which involves a specific series of rabies vaccinations and rabies immune globulin administered according to national health guidelines. Environmental hygiene, proper biomedical waste disposal of soiled dressings, and strict aseptic technique during dressing changes are vital institutional measures to prevent cross-contamination and nosocomial infections within the emergency department or inpatient ward.

Patient Education and Discharge Planning

Comprehensive patient education and structured discharge planning ensure continuity of care and prevent recurrent injuries once the patient leaves the healthcare facility. The nurse must provide clear, verbal and written instructions on proper wound care, including how to perform gentle daily cleansing, how to apply prescribed topical or oral medications, and how to recognize red-flag symptoms that necessitate immediate return to the hospital, such as spreading redness, red streaks, fever, or worsening pain. Patients should be educated on completing their full course of prescribed antibiotics even if symptoms improve. For animal bite victims, education must cover safe handling practices with pets, avoiding unfamiliar stray animals, and monitoring the biting animal for signs of illness over a ten-day observation period where applicable. Discharge planning should also coordinate follow-up appointments for wound re-checks, suture removal, and scheduled doses of multi-dose vaccine regimens like tetanus boosters or rabies series completions. By empowering patients and their families with this knowledge, nurses actively promote safe healing and long-term injury prevention within the community.
