Bites

Introduction and Types of Bite Wounds

Bite injuries are a common presentation in emergency departments and primary care clinics, resulting from animals, humans, or insects. Each type of bite carries unique risks depending on the oral flora of the biter and the mechanics of the jaw. Understanding these distinctions helps nurses anticipate complications like severe infection or tissue destruction.

  • Animal Bites: Primarily caused by dogs and cats. Dog bites often involve crush injury with tearing of skin and underlying tissue due to powerful jaws. Cat bites feature sharp, slender teeth that act like hypodermic needles, inoculating bacteria deep into joints or tendon sheaths.
  • Human Bites: Considered clinically more dangerous than most animal bites because human saliva contains a dense mixture of virulent pathogens, including *Eikenella corrodens* and *Staphylococcus aureus*. They often occur during altercations (fight bites over the knuckles) or as accidental injuries in children.
  • Insect and Spider Bites: Range from minor local irritation to systemic envenomation or severe allergic reactions like anaphylaxis.

Pathophysiology and Infection Risks

When teeth penetrate the skin barrier, the human or animal oral flora is directly introduced into sterile tissue planes. This creates an ideal anaerobic or aerobic environment for bacterial proliferation.

The most frequent culprit in dog and cat bite infections is *Pasteurella multocida*, which can cause rapidly spreading cellulitis within hours of the incident. Other common pathogens include *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus* species. In human bites, *Eikenella corrodens* is a notable anaerobic bacterium that thrives in deep puncture wounds and is resistant to certain standard first-line antibiotics.

Beyond localized bacterial growth, deep bites can compromise blood vessels, nerves, and tendons, leading to ischemia, permanent nerve damage, or loss of function if not managed promptly.

Initial Assessment and Triage

A systematic nursing assessment is critical upon the patient's arrival. Triage priority is determined by airway, breathing, circulation (ABCs), followed by a focused examination of the injured site.

  1. History Taking: Establish the exact time of the bite, the animal or person involved, whether the animal can be quarantined for rabies observation, and the patient's tetanus vaccination history.
  2. Vascular and Neurological Check: Assess distal pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature, motor function, and sensation below the injury site to rule out nerve or vessel laceration.
  3. Wound Characteristics: Inspect whether the injury is a clean laceration, a crush wound, or a deep puncture. Punctures carry the highest risk of occult infection because they are difficult to irrigate thoroughly.

First Aid and Pre-Hospital Management

Immediate first aid can drastically lower the bacterial load in a fresh bite wound before it develops into a clinical infection.

  • Irrigation: Vigorously flush the wound with large volumes of sterile normal saline or clean tap water under pressure using a syringe. This mechanical action dislodges bacteria and debris.
  • Cleansing: Gently wash the surrounding skin with mild soap and water. Avoid harsh antiseptics like hydrogen peroxide or iodine directly inside deep wounds, as they can damage healthy granulation tissue and delay healing.
  • Elevation: Elevate the injured limb if possible to minimize localized edema and swelling.
  • Avoid Premature Closure: Except for simple facial lacerations managed by a physician for cosmetic reasons, most puncture and deep animal bites are left open to heal by secondary intention to prevent trapping bacteria inside.

Medical Interventions and Prophylaxis

Hospital management often involves targeted pharmacological therapy and surgical consultation based on the severity of tissue involvement.

  • Antibiotic Prophylaxis: Not every bite requires antibiotics, but high-risk wounds—such as cat bites, deep punctures, human bites, and wounds on immunocompromised patients or the hands—typically warrant a prophylactic course of broad-spectrum antibiotics like amoxicillin-clavulanate.
  • Tetanus Immunization: Administer a tetanus toxoid-containing vaccine if the patient has not received a booster within the last 5 years for dirty or high-risk wounds, or 10 years for clean minor wounds. Tetanus immune globulin (TIG) may be added for non-immunized individuals.
  • Rabies Prophylaxis: If bitten by a high-risk animal (like bats, raccoons, or unvaccinated dogs) where the animal cannot be observed, a regimen of rabies immune globulin and a series of vaccinations is initiated immediately.

Infectious Complications and Ongoing Monitoring

Even with proper initial treatment, bite wounds can deteriorate. Nurses play a vital role in monitoring hospitalized patients or educating outpatients on what warning signs to watch for.

ComplicationDescription & Nursing Observation
Cellulitis & AbscessSpreading redness, warmth, induration, and purulent drainage around the wound site.
TenosynovitisInfection within tendon sheaths, often marked by the four Kanavel signs: uniform finger swelling, resting flexion of the digit, tenderness along the sheath, and pain with passive extension.
OsteomyelitisDeep bone infection occurring when teeth penetrate directly down to the periosteum, requiring prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy.

Regularly reassess vital signs for systemic systemic inflammatory signs such as fever, tachycardia, and leukocytosis.

Patient Education and Discharge Instructions

Comprehensive discharge teaching ensures continuity of care and prevents avoidable readmissions. Nurses must provide clear, verbal and written instructions before the patient leaves the facility.

Instruct the patient to keep the affected limb elevated to reduce swelling and to finish the entire prescribed course of antibiotics, even if symptoms improve within a couple of days. Emphasize meticulous hand hygiene before touching dressing changes.

Provide clear parameters on when to return immediately to the emergency department, such as fever, chills, red streaks spreading away from the wound, numbness, or inability to move the fingers or toes.
