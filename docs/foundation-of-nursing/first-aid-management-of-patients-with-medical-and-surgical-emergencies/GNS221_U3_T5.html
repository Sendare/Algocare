Altered Level of Consciousness

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Altered Consciousness

Consciousness relies on two main components functioning together: the reticular activating system (RAS) located in the brainstem, and the cerebral hemispheres. When a pathological process disrupts either the RAS or both sides of the cerebral cortex, a person experiences an altered level of consciousness (ALOC). This condition ranges from mild confusion to deep coma.

Etiologies are broadly divided into structural and metabolic categories. Structural causes involve physical damage, such as traumatic brain injury, stroke, or brain tumors. Metabolic causes involve systemic chemical disturbances that starve or poison the brain cells, including severe hypoxia, hypoglycemia, electrolyte imbalances, and drug overdoses. A common clinical mistake is assuming every unresponsive patient has suffered a stroke; rapid screening must rule out easily reversible metabolic insults like low blood sugar.

Classification and Assessment Scales

Clinical evaluation of consciousness requires standardized tools to communicate patient status accurately across healthcare teams. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is the universal standard, evaluating three distinct behavioral responses:

  1. Eye Opening: Ranging from spontaneous opening to no response at all.
  2. Verbal Response: Ranging from fully oriented conversation to incomprehensible sounds or silence.
  3. Motor Response: Ranging from obeying simple commands to abnormal posturing or flaccid paralysis.

Other descriptive terms help paint a clear clinical picture before assigning a numerical score. Lethargy describes a state of drowsiness where the patient rouses to speech. Stupor means the patient only responds to vigorous and painful stimuli. Coma indicates total unresponsiveness where the patient cannot be awakened even with painful stimuli.

Initial Emergency Triage and Primary Survey

When an unresponsive or confused patient arrives at the emergency unit, nurses follow the ABCDE priority framework to stabilize life-threatening issues first.

  • Airway: Assess for patency. Clear any secretions, blood, or vomit, and insert an oral airway if the gag reflex is absent.
  • Breathing: Check respiratory rate, depth, and oxygen saturation. Administer supplemental oxygen if saturation drops below normal limits.
  • Circulation: Measure pulse, blood pressure, and capillary refill time. Establish intravenous access immediately for fluid resuscitation or emergency medications.
  • Disability: Perform a quick neurological check using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or GCS, and check pupil reaction to light.
  • Exposure: Undress the patient briefly to inspect for hidden trauma, needle tracks, or rashes, while preventing hypothermia.

Immediate Nursing Management and Life Support

Immediate care for a patient with compromised consciousness focuses on protecting vital organ function and identifying the root trigger. Nurses must maintain effective ventilation, often preparing for endotracheal intubation if the GCS score falls to 8 or lower, following the old clinical rule: 'less than eight, intubate.'

Intravenous access is secured for drawing baseline blood investigations, including complete blood count, electrolyte panels, toxicology screens, and blood glucose. Administering emergency reversal agents such as naloxone for suspected opioid overdose or thiamine followed by glucose for suspected alcohol dependency can be lifesaving diagnostic steps. Position the patient on their side in the recovery position if they are breathing spontaneously but remain unconscious, which prevents tongue displacement and aspiration of oral secretions.

Ongoing Nursing Interventions and Monitoring

Continuous observation is essential because a patient's neurological status can deteriorate rapidly. Nurses perform frequent neurological checks, comparing pupil size, symmetry, and light reactivity against baseline values. Unequal or sluggish pupils often signal dangerous increases in intracranial pressure (ICP) or brain herniation.

Monitoring vital signs reveals subtle trends; Cushing's triad—consisting of widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations—is a late, ominous sign of severe intracranial pressure elevation. Beyond neurology, care includes maintaining skin integrity through regular turning, providing passive range of motion exercises, protecting the corneas with lubricating drops if eyelids remain open, and monitoring strict fluid balance through an indwelling urinary catheter.

Prevention of Complications

Prolonged alteration of consciousness exposes patients to numerous secondary physical complications that nurses can actively prevent through meticulous care planning. Immobility leads quickly to pressure injuries, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and muscle contractures. Using specialized mattresses, applying sequential compression devices, and performing scheduled repositioning mitigate these risks.

Pulmonary hygiene is another major focus. Unconscious individuals cannot clear their lungs, making them prime targets for hypostatic pneumonia. Regular suctioning, chest physiotherapy, and elevating the head of the bed to thirty degrees help keep secretions moving and reduce aspiration risks. Finally, nutritional support must be initiated early via enteral tube feeding if unconsciousness persists beyond a few days, preventing the catabolic breakdown of body tissues.
