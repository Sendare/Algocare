Altered Level of Consciousness

Introduction to Altered Level of Consciousness

Consciousness requires the normal functioning of both the cerebral hemispheres and the **reticular activating system (RAS)** located within the brainstem. When either component suffers disruption from metabolic, structural, or toxic insults, the individual experiences an **altered level of consciousness (ALOC)**. This condition ranges from mild confusion and lethargy to deep, unarousable coma. Recognizing these subtle shifts early prevents irreversible neurological damage. In busy Nigerian emergency wards, a patient presenting with quiet withdrawal or mild disorientation is frequently misattributed to fatigue or spiritual factors, delaying critical medical evaluation.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The triggers for altered consciousness are broadly divided into **structural neurological lesions** and **toxic-metabolic derangements**. Structural causes physically compress or destroy brain tissue, such as intracranial hemorrhage, ischemic stroke, brain tumors, or traumatic brain injury. Toxic-metabolic causes disrupt cellular energy production across the entire brain without a focal mass effect; common examples include severe hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, uremia, hepatic encephalopathy, and exogenous drug overdoses. Hypoxia and severe hypotension also rapidly compromise cerebral perfusion, shutting down the energy-dependent sodium-potassium pumps required for normal neuronal firing.

Classification and Scales of Consciousness

- **Alertness**: The patient is fully awake, oriented to person, time, and place, and responds appropriately to stimuli. - **Lethargy**: The patient appears drowsy or sluggish but can be roused to speak and answer questions before drifting back to sleep. - **Obtundation**: The patient sleeps most of the time, responds slowly to verbal prompts, and shows diminished interest in the environment. - **Stupor**: The patient remains largely unresponsive, waking only briefly and defensively to vigorous or painful physical stimuli. - **Coma**: The patient is completely unarousable, failing to open eyes, speak, or obey commands even with painful stimulation. The **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** provides a standardized numerical tool across these states, evaluating eye opening, verbal response, and motor response.

Initial Assessment and Triage in Emergency Settings

Initial evaluation follows the **ABCDE approach**—Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure. Securing a patent airway takes immediate precedence if the patient cannot protect it independently. Under the 'Disability' component, quickly assess pupillary light reflexes, blood glucose levels via a bedside glucometer, and calculate the GCS score. A bedside finger-prick test is a non-negotiable first step in any unstable or unresponsive patient because undetected hypoglycemia mimics severe neurological emergencies and causes permanent brain injury within minutes if left untreated.

Nursing Interventions and Continuous Monitoring

Once immediate stabilization is achieved, nursing care pivots to maintaining physiological stability and preventing secondary brain injury. Position unconscious patients in a **lateral recovery position** or elevate the head of the bed to thirty degrees to minimize aspiration risks and promote venous cerebral drainage. Perform frequent neurological checks, tracking trends in pupillary size, light reactivity, and motor symmetry. Monitor intake and output rigorously, keeping bladder distension in check. Document any subtle alterations promptly, as a dropping GCS score signals worsening intracranial pressure or ongoing cerebral ischemia.

Potential Complications and Prevention Strategies

Prolonged alteration of consciousness exposes patients to severe secondary complications. Immobility leads rapidly to pressure injuries, deep vein thrombosis, and contractures. Loss of protective airway reflexes invites aspiration pneumonia, a frequent cause of mortality in long-term unconsciousness. Corneal abrasions develop easily if unblinking eyes remain unprotected from drying. Preventive nursing strategies include scheduled turning every two hours, passive range-of-motion exercises, application of lubricating eye drops with protective taping, and meticulous oral hygiene to curtail bacterial colonization in the oropharynx.
