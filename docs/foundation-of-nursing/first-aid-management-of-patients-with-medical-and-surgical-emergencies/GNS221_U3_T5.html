Altered Level of Consciousness

Introduction to Altered Level of Consciousness

Altered level of consciousness refers to any state where a patient is not fully awake, alert, and oriented to person, place, and time. This clinical sign ranges from mild confusion to deep coma, indicating a disruption in the normal functioning of the cerebral hemispheres or the reticular activating system. In clinical practice across Nigerian health facilities, recognizing this shift promptly helps prevent irreversible brain damage. Nurses must observe subtle changes in responsiveness, as a delay in identifying declining consciousness can lead to fatal outcomes.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The brain requires a constant supply of oxygen and glucose to maintain consciousness. Any insult that disrupts cerebral perfusion, metabolic balance, or structural integrity can alter mental status. Common etiologies are categorized using the mnemonic AEIOU-TIPS, which covers Alcohol, Epilepsy, Insulin errors, Uremia, Trauma, Infection, Poisoning, and Stroke.

  • Metabolic disturbances: Hypoglycemia, severe electrolyte imbalances, and hepatic encephalopathy.
  • Structural lesions: Brain tumors, intracranial hemorrhage, and cerebral edema.
  • Toxins and drugs: Opioid overdose, alcohol intoxication, and organophosphate poisoning.
  • Infections: Meningitis, cerebral malaria, and sepsis-associated encephalopathy.

Classification and Scales of Consciousness

Objective tools are essential to monitor changes in a patient's neurological status over time. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) remains the global standard, evaluating three distinct behavioral responses: eye opening, verbal performance, and motor responsiveness.

A lower total score signifies severe neurological impairment. For rapid bedside screenings, nurses frequently use the AVPU mnemonic:

  • Alert: The patient is fully awake and responsive to environment.
  • Verbal: The patient responds only when spoken to.
  • Pain: The patient reacts exclusively to painful stimuli.
  • Unresponsive: The patient shows no reaction to any stimulus.

Initial Assessment and Triage in Emergency Settings

Emergency management of an unresponsive patient follows the primary survey framework: Airway, Breathing, and Circulation. Nurses must quickly clear the airway, assess breathing effort, and check pulse stability before conducting a detailed neurological examination. A common misconception is that a full neurological history must take precedence over stabilization. Immediate bedside checks must include a rapid blood glucose test using a glucometer to rule out reversible hypoglycemia.

First Aid and Immediate Life Support Management

Once immediate life threats are managed, supportive care aims to protect brain function and prevent secondary injury. The unconscious patient must be placed in the recovery position (lateral recumbent position) to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. Oxygen therapy is administered if oxygen saturation drops below normal thresholds. Intravenous access is secured for fluid resuscitation and emergency medications. Restraints should be avoided unless the patient poses an immediate danger to themselves or clinical staff during periods of agitation.

Nursing Interventions and Continuous Monitoring

Continuous observation is vital to detect sudden neurological deterioration. Nurses track vital signs hourly, watching for Cushing's triad—bradycardia, widened pulse pressure, and irregular respirations—which signals dangerously high intracranial pressure.

Eye care is critical for patients who cannot blink, requiring regular saline drops to prevent corneal ulceration. Passive range of motion exercises prevent joint contractures. Nutritional support via nasogastric tube feeding is initiated once the acute phase stabilizes, provided bowel sounds are present.

Potential Complications and Prevention Strategies

Prolonged altered consciousness exposes patients to severe secondary complications. Immobility leads to pressure ulcers, deep vein thrombosis, and hypostatic pneumonia. Bladder dysfunction requires careful catheter management to avoid urinary tract infections. Nurses prevent these risks through diligent turning schedules every two hours, early mobilization protocols when feasible, and meticulous hygiene practices.
