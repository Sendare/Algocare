Altered Level of Consciousness

Introduction to Altered Level of Consciousness

Altered Level of Consciousness (ALOC) is a critical neurological emergency frequently encountered in nursing practice across Nigerian healthcare settings, ranging from bustling emergency departments in urban tertiary hospitals to primary rural health clinics. Consciousness is maintained by the delicate, continuous interplay between the cerebral hemispheres and the Reticular Activating System (RAS) located within the brainstem. When this complex network is disrupted by structural injury, metabolic imbalance, toxins, or hypoxia, the patient's awareness of self and environment becomes compromised. As nursing students preparing for professional practice, understanding ALOC is vital because prompt recognition directly impacts patient morbidity and mortality. A patient with an altered sensorium may present with anything from mild lethargy and confusion to deep, unarousable coma. In resource-constrained environments, nurses must rely heavily on rigorous baseline assessments, sharp clinical observation, and a solid understanding of neuroanatomy to detect subtle changes in mentation before irreversible neurological damage occurs. Recognizing that ALOC is never a definitive diagnosis in itself, but rather a profound symptom of an underlying systemic or neurological disturbance, forms the cornerstone of effective nursing care. Nurses serve as the primary sentinel in monitoring neurological trends, ensuring that any deterioration is communicated and managed immediately.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The pathophysiology of Altered Level of Consciousness generally stems from two main categories of neurological dysfunction: diffuse bilateral cerebral hemispheric disease and disorders affecting the brainstem Reticular Activating System. These can be further divided into primary neurological causes and secondary systemic causes. Primary insults include traumatic brain injury (TBI), stroke (cerebrovascular accident), intracranial hemorrhage, central nervous system infections such as meningitis or cerebral malaria—which is highly prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa—and space-occupying lesions like brain tumors or abscesses. Secondary systemic causes occur when the brain is deprived of its vital metabolic substrates, oxygen, and glucose, or when it is exposed to circulating toxins. Common metabolic triggers include severe hypoglycemia, diabetic ketoacidosis, hepatic encephalopathy due to liver failure, uremia from renal shutdown, and severe electrolyte imbalances such as severe hyponatremia. Furthermore, hypoxia, hypercapnia, systemic sepsis, and exogenous intoxications from alcohol, opioids, or organophosphate poisoning frequently precipitate acute ALOC. Understanding these underlying mechanisms allows nursing students to anticipate specific medical or surgical interventions, such as administering intravenous hypertonic glucose for suspected hypoglycemia or administering specific antimalarials for cerebral malaria, thereby addressing the root cause of the altered mental state rather than just treating the symptom.

Classification and Scales of Consciousness

Accurately classifying and grading the level of consciousness is essential for objective communication among healthcare team members and for tracking a patient's neurological trajectory over time. Clinical terminologies used to describe consciousness range from full alertness to deep coma. Confusion implies an inability to think clearly and rapidly; lethargy represents a state of drowsiness where the patient can be aroused by mild stimuli but quickly drifts back to sleep; obtundation describes a more profound reduction in alertness with slowed responses to stimulation; stupor indicates that the patient can only be aroused by vigorous and repeated physical or painful stimuli; and coma represents a state of unarousable unresponsiveness where no purposeful response is elicited. To standardize these assessments, the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is universally utilized in nursing and medical practice. The GCS evaluates three distinct behavioral parameters: Eye Opening (E), Motor Response (M), and Verbal Response (V). Scores range from a maximum of 15 for a fully oriented patient to a minimum of 3, which indicates deep coma or brain death. In pediatric patients, especially infants, modified pediatric scales are applied because traditional verbal and motor responses cannot be accurately measured. Mastering the GCS allows nurses to swiftly identify clinical deterioration, such as a drop of two or more points in the total score, which often triggers an immediate emergency response.

Initial Assessment and Triage in Emergency Settings

When an adult or pediatric patient presents with an altered level of consciousness in an emergency setting, the nurse must immediately initiate a rapid, structured initial assessment and triage protocol based on the ABCDE framework (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure). The first priority is always securing a patent airway and ensuring adequate ventilation, as cerebral hypoxia rapidly worsens brain injury. Cervical spine stabilization must be maintained concurrently if trauma is suspected. Once airway and breathing are stabilized, the nurse evaluates circulation by checking pulses, capillary refill, blood pressure, and skin perfusion to rule out shock. The 'Disability' step involves a rapid neurological screening using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or the Glasgow Coma Scale, alongside a quick assessment of pupillary size, symmetry, and light reactivity. Concurrently, a point-of-care blood glucose test is mandatory for any patient with ALOC to rule out reversible hypoglycemia. The nurse also looks for gross physical signs of trauma, needle tracks, signs of infection, or meningeal irritation such as nuchal rigidity. Rapid triage ensures that critically unstable patients are funneled instantly to resuscitation bays, while diagnostic investigations such as malaria parasite tests, packed cell volume, electrolytes, and neuroimaging are ordered without delay. Systematic triage prevents oversight and structures the chaotic emergency environment into lifesaving steps.

First Aid and Immediate Life Support Management

First aid and immediate life support management for a patient with Altered Level of Consciousness focus on stabilizing vital physiological functions and preventing secondary brain injury. If the patient is unresponsive and breathing spontaneously, the nurse or first responder should immediately place them in the recovery position (lateral decubitus position) to prevent aspiration of saliva, vomit, or blood, and to keep the tongue from obstructing the upper airway. Suction equipment must be readily available at the bedside. If breathing is inadequate or absent, bag-valve-mask ventilation with high-flow oxygen should be initiated, and preparations for endotracheal intubation by skilled clinicians must be made. Intravenous access should be established promptly to allow for fluid resuscitation if the patient is hypotensive, or for the administration of emergency antidotes and medications. For instance, if hypoglycemia is confirmed or strongly suspected, intravenous 50% dextrose is administered, while suspected opioid overdose warrants the administration of naloxone. In regions where severe malaria is endemic, parenteral antimalarials such as artesunate are prioritized if malarial encephalopathy is suspected. Throughout this acute phase, nurses must avoid unprescribed sedatives, closely monitor oxygen saturation levels using pulse oximetry, and ensure that the patient's neck remains in a neutral position to optimize cerebral venous drainage and prevent spikes in intracranial pressure.

Nursing Interventions and Continuous Monitoring

Continuous monitoring and targeted nursing interventions are critical for patients with ongoing Altered Level of Consciousness to promote recovery and prevent secondary complications. Nurses must perform serial neurological assessments, documenting GCS scores, pupillary response to light, and limb movements at regular, frequent intervals (e.g., every 15 to 60 minutes depending on stability). Any sudden asymmetry in pupil size or a downward trend in GCS score must be reported immediately as it suggests increasing intracranial pressure (ICP) or brain herniation. Vital signs must be tracked meticulously; the presence of Cushing's triad—widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations—is a late and ominous sign of severely elevated ICP. Respiratory status requires constant vigilance, including monitoring arterial blood gases and maintaining effective mechanical ventilation or oxygen therapy. Nursing care must also address basic physiological needs: maintaining skin integrity through regular two-hourly turns (unless contraindicated), providing passive range-of-motion exercises to prevent contractures, performing meticulous oral and eye care to prevent corneal ulceration, and managing urinary elimination via indwelling catheters with strict fluid balance charting. Nutrition is managed through enteral tube feeding or parenteral nutrition once the acute phase stabilizes. Throughout, the nurse provides compassionate psychological support and clear, reassuring verbal communication to the patient and their family, even when the patient appears unresponsive.

Potential Complications and Prevention Strategies

Patients with prolonged Altered Level of Consciousness are vulnerable to a wide array of secondary complications that can severely hinder recovery or result in permanent disability or death. Common complications include pressure injuries (bedsores), ventilator-associated pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism, muscle contractures, urinary tract infections, and aspiration pneumonia. Prevention strategies require a proactive, multi-disciplinary nursing care plan. To prevent pressure ulcers, nurses must implement regular repositioning schedules, use pressure-relieving mattresses, and keep the skin clean and dry. Pulmonary complications are minimized through aggressive pulmonary hygiene, including regular chest physiotherapy, suctioning, elevating the head of the bed to 30 to 45 degrees to prevent aspiration, and maintaining oral hygiene protocols. To prevent thromboembolic events such as DVT, nurses apply sequential compression devices, administer prophylactic anticoagulants as prescribed, and encourage early passive or active mobilization when clinically appropriate. Nutritional support must be initiated early to prevent catabolism and support immune function. Additionally, careful aseptic technique during catheterization and intravenous line management reduces the risk of nosocomial sepsis. By anticipating these risks and executing rigorous preventive nursing interventions, student nurses ensure that the patient is protected from avoidable harm while their primary neurological condition heals.
