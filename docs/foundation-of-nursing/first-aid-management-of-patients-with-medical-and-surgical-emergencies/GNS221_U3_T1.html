Overview of Medical and Surgical Emergencies

Introduction to Medical and Surgical Emergencies

An emergency is any sudden, unexpected event that threatens a person's life or limb, requiring immediate medical intervention. In clinical practice, emergencies are broadly divided into medical and surgical categories. A medical emergency stems from internal physiological failures or illnesses—such as a heart attack, severe asthma attack, or diabetic coma—where body systems break down without necessarily needing an operation. Conversely, a surgical emergency involves acute physical trauma, internal bleeding, or sudden organ failure that typically requires operative intervention to fix, such as a ruptured appendix, a gunshot wound, or a strangulated hernia.

Time is the single most critical factor in emergency care. The phrase the golden hour refers to the first sixty minutes after a traumatic injury or sudden medical collapse, during which medical treatment gives the highest chance of survival. Delays during this window significantly increase morbidity and mortality rates. Nurses must recognize that emergency care is not just about speed; it requires systematic thinking, calm leadership, and precise clinical judgment to stabilize crashing patients before irreversible damage occurs.

Principles of Emergency Triage and Assessment

When multiple patients arrive at a healthcare facility simultaneously, nurses use triage to sort them based on the severity of their illness or injury. Triage ensures that those with life-threatening conditions receive immediate care, while patients with minor ailments wait safely. Systems like the Emergency Severity Index use a scale from level one, representing cardiac arrest, to level five, representing minor non-urgent issues.

Once a patient is assigned a treatment space, the nurse performs a rapid primary survey followed by a more detailed secondary survey. The primary survey spots immediate threats to life, while the secondary survey is a head-to-toe physical examination to uncover other injuries or medical problems. A common novice mistake is spending too much time taking a detailed patient history before stabilizing vital signs. In emergency nursing, immediate stabilization always comes before history-taking.

Initial Resuscitation and the ABCDE Approach

The ABCDE approach is a universal framework used across all emergency settings to assess and treat deteriorating patients systematically. It stands for Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure. By following this strict alphabetical order, the nurse ensures that the most life-threatening issues are addressed before moving to less critical checks.

  • A - Airway: Check if the airway is open and clear. Look for tongue obstruction, foreign bodies, or swelling. If the patient can speak clearly, the airway is at least temporarily patent.
  • B - Breathing: Assess respiratory rate, depth, chest movement, and oxygen saturation. Administer high-flow oxygen if distress is present.
  • C - Circulation: Check pulse rate, blood pressure, capillary refill time, and skin color. Control any major external bleeding immediately.
  • D - Disability: Assess neurological status using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or the Glasgow Coma Scale, and check blood glucose levels.
  • E - Exposure: Remove clothing carefully to inspect the entire body for hidden injuries or rashes, while maintaining the patient's dignity and preventing hypothermia.

Common Medical Emergencies

Medical emergencies demand swift recognition to prevent multi-organ failure or cardiac arrest. Nurses frequently encounter several distinct crises:

  • Acute Myocardial Infarction: Heart attack caused by blocked coronary blood vessels. Patients present with crushing chest pain radiating to the jaw or left arm, diaphoresis, and shortness of breath.
  • Cerebrovascular Accident: Stroke caused by blocked or ruptured cerebral blood vessels. Nurses use the FAST mnemonic (Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call) to identify strokes quickly.
  • Status Epilepticus: A continuous seizure lasting longer than five minutes or back-to-back seizures without regaining consciousness, which can cause permanent brain damage.
  • Severe Anaphylaxis: A life-threatening allergic reaction causing airway swelling, wheezing, and profound hypotension, treated immediately with intramuscular epinephrine.

Common Surgical Emergencies and Acute Abdomens

Surgical emergencies often present with sudden, severe abdominal pain known clinically as an acute abdomen. This is a symptom complex that frequently indicates intra-abdominal inflammation, perforation, or ischemia requiring urgent surgical exploration. Common causes include acute appendicitis, perforated peptic ulcers, bowel obstruction, and ectopic pregnancy rupture.

When assessing an acute abdomen, nurses must check for rebound tenderness—pain that increases when pressure on the abdomen is suddenly released, which strongly suggests peritoneal irritation. One dangerous pitfall in surgical nursing is administering strong pain relief before a definitive surgical diagnosis is made. While patients deserve comfort, masking abdominal pain with potent analgesics can obscure key diagnostic physical signs and delay life-saving operations for conditions like a ruptured bowel.

First Aid and Immediate Nursing Interventions

Immediate nursing interventions in emergencies bridge the gap between patient collapse and definitive medical care. Basic life support, including chest compressions and rescue breaths, must be initiated without delay if a patient is pulseless and unresponsive. For bleeding wounds, applying direct, firm pressure with a clean dressing is the most effective first-aid step to stop hemorrhage before applying tourniquets.

Positioning also plays a vital role. An unconscious, breathing patient should be placed in the recovery position (lateral recumbent position) to prevent the tongue from blocking the airway and to allow fluids or vomit to drain safely. Conversely, a patient in shock should generally be laid flat with legs slightly elevated to improve venous return to the heart, unless head or spinal injuries are suspected.

Monitoring, Documentation, and Interprofessional Handover

Once an emergency patient is stabilized, continuous monitoring is vital to catch secondary deterioration early. Nurses must frequently re-check vital signs, neurological status, and urine output. Meticulous documentation is equally crucial; every intervention, medication dose, and patient response must be recorded with exact timestamps to provide a clear legal and clinical record of care.

When transferring care to another department or shift, nurses use standardized communication tools like ISOBAR or SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). A structured handover prevents critical details from getting lost during transitions. Failing to communicate subtle changes in a patient's condition during a handover is a frequent root cause of medical errors in emergency departments.
