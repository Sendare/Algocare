Overview of Medical and Surgical Emergencies

Introduction and Classification of Emergencies

Medical and surgical emergencies demand rapid, systematic intervention to prevent irreversible physiological deterioration or death. A medical emergency typically arises from acute systemic, metabolic, or organ-system failure, such as severe asthma, acute myocardial infarction, or diabetic ketoacidosis, which are primarily managed through pharmacotherapy and stabilization. Conversely, a surgical emergency often involves acute structural failure, vascular compromise, or hollow viscous perforation—exemplified by acute appendicitis, strangulated hernias, or severe internal hemorrhage—where definitive management relies on operative intervention. Recognizing the overlap between these categories is vital in Nigerian clinical settings, where patients frequently present late with advanced pathology, blurring the lines between primary medical instability and secondary surgical catastrophe.

Principles of Emergency Triage and Assessment

Triage is the sorting of patients according to the severity of their illness or injury to ensure that those with life-threatening conditions receive immediate care. Standard emergency departments utilize tiered sorting models, such as the South African Triage Scale (SATS) widely adopted across West Africa, which categorizes patients using physiological vital signs and presenting complaints.

  • Emergency/Red: Immediate, life-saving intervention required.
  • Very Urgent/Orange: High risk, rapid treatment needed within minutes.
  • Urgent/Yellow: Stable but requires prompt evaluation.
  • Routine/Green: Non-life-threatening, ambulatory care.

The initial primary survey follows a strict sequence to identify immediate threats to life, minimizing diagnostic paralysis while maximizing survival probability.

Initial Resuscitation and the ABCDE Approach

The ABCDE systematic framework provides a standardized protocol for evaluating and managing any critically ill patient, regardless of the underlying pathology.

  • Airway: Assess patency, clear obstructions, and implement adjuncts like an oropharyngeal airway or cervical spine protection if trauma is suspected.
  • Breathing: Evaluate respiratory rate, depth, symmetry, and oxygen saturation; administer high-flow oxygen and treat tension pneumothorax or severe bronchospasm.
  • Circulation: Check pulse quality, capillary refill, blood pressure, and peripheral perfusion; establish large-bore intravenous access for fluid resuscitation or blood products.
  • Disability: Perform a rapid neurological evaluation using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or Glasgow Coma Scale, alongside checking blood glucose levels.
  • Exposure: Undress the patient briefly to identify hidden injuries, rashes, or bleeding sites while actively preventing hypothermia.

Simultaneous intervention and assessment characterize this approach; clinicians must treat life-threatening abnormalities in Airway or Breathing before moving down the sequence.

Common Medical Emergencies

Medical emergencies encompass sudden physiological crises requiring rapid pharmacological and supportive management. Acute myocardial infarction manifests as central crushing chest pain radiating to the jaw or left arm, requiring immediate antiplatelet therapy, oxygen, and potential thrombolysis or PCI. Severe acute asthma exacerbations present with wheezing, tachypnea, and accessory muscle use; management centers on nebulized bronchodilators, systemic corticosteroids, and close monitoring of peak expiratory flow. Hypertensive emergencies feature severely elevated blood pressure accompanied by acute end-organ damage, such as stroke or pulmonary edema, necessitating controlled intravenous antihypertensive administration to prevent rapid neurological or vascular decline. Recognizing early prodromal signs prevents full-scale cardiopulmonary arrest.

Surgical Emergencies and Acute Abdomens

Acute abdominal pain is one of the most frequent presentations in surgical emergency units. An acute abdomen implies intra-abdominal pathology requiring urgent diagnosis and frequently surgical intervention. Key differentials include acute appendicitis, perforated peptic ulcer, intestinal obstruction, and acute cholecystitis. Physical examination focuses on identifying peritoneal signs such as rebound tenderness, abdominal rigidity, and involuntary guarding, which strongly suggest peritonitis. Misdiagnosing these conditions can lead to septic shock, bowel necrosis, and high mortality. Fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and urgent surgical consultation form the cornerstone of safe patient management.

First Aid and Immediate Nursing Interventions

Immediate nursing actions in emergency scenarios bridge the gap between initial triage and definitive medical or surgical treatment. Nurses must master basic life support, hemorrhage control techniques such as direct pressure and wound packing, and proper patient positioning. For instance, an unconscious breathing patient is placed in the recovery position to prevent airway obstruction from saliva or vomitus, whereas a patient in hypovolemic shock benefits from flat positioning with lower limbs elevated if head injury is absent. Administering prescribed emergency medications, securing reliable vascular access, and maintaining calm, authoritative communication with both the patient and distressed relatives help stabilize the clinical environment.

Monitoring, Documentation, and Interprofessional Handover

Continuous physiological monitoring is mandatory following initial stabilization. Vital signs must be recorded frequently on standardized early warning score charts to detect subtle trends of deterioration. Clear, contemporaneous documentation of all assessment findings, administered medications, and patient responses protects both the patient and the professional legally. When transferring care to an operating theater, intensive care unit, or specialized ward, structured communication tools such as ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) ensure vital clinical details are not omitted. Effective interprofessional handover prevents critical communication breakdowns that frequently jeopardize patient safety during emergency transitions.
