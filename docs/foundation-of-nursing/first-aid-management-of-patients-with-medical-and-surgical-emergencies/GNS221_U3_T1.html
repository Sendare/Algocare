Overview of Medical and Surgical Emergencies

Introduction to Medical and Surgical Emergencies

Medical and surgical emergencies represent acute, life-threatening conditions that require immediate recognition, prompt evaluation, and rapid intervention to prevent patient deterioration or death. As nursing students in Nigeria, understanding the fundamental differences between medical and surgical emergencies is vital for clinical competence in busy emergency departments and general wards. A medical emergency typically stems from internal physiological failures—such as acute myocardial infarctions, severe asthma exacerbations, diabetic ketoacidosis, or cerebrovascular accidents—where organ systems fail due to disease processes, metabolic derangements, or infections. Conversely, surgical emergencies often involve acute structural, traumatic, or inflammatory conditions that require urgent operative intervention, such as acute appendicitis, perforated peptic ulcers, strangulated hernias, or major blunt abdominal trauma. Despite these distinct etiologies, both categories demand systematic nursing assessment, stabilization, and prioritization using structured frameworks. Nurses must act as the first line of defense, recognizing subtle warning signs of impending cardiovascular collapse, respiratory failure, or septic shock. Rapid identification allows for timely escalation to attending physicians, surgeons, and other interprofessional team members, ultimately reducing patient morbidity and mortality across diverse healthcare settings in Nigeria.

Principles of Emergency Triage and Assessment

Emergency triage is the critical process of sorting patients based on the severity of their illness or injury to ensure that those with the most life-threatening conditions receive immediate care. In Nigerian clinical practice, where emergency departments are often overburdened, utilizing a standardized triage system—such as the South African Triage Scale (SATS) or the Manchester Triage System—is essential for patient safety. Triage separates patients into priority categories ranging from resuscitation (immediate, life-saving intervention needed) to routine or non-urgent cases. During the initial assessment, nurses must perform a rapid primary survey to identify immediate threats to life, followed by a secondary survey once the patient is stabilized. Key vital signs, including respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, and level of consciousness (often assessed via the Glasgow Coma Scale or AVPU scale), form the backbone of triage decision-making. Nurses must remain vigilant for red flags such as airway compromise, severe respiratory distress, hemodynamic instability, altered mental status, and excruciating pain. Effective triage requires clinical judgment, clear communication, and adherence to protocols, ensuring that scarce medical resources are allocated efficiently to patients who need them the most.

Common Medical Emergencies in Clinical Practice

Common medical emergencies demand swift, protocol-driven nursing interventions to stabilize patients and prevent irreversible organ damage. Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) presents with crushing retrosternal chest pain radiating to the jaw or left arm, diaphoresis, and shortness of breath; nursing actions include administering oxygen, providing aspirin, establishing intravenous access, and preparing for electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring. Cerebrovascular accidents (stroke) manifest as sudden neurological deficits such as facial drooping, arm weakness, and speech difficulties (FAST acronym); rapid identification and blood pressure management are crucial. Severe asthma exacerbations and status asthmaticus involve acute bronchospasm, wheezing, and use of accessory muscles, requiring high-flow oxygen, nebulized bronchodilators, and systemic corticosteroids. Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a serious complication of type 1 diabetes, presents with hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, ketonuria, and Kussmaul breathing; nursing management entails aggressive fluid resuscitation, regular blood glucose monitoring, and careful insulin administration. Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic allergic reaction causing airway edema, hypotension, and urticaria, treated immediately with intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine), airway maintenance, and antihistamines. Recognizing these classic presentations enables student nurses to initiate life-saving measures before definitive medical therapy is administered.

Common Surgical Emergencies and Acute Abdomens

Surgical emergencies and acute abdomens represent high-acuity scenarios where delayed diagnosis can lead to bowel perforation, peritonitis, septic shock, and death. An acute abdomen refers to any sudden, severe abdominal pain of unclear etiology that frequently requires surgical intervention. Common surgical emergencies include acute appendicitis, characterized by periumbilical pain shifting to the right lower quadrant (McBurney's point), accompanied by low-grade fever, nausea, and positive Rovsing's or psoas signs. Intestinal obstruction presents with colicky abdominal pain, abdominal distension, obstipation (failure to pass stool or gas), and projectile vomiting. Peptic ulcer perforation manifests as sudden, excruciating 'board-like' abdominal pain, generalized rigidity, and signs of chemical peritonitis. Strangulated hernias occur when a portion of the bowel becomes trapped and its blood supply is compromised, presenting as a painful, irreducible bulge with systemic signs of ischemia. Ectopic pregnancy rupture in female patients of childbearing age presents with acute lower abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, and hemodynamic instability. Nursing care for these conditions includes keeping the patient nil per os (NPO), establishing large-bore IV access for fluid resuscitation, inserting a nasogastric tube if vomiting is present, administering prescribed analgesics cautiously after surgical consultation, and preparing the patient for urgent theatre.

Initial Resuscitation and the ABCDE Approach

The ABCDE approach is a systematic, evidence-based framework used worldwide for the immediate assessment and resuscitation of critically ill or injured patients, regardless of whether the underlying pathology is medical or surgical. Standing for Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure, this approach prioritizes interventions based on the principle that the most immediately life-threatening problem must be corrected first. 'A' focuses on airway patency; nurses must inspect for obstructions, suction secretions, and use adjuncts like oropharyngeal airways if needed while maintaining cervical spine precautions in trauma. 'B' evaluates breathing; the nurse assesses respiratory rate, depth, chest expansion, and oxygen saturation, administering high-flow oxygen and assisting ventilation as necessary. 'C' assesses circulation; the nurse checks capillary refill, pulse rate and quality, blood pressure, and skin color, quickly establishing large-bore IV lines to treat hemorrhage or shock. 'D' assesses neurological disability using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale, checking papillary response and blood glucose levels. 'E' involves exposure and environmental control; the patient is fully undressed to look for hidden injuries, rashes, or bleeding while preventing hypothermia with warm blankets. Working through this sequence in an organized manner ensures comprehensive, lifesaving emergency care.

First Aid and Immediate Nursing Interventions

First aid and immediate nursing interventions are vital components of emergency stabilization that bridge the gap between initial presentation and definitive clinical management. When dealing with trauma, bleeding, or sudden physiological collapse, nurses must execute prompt, life-saving measures with confidence and precision. For external hemorrhage, direct pressure using sterile dressings and elevation of the bleeding limb is the primary intervention; tourniquets are reserved for severe, life-threatening limb bleeding that fails direct pressure. In cases of airway obstruction, nurses apply back slaps and abdominal thrusts (Heimlich maneuver) for conscious choking victims, or finger sweeps and suctioning for unconscious patients. For patients experiencing shock—whether hypovolemic, septic, or cardiogenic—immediate nursing actions include positioning the patient flat with legs elevated (unless contraindicated by head injury or trauma), administering supplemental oxygen, securing reliable intravenous access, and initiating rapid fluid boluses as prescribed. Wound care for acute traumatic lacerations involves cleaning with sterile saline, applying pressure dressings, and administering tetanus prophylaxis. Throughout these interventions, psychological support and reassurance must be provided to the patient and their anxious relatives, maintaining a calm and professional demeanor in high-stress clinical environments.

Monitoring, Documentation, and Interprofessional Handover

Continuous monitoring, accurate documentation, and structured interprofessional handover are cornerstones of safe, high-quality emergency nursing care. In fast-paced emergency and surgical environments, patient conditions can fluctuate within minutes, making serial vital sign assessments and neurological checks mandatory. Nurses must utilize bedside monitors, charts, and fluid balance records to track trends in heart rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and urine output, immediately reporting any deterioration to the medical team. Documentation must be contemporaneous, factual, and legally sound, detailing the exact time interventions were performed, patient responses to treatments, and assessments using standardized charting formats. Furthermore, when transferring a patient to the operating theatre, intensive care unit, or general ward, nurses must conduct a structured interprofessional handover using communication tools such as ISBAR (Identification, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). ISBAR ensures that vital clinical data—such as current ABCDE status, medications given, laboratory results, and pending investigations—is communicated clearly and concisely without omissions. Effective handover minimizes clinical errors, fosters teamwork, and ensures continuity of care for vulnerable medical and surgical patients.
