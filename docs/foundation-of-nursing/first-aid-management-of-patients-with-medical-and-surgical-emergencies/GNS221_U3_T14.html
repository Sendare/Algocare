Poisoning

Introduction and Definition of Poisoning

Poisoning occurs when any substance—solid, liquid, or gas—interferes with normal body metabolism, disrupts physiological functions, or causes structural damage after entering the body. The severity of toxicity depends on the intrinsic chemical properties of the agent, the dose administered, the route of exposure, and individual host factors such as age and pre-existing organ function. In clinical settings, exposures broadly categorize into accidental, intentional, occupational, or iatrogenic incidents. Recognizing that virtually any chemical can act as a poison if the dose is high enough remains a core toxicological principle. Public health data in Nigeria highlight kerosene ingestion among toddlers and organophosphate pesticide exposure among agricultural workers as particularly prevalent presentations.

Routes of Exposure and Common Toxins

Toxins enter the human body through various pathways, each dictating the speed of onset and initial clinical manifestations. - **Ingestion**: Swallowing household cleaners, medications, or caustic substances; highly prevalent in domestic accidents. - **Inhalation**: Breathing in carbon monoxide, industrial fumes, or toxic gases, rapidly crossing the alveolar-capillary membrane. - **Absorption**: Skin or mucosal contact with agricultural pesticides, organic solvents, or chemical irritants. - **Injection**: Puncture wounds, animal envenomation from snakebites, or illicit intravenous drug overdoses. - **Ocular**: Splashes of acids, alkalis, or particulate matter directly onto the conjunctiva.

Emergency Assessment and Triage

Initial management of any poisoned patient begins with the standard resuscitation framework: Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABCDE approach). Rapid triage requires establishing responsiveness, checking vital signs for life-threatening derangements, and identifying specific toxic syndromes, known as toxidromes. For instance, cholinergic toxidromes present with excessive salivation, lacrimation, urination, and defecation, whereas sympathomimetic toxidromes feature hypertension, tachycardia, and mydriasis. Obtaining a reliable history from bystanders, family members, or emergency medical services remains critical when the patient is unconscious or unable to communicate.

Prevention of Further Absorption

Once the patient is stabilized, interventions shift toward halting systemic toxin uptake. Gastrointestinal decontamination methods vary based on the agent involved and the elapsed time since ingestion. - **Activated Charcoal**: Binds many organic toxins in the gut lumen, preventing absorption; most effective within one hour of ingestion. - **Gastric Lavage**: Involves flushing the stomach via an orogastric tube; rarely performed due to high risks of aspiration and esophageal perforation. - **Whole Bowel Irrigation**: Administering polyethylene glycol electrolyte solution to flush the entire gastrointestinal tract, useful for body packers or sustained-release drugs. - **Dermal Decontamination**: Stripping contaminated clothing and thoroughly washing skin and hair with copious amounts of water.

Nursing Care and Supportive Management

Comprehensive nursing care focuses on continuous hemodynamic monitoring, maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance, and safeguarding patient safety. Frequent neurological checks detect early signs of central nervous system depression or impending seizures. For unconscious patients, meticulous airway clearance, regular repositioning to prevent pressure ulcers, and strict fluid balance charting are mandatory. Emotional support and psychological evaluation must be initiated promptly if the poisoning stems from intentional self-harm or psychiatric distress, ensuring safe transition to mental health specialists.

Complications and Preventive Measures

Severe poisonings can lead to long-term organ damage or fatal systemic collapse. Common medical complications include acute kidney injury secondary to rhabdomyolysis or toxin clearance strain, hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy from prolonged respiratory depression, and severe esophageal strictures following caustic ingestions. Prevention strategies remain the cornerstone of reducing toxicological emergencies. Community health education campaigns in Nigeria emphasize storing dangerous chemicals, kerosene, and medications in original, labeled containers well out of the reach of inquisitive young children.
