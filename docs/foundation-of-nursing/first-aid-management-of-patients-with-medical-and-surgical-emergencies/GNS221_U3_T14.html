Poisoning

Introduction and Definition of Poisoning

Poisoning occurs when any substance, whether solid, liquid, or gas, interferes with normal body functions after it enters the living body. This entry can happen through swallowing, breathing in, injection, or direct contact with the skin or eyes. The severity of poisoning depends entirely on the chemical nature of the substance, the amount taken, the route of exposure, and the age and general health status of the victim. In clinical practice, exposure is generally categorized as accidental, such as a toddler swallowing household cleaner, or intentional, as seen in self-harm attempts. Recognizing that a poison can be therapeutic medication taken in the wrong dose is just as vital as identifying dangerous industrial chemicals.

Routes of Exposure and Common Toxins

Toxins enter the human body through four primary routes: ingestion, inhalation, absorption, and injection. Ingestion remains the most frequent route in domestic environments, encompassing household cleaning agents, insecticides, and overdosed medications. Inhalation involves breathing toxic gases, carbon monoxide, or chemical fumes, which rapidly enter the bloodstream via the lungs. Absorption occurs when corrosive liquids, agricultural pesticides, or certain plants touch the skin or mucous membranes. Injection typically happens via insect stings, snakebites, or illicit drug use with contaminated needles. Each route dictates how quickly the poison spreads and influences the immediate steps a nurse must take during emergency care.

Emergency Assessment and Triage

When a poisoned patient arrives at the healthcare facility, the immediate priority is stabilizing vital functions using the ABCDE approach: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure. Nurses must quickly assess the level of consciousness, pupil size, skin color, and respiratory rate, as these offer vital clues regarding the class of poison involved. For instance, pinpoint pupils strongly suggest opioid toxicity, while wide, dilated pupils often point toward stimulants or anticholinergic drugs. Gathering a history from relatives, bystanders, or searching pockets for pill bottles, containers, or suicide notes is just as important as physical examination to guide urgent medical management.

General Principles of First Aid Management

First aid for poisoning focuses on stopping further exposure, supporting life, and arranging rapid transport to a medical facility.

  • Skin exposure: Immediately remove contaminated clothing and rinse the skin continuously with plenty of clean water for at least fifteen minutes.
  • Eye exposure: Flush the eyes gently with sterile normal saline or clean tap water, making sure the eyelid is held open.
  • Inhaled poisons: Safely remove the victim to fresh air immediately, provided the rescuer is wearing appropriate protective gear.
  • Swallowed poisons: Never induce vomiting unless specifically instructed by a certified poison control center or medical doctor, because corrosive substances can cause severe secondary burns to the esophagus upon coming back up.

Prevention of Further Absorption

Once the patient is stable, healthcare providers focus on minimizing the absorption of any remaining poison in the gastrointestinal tract. Activated charcoal is frequently administered orally or via a nasogastric tube to bind various toxins and prevent them from entering the bloodstream through the gut wall. Gastric lavage, often called stomach pumping, may be performed if the patient presents within one hour of ingesting a life-threatening amount of poison, though its routine use has declined due to aspiration risks. Whole bowel irrigation using a special polyethylene glycol solution is sometimes utilized for sustained-release medications or ingested drug packets.

Nursing Care and Supportive Management

Ongoing nursing care for the poisoned patient is centered on close monitoring, comfort, and psychological reassurance. Nurses must maintain accurate fluid balance charts, monitor urine output to ensure adequate kidney function, and regularly check vital signs. For sedated or comised patients, frequent repositioning prevents pressure sores, and proper oral care prevents secondary infections. If specific antidotes like naloxone for opioids or flumazenil for benzodiazepines are ordered, the nurse must administer them carefully while watching for rapid changes in neurological status. Emotional support is also crucial, especially when caring for individuals recovering from intentional self-harm attempts.

Complications and Preventive Measures

Poisoning can lead to severe long-term complications including permanent organ damage to the liver, kidneys, or brain, chronic esophageal strictures from caustic burns, and psychological trauma. Prevention remains the single most effective strategy against poisoning incidents. Nurses play a major community role by educating families on safe storage practices, such as keeping household chemicals and medications in their original, labeled containers and locking them away from children. Public awareness campaigns regarding proper disposal of expired drugs and installation of carbon monoxide detectors in homes significantly reduce accidental exposures.
