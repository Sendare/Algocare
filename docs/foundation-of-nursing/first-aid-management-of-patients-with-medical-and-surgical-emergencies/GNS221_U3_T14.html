Poisoning

Introduction and Definition of Poisoning

Poisoning is a critical medical emergency that occurs when any substance—solid, liquid, or gas—interferes with normal body physiology after coming into contact with, being absorbed by, ingested into, or inhaled by the human body. In the context of nursing practice in Nigeria, understanding poisoning requires recognizing both intentional exposures, such as deliberate self-harm or suicide attempts, and unintentional exposures, which are very common among children who accidentally ingest kerosene, insecticides, or leftover medications stored in unlabelled containers. Toxins can disrupt vital organ systems rapidly, leading to cellular hypoxia, metabolic acidosis, neurological depression, or multi-organ failure. As student nurses, you must develop a high index of suspicion when encountering patients presenting with sudden, unexplained alterations in mental status, respiratory distress, or gastrointestinal symptoms. Prompt identification of the offending agent, rapid initiation of basic life support, and timely reporting to the attending physician or toxicologist can drastically reduce morbidity and mortality. Public health education also forms a core duty for nurses, as teaching families about safe storage of chemicals and medications at home helps mitigate accidental poisoning cases within our communities.

Routes of Exposure and Common Toxins

Toxins enter the human body through several distinct routes of exposure, each determining the speed of onset and systemic manifestations. The primary routes include ingestion, inhalation, absorption through the skin or mucous membranes, and injection via animal bites, stings, or intravenous drug use. Ingested poisons are absorbed primarily through the gastrointestinal tract and pass through the liver, where first-pass metabolism may alter toxicity. Inhaled toxins, such as carbon monoxide or industrial fumes, enter the alveoli rapidly and cross directly into the bloodstream, causing swift systemic symptoms like central nervous system depression or asphyxiation. Transdermal absorption occurs with organophosphate pesticides and certain industrial chemicals commonly handled in agricultural or manufacturing sectors in Nigeria. Common toxins encountered in clinical and community settings include household cleaning agents, caustic substances like bleach and acid, agricultural pesticides, over-the-counter or prescription medications such as paracetamol and iron tablets, and traditional remedies or herbal preparations of unknown composition. Identifying the exact route and type of toxin is essential because it dictates the immediate safety precautions the nurse must take, such as wearing proper personal protective equipment to prevent secondary contamination from organophosphates.

Emergency Assessment and Triage

When a poisoned patient arrives at the emergency department, immediate assessment and triage are vital to preserve life and prevent secondary complications. The nurse must systematically apply the Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABCDE) approach. First, ensure a patent airway, suctioning secretions if necessary, and assess respiratory rate, depth, and oxygen saturation. Second, check circulatory status by monitoring heart rate, blood pressure, peripheral pulses, and capillary refill time. Third, perform a rapid neurological assessment using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) to determine the level of consciousness. Triage requires quickly gathering a history from witnesses, relatives, or emergency responders regarding the 'toxidrome'—a constellation of signs and symptoms associated with a specific class of poisons, such as anticholinergic, cholinergic, opioid, or sympathomimetic syndromes. Emergency nurses must look out for specific clues like pinpoint pupils, unusual breath odors, excessive salivation, or abnormal skin color and temperature. Rapid identification of life-threatening instabilities allows the multidisciplinary team to intervene immediately with resuscitation measures before performing detailed diagnostic evaluations.

General Principles of First Aid Management

General principles of first aid management for poisoning focus on stabilizing the patient, terminating ongoing exposure, and preventing systemic toxicity while ensuring the safety of both the rescuer and the victim. If the incident occurred at home or in the community, bystanders and first responders should immediately remove the victim from the toxic environment, provided it is safe to do so. For skin or eye contact, copious flushing with clean water for at least 15 to 20 minutes is mandatory to dilute and remove chemical agents. In cases of ingestion, traditional and dangerous practices—such as forcing the patient to drink salty water, palm oil, or raw eggs, or inducing vomiting without professional guidance—must be strongly discouraged by nurses during community health education, as these practices can cause aspiration pneumonia or esophageal burns. Instead, the focus should be on keeping the airway clear, placing an unconscious breathing patient in the recovery position, and transporting them rapidly to the nearest healthcare facility. Nurses receiving the patient should maintain calm, supportive communication, reassure anxious relatives, and prepare basic emergency equipment such as suction machines, oxygen delivery devices, and intravenous cannulas.

Prevention of Further Absorption

Preventing further absorption of the poison is a critical intervention in clinical toxicology. The choice of method depends on the time elapsed since ingestion, the nature of the toxin, and the patient's clinical state. Gastric lavage, once widely performed, is now used selectively for life-threatening ingestions if the patient presents within one hour, provided the airway is fully protected. Activated charcoal is frequently administered orally or via a nasogastric tube to adsorb toxins in the gastrointestinal tract, preventing their systemic absorption; however, it is ineffective against heavy metals, alcohols, and caustic substances. Whole bowel irrigation using a polyethylene glycol solution may be utilized for sustained-release medications or body packers. If a corrosive substance like strong acid or alkali has been ingested, attempting to empty the stomach is strictly contraindicated because re-exposing the esophagus to the chemical causes severe secondary trauma and perforation. Nurses play an essential role in preparing equipment, positioning the patient safely (often left lateral tilt to prevent aspiration), monitoring vital signs during these procedures, and documenting the exact amounts of substances administered or recovered.

Nursing Care and Supportive Management

Nursing care and supportive management form the cornerstone of recovery for poisoned patients once immediate life threats are controlled. Continuous monitoring of vital signs—including temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation—is essential to detect early signs of clinical deterioration. Neurological status must be reassessed frequently. The nurse must maintain accurate fluid balance charts, monitoring intravenous fluid infusions and accurately measuring urine output, which is especially important when dealing with nephrotoxic poisons or when attempting forced diuresis. Administration of specific antidotes, when available and ordered by the physician (such as naloxone for opioid overdose or flumazenil for benzodiazepines), requires precise calculation, vigilant observation for adverse drug reactions, and readiness to manage sudden withdrawal symptoms. Psychological and emotional support is equally vital, particularly for patients admitted following deliberate self-harm. The nurse should provide a non-judgmental, empathetic environment, ensure physical safety by implementing suicide precautions if indicated, and facilitate early psychiatric consultation and social work involvement before discharge planning begins.

Complications and Preventive Measures

Complications arising from poisoning can affect multiple organ systems, requiring diligent nursing surveillance and proactive preventive measures. Pulmonary complications, such as aspiration pneumonia and non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, frequently occur in patients with depressed levels of consciousness or those who underwent improper emesis induction. Cardiovascular complications, including arrhythmias, severe hypotension, or shock, can result from cardiotoxic drugs like tricyclic antidepressants or organophosphates. Neurological sequelae may range from prolonged coma to permanent hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Preventive measures focus on minimizing these risks through meticulous nursing care: maintaining head-of-bed elevation for unconscious patients, performing regular oral and airway suctioning, turning immobile patients every two hours to prevent pressure ulcers, and monitoring cardiac telemetry or ECG readings. Furthermore, comprehensive discharge education is vital to prevent recurrence. Nurses must counsel patients and caregivers on safe household storage of chemicals in original containers out of children's reach, proper disposal of expired medications, and providing mental health resources and coping strategies for individuals recovering from intentional poisoning episodes.
