Decontamination

Introduction to Decontamination in Nursing

Decontamination serves as the foundational barrier against healthcare-associated infections across clinical settings in Nigeria. Every nurse interacts with contaminated items daily, from simple thermometers to complex surgical instruments. Without systematic removal or destruction of pathogens, hospitals risk becoming reservoirs of multi-resistant organisms. Effective decontamination protects both the vulnerable patient whose immune system may be compromised and the healthcare worker handling the equipment. Mastering these protocols ensures patient safety whether working in a tertiary teaching hospital or a primary health care clinic with limited resources.

Definitions and Key Terminology: Cleaning, Disinfection, and Sterilization

Infection prevention relies on precise terminology that dictates the level of processing an item requires. Misunderstanding these terms can lead to incomplete processing and subsequent patient infection.

  • Cleaning: The physical removal of organic matter, blood, and dirt, usually done with water and detergents. It reduces the microbial load but does not destroy all pathogens.
  • Disinfection: A process that eliminates most pathogenic microorganisms, excluding bacterial spores, on inanimate objects using chemical or thermal agents.
  • Sterilization: The complete elimination or destruction of all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial spores, achieved through physical or chemical methods.

Cleaning must always precede disinfection and sterilization because organic debris shields microorganisms from chemical agents and heat.

The Spaulding Classification System for Patient Care Items

E. Spaulding categorized medical devices into three distinct tiers based on the degree of risk of infection involved in their use. This classification dictates the minimum level of decontamination required for each item before reuse.

  • Critical Items: Objects that enter sterile tissue or the vascular system, carrying a high risk of infection if contaminated. Examples include surgical instruments, cardiac catheters, and implants. These require sterilization.
  • Semi-critical Items: Objects that touch mucous membranes or non-intact skin. Examples include respiratory therapy equipment, endoscopy scopes, and vaginal specula. These require high-level disinfection.
  • Non-critical Items: Objects that touch only intact skin but not mucous membranes. Examples include blood pressure cuffs, stethoscopes, and bedside commodes. These require low-level to intermediate-level disinfection.

Step-by-Step Decontamination Process for Instruments

Processing reusable medical devices requires a strict, unidirectional workflow to prevent recontaminating clean items.

  1. Point-of-Use Treatment: Wipe off gross soil immediately after use and keep items moist to prevent drying.
  2. Transportation: Carry used instruments in leak-proof, puncture-resistant containers to the processing room.
  3. Decontamination/Cleaning: Wash items thoroughly using enzymatic detergents and soft brushes, preferably submerged to avoid splashing.
  4. Rinsing: Rinse with clean water to remove residual detergent and loosened debris.
  5. Drying: Thoroughly dry instruments to prevent corrosion and dilution of subsequent chemical disinfectants.
  6. Packaging and Processing: Wrap instruments appropriately and subject them to the required sterilization or disinfection method.

Selection and Proper Use of Chemical Disinfectants

Selecting a chemical disinfectant depends on the target pathogen, the nature of the surface, and contact time. Common agents used in Nigerian hospitals include chlorine-releasing compounds and alcohol.

Dilution accuracy is a frequent pitfall. Hypochlorite solutions must be mixed fresh daily because chlorine gas evaporates rapidly, rendering the solution ineffective.

Occupational Health and Safety Measures

Decontamination exposes nursing staff to biological hazards, sharp instruments, and harsh chemical fumes. Standard safety practices are mandatory to prevent injury and infection. Personal protective equipment (PPE)—including heavy-duty utility gloves, plastic aprons, eye protection, and masks—must be worn during manual cleaning procedures. Puncture-resistant utility gloves protect against accidental needle-sticks during instrument washing. Eye-wash stations and running water must be readily accessible in decontamination areas in case chemical splashes occur. Regular training on safe dilution and spill management mitigates chronic chemical exposure risks.

Management of Healthcare Waste and Spillages

Safe disposal of waste generated during decontamination prevents environmental contamination and community exposure. Colour-coded waste segregation is standard: black bins for non-hazardous municipal waste, yellow bins for infectious and pathological waste, and brown or yellow puncture-proof boxes for sharps.

When managing blood and body fluid spillages, nurses follow a strict protocol:

  1. Restrict access to the area.
  2. Put on appropriate PPE.
  3. Confine and absorb the spill using disposable paper towels.
  4. Clean the area with detergent.
  5. Apply a 1-in-10 sodium hypochlorite solution for large spills or 1-in-100 for small spills, leaving it for the required contact time before a final wipe.
