Use of Masks

Introduction to Respiratory Protection in Nursing

Respiratory protection is a cornerstone of infection control in clinical practice. Airborne and droplet pathogens pose constant threats to nurses, patients, and visitors alike. Pathogens like Mycobacterium tuberculosis, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2 travel through the air on respiratory droplets or tiny aerosols generated when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks. Understanding how these microorganisms spread allows the nurse to select and use the correct protective barriers effectively.

Barriers do not just protect the healthcare worker from acquiring an infection; they also prevent colonized or infected staff from transmitting germs to vulnerable patients, such as those undergoing chemotherapy or recovering from major surgery. Effective respiratory protection relies on a combination of engineering controls, administrative policies, and the consistent use of personal protective equipment. As a nurse, knowing the physics of droplet transmission versus airborne transmission dictates whether a simple surgical mask or a specialized respirator is required for safe patient care.

Types of Medical and Surgical Masks

Medical and surgical masks are loose-fitting devices designed to create a physical barrier between the mouth and nose of the wearer and potential contaminants in the immediate environment. They are regulated by fluid resistance, filtration efficiency, and breathability.

  • Surgical Masks: Flat or pleated disposable masks secured with ties or ear loops. They protect the wearer from splashes, sprays, and large-droplet splatters, and they catch large respiratory emissions from the wearer.
  • Procedural Masks: Similar to surgical masks, these are often used during non-surgical medical procedures, examinations, and general patient care tasks to maintain basic hygiene standards.
  • Fluid-Resistant Masks: Specialized surgical masks treated with a special coating to resist high-velocity splashes of blood or body fluids, commonly used in operating theatres.

A common clinical misconception is that standard medical masks protect against tiny airborne pathogens. In reality, their loose fit allows air to leak around the edges, meaning they filter splashes rather than microscopic aerosols.

Understanding Respirators (N95 and Higher)

Respirators are personal protective devices designed to achieve a very close facial seal and efficient filtration of airborne particles. Unlike loose-fitting surgical masks, an N95 respirator filters at least 95 percent of airborne particles, including very small test aerosols.

  1. Filtering Facepiece Respirators (N95, N99, N100): Disposable units where the entire mask body serves as the filter media.
  2. Elastomeric Respirators: Reusable facepieces with replaceable particulate filter cartridges or canisters.
  3. Powered Air-Purifying Respirators (PAPRs): Battery-powered systems that use a blower to pass contaminated air through a high-efficiency filter, supplying purified air to a hood or helmet.

Respirators require a proper seal to function. Any facial hair, such as a beard or heavy stubble, creates gaps that allow unfiltered air to enter the respiratory tract. Nurses must verify their institution's guidelines when caring for patients with airborne precautions to ensure the correct level of respiratory defense is utilized.

Indications for Mask Usage in Healthcare Settings

Deciding when to wear a mask depends on the mode of disease transmission, the specific clinical procedure, and local infection control policies. Healthcare facilities categorize precautions into standard, contact, droplet, and airborne tiers.

  • Droplet Precautions: Required for pathogens spread through close respiratory or mucous membrane contact, such as influenza or pertussis. Nurses wear a standard surgical mask when entering the room.
  • Airborne Precautions: Required for tiny infectious particles that remain suspended in the air over long distances, such as measles, varicella, or tuberculosis. Nurses wear fit-tested N95 or higher respirators.
  • Sterile Procedures: Surgical masks are worn by all team members in operating rooms or during sterile central line insertions to prevent contamination of sterile fields from the clinician's oral flora.

Context matters deeply. During outbreaks, universal masking policies may require all staff and visitors to wear a medical mask in clinical areas regardless of patient diagnosis to suppress community-acquired spread.

Proper Technique for Donning and Fit Testing

Putting on a mask correctly ensures maximum protection. Before touching any protective gear, perform hand hygiene. Position the mask over your nose and mouth, secure the ties or elastic bands around your head or ears, and mold the flexible metal nose piece tightly against the bridge of your nose.

For N95 respirators, a fit test must be performed annually using qualitative or quantitative methods to verify that the specific brand, model, and size fits the healthcare worker's unique facial structure. Qualitative fit testing relies on the user's taste response to a test agent like sweet or bitter aerosol, while quantitative testing uses specialized machinery to measure particle leakage directly around the seal.

In addition to annual testing, the nurse must perform a user seal check every single time a respirator is donned. Inhale sharply to check if the respirator collapses inward, and exhale gently to feel for air leaks around the edges of the facepiece.

Safe Removal and Disposal Procedures

Removing protective gear improperly can contaminate your hands, face, or uniform. The outer surface of any used mask is considered heavily contaminated with pathogens from the patient or the surrounding environment.

To remove a mask safely, handle only the ties, elastic bands, or ear loops. Never touch the front filter portion of the mask. Tilt your head slightly forward, unfasten the bottom straps first, followed by the top straps, and pull the mask straight away from your face. Immediately discard the mask into a designated infectious waste or biohazard receptacle.

Hand hygiene must be performed immediately after removal. If your hands accidentally brush against the front of a contaminated mask during the process, wash or sanitize them right away to prevent self-inoculation of your mucous membranes.

Common Mistakes and Infection Control Pitfalls

Infection control protocols fail when protective equipment is worn or handled incorrectly. A frequent pitfall is wearing a mask pulled down around the chin or hanging from one ear when not in active patient care areas. This practice transfers facial skin oils and environmental germs to the inner lining of the mask, which then rests directly against the nose and mouth.

Other common errors include touching or adjusting the mask continuously with unwashed hands, reusing single-use disposable masks beyond manufacturer recommendations, and storing removed masks loosely in uniform pockets or handbags. Masks that become damp, soiled, or structurally damaged lose their filtration integrity and must be discarded immediately. Maintaining strict discipline with mask hygiene protects both the nurse and the wider healthcare environment.
