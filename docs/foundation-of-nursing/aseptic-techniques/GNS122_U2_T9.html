Use of Masks

Introduction to Respiratory Protection in Nursing

Respiratory protection forms a primary defense line against airborne and droplet-transmitted pathogens in clinical environments. Pathogens such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, influenza viruses, and novel respiratory agents spread easily through microscopic respiratory secretions generated during coughing, talking, or medical procedures. Nursing personnel encounter these biological hazards daily, making proper barrier usage non-negotiable for personal safety and nosocomial infection prevention. Standard precautions require evaluating the transmission route before entering a patient's care zone, ensuring the appropriate facial barrier is selected. Without adequate respiratory barriers, healthcare workers risk becoming vectors of disease, spreading infections between vulnerable hospitalized populations and community members.

Types of Medical and Surgical Masks

Surgical masks are loose-fitting, disposable devices designed to create a physical barrier between the mouth and nose of the wearer and potential contaminants in the immediate environment. They primarily protect patients from the wearer's respiratory emissions by trapping large particle droplets, splashes, and sprays. - **Type I / Level 1**: Low fluid resistance, generally used for basic patient examination and general outpatient procedures. - **Type II / Level 2**: Moderate fluid resistance and bacterial filtration efficiency, suitable for standard surgical procedures. - **Type IIR / Level 3**: High fluid resistance, designed to withstand splashes of blood or bodily fluids during intensive surgical interventions.

Understanding Respirators (N95 and Higher)

Unlike loose-fitting surgical masks, particulate respirators like the N95, FFP2, or N100 are engineered to achieve a very close facial fit and efficient filtration of airborne particles. An N95 respirator blocks at least 95 percent of test particles measuring 0.3 microns in diameter. These devices protect the wearer from inhaling hazardous microscopic aerosols, including tubercle bacilli, varicella-zoster virus, and measles. The structural integrity depends entirely on a tight facial seal; any gap compromises filtration performance, allowing unfiltered air to bypass the filter media.

Indications for Mask Usage in Healthcare Settings

Clinical protocols dictate specific scenarios for wearing facial protection based on transmission-based precautions. Surgical masks are indicated when providing routine care to patients with droplet-spread infections, during sterile procedures to protect the sterile field, and during standard universal precautions. Particulate respirators are mandatory when entering isolation environments for diseases spread through the airborne route, such as active pulmonary tuberculosis or chickenpox. In busy Nigerian tertiary health centers managing Lassa fever or viral hemorrhagic fevers, strict adherence to specialized respirator protocols prevents catastrophic occupational exposure.

Proper Technique for Donning and Fit Testing

Donning a mask requires a methodical sequence to maintain sterility and structural integrity. Hand hygiene must be performed before touching any protective equipment. For respirators, qualitative or quantitative fit testing is a mandatory regulatory requirement prior to initial use to verify that the specific model and size match the wearer's facial structure. 1. **Placement**: Cup the respirator in the hand, positioning it under the chin with the nosepiece up. 2. **Straps**: Pull the top strap over the crown of the head and the bottom strap around the neck below the ears. 3. **Contouring**: Press the flexible metal nose clip firmly using both hands to conform snugly to the bridge of the nose. 4. **Seal Check**: Perform a user seal check by exhaling sharply and inhaling deeply to detect any air leaks around the edges.

Safe Removal and Disposal Procedures

The removal phase carries a high risk of self-contamination because the outer surface of the mask traps infectious pathogens during wear. Hands must never touch the front filtering material. The wearer should unfasten the bottom strap first, bringing it up and over the head, followed by the top strap, handling only the elastic bands. Once detached, the device is dropped directly into a designated infectious waste receptacle or biohazard bin. Immediate hand hygiene using alcohol-based rub or soap and water concludes the sequence, neutralizing any accidental microbial transfer.

Common Mistakes and Infection Control Pitfalls

Infection control breaches often stem from improper handling habits rather than equipment failure. Wearing a mask under the chin, leaving the nose exposed, or hanging it loosely around the neck defeats its protective purpose and transfers pathogens to the skin or uniform. Touching the front of the mask repeatedly during patient care contaminates fingers. Furthermore, reusing disposable single-use masks beyond manufacturer guidelines or failing to replace damp, soiled, or damaged devices compromises filtration integrity, putting both nurse and patient at avoidable risk.
