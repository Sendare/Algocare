Theatre Sterile Supply Unit (TSSU)

Introduction to Theatre Sterile Supply Unit (TSSU)

The Theatre Sterile Supply Unit (TSSU) functions as the centralized powerhouse for processing, sterilizing, and distributing medical devices and surgical instruments used across healthcare facilities. Operating closely with the operating theatre and various clinical wards, this department eliminates microbial life to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Instruments move through a strict, unidirectional workflow that physically separates contaminated items from sterile goods, preventing cross-contamination within the facility.

Zoning and Layout of a Standard TSSU

A well-designed TSSU utilizes structural zoning to control microbial spread. The facility is divided into distinct operational areas:

  • Decontamination Area: The receiving and washing zone where soiled instruments arrive. It operates under negative air pressure to keep aerosols from escaping into other rooms.
  • Preparation and Packaging Area: A clean zone where dried instruments are inspected, assembled into sets, and wrapped.
  • Sterile Storage Area: The cleanest zone where sterilized packs are stored under positive air pressure until needed in clinical areas.

The Decontamination Process: Cleaning and Decontamination

Decontamination serves as the critical first step in rendering soiled items safe for handling and subsequent sterilization. If organic matter like blood or tissue remains on an instrument, it shields microorganisms from sterilizing agents.

  1. Sorting and Disassembly: Complex instruments are taken apart to expose hidden surfaces.
  2. Manual Cleaning: Initial scrubbing is done underwater to prevent splashing and aerosol generation.
  3. Mechanical Washing: Automated washer-disinfectors use thermal disinfection and enzymatic detergents to remove stubborn residues.

Inspection, Assembly, and Packaging of Instruments

Once cleaned and dried, instruments enter the clean assembly zone. Technicians inspect every item using magnifying lamps to check for residual stains, pitting, or mechanical failure. Hinged instruments like forceps and needle holders are tested for smooth movement.

Instruments are then arranged into specific surgical tray configurations using perforated baskets. Packaging materials—such as medical-grade paper bags, woven wraps, or rigid container systems—must allow the sterilizing agent to penetrate while maintaining a sterile barrier after processing.

Sterilization Methods and Quality Assurance

Sterilization achieves the complete destruction of all forms of microbial life, including bacterial endospores. Steam sterilization via autoclaving is the gold standard for heat- and moisture-stable items. Ethylene oxide and hydrogen peroxide gas plasma serve as alternatives for heat-sensitive equipment.

Quality assurance relies on three monitoring pillars: physical indicators (gauges and printouts), chemical indicators (strip indicators that change color when exposed to specific parameters), and biological indicators (spore tests containing *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* that confirm true microbial destruction).

Storage, Distribution, and Inventory Management

Proper handling after sterilization preserves package integrity. Sterile items must cool completely on sterilizer carts before being touched, as warm packages absorb ambient moisture and draw in airborne bacteria. Storage shelves should be positioned away from exterior walls, floors, and ceiling vents to avoid temperature fluctuations and moisture accumulation.

Stock rotation follows the First-In, First-Out (FIFO) principle to prevent items from sitting past their sterile shelf life. Transport carts must be enclosed or covered to protect clean and sterile supplies during transit through general hospital corridors.

Infection Control and Safety Protocols in TSSU

Staff safety and infection prevention govern every daily procedure in the sterile supply unit. Personnel in the decontamination zone must wear heavy-duty utility gloves, waterproof aprons, face shields, and masks to guard against splashes and airborne droplets. Universal precautions treat all used instruments as potentially infectious.

Routine equipment validation, maintenance logs, and strict hand hygiene compliance protect both the workforce and surgical patients from preventable nosocomial complications.
