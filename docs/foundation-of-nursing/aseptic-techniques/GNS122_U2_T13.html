Injection Procedure

Principles and Routes of Injection Administration

Administering medication via injection is an invasive procedure that bypasses the digestive tract, delivering drugs directly into body tissues or the bloodstream. Because the skin barrier is broken, strict aseptic technique is mandatory to prevent introducing pathogens. Nurses must always verify the order using the rights of medication administration: right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, and right documentation.

The four primary routes differ by the tissue layer targeted, absorption rate, and volume capacity:

  • Intradermal (ID): Administered into the dermis layer just beneath the epidermis, used mostly for allergy testing or local anesthesia, utilizing very small volumes (0.1 mL or less).
  • Subcutaneous (SubQ): Placed in the loose connective tissue under the dermis, ideal for slow, sustained systemic absorption of medications like insulin and heparin.
  • Intramuscular (IM): Delivered deep into muscle tissue, providing faster absorption than subcutaneous routes due to a rich vascular supply.
  • Intravenous (IV): Injected directly into a vein, offering immediate peak concentration and total bioavailability.

Anatomy and Selection of Injection Sites

Choosing the correct anatomical site protects underlying nerves, large blood vessels, and bone structures from accidental injury. For intramuscular injections, three common sites are used depending on patient age and muscle mass:

  1. Ventrogluteal: Considered the safest and most preferred site for adults and older children because it is free of major nerves and blood vessels, overlying the gluteus medius.
  2. Vastus Lateralis: Located on the anterior-lateral thigh, this site is thick and well-developed, making it the primary choice for infants and toddlers.
  3. Deltoid: Situated on the lateral upper arm, easily accessible but limited to small volume injections (typically 1 mL or less) due to its smaller muscle mass.

Subcutaneous sites include the outer posterior aspect of the upper arms, the abdomen (keeping a two-inch radius away from the umbilicus), the anterior thighs, and the scapular area of the upper back. Rotating sites within the same anatomical region is essential for chronic medication users, such as diabetic patients taking daily insulin, to prevent lipodystrophy—the breakdown or thickening of subcutaneous fat.

Equipment: Syringes, Needles, and Safety Devices

Selecting the proper equipment ensures accurate dosing and minimizes patient trauma. Syringes consist of a barrel, plunger, and tip, measured in milliliters. Standard sizes range from 1 mL to 60 mL, where tuberculin syringes measure small accurate doses up to 1 mL, and insulin syringes are calibrated specifically in units.

Needle selection depends on patient weight, age, tissue depth, and medication viscosity. A needle is defined by two measurements:

  • Length: Distance from the point to the hub, varying from 1/4 inch to 3 inches.
  • Gauge: The diameter of the lumen; the larger the gauge number, the smaller the actual needle diameter.

Safety devices, including needleless systems, retractable shields, and self-sheathing needles, are critical to prevent accidental needlestick injuries in healthcare environments.

Preparing Medications from Vials and Ampoules

Drawing medication safely requires careful handling of different packaging types. A vial is a glass or plastic container with a rubber seal secured by a metal cap. Because vials are closed systems, air must be injected into the vial equal to the volume of liquid being withdrawn to prevent a vacuum from forming.

An ampoule is a fragile glass container with a constricted neck. To open an ampoule safely, the nurse wraps a small alcohol swab or gauze around the neck to protect the fingers and snaps the top away from their body. A filter needle or filter straw must be used when drawing medication from an ampoule to prevent tiny glass shards from entering the syringe.

When mixing two medications in a single syringe, such as rapid-acting and intermediate-acting insulins, the nurse draws clear insulin before cloudy insulin to avoid contaminating the clear vial with cloudy sediment.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administration

Executing an injection smoothly minimizes patient anxiety and physical trauma. Begin by performing hand hygiene and gathering all required supplies. Double-check the medication label against the medication administration record at the bedside. Position the patient comfortably, exposing only the targeted anatomical area.

Cleanse the selected site using an antiseptic swab, moving in a firm circular motion from the center outward for about two inches, and allow the skin to air dry completely. Wet antiseptic can sting and destroy the germ-killing effect.

Spread the skin taut for subcutaneous or intramuscular injections, or bunch the tissue for thin patients. Insert the needle quickly and firmly at the correct angle: 15 degrees for intradermal, 45 to 90 degrees for subcutaneous, and 90 degrees for intramuscular. Inject the medication slowly at a rate of approximately 1 mL per 10 seconds to reduce tissue tearing and pain. Withdraw the needle swiftly along the same angle it entered, and immediately engage the safety guard without recapping manually.

Special Techniques: Z-Track Method

The Z-track method is a specialized technique used for administering intramuscular injections, particularly with irritating, staining, or viscous medications like iron dextran. This method seals the medication deep within the muscle tissue, preventing leakage into the sensitive subcutaneous layers and skin.

To execute the Z-track technique:

  1. Place the non-dominant hand on the skin adjacent to the injection site and pull the skin and subcutaneous tissue laterally by about 1 to 1.5 inches.
  2. Hold the skin taut in this displaced position throughout the entire injection process.
  3. Insert the needle at a 90-degree angle into the muscle.
  4. Inject the medication slowly, then wait approximately 10 seconds to allow the drug to disperse into the muscle tissue.
  5. Withdraw the needle smoothly while maintaining the skin displacement.
  6. Release the pulled skin, which causes the needle track to zigzag, sealing the medication inside.

Prevention of Complications and Needle-Stick Injuries

Infection control and injury prevention are core responsibilities during any injection procedure. Needle-stick injuries expose healthcare personnel to bloodborne pathogens such as Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV. Nurses must never recap used needles by hand. If a safety device is absent, use a one-handed scoop technique to engage the cap or discard the entire syringe directly into a puncture-resistant sharps container.

Local and systemic complications can arise from improper technique. Nerve injury can result from selecting incorrect anatomical landmarks, such as hitting the sciatic nerve during a poorly placed gluteal injection. Hematomas and abscesses can develop from vessel laceration or unsterile technique. Nurses must monitor patients for adverse drug reactions, including anaphylaxis, and keep emergency equipment nearby when administering high-risk medications.

Post-Procedure Documentation and Patient Monitoring

Accurate documentation completes the medication administration process and ensures continuity of care. The nurse must record the medication name, dose, route, exact site used, date, time, and their signature or electronic identification immediately after administration. Documenting the patient's immediate response and any local skin reactions is equally vital.

Patient monitoring involves observing for therapeutic effects and potential adverse reactions. For vaccines and IV antibiotics, nurses must monitor patients for at least 15 to 30 minutes post-injection for signs of allergic reactions, including urticaria, wheezing, or facial swelling. For routine subcutaneous and intramuscular injections, checking the site for bleeding, swelling, or redness during subsequent rounds confirms normal recovery.
