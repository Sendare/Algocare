Injection Procedure

Introduction and Principles of Medication Administration via Injection

Parenteral medication administration bypasses the gastrointestinal tract, delivering drugs directly into tissues or the circulatory system. This route ensures rapid absorption, making it invaluable in emergency situations or when patients cannot tolerate oral intake. Clinical safety rests upon strict adherence to established protocols, particularly the classic checks of medication safety. Every practitioner must verify the correct patient, drug, dose, route, and time before touching a patient with a needle. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, verbal verifications with colleagues often catch critical discrepancies before administration occurs. Sterile technique remains non-negotiable throughout the entire process to prevent the introduction of pathogens into deep tissues. Environmental preparation, hand hygiene, and respect for the sterile boundaries of equipment protect both patient and provider.

Anatomy and Selection of Injection Sites

Choosing the correct anatomical site safeguards underlying nerves, bones, and major blood vessels from mechanical injury. Landmark identification must be performed meticulously using palpable bony prominences rather than visual estimation alone. - **Ventrogluteal**: Considered the safest site for intramuscular injections in adults due to the absence of major nerves and large blood vessels. - **Vastus Lateralis**: Located on the anterior-lateral thigh, making it the preferred site for infants and toddlers. - **Deltoid**: Easily accessible on the upper arm but limited to small volume injections, typically one milliliter or less. - **Dorsogluteal**: Historically common, but its use is strongly discouraged due to its close proximity to the sciatic nerve and superior gluteal artery.

Types of Injections: Routes and Depths

Different injection routes target specific tissue layers, dictating needle length, gauge, and insertion angle. Understanding these tissue characteristics ensures the drug achieves its intended pharmacological profile. | Route | Target Tissue | Insertion Angle | Common Needle Gauge/Length | | --- | --- | --- | --- | | Intradermal (ID) | Dermis layer just beneath epidermis | 5 to 15 degrees | 25-27 gauge, 1/2 to 5/8 inch | | Subcutaneous (SC) | Loose connective tissue under dermis | 45 or 90 degrees | 25-30 gauge, 3/8 to 1 inch | | Intramuscular (IM) | Deep skeletal muscle tissue | 90 degrees | 20-25 gauge, 1 to 1.5 inches | | Intravenous (IV) | Directly into the venous lumen | 15 to 30 degrees | 18-24 gauge, varies | Intradermal injections are used primarily for diagnostic testing, such as tuberculin or allergy screenings, where rapid systemic absorption is undesirable. Subcutaneous injections accommodate slow, sustained absorption of non-irritating water-soluble drugs like insulin and heparin. Intramuscular routes handle larger volumes and irritating substances, while intravenous access provides immediate peak concentrations.

Equipment Selection: Syringes, Needles, and Safety Devices

Selecting appropriate hardware prevents waste, tissue trauma, and accidental injuries. Syringe capacities range from tuberculin units up to fifty milliliters, matched to the prescribed volume. Needle gauge inversely correlates with diameter; a larger gauge number denotes a finer needle. Viscous solutions or blood products demand wider gauges, such as eighteen or twenty, whereas aqueous medications pass easily through twenty-five or twenty-seven gauge needles. Modern healthcare facilities mandate the use of engineered safety devices. These mechanisms either shield the needle tip automatically or allow manual activation immediately following withdrawal. Retractable needles and hinged protective guards successfully reduce sharps injuries on hospital wards. Practitioners must verify that all components remain sterile and intact within their blister packaging before clinical use.

Preparation of Medications: Vials, Ampoules, and Reconstitution

Drawing medication accurately requires distinct techniques depending on the packaging format. Ampoules are glass containers with a constricted neck that must be snapped open. Snapping away from the body and using a filter needle prevents glass shards from entering the fluid pathway. Vials are sealed containers featuring a rubber diaphragm secured by a metal cap. Because multi-dose or single-dose vials contain negative pressure, an equal volume of air must be injected into the vial before withdrawing the liquid. Reconstitution involves mixing a sterile diluent with powdered medication. The nurse injects the diluent into the powder vial, rolls the container gently between the palms until completely dissolved, and then withdraws the final concentration. Labeling reconstituted medications with the exact concentration, date, time, and nurse initials is mandatory in all clinical environments.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Safe Injection Administration

Executing an injection follows a logical sequence that prioritizes asepsis and patient comfort. Begin by performing hand hygiene and gathering all necessary supplies at the bedside. Greet the patient, explain the procedure in clear terms, and check identification bands. Select the appropriate anatomical site and position the patient to relax the target muscle or expose the skin surface. Don clean gloves and cleanse the skin using an alcohol swab in a firm circular motion, moving outward from the center for about five centimeters. Allow the antiseptic to dry completely; applying alcohol while wet causes burning and neutralizes its antimicrobial action. Remove the needle cap, pull the skin taut or bunch it depending on the route, and insert the needle smoothly at the correct angle. Administer the medication at a steady, controlled rate, pause briefly to allow tissue dispersion, and withdraw smoothly along the insertion axis. Engage the safety mechanism immediately and discard the unit into a puncture-resistant sharps container.

Special Techniques: The Z-Track Method

Certain intramuscular medications, such as iron dextran or irritating neuroleptic drugs, cause severe skin discoloration, pain, or subcutaneous inflammation if they leak back into superficial tracts. The Z-track technique prevents this leakage by sealing the medication deep within the muscle. To execute this method, pull the skin and subcutaneous tissue laterally about two to three centimeters before insertion. Hold the tissue in this displaced position throughout the injection and the brief pause after delivery. Withdraw the needle smoothly, and only then release the stretched skin. The tissue snaps back into its natural alignment, creating a zigzag path that traps the medication securely inside the muscle belly.

Prevention of Complications and Needle-Stick Injuries

Injections carry inherent risks for both patients and healthcare providers. Local complications include hematoma formation, nerve damage, sterile abscesses, and muscle fibrosis from repeated punctures in identical spots. Rotating injection sites diligently prevents tissue breakdown and erratic drug absorption. Needle-stick injuries represent an occupational hazard associated with bloodborne pathogen transmission, including hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and human immunodeficiency virus. Standard precautions dictate that needles must NEVER be recapped manually using both hands. Safety devices must be activated instantly, and all sharps disposed of in rigid, yellow, puncture-proof containers located at the point of use. If an accidental exposure occurs, staff must wash the wound immediately with soap and water, report the incident, and initiate post-exposure prophylaxis protocols without delay.

Post-Procedure Documentation and Patient Monitoring

Clinical accountability requires prompt and accurate documentation following any medication administration. The patient chart must reflect the exact drug name, dose, route, anatomical site used, date, time, and the nurse signature or electronic stamp. Any patient refusal, adverse reaction, or omitted dose must be recorded with explanatory notes. Patient monitoring extends beyond the immediate injection act. Observe the individual for acute allergic manifestations, including urticaria, respiratory distress, or anaphylaxis, particularly when administering antibiotics or vaccines for the first time. Inspect the local injection site for signs of bleeding, swelling, or undue tenderness. Effective handover communication ensures that subsequent nursing shifts remain aware of the patient response and scheduled follow-up doses.
