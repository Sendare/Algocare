Standard Precautions

Introduction and Rationale for Standard Precautions

Standard precautions serve as the foundational tier of infection prevention and control in clinical settings. They apply universally to all patients receiving care, regardless of their diagnosed or presumed infection status. The primary philosophy driving this practice is that every individual's blood, body fluids, secretions, and non-intact skin must be treated as potentially infectious. By assuming universal risk, healthcare personnel protect both themselves and vulnerable patients from cross-contamination, especially during routine nursing procedures where pathogens may not be immediately obvious.

Core Components of Hand Hygiene

Hand hygiene is universally recognized as the single most effective intervention for interrupting the chain of infection in healthcare environments. Nurses must perform hand cleansing before and after every direct patient contact, after touching inanimate objects in the immediate patient vicinity, and immediately following the removal of gloves. - **Alcohol-based hand rub**: Preferred for routine clinical situations when hands are not visibly soiled, requiring complete coverage and friction until dry. - **Plain or antimicrobial soap and water**: Indicated whenever hands are visibly dirty, contaminated with proteinaceous material, or after exposure to spore-forming pathogens like *Clostridioides difficile*.

Selection and Proper Application of Personal Protective Equipment

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical barrier between the nurse's body and infectious agents. Selection is dictated by the anticipated mode of transmission and the likelihood of exposure during a specific task. | PPE Item | Primary Clinical Indication | Key Application Rule | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Gloves** | Anticipated contact with blood, fluids, or mucous membranes | Put on last, remove first without touching outer surface | | **Gowns** | Risk of clothing contamination from splashing fluids | Tie securely at neck and back to ensure full coverage | | **Masks & Eye Protection** | Potential generation of splashes, sprays, or respiratory droplets | Ensure a tight seal across the bridge of the nose | Donning follows a logical sequence to maximize protection before exposure, whereas doffing requires meticulous technique to avoid contaminating skin or scrubs.

Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette

Respiratory hygiene targets the source of droplet-borne pathogens originating from the respiratory tract. Patients, visitors, and healthcare workers exhibiting signs of illness must practice containment strategies. This involves covering the mouth and nose with a tissue during coughing or sneezing, immediately disposing of used tissues into the nearest waste receptacle, and performing hand hygiene afterward. In busy outpatient clinics or emergency waiting areas, signage, facial masks, and accessible hand sanitizing stations should be prominently maintained to limit community spread.

Safe Management and Disposal of Sharps

Needles, scalpels, and other sharp instruments represent a high-risk vector for blood-borne pathogen transmission, including Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV. Safe management requires strict adherence to handling rules. Never recap used needles using two hands, bend, break, or remove needles from syringes by hand. Immediately after use, deposit the entire sharp unit into a puncture-resistant, leak-proof sharps container located within arm's reach of the procedure area. Containers must never be filled above the designated fill line to prevent accidental punctures during disposal.

Environmental Decontamination and Patient Care Equipment

Patient care items and surrounding surfaces can act as reservoirs for resilient nosocomial pathogens. Reusable equipment, such as blood pressure cuffs, stethoscopes, and glucose meters, must be thoroughly cleaned and disinfected between patient uses according to manufacturer guidelines. High-touch environmental surfaces—including bed rails, bedside tables, and call buttons—require routine disinfection using hospital-approved germicidal agents. Soiled linens should be handled with minimum agitation, rolled inward, and placed directly into leak-resistant laundry bags to prevent the dispersal of infectious dust particles into the air.

Prevention and Management of Occupational Exposure

Despite rigorous safety protocols, accidental exposures through needle sticks, cuts, or mucous membrane splashes still occur in clinical practice. Immediate post-exposure management is critical: wash the affected skin area thoroughly with soap and water, or flush exposed mucous membranes with copious amounts of clean water or saline. Do not squeeze, scrub, or bleed the puncture wound. Report the incident immediately to the occupational health unit or supervisor to evaluate infection risk and initiate timely post-exposure prophylaxis when indicated.
