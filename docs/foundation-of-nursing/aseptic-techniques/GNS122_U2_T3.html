Standard Precautions

Introduction and Core Principles of Standard Precautions

Standard precautions serve as the foundational infection control practices designed to reduce the risk of transmission of bloodborne and other pathogens in healthcare settings. These measures apply to all patients receiving care, regardless of their diagnosed or presumed infection status. Treating every patient interaction with the same baseline level of safety protects both the healthcare worker and subsequent patients from cross-contamination.

  • Universal applicability: Used for all patients at all times in all healthcare environments.
  • Blood and body fluid focus: Applies to blood, all body fluids, secretions, and excretions except sweat, regardless of whether they contain visible blood.
  • Non-intact skin and mucous membranes: Precautions extend to damaged skin and moist tissues where pathogen entry or exit is likely.

Hand Hygiene: The Single Most Effective Measure

Hand hygiene is widely recognized as the most critical intervention for preventing healthcare-associated infections. Microorganisms are easily transferred from patient to patient via the hands of healthcare personnel.

Handwashing with soap and water must be performed when hands are visibly soiled, dirty, or contaminated with proteinaceous material, as well as after caring for patients with known or suspected infectious diarrhea like Clostridioides difficile. Alcohol-based hand rubs are preferred for routine clinical work when hands are not visibly dirty because they kill microbes effectively and cause less skin dryness.

Selection and Proper Use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical barrier between the nurse and potential infectious agents. Selecting the correct item depends on the expected exposure during a task.

  1. Gloves: Wear when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin. Change gloves between tasks on the same patient if moving from a contaminated body site to a clean one.
  2. Gowns: Wear to protect skin and clothing during procedures that are likely to generate splashes of blood or body fluids.
  3. Masks and Eye Protection: Use surgical masks and face shields or goggles whenever splashes or sprays to the face, eyes, nose, or mouth are anticipated.

Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette

Respiratory hygiene targets the source of respiratory pathogens, particularly during seasonal outbreaks of influenza or other droplet-spread illnesses. Patients, visitors, and staff must contain respiratory secretions at the point of origin.

Key elements include covering the mouth and nose with a tissue when coughing or sneezing, promptly disposing of used tissues in the nearest waste receptacle, and performing hand hygiene immediately after contact with respiratory secretions. Healthcare facilities should provide tissues, no-touch waste bins, and alcohol-based hand rub dispensers near entrances and waiting areas.

Safe Handling and Disposal of Sharps

Needle-stick injuries and cuts from sharp instruments pose serious occupational risks for nursing personnel due to potential transmission of bloodborne viruses such as HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C.

Never recap used needles using a two-handed technique. If a needle must be recapped to prevent accidental pricks before disposal, use a one-hand scoop method or a mechanical safety device. Always dispose of needles, scalpels, and other sharp objects immediately in puncture-resistant, leak-proof sharps containers located close to the point of use. Never overfill a sharps container.

Environmental Cleaning and Equipment Management

Patient care environments harbor microorganisms that survive on dry surfaces for extended periods. Routine cleaning and disinfection of environmental surfaces—such as bed rails, bedside tables, and call buttons—interrupt indirect contact transmission.

Reusable medical equipment must be cleaned and reprocessed properly between patient uses. Non-critical items like blood pressure cuffs and stethoscopes require low- or intermediate-level disinfection. Linens soiled with blood or body fluids should be handled with minimum agitation to avoid dispersing pathogens into the air and placed in leak-resistant bags.
