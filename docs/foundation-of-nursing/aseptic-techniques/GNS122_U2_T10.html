Use of Gloves

Principles of Medical Asepsis and Hand Hygiene

Gloves never replace hand hygiene. Medical asepsis aims to reduce the number and spread of microorganisms in the healthcare environment. Microorganisms easily colonize the warm, moist environment inside synthetic barriers. Failing to perform hand hygiene before putting on gloves allows resident flora to multiply rapidly, compromising patient safety if a glove micro-perforates. Alcohol-based hand rub is appropriate for routine non-sterile care, while hand washing with soap and water is mandatory when hands are visibly soiled or following contact with spore-forming pathogens like Clostridioides difficile.

Indications and Purpose of Wearing Gloves

Personal protective barriers serve two distinct functions: protecting healthcare personnel from infectious agents and preventing microbial transmission from staff to vulnerable patients. Clinical guidelines dictate barrier use whenever contact with blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or contaminated equipment is anticipated. Routine vital sign acquisition on intact skin does not require gloves unless infectious outbreaks dictate universal precautions. Overusing barriers wastes resources and breeds a false sense of security that frequently leads to lax hand hygiene practices.

Examination vs. Surgical Gloves

Medical barriers are manufactured to distinct quality standards depending on their intended application.

  • Examination Gloves: Non-sterile barriers used for routine patient care, diagnostic procedures, and non-invasive specimen handling. They come in ambidextrous designs and acceptable quality limit standards tailored for general tasks.
  • Surgical Gloves: Sterile barriers manufactured with anatomical shaping, longer cuffs to cover gown sleeves, and rigorous testing for pinholes. They are sized precisely by number (e.g., 6.0, 6.5, 7.0) to ensure tactile sensitivity and reduce hand fatigue during invasive procedures.

Material Selection: Latex, Nitrile, and Vinyl

Selecting the appropriate barrier material depends on allergy risks, chemical exposure, and task duration.

  • Natural Rubber Latex: Offers superior fit, high tactile sensitivity, and elasticity. However, proteins in latex can trigger severe IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reactions in staff and patients.
  • Nitrile: A synthetic copolymer providing exceptional puncture resistance and chemical durability. It serves as the primary alternative for latex-allergic individuals and withstands many harsh hospital disinfectants.
  • Vinyl: Polyvinyl chloride lacks the elasticity of latex or nitrile, resulting in a looser fit and higher failure rate under stress. It suits short, low-risk procedures where chemical resistance is not required.

Procedure for Proper Glove Donning

Donning clean gloves requires minimal technique, but maintaining sterility demands strict adherence to protocol. For non-sterile procedures, perform hand hygiene, select the correct size, and pull the gloves over the hands, ensuring the cuffs extend over the wrists of long-sleeved garments. For sterile procedures, open the outer wrapper carefully to maintain a sterile field, pick up the first glove by the inner cuff fold touching only the inner surface, slip it onto the dominant hand, slide the gloved fingers of the dominant hand under the cuff of the second sterile glove, and pull it onto the non-dominant hand without touching bare skin.

Safe Removal and Doffing Techniques

Improper glove removal is a leading cause of self-contamination in clinical settings. Pathogens accumulated on the outer surface easily transfer to bare skin if doffing is executed carelessly. Grasp the outside of the first glove near the cuff, peel it away from the hand, and turn it inside out as it is removed, holding it within the gloved hand. Slide the bare fingers of the un-gloved hand under the remaining cuff of the second glove, peel it down inside out over the first glove, and discard both items immediately into the designated waste receptacle.

Common Errors and Prevention of Cross-Contamination

Clinical environments harbor hidden risks when protective barriers are mishandled. A frequent pitfall involves touching environmental surfaces, chart folders, or mobile phones while wearing soiled gloves, instantly transferring microorganisms across clean zones. Never wash or apply alcohol rub to disposable gloves; alcohol breaks down the structural integrity of synthetic polymers, creating micro-channels for fluid penetration. Changing gloves and performing hand hygiene between different care activities on the same patient—such as moving from a wound care site to vascular access maintenance—is critical to halt endogenous infection transfer.

Role in Standard and Transmission-Based Precautions

Protective barriers form the foundation of tiered isolation systems used to control infectious disease spread. Standard Precautions treat all patients as potentially infectious, requiring gloves whenever exposure to blood, body fluids, non-intact skin, or mucous membranes is likely, regardless of diagnosis. Transmission-Based Precautions add specific barriers for targeted routes of spread: Contact Precautions mandate gloves and gowns for any room entry; Droplet Precautions require masks along with eye protection; and Airborne Precautions utilize specialized respirators. Mastering these hierarchies protects both staff and community health.
