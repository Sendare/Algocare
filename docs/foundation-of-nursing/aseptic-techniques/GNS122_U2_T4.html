Sterilization

Introduction and Definition of Sterilization

Sterilization is the complete destruction or removal of all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial spores, fungi, viruses, and prions. In clinical nursing practice, achieving a state of absolute sterility is non-negotiable when dealing with items that enter sterile body tissues or the vascular system. Unlike disinfection, which merely reduces the number of microorganisms to a safe level, sterilization leaves no room for survival. If an object is sterile, the probability of a living microorganism surviving on it must be theoretically zero, usually expressed as a sterility assurance level of ten to the negative sixth power. Nurses encounter sterilization daily, from surgical instruments to indwelling catheters and injectable medications, making it a foundational pillar of infection prevention and patient safety.

Cleaning, Disinfection, and Sterilization Compared

Infection control relies on a clear hierarchy of processing methods. Understanding how cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization differ prevents cross-contamination and improper equipment use.

  • Cleaning: The physical removal of visible organic matter, blood, and dirt using water, detergents, and friction. This must always happen first because dirt protects germs from chemical or thermal agents.
  • Disinfection: A process that eliminates most pathogenic microorganisms on inanimate objects, excluding bacterial spores. It uses liquid chemicals or pasteurization and is split into low, intermediate, and high levels.
  • Sterilization: The ultimate level of decontamination that destroys all microbial forms completely using extreme heat, pressurized steam, gas, or chemical sterilants.

The Spaulding Classification System

Dr. Earle Spaulding created a rational approach for disinfecting and sterilizing patient care items based on the degree of risk for infection. This classification guides nurses and central processing staff on how to treat every tool entering the clinical environment.

Physical Methods of Sterilization

Physical methods are the gold standard for hospital sterilization because they are reliable, leave no toxic residue, and are easy to monitor.

  1. Autoclaving (Steam Under Pressure): Uses moist heat at temperatures like 121 degrees Celsius or 132 degrees Celsius under pressure. The moisture helps coagulate cellular proteins quickly, destroying all microbes.
  2. Dry Heat: Uses hot air ovens at higher temperatures, typically 160 to 170 degrees Celsius, for items that are sensitive to moisture or cannot rust, such as powders, oils, and sharp metal instruments.
  3. Radiation: Gamma rays and electron beams are used mainly by commercial manufacturers for pre-packaged single-use disposable medical supplies like syringes and sutures.

Chemical Methods of Sterilization

When instruments are sensitive to heat or moisture, chemical sterilization provides a safe alternative. Liquid chemicals like glutaraldehyde, hydrogen peroxide gas plasma, and ethylene oxide gas are common agents. Ethylene oxide is highly effective for delicate instruments like fiber-optic scopes and electronics, but it requires lengthy aeration periods because the gas is toxic and leaves dangerous residues. Hydrogen peroxide gas plasma uses a low-temperature vapor state that is safe for heat-sensitive tools and leaves no toxic byproducts, making it popular in modern healthcare facilities. A common misconception is that soaking instruments in standard liquid disinfectant for a long time achieves sterilization; however, liquid chemicals alone are unreliable for true sterilization unless strictly controlled.

Sterilization Monitoring and Quality Control

Ensuring that a sterilization cycle actually worked requires a multi-layered quality control system combining mechanical, chemical, and biological indicators.

  • Mechanical Monitoring: Involves checking gauges, computer printouts, and digital displays on the sterilizer machine during every cycle to verify time, temperature, and pressure.
  • Chemical Indicators: Uses special tape, strips, or cards containing ink that changes color when exposed to specific temperatures or sterilizing agents. This proves the package was processed, though it does not guarantee complete sterility.
  • Biological Indicators: The gold standard for verification. These contain highly resistant bacterial spores, such as Geobacillus stearothermophilus. If the spores are killed during the cycle, it proves the machine successfully destroyed all life.

Safe Handling, Packaging, and Storage of Sterile Supplies

Even the most rigorous sterilization cycle is useless if items become contaminated before use. Packaging materials must allow the sterilizing agent to penetrate while keeping microorganisms out afterward. Woven wrappers, porous medical paper, and specialized pouches are common choices. When handling sterile packs, nurses must inspect the wrapper for tears, moisture, or broken seals. Event-related sterility replaced old expiration dates: a package remains sterile indefinitely unless the integrity of the packaging is compromised by moisture, dust, or handling. Store sterile supplies in clean, dry, well-ventilated areas away from floors and ceilings.
