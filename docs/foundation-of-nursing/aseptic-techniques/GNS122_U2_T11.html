Wound Dressing

Introduction and Purpose of Wound Dressing

A wound dressing is a sterile pad or protective application applied directly to an injured body part. Unlike a simple bandage—which usually holds a dressing in place or provides compression—a dressing makes direct contact with the wound bed itself.

The primary goals of wound care go beyond simply covering an injury. Effective dressings serve several vital functions:

  • Protecting against contamination: Acting as a physical barrier against dirt, bacteria, and environmental pathogens.
  • Managing moisture: Maintaining a moist wound environment while absorbing excess exudate (fluid drainage), which speeds up cellular repair and reduces scar formation.
  • Providing thermal insulation: Keeping the wound bed at an optimal body temperature to support cellular activity.
  • Controlling bleeding: Applying gentle pressure or delivering heostatic agents to halt minor hemorrhages.
  • Relieving pain: Cushioning the raw nerve endings exposed in the injured tissue.

Classification of Wounds

Wounds are broadly categorized based on how they occur, their depth, and their level of microbial contamination. Understanding these categories guides the choice of dressing and anticipates healing timelines.

  • Intentional vs. Unintentional: Surgical incisions are planned and created under sterile conditions, whereas traumatic lacerations or abrasions happen unexpectedly.
  • Clean, Clean-Contaminated, Contaminated, and Infected: Wounds range from uninfected surgical entries into non-contaminated tracts up to dirty wounds containing necrotic tissue or active purulent drainage.
  • Partial-Thickness vs. Full-Thickness: Partial-thickness wounds involve only the epidermis and partial dermis, healing through regeneration. Full-thickness wounds extend into subcutaneous tissue, muscle, or bone, requiring scar formation and granulation.

Wound Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before touching a patient's wound, nurses must thoroughly assess the physical site and prepare the individual both physically and emotionally.

Begin by checking the patient's chart for allergies to tape, latex, or antiseptic solutions like iodine. Assess the level of pain and administer prescribed analgesics roughly thirty minutes before the dressing change if the procedure is expected to be uncomfortable.

When inspecting the wound, look for location, approximate dimensions (length, width, depth), and the color of the tissue. Check the edges for ecchymosis (bruising) or maceration (softening of the skin due to excess moisture). Note the character of any drainage: serous (clear and watery), sanguineous (bloody), or purulent (thick, cloudy, and often foul-smelling, indicating infection). Always explain each step of the process to the patient to reduce anxiety and secure cooperation.

Aseptic Technique and Infection Control

Preventing healthcare-associated infections is a core nursing responsibility during wound care. Depending on the setting and wound type, either clean technique or strict sterile technique is required.

Always perform hand hygiene before and after patient contact, putting on non-sterile gloves to remove old dressings and sterile gloves when applying new ones to the sterile wound bed. Work from the area of least contamination to the area of greatest contamination—usually cleaning directly over the wound first, then moving outward toward the surrounding skin. Never pass unsterile arms or items over a sterile field. If a sterile field becomes wet or touched by an unsterile item, consider it contaminated immediately and start over.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Performing a Wound Dressing

  1. Preparation: Gather all supplies, check the care plan, verify patient identity, and ensure privacy.
  2. Removal: Put on clean gloves, gently loosen the old tape, and remove the soiled dressing. If the gauze sticks to the wound, moisten it slightly with sterile normal saline to loosen it.
  3. Inspection: Inspect the wound for drainage, odor, and color, then discard the old dressing and dirty gloves into a biohazard bag.
  4. Hand Hygiene and Sterility: Perform hand hygiene again and open your sterile supply kit, donning sterile gloves.
  5. Cleansing: Clean the wound using prescribed solutions or normal saline using sterile swabs or forceps.
  6. Drying and Covering: Gently pat the surrounding skin dry with a sterile gauze pad, apply any prescribed topical ointments, and place the fresh sterile dressing over the wound.
  7. Securing: Secure the dressing with appropriate tape or bandages, label it with the date, time, and your initials, and make the patient comfortable.

Documentation and Evaluation

Accurate documentation proves that care was delivered safely and allows the healthcare team to track healing progress over time.

Your clinical notes must describe the wound appearance objectively. Record the exact dimensions (length, width, and depth in centimeters), the presence of tunneling or undermining, the color of the wound bed, and the type and amount of drainage. Note the patient's tolerance of the procedure and any reported pain levels.

Evaluation is an ongoing process. Compare current findings with previous nursing notes. Healthy wounds show progressive reduction in size, healthy pink or red granulation tissue, and diminishing drainage. If a wound appears stagnant, increases in size, or develops a foul odor and surrounding warmth, report these signs of potential infection to the primary provider immediately.

Complications of Improper Wound Management

Poor wound care techniques or delayed treatments can lead to serious complications that prolong hospital stays and increase patient suffering.

  • Infection: Bacteria multiplying in the wound bed, leading to spreading redness, local warmth, swelling, pus, and systemic fever.
  • Dehiscence: The partial or complete separation of outer wound layers, commonly seen in abdominal surgical incisions under high tension (like coughing or vomiting).
  • Evisceration: A surgical emergency where internal organs protrude through an open wound dehiscence. If this happens, cover the exposed organs immediately with sterile saline-soaked gauze and notify the surgeon at once.
  • Hemorrhage: Excessive bleeding caused by slipped sutures, unsealed blood vessels, or clotting disorders.
