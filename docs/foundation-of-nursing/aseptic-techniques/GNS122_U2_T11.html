Wound Dressing

Introduction and Definition of Wound Dressing

A wound dressing is a sterile pad or application applied directly to a wound to promote healing and protect the lesion from further harm. It serves as a physical barrier against environmental microbes, physical trauma, and chemical irritants. While people often confuse dressings with bandages, they have distinct functions: dressings make direct contact with the wound bed, absorbing exudate and maintaining an optimal moist environment, whereas bandages are secondary materials used primarily to hold a dressing in place. In Nigerian clinical practice, where tropical climates and delayed presentations can accelerate microbial proliferation, choosing the right interface is a critical nursing competency. Effective wound care balances protection with physiological support, ensuring the vulnerable cellular matrix can repair itself efficiently without unnecessary disruption.

Purposes and Objectives of Wound Dressing

Applying a dressing is never a passive task; every material chosen fulfills specific physiological and protective goals.

  • Hemostasis: Applying immediate gentle pressure via initial gauze layers helps control capillary bleeding.
  • Exudate Management: Absorbing excess fluid prevents maceration of surrounding intact peri-wound skin.
  • Microbial Defense: Creating a seal blocks nosocomial pathogens and environmental contaminants.
  • Thermal Insulation: Keeping the wound bed at core body temperature optimizes enzymatic activity necessary for cellular repair.
  • Debridement Support: Certain materials facilitate autolytic breakdown of necrotic tissue, clearing the path for granulation.

Wounds are broadly classified by their etiology, depth, and cleanliness. Understanding these categories guides clinical decision-making regarding dressing frequency and material selection.

Types of Dressing Materials and Their Indications

Modern nursing requires familiarity with diverse dressing categories, moving beyond traditional cotton gauze to advanced bioactive interfaces.

  • Gauze Pads: Woven or non-woven cotton; inexpensive, used for packing, cleaning, or secondary coverage, but can shed fibers and adhere to moist beds.
  • Hydrocolloids: Gel-forming agents backed by waterproof film; provide a moist environment and autolytic debridement for low-to-moderate exudate wounds, but not for infected sites.
  • Foam Dressings: Polyurethane sheets that absorb heavy exudate while providing cushioning and thermal insulation.
  • Alginates: Derived from seaweed; form a hydrophilic gel upon contact with sodium in blood and exudate, ideal for heavily bleeding or draining wounds.
  • Transparent Films: Clear polyurethane membranes that allow gas exchange and visual inspection while blocking liquids and bacteria.

Assessment of Wounds and Patient Preparation

Before touching a dressing, clinical evaluation begins with the whole patient. Assess pain levels, as dressing changes can provoke significant anxiety and physical discomfort; administering prescribed analgesics 30 minutes prior to the procedure is standard best practice. Inspect the existing dressing for strike-through, odor, or displacement. Once exposed, evaluate the wound bed systematically: note its exact dimensions, the presence of tunneling or undermining, tissue color (red granulation, yellow slough, black necrotic eschar), and the character of any exudate (serous, sanguineous, purulent). Always evaluate the surrounding skin for erythema, warmth, or maceration, which signal expanding inflammation or improper moisture balance.

Aseptic Technique and Infection Control Principles

Wound dressings act as portals of entry; introducing pathogens during a dressing change can transform a healing surgical site into a complicated abscess. Nurses must strictly maintain aseptic technique, treating all sterile supplies with rigorous vigilance. Always clean the wound from the area of least contamination to the area of greatest contamination—typically from the center of the wound outward, or from a clean surgical incision toward surrounding skin. Never pass unsterile items or hands over a sterile field. If a sterile glove touches an unsterile surface, it is immediately compromised and must be replaced. Hand hygiene before and after the procedure remains the single most effective intervention to prevent cross-contamination in busy hospital wards.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Performing a Wound Dressing

Executing a clean dressing change requires methodical organization and calm confidence at the bedside.

  1. Preparation: Verify the order, gather supplies, ensure privacy, and administer pre-procedure analgesia.
  2. Removal: Perform hand hygiene, don clean gloves, and gently remove the old dressing in the direction of hair growth. Observe and discard the soiled material.
  3. Inspection: Remove clean gloves, perform hand hygiene, open sterile supplies, and don sterile gloves to inspect the wound.
  4. Cleansing: Clean the wound bed using sterile saline or prescribed antiseptic, moving from clean to dirty.
  5. Drying and Protection: Pat the peri-wound skin dry with sterile gauze, apply prescribed topical treatments, and place the appropriate primary and secondary dressings.
  6. Securing: Fix the dressing securely with tape or bandages, ensuring adequate circulation is maintained.

Documentation and Evaluation of Wound Healing

Accurate clinical documentation provides a legal record and tracks therapeutic progress over time. Every nursing note must detail the exact location, dimensions (length, width, depth in centimeters), volume and color of drainage, appearance of the wound bed, condition of surrounding skin, and the patient's subjective tolerance of the procedure. Evaluating healing requires comparing current findings against baseline assessments. Nurses look for signs of progress such as decreasing wound surface area, the appearance of pink beefy granulation tissue, and advancing epithelial borders. If measurements remain static or worsen over a two-week period, the interprofessional team must re-evaluate the underlying treatment plan.

Common Complications of Improper Wound Management

Substandard dressing techniques or poor material choices can trigger severe clinical setbacks. Infection remains the most prevalent complication, marked by spreading erythema, localized heat, increasing pain, and purulent discharge. Dehiscence—the partial or complete separation of outer wound layers—and evisceration—protrusion of visceral organs through a disrupted surgical wound—are surgical emergencies requiring immediate sterile saline-soaked gauze coverage and urgent surgical consultation. Maceration from poorly managed moisture breaks down skin integrity, while inappropriate dry dressings can cause mechanical trauma during changes, tearing delicate newly formed epithelium and delaying overall recovery.
