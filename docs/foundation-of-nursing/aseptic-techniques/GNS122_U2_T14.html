Care of Drainage Tubes

Purpose and Types of Drainage Systems

Drainage tubes are medical devices placed in surgical wounds, body cavities, or organs to remove fluid, blood, or air that could otherwise accumulate and cause complications. Effective drainage prevents infection, reduces pressure on suture lines, and promotes faster wound healing. Drainage systems generally fall into two main categories: open and closed.

  • Open Drainage Systems: These drain fluid directly onto dressings or into open receptacles. A classic example is the Penrose drain, a soft rubber flat tube that uses gravity and capillary action. While simple, open systems carry a higher risk of outside bacteria entering the wound.
  • Closed Drainage Systems: These use tubing attached to a sealed bag or bottle. Some rely on simple gravity, while others use negative pressure. Closed systems greatly lower infection rates because external contaminants cannot easily reach the wound site.
  • Active vs. Passive Systems: Passive drains, like Penrose or simple T-tubes, rely purely on gravity or pressure differences without mechanical help. Active drains, such as Jackson-Pratt (JP) or Hemovac reservoirs, use a manual bulb or spring mechanism to create continuous suction, pulling fluid away from the tissue.

Routine Assessment and Site Care

Caring for a patient with a drainage tube requires systematic, regular assessment of both the patient and the equipment. Nurses must inspect the insertion site every shift for signs of complications, including redness, swelling, warmth, foul odor, or purulent drainage.

Check that the tube is securely anchored to the patient's skin with sutures or a securement device to prevent accidental dislodgement during movement. Always inspect the entire length of the tubing to ensure there are no kinks, sharp bends, or loops where fluid can pool and block flow. Ensure the collection reservoir hangs below the level of the wound to maintain gravity drainage, unless it is an active suction device that functions independently of positioning. Document the appearance of the surrounding skin and confirm that the dressing remains clean, dry, and intact.

Managing Patency: Stripping, Milking, and Flushing

Maintaining tube patency ensures that drainage flows freely without obstruction from blood clots or thick fibrin strands. When blockages occur, fluid builds up inside the body, causing pain and potential complications.

  • Milking: Squeezing the tubing at intervals, starting close to the insertion site and moving your fingers downward toward the collection container, then releasing. This gentle action helps push clots along without generating extreme pressure.
  • Stripping: Pulling your fingers firmly down a longer stretch of tubing. Modern clinical guidelines often discourage aggressive stripping because it creates a powerful negative vacuum inside the tube that can damage fragile internal tissues and pull the lining of the wound.
  • Flushing: Some specialized tubes, such as certain surgical irrigation lines or feeding tubes, require sterile saline flushes ordered by the physician to clear blockages. Never flush a closed surgical wound drain unless there is a specific, written medical order to do so.

Measuring and Recording Output

Accurate measurement of drainage output provides vital clues about a patient's recovery and internal bleeding status. Nurses must measure drainage at regular intervals, typically every shift or every four hours for unstable patients.

To measure output safely, put on clean gloves, empty the fluid from the collection chamber into a graduated measuring container, and read the volume at eye level on a flat surface. Note the color, consistency, and clarity of the fluid—whether it is bright red blood, serosanguineous (pink and watery), or serous (clear yellow). Record this volume clearly on the fluid balance chart.

Sudden changes require immediate clinical attention. For instance, a sharp decrease in output might mean the tube is blocked, while a sudden rush of bright red blood can signal active internal hemorrhage.

Dressing Changes and Infection Control

The skin around a drainage tube is a primary entry point for bacteria. Keeping this area clean and dry is essential to preventing surgical site infections.

Perform dressing changes using strict aseptic technique. Always wash your hands thoroughly and wear clean gloves for removing old dressings, switching to sterile gloves when cleansing the site and applying new materials. Inspect the skin for breakdown caused by constant moisture or pressure from the tubing. Clean the insertion site using a circular motion, starting right at the tubing and moving outward away from the wound.

Apply a sterile, split-drain gauze pad (often called a drain sponge) around the tube to absorb moisture without leaving loose cotton fibers behind. Secure the dressing with medical tape, ensuring the tube remains slack enough that it does not pull on the skin when the patient moves.

Troubleshooting Common Complications

Even with careful management, complications with drainage tubes can arise unexpectedly. Knowing how to identify and solve these issues quickly ensures patient safety.

  • Accidental Dislodgement: If a drain falls out partially or completely, cover the site immediately with a sterile dressing and notify the surgeon. Never attempt to push a slipped tube back inside, as this introduces severe contamination.
  • Sudden Cessation of Flow: If drainage stops completely, check for kinks, twisted tubing, or a patient lying directly on top of the drain. If the tubing is clear and the patient is at risk for fluid buildup, report the stoppage promptly.
  • Leaking Around the Insertion Site: Fluid leaking past the tube onto the skin usually indicates a loose seal, a displaced tube, or a saturated dressing. Change the dressing, assess the site, and inform the medical team if the leakage continues.

Removal of Drainage Tubes and Post-Removal Care

Drainage tubes are removed as soon as output drops to safe levels or when clinical imaging confirms the surgical site is healing well. Proper removal minimizes patient discomfort and prevents infection.

Explain the procedure to the patient beforehand, letting them know they may feel a brief pulling or burning sensation. Gather your supplies, including clean gloves, suture removal scissors, antiseptic wipes, and an occlusive sterile dressing. Cut any retention sutures holding the tube in place. Instruct the patient to take a deep breath and hold it, or perform a Valsalva maneuver, during the actual removal. This increases internal pressure and prevents air from being sucked into the body cavity. Pull the tube out smoothly and steadily in one quick motion. Immediately apply a airtight sterile dressing over the site. Monitor the wound closely over the next 24 to 48 hours for any signs of leaking or re-accumulation of fluid.
