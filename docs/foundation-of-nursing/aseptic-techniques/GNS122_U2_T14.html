Care of Drainage Tubes

Introduction and Purpose of Drainage Tubes

Surgical drains are hollow conduits placed in body cavities, wounds, or infected spaces to evacuate unwanted fluids such as blood, pus, serous exudate, and air. By preventing internal fluid collections, these devices reduce the risk of localized infection, minimize pressure on suture lines, and obliterate dead space where bacteria thrive. In many Nigerian surgical wards, monitoring drainage output provides a real-time window into internal hemostasis long before systemic signs of shock manifest. - **Prophylactic Drains**: Placed after clean or contaminated surgeries to prevent anticipated fluid accumulation. - **Therapeutic Drains**: Inserted to evacuate existing collections like abscesses, hematomas, or pleural effusions. - **Diagnostic Drains**: Utilized primarily to monitor for early anastomotic leaks or occult hemorrhage following abdominal operations.

Types of Drainage Systems and Mechanisms

Drainage systems operate either through passive gravity flow or active negative pressure. Understanding the physical mechanism of each system dictates how nurses handle, position, and troubleshoot them. - **Penrose Drain**: A flat, soft latex strip relying on capillary action and gravity to draw fluid onto an absorbent dressing. - **Jackson-Pratt (JP) Drain**: A closed, bulb-shaped suction device that provides gentle, constant negative pressure when compressed and capped. - **Hemovac Drain**: A circular, accordion-style reservoir capable of maintaining higher suction volumes for orthopedic procedures with substantial blood loss. - **Chest Drainage System**: A multi-chamber water-seal or dry-suction unit designed to restore negative intrapleural pressure in the chest cavity.

Routine Assessment and Monitoring of Drainage Sites

Maintaining a drainage tube requires systematic, head-to-toe checks of the entire circuit. The nurse inspects the insertion site for erythema, induration, foul-smelling discharge, or surrounding skin breakdown. Tubing must be free of kinks, loops, or tension that could pull the anchor suture out. Ensuring the collection reservoir hangs or is pinned correctly prevents gravity resistance or accidental dislodgement when the patient moves. In busy environments, documenting the color, consistency, and exact volume of output at the end of every shift establishes vital baselines for detecting sudden complications.

Measuring, Recording, and Assessing Drainage Output

Accurate quantification of drainage is a cornerstone of postoperative management. Nurses measure output using a graduated cylinder for each specific drain, noting the characteristics of the fluid—whether it is sanguineous, serosanguineous, serous, or purulent. Sudden shifts in volume warrant immediate clinical evaluation. A drastic cessation of output in a patient who previously drained significant volumes often indicates a clogged lumen or a twisted tube rather than spontaneous healing. Conversely, a sudden surge of bright red blood suggests active internal hemorrhage requiring urgent surgical notification.

Infection Control and Dressing Changes

Drainage insertion sites represent direct portals of entry for nosocomial pathogens. Strict aseptic technique during dressing changes is non-negotiable. Nurses assess the skin surrounding the drain for signs of maceration, infection, or excoriation. Dressings should be changed using sterile gloves and instruments whenever they become damp, loose, or soiled, as moisture breeds bacterial growth. Cleansing typically proceeds in a circular motion outward from the insertion site. Secure the drain with clean tape or a designated anchor device to prevent piston-like movement of the tube in and out of the tissue tract, which can introduce skin flora into deeper sterile spaces.

Troubleshooting Common Complications

Drain management frequently involves resolving mechanical and clinical hitches. If a closed suction bulb expands prematurely and refuses to hold a vacuum, the culprit is usually an improperly sealed plug or a loose connection along the tubing junction. Accidental dislodgement is another high-stress scenario. If a drain slips out partially, the nurse must never push it back inward, as this introduces contaminated skin flora directly into the sterile body cavity; instead, sterile dressings are applied, and the surgeon is notified. If the entire tube falls out, cover the site immediately with an occlusive sterile dressing and monitor the patient's vital signs for signs of hemorrhage or visceral perforation.

Removal of Drainage Tubes and Post-Removal Care

Drains are removed as soon as daily output drops below clinical thresholds—typically under 30 milliliters in a 24-hour period, depending on surgeon preference and procedure type. The removal procedure requires premedication for pain control if ordered, patient education on deep breathing, and the assembly of suture-removal kits, sterile gauze, and biohazard waste bags. After cutting any anchoring sutures, the nurse instructs the patient to take a deep breath and hold it (or perform the Valsalva maneuver), which closes thoracic or abdominal pressure barriers and minimizes air entry or discomfort as the tube is pulled out in a steady, smooth motion. A sterile occlusive dressing is applied immediately to the site to seal the wound tract and absorb any residual oozing.
