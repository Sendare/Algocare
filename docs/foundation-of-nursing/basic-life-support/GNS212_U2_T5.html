Inhalation Therapy

Introduction and Principles of Inhalation Therapy

Inhalation therapy involves the administration of medication directly into the pulmonary tree through the patient's respiratory efforts. This route bypasses the systemic circulation initially, delivering high concentrations of pharmacological agents straight to the bronchial smooth muscle and alveolar membranes. Clinical goals typically include relieving bronchospasm, reducing airway inflammation, liquefying thick pulmonary secretions, or treating systemic conditions via the vast alveolar surface area. Compared to oral or parenteral routes, inhalation therapy achieves a rapid onset of action with significantly smaller doses, which minimizes systemic adverse effects. The effectiveness of this therapy, however, depends almost entirely on particle size and the patient's breathing technique. Particles ranging from 1 to 5 micrometers are small enough to reach the lower respiratory tract, whereas larger particles deposit prematurely in the mouth and pharynx.

Respiratory Tract Anatomy and Drug Deposition

The respiratory tract presents a complex anatomical pathway that filters, warms, and humidifies inspired air. For inhaled medications to work, they must successfully navigate this pathway without impacting the oropharynx or large central airways. - **Upper Respiratory Tract**: Comprising the nose, pharynx, and larynx, this region acts as a natural filter. Large inertial particles strike the mucosal walls here and are swallowed rather than reaching the lungs. - **Conducting Airways**: The trachea and bronchi branch extensively, slowing airflow velocity. Branching points frequently cause inertial impaction if the airflow is too turbulent. - **Respiratory Zone**: The respiratory bronchioles and alveoli offer an immense surface area with minimal diffusion barriers, making this the primary site for local and systemic drug absorption.

Types of Inhalation Equipment

Various delivery devices are utilized in clinical practice, each requiring distinct patient coordination and handling techniques. | Device Type | Mechanism of Action | Patient Coordination Required | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Metered-Dose Inhaler (MDI)** | Propellant-driven chemical aerosol | High (requires slow, deep breath and actuation synchronization) | | **Dry Powder Inhaler (DPI)** | Breath-activated micronized powder | Moderate (requires a fast, forceful inspiratory effort) | | **Nebulizer** | Compressed gas or ultrasonic waves creating liquid mist | Low (normal tidal breathing through a mask or mouthpiece) | Spacers and valved holding chambers are frequently attached to MDIs to reduce oral deposition and trap larger particles, helping patients who struggle with hand-breath coordination.

Clinical Indications and Nursing Applications

Inhalation therapy forms the cornerstone of management for acute and chronic respiratory disorders. Nurses must understand the underlying pathology to evaluate the appropriateness of prescribed regimens. Common clinical indications include: - **Bronchospasm**: Relaxation of constricted airway smooth muscle in conditions like asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). - **Airway Inflammation**: Reduction of mucosal edema and immune-mediated swelling using inhaled corticosteroids. - **Mucus Clearance**: Thinning of tenacious secretions in bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, or severe lower respiratory tract infections. - **Systemic Delivery**: Administration of specialized therapies, such as inhaled insulin or targeted pulmonary vasodilators. Recognizing the difference between rescue medications (short-acting beta-agonists for rapid relief) and controller medications (long-acting agents for baseline control) remains essential for safe medication administration.

Patient Assessment and Preparation

Before initiating inhalation therapy, a thorough clinical assessment establishes baseline parameters and ensures safe delivery. The nurse evaluates respiratory rate, pattern, depth, and auscultates lung fields for wheezes, crackles, or diminished breath sounds. Assessment must also gauge the patient's cognitive status, physical dexterity, and ability to follow multi-step instructions. For instance, a patient with acute confusion or severe respiratory distress cannot effectively utilize a dry powder inhaler and will require a nebulizer setup instead. Furthermore, checking the oral cavity for fungal plaques or irritation is vital, particularly for patients already maintained on steroid inhalers.

Administration Procedure and Technique

Executing inhalation therapy correctly determines whether the drug reaches the lungs or merely coats the digestive tract. For metered-dose inhalers without a spacer, the steps require careful sequencing: 1. **Inspect and Prepare**: Check the medication label, expiry date, and dose counter. Shake the canister vigorously if specified. 2. **Posture**: Sit the patient upright or position them in high Fowler's to maximize thoracic expansion. 3. **Exhalation**: Instruct the patient to breathe out completely, emptying the lungs of normal tidal volume. 4. **Actuation**: Place the mouthpiece between the lips with teeth parted, or position it two finger-widths away from the open mouth. Press down once on the canister while beginning a slow, deep inhalation. 5. **Breath Holding**: Instruct the patient to hold their breath for 10 seconds, or as long as comfortably possible, to allow gravitational settling of particles in the terminal airways. 6. **Interval**: Wait one full minute before administering a second puff of the same medication.

Complications and Safety Precautions

While inhalation therapy is generally safe, local and systemic adverse effects can occur if precautions are ignored. Inhaled corticosteroids frequently deposit in the oropharynx, predisposing patients to oral candidiasis (thrush) and hoarseness. To prevent this, the nurse must instruct patients to rinse their mouth thoroughly with water and spit it out after every steroid treatment. Systemic side effects often stem from beta-agonist absorption, manifesting as tachycardia, palpitations, skeletal muscle tremors, and nervousness. Patients with underlying cardiac disease require close monitoring during bronchodilator therapy. Furthermore, device contamination poses an infection risk; nebulizer mouthpieces and masks must be cleaned and dried regularly to prevent bacterial colonization.

Patient Education and Self-Management

Empowering patients to manage their respiratory condition independently improves long-term outcomes and reduces emergency hospital admissions. Education must cover proper device maintenance, drug schedules, and recognition of worsening symptoms. Patients should learn to clean plastic MDI holders and spacer devices weekly by washing them in warm, soapy water and allowing them to air dry without towel drying, which creates static. They should also understand how to read dose counters to avoid running out of medication unexpectedly. An action plan helps patients distinguish between daily maintenance medications and emergency rescue drugs, outlining specific steps to take if peak flow readings decline.
