Inhalation Therapy

Introduction and Definition of Inhalation Therapy

Inhalation therapy is a targeted medical approach where therapeutic substances are delivered directly into the respiratory tract in the form of gases, mists, or fine powders. By introducing medication straight to the site of action in the lungs, this route achieves a rapid local effect while minimizing the widespread side effects often seen with oral or injectable drugs. It serves as a cornerstone in managing both acute and chronic breathing disorders across all age groups in clinical practice.

Respiratory Tract Anatomy in Drug Delivery

The respiratory tract acts as a natural filter and passageway, which directly impacts how inhaled particles travel. The upper airway warms and humidifies air while trapping large particles in the nose and pharynx. Once medication passes the larynx and enters the lower airway—including the trachea, bronchi, and tiny alveoli—particle size dictates deposition. Particles larger than 5 micrometers usually impact the upper airways, whereas smaller particles between 1 and 5 micrometers successfully reach the bronchioles and alveoli where most respiratory drugs need to act.

Types of Inhalation Devices

Different inhalation devices suit different patient needs, medications, and age groups. Nurses must understand how each device functions to ensure proper delivery.

  • Metered-Dose Inhaler (MDI): A pressurized canister that delivers a specific puff of medication, often used with a spacer to improve lung delivery.
  • Dry Powder Inhaler (DPI): A breath-activated device that delivers medication as a dry powder, requiring a fast, forceful inhalation from the patient.
  • Nebulizer: A machine that converts liquid medication into a fine aerosol mist using compressed air or oxygen, ideal for patients who cannot coordinate breathing with an MDI.

Indications and Clinical Uses

Inhalation therapy is primarily prescribed to treat obstructive airway diseases like asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). It is also used to deliver antibiotics for severe respiratory infections, mucolytics to thin thick secretions, and anti-inflammatory corticosteroids to reduce airway swelling. In emergency settings, rapid-acting bronchodilators administered via inhalation can reverse acute bronchospasm and save lives.

Patient Assessment and Preparation

Before administering any inhalation therapy, the nurse must assess the patient's current respiratory status, including breath sounds, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and level of distress. Check the medical order to confirm the correct drug, dose, and timing. Evaluate the patient's physical ability and cognitive readiness to use the specific prescribed device. Correct positioning—ideally sitting upright in a chair or high Fowler's position in bed—is vital to allow maximum expansion of the chest cavity and optimal drug deposition.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Executing inhalation therapy correctly ensures the medication reaches the target tissues. For a Metered-Dose Inhaler with a spacer, shake the canister, insert it into the spacer, and have the patient exhale completely. Place the mouthpiece inside the mouth, sealing the lips tightly around it. Depress the canister once to release a puff into the spacer, then instruct the patient to take a slow, deep breath lasting about five seconds. Ask the patient to hold their breath for 10 seconds, or as long as comfortably possible, before exhaling slowly. Wait about one minute if a second puff of the same medication is ordered.

Potential Complications and Safety Precautions

While generally safe, inhalation therapy can cause local and systemic side effects. Inhaled corticosteroids often leave residual medication in the mouth, which can lead to fungal infections like oral candidiasis (thrush) or hoarseness if the mouth is not rinsed. Bronchodilators can cause systemic side effects such as tachycardia, tremors, and nervousness due to beta-adrenergic stimulation. Nurses must teach patients to rinse their mouths and spit out the water after using steroid inhalers, and monitor vital signs closely when administering fast-acting rescue medications.

Patient Education and Home Care Management

Empowering patients to manage their inhalation therapy at home leads to better disease control and fewer emergency visits. Nurses must verify that patients know the correct order of medications when multiple inhalers are prescribed—usually using bronchodilators first to open the airways before taking anti-inflammatory steroids. Patients need clear instructions on how to clean their devices, track dose counters to avoid running out of medication, and recognize early warning signs of worsening respiratory status.
