Paediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS)

Introduction and the Pediatric Assessment Triangle (PAT)

Paediatric Advanced Life Support (PALS) focuses on saving the lives of critically ill or injured infants and children. Unlike adult resuscitation, paediatric emergencies often stem from unmanaged respiratory failure or shock, which eventually leads to cardiac arrest if untreated. The first tool a nurse uses in any paediatric emergency is the Pediatric Assessment Triangle (PAT). This tool allows for a rapid, hands-off evaluation within seconds just by looking at the child from the doorway.

The PAT evaluates three distinct components:

  • Appearance: Assesses the child's tone, interactiveness, consolability, look or gaze, and speech or cry. A limp or unresponsive child indicates severe central nervous system dysfunction.
  • Work of Breathing: Observes abnormal breath sounds, abnormal positioning, retractions of the chest wall, and tachypnea. This reflects the child's effort to maintain adequate oxygenation and ventilation.
  • Circulation to the Skin: Evaluates skin color and pallor, mottling, or cyanosis. Pale or blue skin reveals poor peripheral perfusion and impending shock.

Anatomical and Physiological Differences in Children

Children are not just small adults; their bodies have unique anatomical and physiological features that change how they respond to illness and injury. Their airways are smaller, more pliable, and easily obstructed by mucus, swelling, or improper head positioning. The tongue is relatively larger in proportion to the oral cavity, and the larynx sits higher and more anteriorly in the neck.

Cardiovascular physiology in infants relies heavily on heart rate rather than stroke volume to maintain cardiac output. Because their heart muscle is less developed, a sick child cannot easily increase stroke volume when stressed. Therefore, bradycardia in a distressed child is a very ominous sign of impending cardiac arrest, unlike in well-conditioned adults where a slow heart rate is normal.

High-Quality Basic Life Support (BLS) for Infants and Children

Effective basic life support forms the foundation of all resuscitation efforts. When performing CPR on a child, the compression-to-ventilation ratio depends on the number of rescuers present. For a single rescuer, the ratio is 30 compressions to 2 breaths, mimicking adult BLS. When two or more healthcare providers are present, the ratio shifts to 15 compressions to 2 breaths to ensure more frequent oxygenation.

Compression depth must reach at least one-third of the anterior-posterior diameter of the chest. For an infant, this is about 4 centimeters or 1.5 inches, and for a child, it is about 5 centimeters or 2 inches. Allowing full chest recoil between compressions is vital to allow blood to flow back into the heart chambers.

Airway Management and Ventilation Strategies

Maintaining an open airway is paramount in pediatric emergencies. Basic maneuvers such as the jaw-thrust or head-tilt chin-lift clear the tongue from the posterior pharynx. Nurses must avoid hyperextending an infant's neck because the flexible tracheal cartilage can actually kink and worsen airway obstruction.

When assisting ventilation with a bag-valve-mask (BVM) device, selecting the correct mask size is critical. The mask must cover the bridge of the nose and the point of the chin without pressing on the eyes or extending past the jawline. Over-ventilating a child—delivering breaths too fast or with too much volume—is dangerous because it increases intrathoracic pressure, reduces venous return to the heart, and can force air into the stomach.

Recognition and Management of Respiratory Emergencies

Respiratory distress can quickly deteriorate into respiratory failure or full arrest in children. Nurses must differentiate between upper airway obstructions (like croup or foreign body aspiration) and lower airway diseases (like asthma or bronchiolitis).

Signs of severe respiratory distress include stridor, wheezing, grunting, extreme retractions, and head bobbing. As respiratory muscles tire, the child enters respiratory failure, characterized by bradypnea, irregular breathing patterns, and cyanosis. Management focuses on delivering supplemental oxygen, positioning the child comfortably, and administering specific medications like bronchodilators or corticosteroids as ordered.

Recognition and Management of Shock States

Shock is a life-threatening condition where tissue perfusion is inadequate to meet metabolic demands. In children, shock is classified into hypovolemic, distributive, cardiogenic, and obstructive types.

Children are remarkably good at compensating for early blood or fluid loss. They maintain their blood pressure through intense vasoconstriction and tachycardia. Therefore, a normal blood pressure does not mean the child is safe; hypotension is a late and ominous sign of uncompensated shock in pediatrics. Management centers on rapid vascular access, fluid resuscitation with isotonic crystalloids (typically 20 mL/kg boluses), and identifying the underlying cause.

Pediatric Cardiac Arrest Algorithms and Rhythm Disturbances

Cardiac arrest rhythms in children differ from those in adults. Shockable rhythms such as ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT) are less common initially, with most pediatric arrests stemming from non-shockable rhythms like asystole or pulseless electrical activity (PEA).

When a shockable rhythm is identified, the standard energy dose for defibrillation is 2 Joules per kilogram for the first shock, increasing to 4 Joules per kilogram for subsequent shocks. For non-shockable rhythms, high-quality CPR and early administration of epinephrine (0.01 mg/kg of the 1:10,000 solution) every 3 to 5 minutes take priority alongside airway management.

Post-Resuscitation Care

Once spontaneous circulation is restored (ROSC), the focus shifts to post-resuscitation care to minimize secondary brain injury and multi-organ dysfunction. Nurses must continuously monitor vital signs, optimize ventilation and oxygenation, and maintain target blood pressure ranges appropriate for the child's age.

Fever and hypoxia are particularly harmful to the recovering post-arrest brain and must be aggressively prevented or treated. Continuous temperature monitoring and normothermia management are standard elements of care. Furthermore, blood glucose levels should be monitored closely to prevent both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia.
