Oxygen Tent

Introduction and Definition of Oxygen Tent

An oxygen tent is a canopy device used to deliver a controlled concentration of oxygen and humidity to a patient. It consists of a clear plastic enclosure that fits over the top half of a bed or a crib, completely enclosing the patient. The edges are tucked tightly under the mattress or sealed to prevent oxygen leakage. While rarely used for adult patients in modern clinical settings due to the availability of more efficient delivery systems like nasal cannula and face masks, oxygen tents remain common in pediatric nursing for infants and toddlers who cannot tolerate masks or nasal prongs.

Indications for Oxygen Tent Therapy

Oxygen tent therapy is indicated for patients who require controlled, humidified oxygen administration but resist standard interfaces. Common clinical scenarios include: - **Croup (Laryngotracheobronchitis)**: The high humidity cools and liquefies upper airway secretions while reducing mucosal edema. - **Bronchiolitis**: A viral lower respiratory infection common in infants causing severe wheezing and hypoxemia. - **Pneumonia**: When pediatric patients present with mild to moderate respiratory distress and desaturation. - **Post-tonsillectomy observation**: To ensure adequate oxygenation while keeping upper airway tissues moist.

Components and Setup of an Oxygen Tent

Setting up an oxygen tent requires coordinating several mechanical parts to ensure a closed, safe system. The apparatus features a canopy connected to a refrigeration and humidification unit, alongside an oxygen supply hose. 1. **Canopy**: The transparent vinyl cover featuring zippered or tucked access ports for nursing interventions. 2. **Cooling Unit**: Houses a compressor that lowers the internal temperature, as enclosed plastic can rapidly become warm and stuffy. 3. **Nebulizer/Humidifier Bottle**: Attaches to the flowmeter to saturate the incoming gas with water vapor. 4. **Flowmeter**: Regulates the liters per minute (LPM) of oxygen entering the system, typically requiring high flow rates (10 to 15 LPM) initially to flush out accumulated carbon dioxide.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Monitoring

Monitoring a patient inside an oxygen tent demands vigilance, as the physical barrier can obscure subtle signs of clinical deterioration. Nurses must perform continuous and intermittent checks: - **Respiratory Rate and Effort**: Observe for nasal flaring, intercostal retractions, and paradoxical breathing. - **Oxygen Saturation**: Continuously track via pulse oximetry, ensuring sensor placement remains accurate despite high humidity. - **Skin Integrity**: Check areas where plastic rests against the face or ears for friction breakdown. - **Internal Gas Concentration**: Use an oxygen analyzer periodically inside the tent to verify that the fraction of inspired oxygen matches the ordered flow setting.

Safety Precautions and Fire Hazards

Oxygen is a potent oxidizer that vigorously accelerates combustion. Even though the gas itself does not burn, it lowers the ignition point of everyday materials. In a unit utilizing an oxygen tent, strict safety protocols are mandatory: - **Prohibit Open Flames**: No smoking, matches, or lighters anywhere near the unit. - **Electrical Safety**: Avoid toys or electronic devices that can produce electrical sparks, such as battery-operated cars or frayed heating pads. - **Avoid Petroleum Products**: Do not apply petroleum jelly (Vaseline) to the child's lips or nose; use water-soluble lubricants instead. - **Tuck the Tent Securely**: Ensure the plastic skirt is completely tucked under the mattress to maintain a stable environment and prevent stray oxygen pockets from forming near the bed frame.

Complications and Troubleshooting

Despite its utility, oxygen tent therapy presents unique operational challenges. The most frequent complication is the sudden drop in oxygen concentration when the canopy is opened for nursing care, feeding, or comforting. To minimize this, nurses should bundle care activities and use zippered access ports efficiently rather than lifting the entire plastic skirt. Another challenge is patient chilling. Because the refrigeration unit cools circulating air, infants can experience hypothermia if clothing or bedding becomes damp from heavy condensation. Nurses must frequently check axillary or skin temperatures and change wet garments promptly.
