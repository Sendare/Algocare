Intranasal Oxygen Administration

Introduction and Indications for Intranasal Oxygen

Intranasal oxygen administration is a common medical intervention used to supplement the oxygen a patient breathes from room air. The primary device used for this route is the nasal cannula, a lightweight tube that rests beneath the nose with two small prongs inserted gently into the nostrils.

Nurses initiate intranasal oxygen therapy whenever a patient's body tissues do not receive enough oxygen. This clinical state, known as hypoxia, shows up as shortness of breath, restlessness, confusion, or a drop in blood oxygen saturation measured by a pulse oximeter. Common conditions requiring this support include acute asthma attacks, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations, heart failure, and recovery immediately following surgery.

Equipment Needed for Intranasal Oxygen Setup

Before starting oxygen therapy, gather all necessary supplies to ensure a smooth, safe procedure. Having equipment ready at the bedside prevents unnecessary delays in emergency situations.

  • Oxygen source: A wall outlet connected to a central pipeline or a portable compressed gas cylinder.
  • Flow meter: A device attached to the oxygen source to regulate the rate of gas flow in liters per minute.
  • Pressure regulator: Used with portable tanks to safely reduce high internal pressure.
  • Nasal cannula: The patient interface tubing.
  • Connecting tubing: Used if the distance between the flow meter and the cannula requires an extension.
  • Humidification bottle and sterile water: Required when delivering higher flow rates to prevent drying of the nasal mucosa.

Patient Assessment and Preparation

Before administering oxygen, assess the patient's baseline respiratory status, including their respiratory rate, depth, effort, and skin color. Check the baseline oxygen saturation using a pulse oximeter and review the medical record for the exact physician order regarding flow rate and delivery method.

Explain the procedure clearly to the patient and their family to ease anxiety. Position the patient comfortably, usually in a semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's position, as this upright posture expands the chest cavity and makes breathing easier. Inspect the nares for any existing skin breakdown, irritation, or crusting before placing the tubing.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

  1. Verify the order: Confirm the oxygen prescription for correct flow rate and delivery device against the patient identification band.
  2. Attach the equipment: Connect the nasal cannula tubing to the oxygen flow meter and set the prescribed liters per minute.
  3. Check for gas flow: Feel for air coming out of the cannula prongs to ensure the system is working properly before touching the patient.
  4. Position the prongs: Curve the prongs downward so they follow the natural downward slope of the nasal passages.
  5. Secure the tubing: Place the tubing over the patient's ears and adjust the plastic slider snugly under the chin, leaving enough room to fit two fingers underneath.
  6. Re-evaluate: Check the patient's comfort and recheck oxygen saturation levels after a few minutes.

Flow Rates and FiO2 Delivery

A nasal cannula is a low-flow device typically operated at flow rates between 1 and 6 liters per minute (Lpm). Delivering flow rates above 6 Lpm through a standard cannula provides little additional benefit because the anatomical reservoir of the nose becomes completely filled, and higher flows simply dry and irritate the mucous membranes.

The fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) delivered varies depending on the patient's breathing pattern, respiratory rate, and tidal volume. As a general clinical guide:

  • 1 Lpm: Delivers approximately 24% FiO2
  • 2 Lpm: Delivers approximately 28% FiO2
  • 3 Lpm: Delivers approximately 32% FiO2
  • 4 Lpm: Delivers approximately 36% FiO2
  • 5 Lpm: Delivers approximately 40% FiO2
  • 6 Lpm: Delivers approximately 44% FiO2

Monitoring, Maintenance, and Nursing Interventions

Continuous monitoring is required to ensure oxygen therapy remains effective and safe. Nurses should check pulse oximetry readings regularly and observe the patient's work of breathing, skin color, and level of consciousness.

Provide daily mouth and nose care, as oxygen dries out mucosal tissues. Inspect the tubing and skin behind the ears and over the cheekbones every shift for signs of pressure injury. Ensure that tubing remains free of kinks and tangles. If the patient goes to the bathroom or walks around, coordinate a safe transition to a portable oxygen cylinder.

Potential Complications and Safety Precautions

Although oxygen is a vital treatment, it behaves as a drug and carries specific risks. Nurses must enforce strict safety rules wherever oxygen is in use.

  • Fire hazard: Oxygen supports combustion vigorously. Keep all open flames, sparks, and electrical appliances away from oxygen equipment. Never use petroleum-based jellies like Vaseline, as they can ignite in the presence of high oxygen concentrations.
  • Skin breakdown: Pressure from tubing can cause skin irritation or breakdown over the ears and nares.
  • Mucosal drying: Dry gas causes cracking of the nasal lining, leading to epistaxis (nosebleeds).
  • Oxygen toxicity: Prolonged exposure to high concentrations of oxygen can damage lung tissue, though this is rare with standard low-flow nasal cannulas.
