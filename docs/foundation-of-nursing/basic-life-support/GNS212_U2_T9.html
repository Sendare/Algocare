Intranasal Oxygen Administration

Introduction and Indications for Intranasal Oxygen Administration

Intranasal oxygen delivery via a nasal cannula represents the most common method of supplementing a patient's inspired oxygen supply. This intervention is indicated whenever clinical signs suggest cellular hypoxia or when arterial blood gas values fall below normal physiological thresholds. Common scenarios encountered in Nigerian clinical practice include acute exacerbations of asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, severe malaria complicated by respiratory distress, post-operative recovery, and acute heart failure. The primary goal is maintaining adequate tissue oxygenation while minimizing patient discomfort. Because the device is lightweight and allows the patient to speak and eat concurrently, compliance remains relatively high compared to tighter-fitting masks.

Equipment and Setup for Nasal Cannula Delivery

Setting up an oxygen delivery system requires specific hardware matched to the hospital's infrastructure, whether utilizing centralized wall outlets or portable compressed gas cylinders. - **Oxygen Source**: A wall-mounted flowmeter or a portable cylinder equipped with a pressure regulator. - **Flowmeter**: A device that measures and controls the liters per minute of gas released. - **Nasal Cannula**: A lightweight plastic tubing set featuring twin prongs designed to curve downward into the nares. - **Extension Tubing**: Used if the distance between the patient and the source requires extra length, keeping safety hazards like tripping in mind. - **Humidifier Bottle**: Filled with sterile water when higher flow rates are prescribed to prevent mucosal drying.

Patient Assessment and Preparation

Before initiating oxygen therapy, a baseline assessment establishes the patient's respiratory status and clinical need. Vital signs, particularly respiratory rate, depth, and oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry, must be documented. Inspect the nares and nasal mucosa for patency, swelling, polyps, or existing skin breakdown. Explain the procedure clearly to the patient and relatives to alleviate anxiety, ensuring they understand that oxygen supports healing. Position the patient in a semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's position if tolerated, as this maximizes thoracic expansion and optimizes lung mechanics.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Executing the procedure safely requires methodical execution. Begin by performing hand hygiene and verifying the medical order using hospital identification protocols. Connect the cannula tubing to the oxygen source and titrate the flowmeter to the prescribed rate before placing the device on the patient. Position the curved prongs into the patient's nostrils following the natural contour of the nasal passage. Loop the plastic tubing over the patient's ears and slide the adjustment collar upward under the chin for a secure yet comfortable fit. Confirm that gas is actively flowing through both prongs prior to securing the apparatus.

Flow Rates, FiO2 Delivery, and Humidification

A standard nasal cannula delivers low-flow oxygen typically ranging from 1 to 6 liters per minute. Each incremental liter per minute roughly increases the fraction of inspired oxygen by about 4 percent over room air, though actual delivered percentages vary based on the patient's respiratory rate and tidal volume. Flow rates exceeding 6 liters per minute fail to significantly increase alveolar oxygenation because the anatomical dead space of the nasopharynx becomes fully saturated, while simultaneously causing severe mucosal drying and epistaxis. Humidification is generally reserved for flows above 4 liters per minute or when dryness complaints arise.

Monitoring, Maintenance, and Nursing Interventions

Continuous observation ensures therapy remains effective and complications are caught early. Inspect pressure points over the ears, cheekbones, and nasal septum every four hours to check for skin breakdown or pressure ulcers. Clean the nares and cannula tips daily or whenever secretions accumulate. Monitor oxygen saturation continuously or via intermittent pulse oximetry checks, correlating readings with clinical presentation such as skin color and level of consciousness. Ensure that no kinks develop in the long connecting tubing, which would silently cut off the oxygen supply.

Potential Complications and Safety Precautions

Although safe, oxygen administration carries inherent risks that demand strict safety precautions. Oxygen acts as a powerful oxidant that vigorously accelerates combustion; therefore, absolute no-smoking rules must be enforced around oxygen sources, alongside prohibiting open flames, petroleum-based lubricants, or spark-generating electrical appliances. Physiologically, drying of the nasal mucosa can lead to irritation, cracking, and epistaxis. In specific chronic respiratory conditions, administering high oxygen concentrations can suppress the hypoxic respiratory drive, leading to dangerous carbon dioxide retention.
