Intranasal Oxygen Administration

Introduction and Indications for Intranasal Oxygen Administration

Intranasal oxygen administration is a fundamental clinical skill utilized in nursing practice across Nigerian healthcare settings to manage hypoxemia and improve tissue oxygenation. Oxygen is considered a medication; therefore, it requires a formal medical or nursing order in non-emergency situations, though emergency administration can be initiated under life-saving protocols. The primary indication for intranasal oxygen via a nasal cannula is mild to moderate hypoxemia, which is frequently assessed using pulse oximetry to maintain target peripheral capillary oxygen saturation levels typically between 92% and 98%, or as specifically prescribed for patients with chronic conditions like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Common clinical scenarios requiring this intervention include acute respiratory distress, exacerbations of asthma or heart failure, post-operative recovery, carbon monoxide poisoning, and severe anemia. Additionally, patients experiencing trauma, shock, or sepsis benefit significantly from supplemental oxygen to meet heightened metabolic demands and prevent cellular hypoxia. As nursing students preparing for clinical practice and licensure examinations, it is vital to recognize that administering oxygen promptly can avert life-threatening multi-organ failure. However, inappropriate administration without proper assessment can mask underlying conditions or cause oxygen toxicity. Understanding these core indications ensures that student nurses allocate resources efficiently and deliver safe, evidence-based care tailored to individual patient needs in both hospital wards and emergency departments.

Anatomy and Physiology Considerations for Nasal Cannula Use

Successful intranasal oxygen administration relies heavily on a foundational understanding of the upper respiratory tract's anatomy and physiology. The nasal cavity serves as the natural passage for air, where it is normally warmed, filtered, and humidified before reaching the lower respiratory airways. When a nasal cannula is inserted, the prongs direct the flow of oxygen into the anterior nares, utilizing the nasal passages as a physiological reservoir. The mucous membranes lining the nasal passages are highly vascular, which facilitates rapid gas exchange and conditioning of the inhaled oxygen. However, because the nasal mucosa is sensitive, the continuous flow of dry, unhumidified medical gas can rapidly dry out these delicate tissues, leading to irritation, cracking, bleeding, and compromised ciliary function. Furthermore, anatomical variations such as a deviated septum, nasal polyps, or severe nasal congestion can obstruct airflow and reduce the efficacy of nasal cannula delivery, potentially forcing the patient to breathe through their mouth and rendering the device less effective. Nursing students must assess nasal patency prior to application and understand that factors like chronic rhinitis or mucosal edema will alter how oxygen is absorbed and tolerated. Recognizing these physiological responses allows nurses to anticipate complications such as mucosal drying and implement preventative measures like humidification early in the patient care plan.

Equipment and Materials Needed for Procedure

Before initiating intranasal oxygen therapy, the nurse must gather and inspect all necessary equipment to ensure safe and efficient delivery. The primary piece of equipment is the nasal cannula, which consists of soft plastic tubing, two nasal prongs, and an adjustable slider (bolo) used to secure the device under the patient's chin or behind their ears. In addition to the cannula, the setup requires an oxygen source, which may be a central wall outlet connected via a flowmeter or a portable compressed oxygen cylinder equipped with a pressure regulator and flowmeter. The flowmeter accurately measures and controls the liters per minute (LPM) of oxygen being delivered to the patient. If the prescribed flow rate exceeds 4 liters per minute, or if the patient complains of severe nasal dryness, a sterile water bubble humidifier should be attached between the flowmeter and the tubing to add moisture to the oxygen gas. Other miscellaneous items include connecting tubing for extension if the patient is mobile, non-allergenic tape to secure tubing against the skin if pressure injuries are a risk, and alcohol swabs for disinfecting equipment ports if necessary. Ensuring all equipment is functioning, unexpired, and undamaged before approaching the patient demonstrates clinical competence and prevents sudden equipment failure during respiratory distress episodes.

Patient Assessment and Preparation

Comprehensive patient assessment and preparation are critical initial steps before placing a nasal cannula. The nurse must begin by verifying the medical order for oxygen therapy, checking the specific flow rate or target oxygen saturation range, and performing hand hygiene while introducing themselves to the patient. Assessment involves evaluating the patient's current respiratory status, including respiratory rate, depth, pattern, use of accessory muscles, skin color, and baseline oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry. The nurse should also inspect the patient's nasal passages for patency, inflammation, septal deviation, or active bleeding, as these conditions can impede effective oxygen delivery. Patient preparation includes explaining the procedure in clear, comforting language to alleviate anxiety, since anxiety can increase oxygen consumption and worsen dyspnea. The nurse should position the patient in a semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's position if tolerated, as this posture maximizes chest expansion, lowers the diaphragm, and optimizes lung mechanics. Educating the patient and their family about the importance of keeping the cannula in place, avoiding smoking near oxygen sources, and reporting any discomfort ensures active participation in their care plan. Thorough preparation establishes a baseline for future evaluations and fosters a collaborative therapeutic relationship between the nurse and the patient.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Intranasal Oxygen

Executing the step-by-step procedure for administering intranasal oxygen requires precision, infection control, and patient comfort. After completing assessment and preparation, the nurse connects the nasal cannula tubing to the oxygen flowmeter or humidifier source and titrates the flow rate to the prescribed liters per minute to ensure oxygen is flowing before application. The nurse then gently positions the nasal cannula on the patient's face, inserting the curved prongs into the nares so that they follow the natural downward contour of the nasal passage. The plastic tubing is looped securely over the patient's ears, and the slider is adjusted gently under the chin to hold the device firmly without causing pressure ulcers or skin breakdown. It is essential to ensure that the tubing does not exert excessive pressure on the top of the ears or the nasal septum. Once the cannula is secured, the nurse reassures the patient, confirms that oxygen is felt flowing through the prongs, and asks if the fit is comfortable. Finally, the nurse documents the initiation of oxygen therapy, the exact flow rate, the patient's vital signs, and their respiratory status in the clinical chart. Adhering strictly to these procedural steps minimizes complications and guarantees that the patient receives the exact therapeutic dose prescribed by the physician.

Flow Rates, FiO2 Delivery, and Humidification

Understanding flow rates, Fraction of Inspired Oxygen (FiO2) delivery, and humidification is essential for effective oxygen management. A standard nasal cannula is considered a low-flow oxygen delivery system, typically operating at flow rates from 1 to 6 liters per minute (LPM). Each incremental liter per minute generally increases the delivered FiO2 by approximately 4 percent, starting from room air (21% at 0 LPM), meaning 1 LPM delivers roughly 24%, 2 LPM delivers 28%, up to approximately 44% FiO2 at 6 LPM. However, the actual FiO2 delivered to the patient's lower airway varies because it is heavily influenced by the patient's respiratory rate, tidal volume, and mouth-breathing habits. If a patient breathes rapidly or through their mouth, room air mixes with the delivered oxygen, diluting the overall FiO2. For flow rates exceeding 4 LPM, or for patients complaining of mucosal irritation, humidification via sterile water in a bubble humidifier is strongly recommended to prevent drying of the respiratory epithelium. Nursing students must remember that pushing flow rates beyond 6 LPM with a standard nasal cannula does not significantly increase FiO2 due to anatomical dead space limits and can cause severe mucosal trauma, necessitating a transition to a different delivery device such as a venturi mask or non-rebreather mask if higher concentrations are required.

Monitoring, Maintenance, and Nursing Interventions

Continuous monitoring, maintenance, and vigilant nursing interventions are imperative for patients receiving intranasal oxygen therapy. The nurse must perform regular respiratory assessments, checking oxygen saturation levels via pulse oximetry, respiratory rate, and breath sounds at least every shift or more frequently if the patient is unstable. Maintenance of the equipment includes verifying that connections are secure, checking that the flow rate matches the current medical order, and ensuring the humidifier bottle contains adequate sterile water. Skin integrity requires special attention; nurses must routinely inspect the bridge of the nose, the tops of the ears, and the mucosal lining of the nares every 4 to 8 hours for signs of pressure irritation, redness, or breakdown. Applying padding or protective dressings over pressure points can prevent skin damage caused by plastic tubing. Additionally, providing oral hygiene and nasal care, such as applying water-soluble lubricants (avoiding petroleum-based jelly due to flammability risks), helps maintain patient comfort and mucosal health. The nurse must also monitor for signs of clinical improvement or deterioration, documenting all findings accurately and communicating any sudden changes in the patient's respiratory status to the attending physician immediately.

Potential Complications and Safety Precautions

While intranasal oxygen administration is generally safe, student nurses must be well-versed in potential complications and essential safety precautions. A major hazard associated with oxygen therapy is its ability to support vigorous combustion; oxygen is not flammable itself, but it drastically accelerates fires. Therefore, strict safety precautions must be enforced, including posting 'No Smoking' signs, ensuring electrical equipment is well-maintained and grounded, and prohibiting open flames, matches, or petroleum-based products near the oxygen source. Another clinical complication is oxygen-induced hypoventilation in patients with chronic hypercapnia (such as advanced COPD), where high levels of supplemental oxygen can depress their hypoxic drive to breathe, leading to dangerous carbon dioxide narcosis. Mucosal irritation, epistaxis (nosebleeds), and skin breakdown over the ears and nares are localized complications caused by dry gas flow and mechanical friction from tubing. Additionally, excessive high-flow oxygen can lead to absorption atelectasis or oxygen toxicity if administered improperly over prolonged periods. By recognizing these hazards, practicing meticulous equipment maintenance, and educating patients and visitors on fire safety, nurses play a critical role in safeguarding patients undergoing respiratory therapy.
