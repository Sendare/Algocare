Supporting Respiratory Functions

Anatomy and Physiology of the Respiratory System

Gas exchange relies on an intact, functioning respiratory tract divided into upper and lower airways. Air enters through the nose or mouth, traveling past the pharynx and larynx into the trachea, which branches into the right and left primary bronchi. These airways continually subdivide into smaller bronchioles, eventually terminating in millions of tiny alveolar sacs surrounded by pulmonary capillaries. This massive surface area facilitates rapid diffusion of oxygen into the bloodstream and elimination of carbon dioxide.

Ventilation is an active process driven primarily by the contraction of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles. When the diaphragm moves downward and the chest wall expands, intrathoracic pressure drops below atmospheric pressure, drawing air inward. Exhalation is normally passive, resulting from elastic recoil of the lungs and relaxation of the respiratory muscles. Proper functioning of this system maintains arterial blood gases within normal physiological ranges.

Assessment of Respiratory Status and Vital Signs

Clinical evaluation of the respiratory system begins with systematic observation. The nurse inspects the patient for symmetrical chest expansion, use of accessory muscles, and abnormal skin colorations like cyanosis. Respiratory rate, depth, and rhythm are counted unobtrusively, preferably while pretending to take the pulse, because conscious awareness alters breathing patterns. Normal adult resting rates typically range between 12 and 20 breaths per minute.

Auscultation using a stethoscope reveals breath sounds across all lung fields. Normal sounds include vesicular sounds heard over peripheral lung tissue and bronchial sounds heard over the trachea. Adventitious sounds—such as crackles, wheezes, or pleural friction rubs—indicate underlying pathology like fluid accumulation or airway narrowing. Pulse oximetry provides a non-invasive estimate of peripheral blood oxygen saturation, serving as an essential parameter alongside traditional vital signs.

Indicators of Respiratory Distress

Recognizing early signs of respiratory compromise prevents progression to acute respiratory failure. Initial indicators often involve behavioral changes such as restlessness, anxiety, and confusion caused by mild cerebral hypoxia. Tachycardia and tachypnea frequently develop as compensatory mechanisms to maintain tissue oxygenation.

As distress worsens, physical signs become more pronounced:

  • Accessory muscle use: Engaging the sternocleidomastoid, scalene, and abdominal muscles to force air in and out.
  • Retractions: Sinking of the skin around the ribs, sternum, or suprasternal notch during inspiration.
  • Nasal flaring: Widening of the nostrils during breathing, common in pediatric patients.
  • Cyanosis: A late, ominous bluish discoloration of the nail beds, lips, or mucous membranes indicating severe hypoxemia.

Basic Airway Management and Positioning

Maintaining a patent airway is the absolute first priority in any patient care scenario. If the patient is unconscious, the tongue frequently falls backward, obstructing the pharynx. Proper positioning can instantly alleviate this mechanical obstruction without invasive tools.

  1. High-Fowler's Position: Sitting the patient upright at 60 to 90 degrees maximizes chest expansion and eases diaphragmatic movement.
  2. Orthopneic Position: Having the patient lean forward over an overbed table utilizes accessory muscles and relieves severe dyspnea.
  3. Chin-Lift / Jaw-Thrust Maneuver: Manual techniques used to lift the tongue away from the posterior pharyngeal wall.
  4. Suctioning: Removing accumulated mucus, blood, or secretions from the upper airway using sterile or clean techniques depending on the route.

Because oxygen supports combustion vigorously, strict safety rules apply. Storage cylinders must be secured upright to prevent falling. Open flames, electrical sparks, and petroleum-based lubricants are strictly prohibited near oxygen delivery equipment.

Lung Expansion Techniques and Breathing Exercises

Patients recovering from surgery, prolonged bed rest, or chronic respiratory illness are vulnerable to atelectasis—the collapse of alveoli. Simple breathing exercises help maintain alveolar recruitment and clear retained secretions.

Incentive Spirometry encourages voluntary deep breathing by providing visual feedback. The patient inhales slowly and deeply through the mouthpiece, trying to elevate the piston or ball to a preset goal, then holds the breath for several seconds before exhaling. This sustains alveolar expansion.

Pursed-Lip Breathing helps patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease control shortness of breath. The patient inhales slowly through the nose, then exhales slowly through pursed lips, creating backpressure that keeps airways open longer and prevents premature airway collapse during expiration.

Monitoring, Documentation, and Complication Prevention

Comprehensive respiratory care requires ongoing monitoring to evaluate the effectiveness of interventions and catch complications early. The nurse tracks trends in pulse oximetry, respiratory rate, and patient effort over time. Documentation must be objective, detailing exact flow rates, delivery devices, patient tolerance, and pre- and post-intervention vital signs.

Preventing complications involves proactive nursing measures. Mobilizing patients early prevents venous stasis and pooled pulmonary secretions. Adequate hydration thins respiratory mucus, making it easier to cough up naturally. Oral care prevents bacterial colonization in the oropharynx, reducing the risk of aspiration pneumonia in vulnerable patients.
