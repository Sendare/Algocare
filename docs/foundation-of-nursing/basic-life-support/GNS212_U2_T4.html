Supporting Respiratory Functions

Introduction to Respiratory Support in Nursing

Respiratory support is a fundamental and critical competency for nursing students and professional nurses practicing in clinical environments across Nigeria. The primary goal of respiratory support is to ensure adequate gas exchange, meaning the efficient uptake of oxygen into the bloodstream and the effective elimination of carbon dioxide from the body. In many clinical scenarios, patients may experience impaired ventilation or perfusion due to acute illnesses, chronic conditions like asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, trauma, or post-operative complications. Nurses play an indispensable role in recognizing early signs of respiratory compromise, initiating timely interventions, and collaborating with the interprofessional healthcare team to restore optimal pulmonary function. Understanding the principles of respiratory care empowers nurses to manage emergency situations confidently, operate specialized equipment safely, and educate patients and their families regarding self-care management at home. Furthermore, thorough knowledge of respiratory pathophysiology and supportive modalities aids in preventing life-threatening hypoxia and hypercapnia. As healthcare providers, nurses must approach respiratory support with clinical vigilance, meticulous assessment skills, and strict adherence to evidence-based protocols to promote positive patient outcomes and enhance overall recovery rates.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Respiratory System

A thorough understanding of the anatomy and physiology of the respiratory system is essential for accurate nursing assessment and intervention. The respiratory system is broadly divided into the upper and lower respiratory tracts. The upper tract, comprising the nose, pharynx, and larynx, filters, warms, and humidifies inspired air. The lower tract includes the trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli, where actual gas exchange takes place. Alveoli are tiny, grape-like clusters surrounded by a rich network of pulmonary capillaries. The thin alveolar-capillary membrane facilitates the passive diffusion of oxygen into the blood and carbon dioxide out of the blood. Ventilation, the mechanical movement of air in and out of the lungs, relies on pressure gradients created by the diaphragm and intercostal muscles. During inspiration, the diaphragm contracts and flattens, increasing thoracic volume and decreasing intrathoracic pressure, causing air to rush in. Expiration is typically a passive process driven by the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall. Gas transport is further supported by the cardiovascular system, which pumps oxygenated blood to body tissues and returns deoxygenated blood to the lungs. Any disruption along this pathway, whether in airway patency, alveolar surface area, or muscular strength, can significantly impair a patient's respiratory status.

Assessment of Respiratory Status and Vital Signs

Assessing respiratory status requires a systematic, head-to-toe clinical approach utilizing inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. The nurse begins by observing the patient's general appearance, level of consciousness, and respiratory rate, rhythm, and depth. Normal adult respiratory rates range typically from 12 to 20 breaths per minute, while abnormalities like tachypnea, bradypnea, or irregular breathing patterns indicate distress. Inspection includes checking for symmetrical chest expansion, the use of accessory muscles, and abnormal skin coloration such as cyanosis, which suggests severe hypoxia. Palpation helps identify areas of tenderness, structural abnormalities, or tactile fremitus. Auscultation with a stethoscope allows the nurse to listen for normal vesicular, bronchovesicular, and bronchial breath sounds, as well as adventitious sounds like crackles, wheezes, rhonchi, or a pleural friction rub, which point toward fluid, inflammation, or airway constriction. Vital signs must be monitored holistically, incorporating pulse oximetry to measure arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2). Normal SpO2 levels generally range from 95% to 100% on room air, though lower targets may be acceptable for patients with chronic respiratory illnesses like COPD. Regular, accurate documentation of these parameters provides an objective baseline to evaluate ongoing treatment efficacy.

Common Causes and Indicators of Respiratory Distress

Recognizing the causes and indicators of respiratory distress is a critical life-saving skill for nurses. Respiratory distress arises when the body has to work harder to breathe, or when gas exchange is compromised to the extent that tissue oxygenation is threatened. Common causes include acute asthma exacerbations, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, heart failure with pulmonary edema, foreign body airway obstruction, and severe chest trauma. Early indicators of respiratory distress include anxiety, restlessness, tachypnea, tachycardia, and mild dyspnea. As the condition deteriorates, patients often exhibit compensatory mechanisms such as nasal flaring, pursed-lip breathing, intercostal and supraclavicular retractions, and the use of sternocleidomastoid accessory muscles. In advanced respiratory distress or impending failure, patients may display exhaustion, bradypnea, paradoxical breathing, lethargy, confusion, or a drop in oxygen saturation despite supplemental oxygen. Cyanosis is a late sign of hypoxia. Nurses must maintain high clinical awareness, immediately report sudden changes in vital signs or mental status, and initiate emergency protocols such as positioning the patient upright, administering prescribed oxygen, and notifying the physician or emergency response team without delay.

Basic Airway Management and Positioning Techniques

Basic airway management and proper positioning techniques are foundational nursing interventions designed to maintain airway patency and improve ventilation. The upper airway can easily become obstructed by the tongue, secretions, or foreign bodies, particularly in unconscious or immobilized patients. The primary and most immediate intervention for an obstructed or compromised airway in an unconscious patient is the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver, provided cervical spine injury is not suspected. If spinal trauma is suspected, the jaw-thrust maneuver must be used instead to open the airway without manipulating the neck. Positioning plays a major role in optimizing lung expansion. The semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's position (sitting upright at 45 to 90 degrees) is standard for patients experiencing shortness of breath, as it uses gravity to pull the abdominal organs down, reducing pressure on the diaphragm and allowing maximum thoracic expansion. Conversely, side-lying recovery positions are utilized for unconscious patients to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. Nurses must also be familiar with artificial airways like oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways, which keep the tongue from blocking the pharynx, and understand the indications for gentle suctioning to remove excessive oral or tracheal secretions that impair airflow.

Oxygen Therapy Delivery Systems and Safety

Oxygen therapy is a medical treatment prescribed to prevent or treat hypoxia by delivering supplemental oxygen at concentrations higher than room air. Oxygen is considered a medication and must be administered according to specific medical orders, specifying the delivery device, flow rate, and target saturation. Various delivery systems exist, categorized as low-flow or high-flow devices. Nasal cannulas are low-flow devices delivering fractional concentrations of inspired oxygen (FiO2) at flow rates from 1 to 6 liters per minute, offering comfort and freedom for eating and talking. Simple face masks, partial rebreather masks, and non-rebreather masks deliver higher oxygen concentrations for patients requiring increased support. High-flow nasal cannula therapy and Venturi masks provide precise, fixed FiO2 concentrations regardless of the patient's respiratory pattern, making them ideal for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease who require controlled oxygen delivery to avoid suppressing their hypoxic respiratory drive. Because oxygen supports combustion, strict safety precautions must be enforced: 'No Smoking' signs must be clearly posted, open flames and electrical sparks must be kept away, petroleum-based lubricants on the face should be avoided, and oxygen cylinders must be securely stored upright to prevent tipping.

Breathing Exercises and Lung Expansion Techniques

Breathing exercises and lung expansion techniques are essential non-pharmacological nursing interventions used to improve ventilation, promote gas exchange, and prevent postoperative pulmonary complications such as atelectasis and pneumonia. Pursed-lip breathing is a valuable technique taught to patients with chronic respiratory conditions like COPD or asthma. It involves inhaling slowly through the nose with the mouth closed, then exhaling slowly and evenly through pursed lips as if whistling, which creates backpressure in the airways, prevents premature airway collapse, and prolongs exhalation to eliminate trapped air. Diaphragmatic breathing, or abdominal breathing, trains patients to use the diaphragm effectively rather than accessory muscles, conserving energy and increasing respiratory efficiency. Incentive spirometry is another crucial bedside tool widely used after surgery. It encourages patients to take slow, deep, sustained inhalations, thereby expanding alveoli, promoting surfactant production, and helping to re-expand collapsed lung tissue. Nurses must educate patients on how to use the incentive spirometer correctly—inhaling slowly to raise the piston to a set target, holding the breath for several seconds, and performing repetitions regularly while coughing to clear mobilized secretions.

Monitoring, Documentation, and Complication Prevention

Effective monitoring, accurate documentation, and proactive complication prevention are the hallmarks of safe, high-quality nursing care for patients requiring respiratory support. Nurses must maintain continuous vigilance, regularly reassessing respiratory rates, breath sounds, oxygen saturation, and clinical responses to interventions. Documentation in the patient's clinical record must be objective, timely, and comprehensive, detailing the exact oxygen delivery device, flow rate, patient tolerance, pre- and post-intervention vital signs, and any complications observed. Prevention of complications associated with respiratory support is paramount. For patients receiving oxygen therapy, nurses must monitor for nasal mucosa drying, skin breakdown around the ears and nose from tubing friction, and oxygen toxicity associated with high concentrations over prolonged periods. For immobilized or intubated patients, preventing hospital-acquired pneumonia requires frequent repositioning every two hours, meticulous oral hygiene using chlorhexidine protocols, early mobilization, and encouraging deep breathing exercises or providing prescribed suctioning. By integrating rigorous monitoring with prompt reporting of declining clinical trends, nurses safeguard patients from preventable adverse events and support their journey toward respiratory stability and recovery.
