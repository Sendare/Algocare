Supporting Respiratory Functions

Anatomy and Physiology of the Respiratory System

The respiratory system is split into two main sections: the upper and lower respiratory tracts. The upper tract includes the nose, nasal cavity, pharynx, and larynx, which filter, warm, and humidify incoming air. The lower tract begins at the trachea and branches into the bronchi, bronchioles, and finally the alveoli, where gas exchange actually happens.

Alveoli are tiny, grape-like air sacs wrapped in a dense network of capillaries. Oxygen passes through the thin alveolar-capillary membrane into the bloodstream, while carbon dioxide moves from the blood into the alveoli to be exhaled. This entire process relies on negative pressure ventilation. When the diaphragm contracts and moves downward, chest volume increases, creating a vacuum that pulls air inside the lungs.

Assessment of Respiratory Status

Assessing a patient's breathing requires careful observation, listening, and measurement. Nurses must look at the rate, rhythm, depth, and effort of breathing.

  • Rate: Count the number of breaths per minute. Normal adult resting rate is usually 12 to 20 breaths per minute.
  • Effort: Look for use of accessory muscles in the neck and shoulders, nasal flaring, or sternal retractions, which all signal hard work to breathe.
  • Color: Check the lips, nail beds, and mucous membranes for cyanosis, a bluish tint indicating low oxygen.
  • Breath Sounds: Use a stethoscope to listen for normal vesicular sounds or abnormal sounds like wheezes, crackles, and rhonchi.

Recognizing Respiratory Distress

Respiratory distress occurs when a person has to work abnormally hard to breathe or when gas exchange begins to fail. Early recognition prevents full respiratory arrest.

Common signs include tachypnea (fast breathing), dyspnea (feeling short of breath), and changes in behavior such as restlessness, anxiety, or confusion caused by lack of oxygen to the brain. In severe cases, patients may assume a tripod position, leaning forward with arms braced on their knees to open up the chest.

A common misconception is that patients with low oxygen will always look blue. In reality, cyanosis is a late sign of hypoxia. A patient can have dangerously low oxygen levels while maintaining a normal skin color, making early vital sign checks and pulse oximetry essential.

Positioning and Airway Clearance

Proper body positioning is one of the fastest ways a nurse can improve a patient's breathing.

  • High-Fowler's Position: Sitting the patient upright at a 60 to 90-degree angle lets gravity pull the diaphragm down, expanding the chest cavity and making lung expansion easier.
  • Tripod Position: Allowing a patient to lean forward over a bedside table reduces the weight of abdominal contents on the diaphragm.

When secretions block the airway, simple repositioning is not enough. Coughing and deep breathing exercises help move mucus up from smaller airways. For patients unable to clear secretions independently, suctioning the pharynx or trachea removes physical blockages and keeps the airway patent.

Oxygen Therapy Delivery Systems

Oxygen is treated as a medical drug; it requires a doctor's order specifying the flow rate or concentration, except in emergency rescue situations.

Different delivery devices suit different patient needs:

  • Nasal Cannula: Delivers low-flow oxygen (1 to 6 liters per minute) and allows the patient to talk and eat easily.
  • Simple Face Mask: Fits over the mouth and nose, delivering moderate oxygen concentrations at flow rates of 5 to 10 liters per minute.
  • Venturi Mask: Delivers a precise, exact percentage of oxygen, making it ideal for patients with chronic lung disease who need careful oxygen regulation.

Safety is paramount around oxygen. Because oxygen supports combustion, open flames, sparks from electrical equipment, and petroleum-based jelly products must be kept completely away from oxygen equipment.

Breathing Exercises and Lung Expansion

Guiding patients through targeted breathing exercises helps prevent postoperative complications like atelectasis, which is the collapse of tiny lung air sacs.

Pursed-Lip Breathing: The patient inhales slowly through the nose and then exhales slowly through pursed lips, making the expiration twice as long as the inhalation. This creates mild back-pressure in the airways, keeping them open longer and helping trap air escape from damaged lungs.

Incentive Spirometry: This handheld device encourages patients to take slow, deep breaths. By watching a marker rise to a target goal, the patient gets visual feedback that motivates them to expand their lungs fully and clear out lower respiratory passages.

Monitoring and Complication Prevention

Continuous monitoring ensures that respiratory interventions are working and catches complications early. Pulse oximetry tracks blood oxygen saturation levels, though nurses must remember that poor circulation, cold fingers, or nail polish can cause false readings.

Preventing respiratory complications involves a team effort. Early ambulation, frequent position changes, adequate hydration to thin mucus, and good oral hygiene all lower the risk of hospital-acquired pneumonia. Nurses document every intervention, noting the patient's respiratory rate, breathing effort, oxygen flow rate, and response to treatment.
