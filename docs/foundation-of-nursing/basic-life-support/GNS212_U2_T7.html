Nebulization

Introduction and Definition of Nebulization

Nebulization is a respiratory therapy technique designed to convert liquid medication into a fine aerosol mist that can be readily inhaled directly into the lungs. This method relies on compressed air, oxygen, or ultrasonic power to break down the liquid solution. By delivering drugs straight to the respiratory tract, nebulization achieves a rapid local effect while minimizing the systemic side effects often associated with oral or intravenous administration. It remains a cornerstone treatment in Nigerian emergency rooms and medical wards for patients experiencing acute respiratory distress, particularly those unable to coordinate metered-dose inhalers properly.

Indications and Clinical Purposes in Nursing

Nurses encounter nebulization across various clinical scenarios, primarily for individuals suffering from obstructive airway diseases. Common indications include:

  • Acute Asthma Exacerbation: To reverse severe bronchospasm and restore adequate airflow.
  • Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD): For routine or rescue management of chronic airway limitation and dyspnea.
  • Sputum Induction: To help mobilize thick, tenacious secretions when a patient cannot expectorated independently.
  • Upper Airway Edema: Administration of specific agents like racemic epinephrine to reduce mucosal swelling.

Required Equipment and Medication Preparation

Executing a successful nebulizer treatment requires gathering specific components and preparing the drug accurately. The setup typically includes a gas source (compressor machine or oxygen flow meter), connecting tubing, a medication cup (nebulizer bowl), and either a mouthpiece or a properly fitted face mask.

When preparing the medication, verify the doctor's prescription against the drug label using the standard medication administration rights. Draw up liquid ampoules or vials cleanly. If the prescribed dose volume is too small to adequately aerosolize in the chamber, sterile normal saline is added to reach the optimal operational volume, usually between 3 to 4 milliliters.

Patient Assessment Prior to the Procedure

Before starting nebulization, baseline assessment establishes the patient's respiratory status and ensures the therapy is safe to proceed. Check the patient's baseline vital signs, focusing on respiratory rate, oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry, and pulse rate, as many bronchodilators cause transient tachycardia. Auscultate the chest to identify baseline breath sounds, noting wheezes, crackles, or diminished air entry. Evaluate the patient's level of consciousness and ability to cooperate with holding a mouthpiece or wearing a mask. Document these baseline parameters clearly so post-treatment changes can be accurately measured.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Administering the treatment demands proper technique to maximize drug deposition in the lower respiratory tract. Position the patient upright in a comfortable sitting posture to allow full chest expansion. Connect the tubing to the air or oxygen source, adjusting the flow rate generally between 6 to 8 liters per minute so a visible mist forms. Instruct the patient to place the mouthpiece firmly between their teeth with lips sealed tightly around it, or apply a well-fitting face mask if they are too weak. Guide the patient to take slow, deep breaths through the mouth, holding each breath for a brief moment at the end of inhalation before exhaling. Continue the session until all the liquid in the cup is completely nebulized, usually lasting around 10 to 15 minutes.

Patient Monitoring and Post-Procedure Evaluation

Evaluating the patient after nebulization determines the therapeutic efficacy of the intervention. Reassess vital signs, specifically noting if heart rate changes occurred and checking if oxygen saturation has improved. Auscultate the chest again to evaluate whether wheezing has subsided or if air entry has normalized. Encourage the patient to perform controlled coughing to clear any loosened secretions mobilized by the treatment. Inspect the oral cavity if corticosteroid medications were used, reminding the patient to rinse their mouth with water to prevent opportunistic fungal infections like oral candidiasis.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although generally safe, nebulization can trigger adverse events that require prompt nursing awareness. Common issues include drug-induced tachycardia, palpitations, tremors, and nervousness resulting from beta-adrenergic medications. Some patients may experience airway irritation, coughing spasms, or paradoxical bronchospasm if the mist triggers sudden airway constriction. If severe respiratory distress or dizziness develops during the session, stop the treatment immediately, notify the attending medical officer, and administer supplemental oxygen if indicated.

Infection Control and Equipment Maintenance

Nebulizer equipment can easily harbor bacterial pathogens if proper hygiene protocols are ignored, posing a serious infection risk to vulnerable respiratory patients. After each treatment, the medication cup and mouthpiece must be disassembled, washed thoroughly with warm soapy water, rinsed with clean water, and allowed to air dry completely on a clean paper towel. In hospital settings, follow institutional policies regarding single-patient use items and high-level disinfection or sterilization intervals. Never store wet equipment in closed containers, as moisture promotes microbial growth.
