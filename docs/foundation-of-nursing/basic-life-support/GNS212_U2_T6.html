Nelson Inhaler

Introduction and Purpose of the Nelson Inhaler

The Nelson inhaler is a traditional piece of nursing equipment designed to deliver medicated steam directly to the upper respiratory tract. It consists of a specialized earthenware or ceramic jar fitted with a cork lid, a central mouthpiece, and a side air inlet. When boiling water and volatile medications are placed inside, the steam travels upward through the mouthpiece as the patient inhales. This moist heat therapy helps soothe irritated mucous membranes, loosen thick respiratory secretions, and relieve nasal congestion. While modern nebulizers and metered-dose inhalers are more common today, understanding the classic Nelson inhaler remains essential for historical context and for practice settings where basic steam inhalation is still utilized.

Anatomy and Parts of the Device

A standard Nelson inhaler is built from specific parts that work together to ensure safe and effective steam delivery.

  • Earthenware jar: Holds the boiling water and volatile medication securely while retaining heat.
  • Cork or rubber bung: Fits tightly into the top opening of the jar to prevent steam from escaping out the sides.
  • Mouthpiece: Extends upward from the lid, allowing the patient to place their lips around it to inhale the vapor.
  • Air inlet: A small side opening or secondary tube that lets fresh air mix with the rising steam so it does not burn the patient's mouth.

Clinical Indications and Nursing Applications

Steam inhalation via a Nelson inhaler is indicated for various upper respiratory conditions where congestion and inflammation cause discomfort.

  • Acute rhinitis and sinusitis: Relieves sinus pressure and promotes drainage of blocked passages.
  • Laryngitis and pharyngitis: Soothes raw, painful throat tissues and reduces hoarseness.
  • Common cold: Eases nasal stuffiness and helps clear upper airway passages.
  • Tracheitis: Delivers moisture to the upper trachea to ease dry, hacking coughs.

Preparation of Equipment and Volatile Medicaments

Before starting the procedure, the nurse gathers all necessary supplies, including the Nelson inhaler, boiling water, a measuring jug, a towel, and the prescribed volatile compound, such as menthol or tincture of benzoin. The nurse inspects the ceramic jar for cracks or chips that could cause leaks or breakage when exposed to thermal shock. Next, the appropriate dosage of medication is measured. A common clinical error is adding volatile drops to cold water before boiling; the medicine must be added directly to the hot water inside the assembled jar so the active vapors release properly during inhalation.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Performing steam inhalation safely requires a structured approach to protect the patient and maximize the treatment's effectiveness. First, explain the procedure to gain the patient's cooperation and place them in a comfortable sitting position at a table. Fill the jar about two-thirds full with freshly boiled water and add the prescribed medicine. Insert the cork lid with the mouthpiece firmly into place. Instruct the patient to place their lips lightly around the mouthpiece—not biting down—and breathe in through the mouth and out through the nose. Sessions usually last between five to ten minutes, during which the nurse should periodically check on the patient's comfort.

Patient Safety Precautions and Hazards

Steam treatments carry inherent risks that require constant vigilance from the nursing staff. The most significant danger is scalding, which can occur if boiling water spills or if the steam is too hot. Never leave a patient unattended with a Nelson inhaler, especially if they are elderly, weak, or drowsy. Ensure the device is placed on a stable, flat surface so it cannot be easily knocked over. If the patient experiences dizziness, shortness of breath, or excessive heat irritation, stop the treatment immediately and remove the device from their face.

Post-Procedure Care and Equipment Maintenance

Once the inhalation session is finished, proper cleanup ensures infection control and prolongs the life of the equipment. Assist the patient in wiping their mouth and face, and offer them a comfortable resting position. Allow the Nelson inhaler to cool down before attempting to empty or clean it to avoid burning your hands. Discard any remaining water, remove the cork lid, and wash all components thoroughly with hot soapy water. Rinse the jar and mouthpiece with clean water, dry them completely, and store the unit in a designated clean utility area.
