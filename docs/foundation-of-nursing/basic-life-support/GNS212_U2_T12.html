Intermittent Suction of the Tracheobronchial Tree

Introduction and Purpose of Tracheobronchial Suctioning

Tracheobronchial suctioning is a vital airway clearance technique used when a patient cannot effectively cough up secretions on their own. This procedure involves passing a sterile catheter through an artificial airway, such as an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy, or directly through the nasopharynx or oropharynx, to remove mucus, blood, or other fluids. The primary goal is to maintain a patent airway, improve gas exchange, and prevent lower respiratory infections caused by pooled secretions. Suctioning is performed intermittently rather than continuously to prevent excessive mucosal trauma and severe oxygen deprivation.

Indications and Contraindications for Intermittent Suction

Knowing when to suction and when to hold off is a fundamental clinical skill. Nurses must assess the patient for signs that indicate an obstructed airway before diving in with a catheter.

  • Visible secretions: Mucus bubbling in the artificial airway or heard in the upper chest.
  • Respiratory distress: Sudden shortness of breath, increased breathing rate, or use of accessory muscles.
  • Desaturation: A sudden drop on the pulse oximeter.
  • Ineffective cough: The patient is too weak, sedated, or paralyzed to clear their own throat.

Contraindications are situations where suctioning causes more harm than good, such as in patients with severe bronchospasm, upper airway edema, bleeding disorders, or recent tracheal surgery. In these cases, alternate therapies or medical stabilization must happen first.

Required Equipment and Preparation

Before touching the patient, the nurse must gather all necessary supplies to ensure a smooth, sterile, and safe procedure. Rushing off to find missing items halfway through compromises patient safety and breaks sterile technique.

  1. Suction source and regulator: Set to the appropriate negative pressure, usually between 80 to 120 mmHg for adults.
  2. Collection container and connecting tubing: To safely trap and hold removed secretions.
  3. Sterile suction kit or catheter: Sized appropriately so it occupies no more than half the diameter of the artificial airway.
  4. Personal protective equipment: Gloves, gown, mask, and eye protection because splashing body fluids is common during coughing spells.
  5. Sterile normal saline: Used only for clearing the connecting tubing, not routinely instilled into the lungs.
  6. Ambu bag and oxygen source: Ready for immediate pre-oxygenation.

Pre-Assessment and Patient Preparation

Suctioning is uncomfortable and frightening for conscious patients, and it places physiological stress on everyone. Taking a moment to assess and prep the patient prevents complications.

Always check baseline vital signs, specifically oxygen saturation, heart rate, and lung sounds. Explain the procedure step-by-step, even if the patient appears unresponsive, because hearing provides comfort. Hyperoxygenate the patient using 100% oxygen via a manual resuscitator bag or ventilator settings for 30 to 60 seconds before starting. This builds up a temporary oxygen reserve to offset the drops that happen while the catheter blocks airflow.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Intermittent Suctioning

Executing the procedure with precision protects the airway lining and minimizes infection risks.

  • Put on appropriate protective gear and maintain a sterile field for your dominant hand.
  • Gently insert the catheter without applying suction until you meet resistance or the patient coughs.
  • Withdraw the catheter about one centimeter to avoid resting directly against the sensitive wall.
  • Apply intermittent suction by placing and releasing your thumb over the control port while withdrawing the catheter in a slow, twisting motion.
  • Keep each pass under 10 to 15 seconds total to prevent severe low oxygen.
  • Allow the patient to rest and recover on oxygen between passes if multiple attempts are needed.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Tracheobronchial suctioning is not a benign procedure; it carries risks that demand watchful nursing care. Hypoxia is the most common issue, treated immediately by stopping the suction and providing supplemental oxygen. Cardiac dysrhythmias can also occur because the vagus nerve is stimulated when the catheter touches the airway walls. Trauma and bleeding happen if suction pressure is too high or if the catheter is forced aggressively. Infection is another hazard, which is why strict aseptic technique is non-negotiable. If a patient shows signs of severe distress, blue discoloration, or a plummeting heart rate, stop immediately and bag-ventilate.

Post-Procedure Evaluation and Documentation

After completing the suctioning session, the nurse must evaluate how well the patient tolerated the procedure and record the findings accurately. Reassess lung sounds to see if the wheezes or coarse crackles have cleared. Check vital signs and oxygen saturation to confirm the patient has returned to their baseline. Document the date, time, amount, consistency, and color of the secretions removed, along with how the patient tolerated the procedure. Good documentation helps the healthcare team track respiratory trends and adjust future care plans.
