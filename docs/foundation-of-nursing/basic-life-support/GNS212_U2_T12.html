Intermittent Suction of the Tracheobronchial Tree

Introduction and Purpose of Tracheobronchial Suctioning

Tracheobronchial suctioning is an essential clinical procedure used to remove accumulated mucus, blood, and other secretions from the lower airway when a patient cannot effectively clear them independently. An artificial airway, such as an endotracheal or tracheostomy tube, bypasses the upper airway's natural filtering, warming, and humidifying mechanisms. Consequently, mucus production increases while the cough reflex becomes mechanically impaired. Intermittent negative pressure applied via a sterile catheter clears the airway, maintains patent ventilation, prevents atelectasis, and reduces the risk of lower respiratory tract infections. In many Nigerian clinical settings where continuous in-line suction systems may not be readily available for every patient, mastering open, intermittent suction technique using a wall regulator or portable suction machine remains a fundamental competency for safe nursing practice.

Indications and Assessment

Suctioning should never be performed routinely on a fixed time schedule. Instead, clinical indicators must guide the intervention to avoid unnecessary mucosal trauma and hypoxemia. Assessment findings that signal a clear need for suctioning include: - **Visible secretions**: Mucus bubbling or pooling within the artificial airway tube. - **Audible crackles or rhonchi**: Coarse breath sounds heard over the trachea and mainstem bronchi during auscultation. - **Respiratory distress**: Sudden tachypnea, increased work of breathing, or use of accessory muscles. - **Oxygen desaturation**: A sudden drop in pulse oximetry readings. - **Ventilator alarms**: Peak inspiratory pressure alarms sounding on mechanical ventilators due to airway resistance. - **Ineffective cough**: The patient's inability to clear secretions despite active coughing efforts.

Required Equipment and Pressure Settings

Gathering proper equipment before beginning the procedure prevents delays and ensures patient safety. The nurse must prepare: - Suction machine or wall-mounted vacuum regulator with connecting tubing. - Sterile suction catheter of appropriate size (the outer diameter of the catheter should not exceed half the internal diameter of the artificial airway). - Sterile gloves, mask, eye protection, and a disposable gown or apron. - Sterile basin and sterile normal saline solution for rinsing the catheter. - Manual resuscitator bag (Ambu bag) connected to an oxygen source. - Pulse oximeter for continuous monitoring. Regulator pressure must be adjusted carefully. For adults, negative pressure should generally be set between 100 to 120 mmHg. Setting the vacuum too high causes severe mucosal damage and rapid hypoxia, while too little pressure fails to clear thick secretions.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Hyperoxygenation

Preparation minimizes patient anxiety and protects against hypoxia during the procedure. The nurse explains the steps to conscious patients, reassuring them that temporary discomfort or coughing is normal. Vital baseline parameters, including heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation, must be recorded. Because suctioning removes air from the lungs along with mucus, transient hypoxemia is a constant risk. To mitigate this, hyperoxygenation is performed immediately before the procedure. The patient is administered 100% oxygen using a mechanical ventilator hyper-inflation feature or a manual resuscitator bag for 30 to 60 seconds. Patients with chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure (such as those with advanced COPD) require cautious, controlled oxygenation rather than sudden high-concentration bursts to prevent blunting their hypoxic respiratory drive.

Step-by-Step Execution of Intermittent Suctioning

Execute the procedure using strict aseptic technique to prevent introducing pathogens directly into the lower respiratory tract. Put on sterile gloves, keeping the dominant hand strictly sterile. Connect the sterile suction catheter to the connecting tubing held by a non-sterile assistant or placed nearby. Insert the catheter gently and rapidly into the artificial airway without applying any suction. Advance the catheter until resistance is met at the carina or until the patient coughs, then withdraw it slightly by about one centimeter to avoid mucosal damage. Apply intermittent suction only during withdrawal by placing and releasing a thumb over the control port, rotating the catheter between the thumb and forefinger to clear all sides of the lumen. Keep the entire suction pass under 10 to 15 seconds to prevent severe hypoxia and cardiac arrhythmias. Afterward, hyperoxygenate the patient again and allow their baseline vitals to recover before attempting any subsequent passes.

Potential Complications and Nursing Management

Although life-saving, tracheobronchial suctioning carries significant physiological risks if performed incorrectly. Complications include: - **Hypoxemia**: Caused by removing ambient oxygen along with secretions; managed by immediate re-oxygenation. - **Cardiac Arrhythmias**: Triggered by vagal nerve stimulation or severe hypoxia; stop suctioning immediately and administer oxygen. - **Mucosal Trauma and Bleeding**: Caused by excessive vacuum pressure or rough catheter insertion; use correct pressure and gentle technique. - **Bronchospasm**: Irritation of the airway triggers sudden smooth muscle constriction; administer prescribed bronchodilators. - **Infection**: Introduction of bacteria from poor hand hygiene or non-sterile technique; adhere strictly to infection control protocols.

Post-Procedure Evaluation and Documentation

After completing suctioning, the nurse must evaluate the effectiveness of the procedure and document findings accurately. Assess breath sounds, respiratory rate, work of breathing, and oxygen saturation to confirm that airway clearance improved gas exchange. Inspect the color, consistency, odor, and amount of the suctioned secretions. Discard disposable equipment properly in accordance with institutional infection control policies, remove PPE, and perform hand hygiene. Documentation in the patient's nursing notes must include the pre- and post-procedure vital signs and oxygen saturation, the reason for suctioning, the color, consistency, and amount of secretions obtained, how the patient tolerated the procedure, and any complications encountered along with corresponding nursing interventions.
