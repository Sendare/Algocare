Face Mask Oxygen Administration

Indications and Goals of Face Mask Oxygen Therapy

Oxygen therapy is a vital intervention used when a patient cannot maintain adequate blood oxygen levels independently. The primary goal is to maintain arterial oxygen saturation within a safe, prescribed range, typically between 94% and 98% for most adults, while reducing the workload on the heart and lungs. Face masks are specifically indicated for patients requiring moderate to high concentrations of oxygen, acute respiratory distress, trauma, carbon monoxide poisoning, or during the post-recovery phase of anesthesia.

Unlike nasal cannulas, which deliver lower and more variable oxygen percentages, face masks cover both the nose and mouth. This allows for the delivery of higher flow rates and precise oxygen percentages, making them suitable for acute medical emergencies. Nurses must carefully evaluate each patient's clinical presentation, work of breathing, and baseline health status before initiating therapy.

Types of Oxygen Face Masks and Flow Rates

Different clinical situations require different types of oxygen masks. Selecting the correct device ensures the patient receives the exact FiO2 (fraction of inspired oxygen) ordered by the physician.

  • Simple Face Mask: Delivers moderate oxygen concentrations at flow rates between 5 to 10 liters per minute. It features side vents to let exhaled carbon dioxide escape.
  • Partial Non-Breeder Mask: Equipped with a reservoir bag that must remain inflated during inspiration. Flow rates range from 6 to 15 liters per minute, delivering higher oxygen percentages.
  • Non-Breeder Mask: Similar to the partial non-rebreather, but includes one-way valves on the side ports and between the mask and bag. This prevents the room air and exhaled air from mixing back into the bag, delivering close to 100% oxygen at 10 to 15 liters per minute.
  • Venturi Mask: Uses interchangeable color-coded adapters to deliver a precise, fixed concentration of oxygen regardless of the patient's breathing pattern. It is especially useful for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Required Equipment and Setup

Before applying an oxygen mask, gather all necessary supplies at the bedside to ensure a smooth and safe procedure.

  1. Oxygen Source: A wall-mounted flowmeter connected to a central pipeline or a portable oxygen cylinder with a pressure regulator.
  2. Flowmeter: A device that regulates the liters per minute of oxygen released to the patient.
  3. Humidifier Bottle: Filled with sterile water to moisten the dry medical gas, preventing irritation of the mucous membranes, especially for flow rates above 4 liters per minute.
  4. Appropriate Face Mask: Selected based on the doctor's prescription and clinical need, complete with its connecting tubing.
  5. Signage: An 'Oxygen in Use' warning sign placed outside the room to reinforce fire safety rules.

Patient Preparation and Safety Checks

Preparing the patient mentally and physically is essential for compliance and safety during oxygen therapy. Explain the procedure in simple, reassuring language to reduce anxiety, as panic can increase oxygen demand. Check the patient's vital signs and baseline oxygen saturation using a pulse oximeter.

Safety is paramount because oxygen supports combustion. Inspect the room to ensure there are no open flames, spark hazards, or electrical devices in disrepair near the bed. Instruct the patient and visitors strictly that smoking is prohibited. Clean and dry the patient's face to ensure a proper seal and prevent skin breakdown underneath the mask straps.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Executing the administration steps correctly ensures the patient receives the prescribed dose comfortably.

  1. Perform hand hygiene and verify the medical order against the patient's identification band.
  2. Connect the oxygen tubing securely to the humidifier and the flowmeter.
  3. Turn on the oxygen source and adjust the flowmeter to the prescribed liters per minute.
  4. For reservoir masks, ensure the bag inflates properly before placing the mask on the face.
  5. Position the mask gently over the patient's nose and mouth, molding the flexible metal strip to the bridge of the nose.
  6. Secure the elastic strap comfortably around the back of the head, ensuring it is neither too tight to cause pressure ulcers nor too loose to slip off.

Ongoing Monitoring and Patient Maintenance

Oxygen therapy requires continuous evaluation to confirm effectiveness and prevent complications. Monitor the patient's respiratory rate, depth, and effort regularly. Use pulse oximetry to check oxygen saturation levels against the target range.

Assess the skin on the bridge of the nose, cheeks, and behind the ears every few hours for signs of pressure breakdown or redness. Remove the mask briefly to clean and dry the skin if allowed by protocol. Ensure the connecting tubing is free of kinks or twists that could block the gas flow. Check the water level in the humidifier bottle frequently and refill with sterile water as needed.

Recognizing and Managing Complications

Although oxygen is therapeutic, misuse or prolonged exposure can lead to adverse effects.

  • Oxygen Toxicity: Occurs when breathing high concentrations of oxygen over a long period, damaging lung tissue. Prevent this by using the lowest effective dose.
  • Mucosal Dryness: Dry medical gas irritates the nasal and oral passages, causing sore throats or nosebleeds. Ensure proper humidification is active.
  • Skin Breakdown: Pressure from mask straps can cause skin erosion on the ears and bridge of the nose. Use protective padding if necessary.
  • Hypoventilation in COPD: Giving high flow rates to chronic carbon dioxide retainers can remove their hypoxic drive to breathe, leading to dangerous carbon dioxide buildup. Always monitor mental status closely.
