Face Mask Oxygen Administration

Clinical Indications and Purpose of Face Mask Oxygen

Oxygen administration via a face mask is a vital intervention used to reverse or prevent tissue hypoxia. In Nigerian clinical settings, nurses frequently initiate this therapy for patients presenting with acute respiratory distress, severe malaria with metabolic acidosis, post-operative recovery, or exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. The primary goal is to maintain adequate arterial oxygen saturation, typically keeping SpO2 above 92% for most adults, while avoiding the risks of hyperoxia in vulnerable populations. Face masks are chosen over nasal cannulas when higher concentrations of inspired oxygen are required or when the patient is a mouth breather.

Essential Equipment for Delivery

Setting up a safe oxygen delivery system requires assembling several interconnected components correctly. - **Oxygen Source**: A wall-outlet piped system or a portable compressed gas cylinder equipped with a pressure regulator. - **Flowmeter**: A device attached to the gas source that measures and controls the liters per minute (L/min) of gas released. - **Humidifier bottle**: Filled with sterile or clean distilled water to moisten dry medical gas, preventing irritation and drying of the patient's mucous membranes, especially when flow rates exceed 4 L/min. - **Connecting tubing**: Kink-resistant plastic tubing that bridges the flowmeter to the delivery mask. - **Appropriate face mask**: Selected based on the prescribed FiO2 and patient anatomical size.

Types of Oxygen Face Masks and Flow Rates

Different mask designs deliver varying fractions of inspired oxygen (FiO2) based on specific structural features and flow settings. Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before applying an oxygen mask, a systematic baseline assessment establishes the patient's current status and uncovers potential safety hazards. The nurse evaluates respiratory rate, depth, pattern, and work of breathing, noting the presence of cyanosis, nasal flaring, or accessory muscle use. Baseline oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry is documented. Clinicians must inspect the skin of the face, bridge of the nose, and ears for existing pressure injury or breakdown. Patient and family education reduces anxiety; explaining that the mask might feel slightly restrictive helps secure cooperation.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Oxygen

Executing oxygen administration safely involves a methodical sequence of actions. First, verify the medical order against the patient's identification band. Attach the flowmeter to the oxygen source and connect the humidifier bottle and tubing. Turn on the oxygen flow to the prescribed rate, ensuring gas is flowing freely before the mask touches the patient's face—especially crucial for reservoir bags, which must be pre-inflated. Gently contour the mask over the patient's nose and mouth, securing the elastic band comfortably around the head. Adjust the flexible metal strip on the bridge of the nose to eliminate gaps, reducing airflow leakage toward the eyes.

Ongoing Monitoring and Safety Precautions

Oxygen is classified as a medical drug and demands rigorous continuous surveillance. Nurses must re-assess vital signs, work of breathing, and oxygen saturation within fifteen to thirty minutes after starting therapy and periodically thereafter. The skin beneath the mask and elastic straps requires inspection every few hours to prevent pressure ulcers. Because oxygen strongly supports combustion, strict safety rules must be enforced: display 'Oxygen in Use' warning signs prominently, prohibit smoking, open flames, and electrical spark hazards near the bedside, and avoid using petroleum-based jelly on the patient's face.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although life-saving, face mask oxygen therapy carries inherent risks if not carefully managed. Oxygen toxicity can occur when high concentrations of oxygen are administered over an extended period, leading to pulmonary damage and alveolar collapse (absorption atelectasis). In patients with chronic hypercapnia, high flow rates can suppress their hypoxic respiratory drive, leading to dangerous carbon dioxide narcosis and lethargy. Localized skin breakdown from tight straps is also common. Nurses mitigate these complications by titrating oxygen to the lowest effective dose, repositioning straps frequently, and reporting declining mental status or lab changes promptly.
