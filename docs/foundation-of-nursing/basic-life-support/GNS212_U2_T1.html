Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Introduction and Principles of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation is an emergency lifesaving procedure performed when the heart stops beating effectively. The primary objective is to manually maintain circulatory flow and oxygenation to vital organs, particularly the brain and heart, until advanced medical help arrives or spontaneous circulation returns. Without intervention, irreversible brain damage begins within minutes as oxygen reserves deplete.

The fundamental components of basic life support revolve around the CAB sequence: Chest compressions, Airway, and Breathing. Reordering this sequence from the older ABC method prioritizes immediate chest compressions, ensuring that oxygen remaining in the bloodstream is circulated with minimal delay. In Nigerian clinical and community settings, recognizing cardiac arrest early and initiating prompt hands-only or conventional CPR dramatically alters patient survival outcomes before emergency medical services can intervene.

The Chain of Survival in Basic Life Support

The chain of survival represents a series of critical, interconnected actions that, when executed sequentially, maximize the likelihood of survival from cardiac arrest. Weakness or failure at any single link adversely impacts the final outcome.

  • Early Recognition and Prevention: Identifying risk factors, preventing emergencies, and calling for help before cardiac arrest occurs.
  • Early CPR: Immediate, high-quality chest compressions to maintain vital organ perfusion.
  • Early Defibrillation: Rapid application of an automated external defibrillator to treat shockable rhythms like ventricular fibrillation.
  • Advanced Life Support: Specialized interventions provided by trained medical personnel involving drugs and advanced airways.
  • Post-Cardiac Arrest Care: Comprehensive medical management in a hospital setting to optimize neurological recovery and cardiac function.

Assessment of the Scene and Patient Responsiveness

Before rendering any form of emergency care, safety must be established. Entering a hazardous scene—such as one involving live electrical wires, structural collapse, or aggressive crowds—endangers the rescuer and creates multiple casualties.

Once safety is confirmed, assess responsiveness by tapping the patient's shoulder firmly and shouting loudly, "Are you okay?" Simultaneously scan the chest for normal breathing movements for no more than 10 seconds. Agonal gasps—occasional, irregular, or snorting breaths—do not constitute normal breathing and indicate cardiac arrest. If the patient is unresponsive and not breathing normally, activate the local emergency response system immediately and send someone to retrieve an automated external defibrillator if available.

Airway Management and Breathing Assessment

Opening the airway is a critical step following initial compressions to facilitate rescue breaths. In patients without suspected spinal trauma, use the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver. Place one hand on the forehead and tilt the head back while lifting the bony part of the chin with the index and middle fingers of the other hand. If spinal injury is suspected, employ the jaw-thrust maneuver instead to minimize cervical spine movement.

To deliver rescue breaths effectively, ensure a tight seal over the mouth and nose using a barrier device or bag-valve-mask apparatus. Each breath should be delivered over one second, watching for visible chest rise. Excessive volume or rapid inflation can force air into the stomach, leading to gastric distension, potential vomiting, and subsequent pulmonary aspiration.

Performing High-Quality Chest Compressions

The effectiveness of cardiopulmonary resuscitation depends entirely on the mechanical quality of chest compressions. Poorly executed compressions fail to generate adequate coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure.

  • Compression Rate: Maintain a rapid frequency of 100 to 120 compressions per minute.
  • Compression Depth: Push hard and deep, compressing the adult sternum at least 5 centimeters (2 inches) but not exceeding 6 centimeters.
  • Chest Recoil: Allow complete recoil of the chest wall after every compression to let the heart fill with blood before the next stroke.
  • Minimizing Interruptions: Keep pauses in compressions strictly under 10 seconds during rhythm checks or ventilations.
  • Rescuer Positioning: Position the patient on a firm, flat surface, kneel beside the chest, keep arms straight, and use upper body weight.

Use of the Automated External Defibrillator

An automated external defibrillator is a sophisticated, computerized device capable of analyzing cardiac rhythms and delivering an electrical shock when ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia is detected. As soon as an AED arrives at the scene, turn it on immediately and follow the spoken voice prompts.

Attach the adhesive electrode pads to the bare chest of the adult patient—one pad on the upper right chest below the collarbone, and the other on the lower left ribcage. Ensure no one is touching the patient while the device analyzes the rhythm or delivers a shock. If a shock is advised, loudly command everyone to stand clear before pressing the shock button. Immediately resume high-quality chest compressions right after the shock is delivered.

Special Considerations and Complications

Resuscitation efforts can present unique clinical challenges and physical complications. Rib fractures and sternal bruising are common, particularly in elderly patients with reduced bone density, but should never deter a nurse from performing necessary, life-saving compressions.

For pregnant patients in cardiac arrest, the enlarged uterus exerts significant pressure on the inferior vena cava, obstructing venous return and reducing cardiac output. Relieving this aortocaval compression by manually displacing the uterus to the left or tilting the patient's entire body at a slight angle is crucial during CPR. In drowning victims, addressing hypoxia through initial rescue breaths before compressions is prioritized. In all scenarios, rescuers must be aware of environmental hazards, wet surfaces, and implanted medical devices like pacemakers, which require placing AED pads at least an inch away from the device generator.

Termination of Resuscitation and Documentation

Deciding when to stop resuscitation efforts involves complex clinical and ethical judgments. Generally, basic life support continues until spontaneous circulation and breathing return, a qualified physician pronounces death or orders cessation, or rescue personnel are physically exhausted and unable to continue safely.

Accurate and detailed nursing documentation is a legal and professional necessity. The nursing record must capture the exact time collapse was identified or estimated, the precise time CPR was initiated, timestamps for defibrillator analysis and shocks delivered, medications administered by advanced teams, and the patient's eventual response or time of pronouncement. In busy Nigerian clinical environments, thorough notes protect the healthcare team legally and ensure seamless continuity of care during patient handovers.
