Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Introduction and Principles of Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation is an emergency life-saving procedure performed when someone's breathing or heartbeat has stopped. The primary goal of CPR is to maintain a flow of oxygenated blood to the brain and vital organs until advanced medical help arrives or normal heart function returns. Without intervention, irreversible brain damage begins within minutes of cardiac arrest. Every nurse must master these core life-saving principles because cardiac arrest can happen anywhere, both inside and outside the hospital setting.

The Chain of Survival in Basic Life Support

The chain of survival represents a sequence of coordinated actions that give the best chance of survival following cardiac arrest. Each link depends heavily on the success of the previous one.

  • Early recognition and prevention: Spotting trouble early and calling for help before the heart actually stops.
  • Immediate high-quality CPR: Pushing hard and fast on the chest to circulate blood.
  • Rapid defibrillation: Using an AED to shock a chaotic heart rhythm back into place.
  • Advanced life support: Skilled medical personnel taking over with drugs and advanced airways.
  • Post-cardiac arrest care: Comprehensive hospital care focused on recovery and neurological function.

Assessment of the Scene and Patient Responsiveness

Before touching any patient, a rescuer must ensure the environment is safe. Rushing into a dangerous situation creates multiple casualties. Once safety is confirmed, approach the patient and check for responsiveness by tapping their shoulder firmly and shouting, "Are you okay?" Simultaneously, quickly scan the chest for normal breathing movements for no more than ten seconds. If the patient does not respond and is either not breathing or only gasping, assume cardiac arrest and call for help immediately.

Airway Management and Breathing Assessment

Once cardiac arrest is suspected, the airway must be opened to allow air to reach the lungs. The standard technique for opening an uninjured airway is the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver. Place one hand on the forehead and tilt the head back while lifting the chin with the fingers of your other hand. Avoid pressing deeply into the soft tissues under the chin, as this can obstruct the airway. Look, listen, and feel for normal breathing for no longer than ten seconds before deciding to start compressions.

Performing High-Quality Chest Compressions

High-quality chest compressions are the cornerstone of effective CPR. Position the heel of one hand on the center of the patient's chest (lower half of the sternum) and place your other hand on top, interlocking your fingers. Keep your arms completely straight and position your shoulders directly over your hands. Push hard and fast, compressing the chest at least two inches deep for adults at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Allow the chest to recoil completely between compressions to let the heart refill with blood.

Use of the Automated External Defibrillator (AED)

An automated external defibrillator is a computerized device that analyzes the heart rhythm and delivers an electrical shock if needed. As soon as the AED arrives, turn it on immediately and follow the clear voice prompts. Expose the patient's chest, wipe it dry if necessary, and apply the adhesive pads as pictured on the device diagrams. Ensure nobody is touching the patient while the AED analyzes the rhythm or delivers a shock. Resume chest compressions immediately after the shock is delivered.

Special Considerations in CPR and Complications

Performing CPR in real-world scenarios often presents unique challenges. For pregnant patients, manual left uterine displacement helps relieve pressure on the large blood vessels to improve blood flow return to the heart. In cases of foreign-body airway obstruction, do not perform blind finger sweeps because they can push objects deeper. Common physical complications of vigorous compressions include rib fractures, sternal bruising, and regurgitation of stomach contents, but rescuers must prioritize life preservation over these injuries.

Termination of Resuscitation and Nursing Documentation

Resuscitation efforts continue until specific criteria are met to stop. These include the return of spontaneous circulation and breathing, the arrival of trained healthcare professionals who take over, or signs of irreversible death such as rigor mortis. Once resuscitation ends, accurate documentation is vital. The nurse must record the exact time the collapse was noticed, the time CPR started, times of AED shocks, medications administered, and the patient's overall response.
