Automated External Defibrillators (AED)

Introduction to Automated External Defibrillators

An **Automated External Defibrillator (AED)** is a sophisticated, portable electronic device designed to diagnose life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias, specifically ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. By analyzing the heart's electrical rhythm, the machine determines whether a shock is required to restore a normal sinus rhythm. In emergency settings across Nigerian healthcare facilities and public spaces, immediate access to an AED drastically alters survival outcomes during sudden cardiac arrest. Every minute delayed in delivering defibrillation reduces the likelihood of survival by approximately ten percent, making early intervention critical.

Indications and Rhythm Recognition

An AED is indicated exclusively for individuals who are unresponsive, not breathing normally, and lacking a palpable pulse. The device is programmed to recognize two specific shockable rhythms: - **Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)**: A chaotic, quivering electrical activity where the ventricles fail to pump blood effectively. - **Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (pVT)**: A rapid, regular or irregular heart rate originating in the ventricles that fails to produce an adequate cardiac output. The device will analyze the rhythm and intentionally advise *against* delivering a shock if it detects asystole or pulseless electrical activity, as these non-shockable rhythms require continued cardiopulmonary resuscitation and medication rather than electrical therapy.

Anatomy and Components of the Device

Standard AED units comprise a few essential, user-friendly components engineered for rapid deployment: - **Power Mechanism**: Usually activated by opening the lid or pressing a prominent green power button. - **Adhesive Electrode Pads**: Feature conductive gel and clear anatomical diagrams indicating correct placement on the bare chest. - **Connector Cable**: Links the electrode pads to the main computer processor of the device. - **Shock Button**: A flashing button that the operator presses to deliver the electrical discharge when prompted. - **Audio and Visual Prompts**: Synthetic voice instructions and graphic screens that guide the rescuer through each phase of operation.

Step-by-Step Operation Protocol

Operating an AED requires following a precise sequence to ensure safety and efficacy: 1. **Power On**: Turn on the AED immediately upon arrival at the scene. 2. **Expose the Chest**: Remove clothing and wipe the skin dry if wet or excessively sweaty. Shave dense chest hair only if it prevents pad adhesion. 3. **Apply Pads**: Place one pad on the upper right chest below the collarbone and the second pad on the lower left lateral ribcage. 4. **Clear the Patient**: Command all bystanders to step back and ensure nobody is touching the victim. 5. **Analyze and Shock**: Allow the device to analyze. If advised, ensure everyone is clear and press the flashing shock button.

Special Considerations and Safety Precautions

Certain patient conditions and environmental factors require modifications when applying an AED: - **Implanted Devices**: Pacemakers or cardioverter-defibrillators leave a bony lump beneath the skin. Place pads at least one inch away from these devices to avoid dampening the shock. - **Medication Patches**: Remove transdermal patches from the chest area and wipe the skin clean before pad application to prevent blocking energy transfer or causing skin burns. - **Water and Moisture**: Move the patient out of standing water and dry the chest thoroughly, as water creates a conductive shunt that wastes electrical energy across the skin surface.

Troubleshooting Common Errors

Rescuers frequently encounter obstacles that disrupt automated analysis. A common error is movement artifact caused by performing compressions during analysis or moving the stretcher in an ambulance. The device will typically prompt "Motion Detected" and halt analysis until the environment is still. Poor pad adhesion resulting from excessive chest hair, oil, or moisture will prompt a "Check Pads" error message. Resolving this requires peeling off old pads, drying the skin, or using a razor from the prep kit to clear the area before applying a fresh set.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Handover

Once the AED indicates a shock has been delivered, or if a non-shockable rhythm prompts no shock, the rescuer must immediately resume chest compressions. Do not remove the AED pads from the patient's chest even if spontaneous circulation returns, as the device continues to monitor the rhythm in case the patient relapses into ventricular fibrillation. When advanced emergency medical services arrive, provide a concise verbal handover detailing the total number of shocks delivered, the time elapsed, and any medication administered before transport to a definitive care facility.
