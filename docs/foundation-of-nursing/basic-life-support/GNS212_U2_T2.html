Automated External Defibrillators (AED)

Introduction to Automated External Defibrillators

An Automated External Defibrillator (AED) is a portable, computerized device that checks heart rhythm and can deliver an electrical shock to restore a normal heartbeat during cardiac arrest. When a person's heart suddenly stops beating effectively, it often enters chaotic rhythms like ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT). The AED analyzes the electrical activity of the heart and decides automatically whether a shock is required. This technology makes it possible for everyday people and frontline healthcare workers to deliver life-saving therapy before emergency medical services arrive.

Time is critical during cardiac arrest. For every minute defibrillation is delayed, the chance of survival drops sharply. While cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) keeps oxygen moving to the brain and heart, an AED is the only definitive treatment to restart a heart stuck in a shockable rhythm. Modern AEDs use clear, recorded voice prompts and visual diagrams to guide the rescuer safely through every phase of the rescue.

Indications for AED Use in Basic Life Support

An AED is indicated whenever a patient is in sudden cardiac arrest. You must suspect cardiac arrest if the person meets three specific clinical criteria:

  • Unresponsiveness: The person does not wake up or respond when you shake their shoulders and shout.
  • Absence of normal breathing: The person is either not breathing at all or only gasping irregularly, which is not normal breathing.
  • Pulselessness: Trained rescuers should check for a carotid pulse for no more than 10 seconds.

If these criteria are met, deploy the AED immediately. Do not delay attaching the device to check for a pulse if you are a lay rescuer; simply rely on unresponsiveness and abnormal breathing. Never use an AED on someone who is conscious, breathing normally, or has a palpable pulse, as delivering a shock to a beating heart can induce dangerous arrhythmias.

Anatomy and Components of a Standard AED

While different manufacturers build AEDs with unique outer casings, all standard devices share core internal and external components designed for rapid deployment:

  • Power Source: High-capacity lithium batteries that provide enough energy for multiple shocks and long monitoring periods.
  • Electrodes (Pads): Sticky adhesive pads equipped with metal conductive plates and cables that stick to the patient's bare chest.
  • Computer Circuitry: The internal processor that analyzes the heart rhythm and determines whether a shock is advised.
  • Shock Button: A flashing button that the rescuer must press to deliver the shock (in semi-automated models) or a countdown speaker (in fully automated models).
  • Speaker and Display Screen: Audio prompts and visual icons that guide the rescuer step-by-step through the rescue sequence.

Always check the status indicator light on the front of the unit during daily or weekly routine checks to ensure the battery and internal components are functional.

Step-by-Step Operation and Application Protocol

Using an AED follows a logical, standardized sequence that minimizes interruptions to chest compressions:

  1. Power On: Open the lid or press the power button. This activates voice prompts.
  2. Expose the Chest: Tear or cut away clothing. Wipe the chest dry if it is wet or sweaty.
  3. Apply Pads: Peel the backing off the pads and place them firmly on the bare chest. Place the first pad on the upper right chest below the collarbone, and the second pad on the lower left ribcage.
  4. Clear the Patient: Command everyone to step away and stop touching the patient while the AED analyzes the rhythm.
  5. Deliver Shock: If prompted, ensure nobody is touching the patient and press the flashing shock button.
  6. Resume CPR: Immediately start chest compressions and ventilations for two minutes before the AED prompts another analysis.

Never let pad cables cross over each other, and ensure good skin contact to prevent electrical arcing.

Special Considerations and Safety Precautions

Certain patient conditions and environmental hazards require minor adjustments when using an AED to ensure safety and effectiveness:

  • Water and Wet Surfaces: Water conducts electricity away from the heart. Pull the patient out of standing water and wipe the chest dry before applying pads.
  • Implanted Pacemakers or Defibrillators: Hard lumps under the skin near the collarbone indicate a device. Place the AED pad at least one inch away from the hardware so it does not absorb the shock.
  • Medication Patches: Transdermal patches on the chest can block pad adhesion or catch fire. Remove patches with gloved hands and wipe the skin clean before placing pads.
  • Excessive Chest Hair: Heavy hair can trap air pockets under the pads, causing poor contact. Quickly shave the hair if a razor is available in the AED kit, or press firmly; use a second set of pads to rip hair off if needed.

Always ensure no metal objects or oxygen sources are directly touching the patient when the shock button is pressed.

Troubleshooting Common AED Errors and Obstacles

During high-stress resuscitation attempts, technical glitches or environmental issues can trigger error messages from the AED. Recognizing these issues prevents panic and delays in care.

If the machine prompts "Check Pads" or "Poor Contact", it usually means the adhesive is not sticking securely due to sweat, oil, chest hair, or loose cables. Press firmly around the edges of the pads or replace them. If the machine warns "Movement Detected", stop all CPR compressions and tell bystanders to step back so the motion sensor can complete its rhythm analysis without interference.

Low battery warnings indicate the device was not maintained properly during routine checks; switch immediately to manual CPR or grab a secondary backup AED if available in the building.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Handover

Once emergency medical services arrive or the patient regains a pulse and begins breathing normally, your focus shifts to post-resuscitation care and formal handover.

If the patient recovers spontaneous circulation, leave the AED pads attached to their chest in case they experience another cardiac arrest event. Place the patient into the recovery position (on their side) if they are breathing and unconscious, to prevent airway blockage from vomit or the tongue. Monitor vital signs continuously.

During the clinical handover to paramedics or hospital staff, provide a clear, concise report covering:

  • Estimated time of collapse and total downtime.
  • Number of AED shocks delivered.
  • Total cycles of CPR performed.
  • Any medications or known medical history discovered.
