Intravenous Injection (IV)

Introduction to Intravenous Therapy

Intravenous therapy involves delivering medications, fluids, and blood products directly into a patient's systemic circulation through a vein. Because substances bypass the digestive tract and first-pass liver metabolism, this route provides the fastest onset of action among all parenteral methods. Nursing students must recognize that the intravenous route is indispensable in emergency situations, severe dehydration, or when a patient cannot take anything by mouth. However, this speed also carries increased risk. Once a substance enters the bloodstream, it cannot be recalled if an adverse reaction or medication error occurs. Therefore, precision, strict asepsis, and vigilant monitoring are non-negotiable foundations of safe practice.

Indications and Contraindications

  • Indications: Rapid fluid resuscitation, administration of blood or blood products, delivery of irritating medications that would destroy peripheral muscle tissue, and correction of severe electrolyte imbalances.
  • Contraindications: Choosing an extremity with active infection, widespread burns, a compromised lymphatic drainage system, or a functioning arteriovenous fistula used for dialysis. Nurses should always inspect both arms thoroughly before deciding where to place a cannula.

Equipment and Supplies Preparation

Gathering supplies before approaching the patient prevents unnecessary delays and reduces anxiety. Essential items include a suitable peripheral cannula, a tourniquet, alcohol swabs or chlorhexidine antiseptic, transparent adhesive dressings, extension tubing, pre-filled normal saline flush syringes, gloves, and a sharp disposal container.

Inspect every package for integrity and expiration dates. A compromised sterile seal means the equipment is contaminated and must be discarded immediately. Setting up the flush syringe and priming the extension set beforehand ensures you can immediately lock the cannula in place once blood return is confirmed.

Vein Selection and Anatomy

Selecting the right vein determines whether your cannulation attempt succeeds. Start distal on the upper extremities, moving upward with subsequent attempts if needed. Prime veins include the metacarpal, cephalic, and basilic veins of the hand and forearm.

Avoid veins in the wrist because of nearby nerves and tendons, and avoid the lower extremities whenever possible due to the high risk of blood clots. Palpate the vein carefully. A healthy vein feels bouncy, soft, and refills quickly after you release light pressure. Hard, sclerosed, or cord-like veins will likely infiltrate or blow out upon needle insertion.

Step-by-Step Procedure for IV Cannulation

Perform hand hygiene and put on non-sterile gloves. Apply a tourniquet four to six inches above your chosen insertion site to engorge the veins. Cleanse the skin using an approved antiseptic in a friction scrub, allowing it to air-dry completely to maximize antimicrobial action.

Anchor the skin gently below the site with your non-dominant hand to keep the vein from rolling. Insert the needle bevel-up at a 15 to 30-degree angle. Watch for a flashback of blood in the catheter hub, which confirms entry into the vessel lumen. Lower the needle almost parallel to the skin, advance the catheter slightly further, and then slide the plastic catheter forward while withdrawing the needle. Release the tourniquet, attach the primed extension set, and secure the site with a transparent dressing.

Administration of Medications and Fluids

Once intravenous access is established and confirmed patent, you can administer prescribed fluids or medications. Check the medication order against the patient wristband using the standard safety rights of medication administration.

Verify compatibility if multiple solutions or drugs run through the same line, as precipitation can occlude the catheter. Flush the line with normal saline before and after pushing direct intravenous medications. Monitor gravity infusion rates or program electronic infusion pumps carefully. Air bubbles, swollen skin around the site, or a beeping infusion pump require immediate troubleshooting.

Monitoring and Maintenance of IV Sites

An active intravenous site demands regular nursing assessment. Inspect the insertion site every shift, or more frequently if infusing vesicant medications. Check that the transparent dressing remains clean, dry, and intact.

Palpate lightly around the dressing edges for tenderness, warmth, or coolness. Ask the patient if they feel any pain or burning. Flush unused intermittent lines regularly according to facility policy to prevent clotting. Change peripheral catheters, administration sets, and dressings based on institutional guidelines to prevent catheter-related bloodstream infections.

Complications of IV Therapy

  • Infiltration: Fluid leaks into surrounding tissue; the skin looks pale, feels cool, and appears swollen.
  • Extravasation: Infiltration involving a vesicant drug that causes severe tissue damage or necrosis.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall marked by pain, redness, and a warm, hard streak along the vein.
  • Air Embolism: Air enters the circulatory system, causing sudden chest pain, shortness of breath, and cyanosis. Place the patient on their left side in the Trendelenburg position if this occurs.

Discontinuation of Intravenous Access

Remove a peripheral intravenous line promptly when therapy is finished, when signs of complications appear, or according to hospital protocol. Wash your hands, put on gloves, and stop any active infusion. Gently peel away the transparent dressing and adhesive tape toward the insertion site.

Place a dry sterile gauze pad over the site without applying pressure on the needle path. Pull the catheter straight out smoothly and quickly, keeping it parallel to the skin to avoid trauma. Inspect the catheter tip to ensure it is intact. Apply firm, direct pressure to the puncture site for at least two to three minutes until bleeding stops, then cover it with a clean adhesive bandage.

Documentation and Legal Responsibilities

Accurate documentation protects both the patient and the nurse. Record every insertion attempt, the date, time, catheter gauge, exact anatomical site, type of dressing used, and the patient's tolerance of the procedure. Document all fluid intake, medication additions, site assessments, and dressing changes meticulously.

Legally, nurses are accountable for every medication and fluid administered through the intravenous route. Failure to monitor a site properly or omitting documentation of complications can result in professional liability and patient harm. Write clearly, objectively, and promptly after every care action.
