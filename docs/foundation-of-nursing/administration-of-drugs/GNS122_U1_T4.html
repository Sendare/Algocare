Intravenous Injection (IV)

Introduction to Intravenous Therapy

Intravenous therapy involves the direct administration of fluids, electrolytes, medications, or blood products into a patient's venous circulation. By bypassing the digestive tract and first-pass metabolism, this route achieves immediate systemic bioavailability, making it indispensable in emergency resuscitation, critical care, and situations where oral intake is impossible. The primary goal is maintaining or restoring fluid and electrolyte balance, delivering targeted pharmacotherapy, and providing nutritional support. In Nigerian clinical settings, where presentation to healthcare facilities is often delayed until conditions become acute, efficient IV access is frequently a life-saving intervention. Understanding the rapid onset of action also highlights the heightened risk of adverse drug reactions compared to oral or subcutaneous routes.

Indications and Contraindications

- **Rapid therapeutic effect**: Delivering emergency medications like adrenaline or anticonvulsants during acute distress. - **Fluid and electrolyte replacement**: Managing severe dehydration from gastroenteritis, cholera, or extensive burns. - **Blood component transfusion**: Administering whole blood, packed cells, or platelets. - **Restricted oral intake**: Maintaining hydration and nutrition in unconscious, nil-per-os (NPO), or severely vomiting patients. - **Vessel compromise**: Avoiding peripheral cannulation on an extremity with localized infection, severe trauma, or an active arteriovenous fistula for hemodialysis.

Equipment and Supplies Preparation

Gathering and organizing equipment before initiating a venipuncture minimizes patient discomfort, reduces vessel trauma, and prevents infection. Essential supplies must be assembled on a clean, disinfected trolley: 1. **IV Cannula / Catheter**: Appropriate gauge selected based on the patient's age, vein size, and prescribed therapy. 2. **Tourniquet**: Used to engorge superficial veins by obstructing venous return. 3. **Antiseptic Swab**: Chlorhexidine in alcohol or 70% isopropyl alcohol for skin disinfection. 4. **Infusion Set and Fluids**: Primed tubing connected to the prescribed intravenous bag, ensuring no air bubbles remain. 5. **Transparent Dressing**: Sterile semi-permeable film to secure the catheter and allow site inspection. 6. **Personal Protective Equipment**: Clean gloves to maintain standard precautions.

Anatomy and Selection of Peripheral Veins

Successful cannulation relies heavily on identifying accessible, healthy veins in the upper extremities. The preferred anatomical sites include the dorsal metacarpal veins of the hand, the cephalic and basilic veins in the forearm, and the median cubital vein located in the antecubital fossa. When selecting a vein, nurses should prioritize distal sites first, preserving proximal areas for future access if complications arise. The ideal vein feels bouncy, straight, and resilient upon palpation, rather than hard, sclerosed, or rolling. Sclerosed veins are common in older adults or patients with a history of chronic IV drug use or frequent blood sampling, and they should be avoided because they resist catheter advancement and leak easily.

Step-by-Step Procedure for IV Cannulation

Performing venipuncture demands precision, aseptic technique, and clear communication to reduce patient anxiety. Begin by verifying the medical order, performing hand hygiene, and explaining the procedure to the patient. Apply the tourniquet four to six inches above the intended site and select a suitable vein. Don clean gloves and cleanse the skin using an approved antiseptic agent in a friction-driven concentric circle, allowing it to air-dry completely to ensure antimicrobial efficacy. Anchor the vein by pulling the skin taut distally. Insert the catheter with the bevel facing upward at a 15- to 30-degree angle. Upon observing a flash of blood in the flashback chamber, lower the catheter almost parallel to the skin and advance it slightly further. Advance the plastic catheter off the stylet while sliding it into the vein, release the tourniquet, apply digital pressure just above the insertion site, withdraw the stylet, and secure the catheter with a sterile dressing.

Administration of IV Medications and Fluids

Administering intravenous substances requires strict adherence to safe medication principles and accurate flow rate calculation. Nurses must verify the five rights of medication administration before starting any infusion. Fluids are delivered either by gravity, requiring manual calculation of drops per minute using the specific drop factor of the administration set, or via an electronic infusion pump for precision. When administering IV push medications, nurses must check drug references for safe administration rates, as pushing certain medications too rapidly can precipitate cardiac arrest or severe hypotension. Regular assessment of the line's patency before and during administration prevents extravasation into surrounding tissues.

Monitoring and Maintenance of IV Sites

Continuous surveillance preserves intravenous access and prevents local and systemic complications. Nurses should inspect the insertion site and palpate the surrounding area during every shift change and before each medication dose. Key indicators of a healthy site include intact dressings, unobstructed fluid flow, and the absence of pain, swelling, or redness. Intravenous dressing changes must be performed using aseptic technique whenever they become damp, loosened, or visibly soiled. Tubing used for continuous infusions should be replaced according to institutional guidelines, typically every 72 to 96 hours, to minimize bacterial colonization.

Complications of Intravenous Therapy and Management

Intravenous therapy carries risks of both local and systemic complications that require prompt recognition and intervention. - **Infiltration**: Occurs when fluid enters surrounding subcutaneous tissue due to catheter dislodgement or puncture of the opposite vein wall; characterized by coolness, swelling, and pallor around the site. Stop the infusion immediately and remove the device. - **Extravasation**: Similar to infiltration, but involves the leakage of a vesicant medication that causes tissue necrosis. Stop the infusion, leave the catheter in place to aspirate residual drug if applicable, and notify the physician. - **Phlebitis**: Inflammation of the vein wall caused by chemical, mechanical, or bacterial irritation, presenting as erythema, warmth, tenderness, and a palpable cord. Remove the cannula and apply a warm compress. - **Air Embolism**: Air entering the circulatory system, manifesting as sudden dyspnea, cyanosis, and hypotension. Place the patient in a left lateral Trendelenburg position and administer oxygen.

Discontinuation of Intravenous Access

Removing an intravenous catheter requires careful technique to prevent bleeding, vessel trauma, or air entry. The procedure is indicated when therapy is completed, the site exhibits complications, or the access is no longer clinically necessary. Begin by explaining the process to the patient and shutting off any active infusion rollers or pumps. Don clean gloves, carefully remove the securing tape and transparent dressing while stabilizing the hub, and gently withdraw the catheter parallel to the skin to avoid scraping the vessel wall. Immediately place a sterile gauze pad over the insertion site and apply firm pressure for at least one to two minutes until bleeding stops. Inspect the removed catheter tip to verify its structural integrity, ensuring no fragment broke off inside the vein.

Documentation and Legal Responsibilities

Accurate documentation is a legal and professional requirement that ensures continuity of care and provides evidence of nursing accountability. Every intravenous intervention—including site insertion attempts, gauge size used, fluid type, infusion rate, medication additives, patient responses, and site discontinuation—must be recorded promptly in the clinical notes. Nurses are legally responsible for verifying orders, monitoring patients for adverse reactions, and executing procedures within their recognized scope of practice. Incomplete or delayed charting can compromise patient safety and leaves the healthcare provider legally vulnerable during malpractice or negligence investigations.
