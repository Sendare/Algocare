Venepuncture

Introduction and Definition of Venepuncture

Venepuncture is the clinical procedure of puncturing a vein with a needle to withdraw blood for diagnostic testing, monitor treatment efficacy, or administer intravenous therapies. In Nigerian healthcare settings, this skill is fundamental for nurses working across emergency rooms, antenatal clinics, and general wards. Mastery of venepuncture ensures patient safety, reduces trauma to fragile blood vessels, and guarantees laboratory sample integrity.

Anatomy and Physiology of Peripheral Veins

Selecting an appropriate vein requires a solid understanding of upper limb vascular anatomy. The ideal peripheral veins for venepuncture lie in the cubital fossa of the arm.

  • Median Cubital Vein: The first choice for venepuncture; it is large, well-anchored, and less prone to rolling.
  • Cephalic Vein: Located on the lateral aspect of the arm; it tends to be painful upon puncture and can roll easily.
  • Basilic Vein: Located on the medial aspect; it is easy to palpate but runs close to the median nerve and brachial artery, increasing complication risks.

Indications and Contraindications

Venepuncture is indicated for routine full blood counts, malaria parasite testing, electrolyte panels, blood cultures, and cross-matching. Conversely, nurses must recognize when to avoid specific sites.

Never perform venepuncture on an arm with an active arteriovenous fistula used for dialysis, on the side of a previous radical mastectomy with lymph node removal, or directly through an inflamed, bruised, or scarred area. Drawing blood from an intravenous infusion arm alters diagnostic results due to fluid dilution, so nurses must always select the opposite arm or wait for the infusion to cease.

Equipment and Materials Required

Gathering all supplies prior to entering the bedside minimizes procedure time and patient anxiety. Essential items include a tourniquet, non-sterile gloves, alcohol swabs (70% isopropyl alcohol), vacuum collection tubes (vacutainers) or syringes, a needle-holder or butterfly needle set, adhesive bandage or cotton wool, and a puncture-resistant sharps container.

Infection Control and Standard Precautions

Standard precautions protect both the nurse and the patient from blood-borne pathogens such as HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C. Hand hygiene must be performed before and after the procedure using soap and water or alcohol-based hand rub. Gloves are mandatory during any step where contact with blood is possible. Under no circumstances should a used needle be recapped using two hands, as this remains a leading cause of accidental needle-stick injuries in Nigerian clinics.

Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Verify Patient Identity: Check the patient wristband against the laboratory request form.
  2. Apply Tourniquet: Position 3 to 4 inches above the chosen site to engorge the vein; do not leave it on for more than one minute.
  3. Palpate the Vein: Use index fingers to assess bounce and depth.
  4. Cleanse Site: Wipe with alcohol in a circular motion outward and allow to air-dry completely.
  5. Anchor and Insert: Pull skin taut below the site and insert the needle bevel-up at a 15 to 30-degree angle.
  6. Collect and Release: Attach tubes, fill in order, release the tourniquet, withdraw the needle gently, and apply pressure with dry cotton.

Troubleshooting and Complications

Difficult draws and adverse events require prompt nurse intervention. If blood does not flow, the needle may have pierced through the opposite wall, rested against a valve, or the bevel may be obstructed against the vein wall. Gently advancing, pulling back slightly, or rotating the needle can restore flow.

Common complications include hematoma formation, vasovagal syncope (fainting), thrombosis, and nerve injury. If a patient feels dizzy, immediately stop the procedure, lower their head, and provide supportive care.

Post-Procedure Care and Specimen Handling

After successful collection, invert additive tubes gently according to manufacturer guidelines to mix anticoagulants without causing hemolysis. Label all specimen tubes at the bedside in the presence of the patient, including full name, hospital number, date, and time. Instruct the patient to keep their arm straight and maintain gentle pressure on the cotton ball for a few minutes without bending the elbow, as bending increases bruising risk.
