Venepuncture

Introduction and Definition of Venepuncture

Venepuncture is the clinical technique of puncturing a vein with a hollow needle to withdraw blood for diagnostic testing, monitor therapy, or administer intravenous medications. Mastery of this skill requires precision, gentle handling of delicate tissues, and strict adherence to asepsis. In Nigerian clinical settings, it remains one of the most frequently performed invasive procedures across both rural primary health centres and bustling tertiary teaching hospitals. Every blood sample collected serves as a window into the patient's internal biochemical environment, guiding crucial medical decisions ranging from malaria parasite screening to electrolyte management.

Anatomy and Physiology of Peripheral Veins

Selecting the appropriate venepuncture site depends on a firm grasp of upper limb superficial venous anatomy. The dorsal venous network of the hand and the veins of the cubital fossa are the primary targets. - **Median Cubital Vein**: Typically the first choice in the antecubital fossa; it is large, well-anchored, and least likely to bruise or move during cannulation. - **Cephalic Vein**: Located on the lateral aspect of the antecubital fossa and forearm; tends to be well-suited for patients with larger builds, though puncturing higher up can cause more discomfort. - **Basilic Vein**: Found on the medial aspect of the antecubital fossa; runs close to the brachial artery and median nerve, demanding heightened caution during access. - **Dorsal Hand Veins**: Useful alternative sites, but veins here are smaller, more rolling, and have thinner walls, increasing the risk of collapse. Veins feature three distinct layers: a thin tunica intima, a muscular tunica media, and a sturdy tunica adventitia. Unlike arteries, veins possess valves that prevent backflow and do not pulsate.

Indications and Contraindications

Venepuncture is indicated whenever laboratory analysis of venous blood is required for screening, diagnosis, or disease monitoring. Common diagnostic requests include full blood counts, malaria parasite tests, blood cultures, urea, electrolytes, and creatinine. However, certain clinical situations contraindicate drawing blood from specific limbs or sites. Avoid cannulating an arm with an active **arteriovenous fistula** used for haemodialysis, as this can ruin the vascular access. Similarly, never perform venepuncture on an ipsilateral arm following a radical **mastectomy with lymph node dissection**, because impaired lymphatic drainage increases infection risk and can cause severe lymphoedema. Avoid areas with extensive scarring, active skin infections, burns, or haematomas.

Equipment and Materials Required

Organising a complete tray or trolley before approaching the bedside ensures procedural efficiency and minimizes patient anxiety. Essential items include: - **Non-sterile gloves**: Personal protection against bloodborne pathogens. - **Tourniquet**: Used to transiently impede venous return, engorging distal veins. - **Antiseptic wipes**: Typically 70% isopropyl alcohol swabs for skin disinfection. - **Venepuncture device**: Straight hypodermic needles, winged butterfly needles, or vacuum tube holder systems. - **Blood collection tubes**: Colour-coded evacuated tubes containing specific additives or anticoagulants. - **Gauze swabs and adhesive plaster**: For post-puncture haemostasis and site dressing. - **Sharps container**: A puncture-resistant bin for immediate safe disposal of used needles.

Infection Control and Standard Precautions

Infection prevention remains paramount during invasive procedures to protect both patients and clinical staff. Hand hygiene must be performed using soap and water or alcohol-based hand rub before and after patient contact. Gloves must be worn for all venepuncture procedures. Skin antisepsis requires applying an approved disinfectant—such as 70% alcohol or chlorhexidine—in a concentric circular motion moving from the center outward, followed by allowing adequate drying time. Blowing on the site or fan-drying with hands reintroduces microorganisms. Never recap needles after use, as this is the leading cause of accidental sharps injuries in busy Nigerian wards.

Step-by-Step Procedure

1. **Verify and Prepare**: Check the laboratory request form against patient identifiers and explain the procedure to gain cooperation. 2. **Position and Expose**: Position the patient comfortably with the arm well-supported in a downward angle. 3. **Apply Tourniquet**: Place the tourniquet 3 to 4 inches above the chosen site. Palpate to locate a bouncy, resilient vein. 4. **Cleanse Site**: Disinfect the skin using an antiseptic swab in concentric circles and let it dry. 5. **Anchor Vein**: Pull the skin taut distal to the insertion site with your non-dominant thumb to stabilise the vessel. 6. **Insert Needle**: Insert the needle bevel-up at a 15 to 30-degree angle through the skin and into the lumen of the vein. 7. **Collect Specimen**: Attach evacuated tubes sequentially, ensuring proper mixing of additives where indicated. Release the tourniquet once blood flow is established. 8. **Withdraw and Secure**: Place clean gauze lightly over the site, withdraw the needle smoothly, and apply immediate gentle pressure.

Common Complications and Troubleshooting

Even with meticulous technique, complications can arise. Haematoma formation occurs when blood leaks from the vein into surrounding tissue; if this happens, release the tourniquet, withdraw the needle immediately, and apply firm pressure. Accidental arterial puncture is recognised by pulsatile, bright red blood flow—withdraw the needle immediately and apply pressure for at least five minutes. If blood fails to flow into the tube, the needle may be resting against the vessel wall, inserted too far past the lumen, or the vein may have collapsed. Gently adjusting the depth or angle, or loosening the tourniquet, often resolves flow issues.

Post-Procedure Care and Specimen Handling

Proper post-procedure care ensures patient safety and sample integrity. Instruct the patient to keep their arm extended and apply gentle pressure with a cotton or gauze ball—remind them not to bend the elbow, as this increases bruising risk. Apply a clean plaster once bleeding stops. Label all specimen tubes accurately at the bedside immediately after collection, including the patient's full name, hospital number, date, time, and collector's initials. Never pre-label tubes before collection. Transport samples to the laboratory promptly to prevent in vitro glycolysis, haemolysis, or cellular degradation that could compromise diagnostic results.
