Principles and Rules of Drug Administration

Introduction to Medication Administration in Nursing Practice

Medication administration represents one of the most frequent yet critical daily responsibilities in clinical nursing. It bridges the gap between medical prescription and patient recovery, transforming physician orders into therapeutic outcomes. A nurse does not merely hand over pills or push plunges; they act as the final, vital safety barrier protecting the patient from pharmaceutical harm. In bustling Nigerian healthcare facilities—from primary health centers in rural communities to tertiary teaching hospitals—this responsibility requires absolute diligence. The process demands a solid grasp of pharmacokinetics, an understanding of local institutional policies, and an unwavering commitment to professional accountability. Every dose administered carries potential benefits alongside inherent risks of adverse reactions, making clinical vigilance non-negotiable.

The Fundamental Rights of Drug Administration

The standard framework of medication safety relies on established checks designed to eliminate human error. Traditionally taught as five rights, modern clinical practice has expanded these principles to ensure comprehensive protection.

  • Right Patient: Always verify identity using at least two unique identifiers, such as checking the wristband and asking the patient to state their full name, rather than relying solely on bed numbers.
  • Right Drug: Compare the medication label against the active prescription order three separate times before administration.
  • Right Dose: Perform independent calculations for pediatric or high-alert medications and have a colleague double-check complex dosages.
  • Right Time: Administer drugs within the prescribed window, usually thirty minutes before or after the scheduled hour, respecting specific pharmacokinetic needs like empty stomach requirements.
  • Right Route: Confirm the delivery method matches the order precisely; giving an oral preparation intravenously is a catastrophic, fatal error.
  • Right Documentation: Record the administration immediately after it occurs, never before.
  • Right Response: Follow up to evaluate whether the medication achieved its intended therapeutic effect or caused unexpected complications.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before any medication touches a patient's lips or enters their bloodstream, targeted clinical assessments must occur. The nurse evaluates current vital signs, laboratory results, and known allergies. For instance, administering a beta-blocker without checking an apical pulse or blood pressure can trigger severe, life-threatening hypotension or bradycardia. Patient preparation extends beyond physical checks into psychological readiness and education. Explaining what the drug is, why it has been prescribed, and what sensations to expect fosters compliance and reduces anxiety. In tropical settings, assessing hydration status before administering diuretics or evaluating baseline renal function before specific antimicrobial therapies prevents compounding systemic complications.

General Rules and Safety Protocols for Drug Handling

Safe pharmaceutical handling requires strict adherence to institutional hygiene and preparation protocols. Hands must be thoroughly washed before touching any medication package. Never crush enteric-coated tablets or open sustained-release capsules, as this destroys their specialized delivery mechanisms and leads to a dangerous, rapid surge of the drug into the bloodstream. If a medication is unlabelled or the packaging is damaged, discard it according to policy rather than guessing its contents. Multi-dose vials must be dated upon opening to track microbial contamination risks. Keep preparation areas clean, well-lit, and free from interruptions to maintain unbroken concentration during calculations and pouring.

Routes of Drug Administration and Associated Techniques

The route chosen for medication delivery dictates how fast and how completely a drug reaches systemic circulation.

RouteOnset SpeedNursing Consideration
Oral (PO)SlowestMost convenient, but contraindicated in vomiting or unconscious patients.
Sublingual / BuccalRapidMucous membranes allow direct absorption into bloodstream, bypassing hepatic first-pass metabolism.
Intramuscular (IM)ModerateRequires correct anatomical landmarking (e.g., ventrogluteal) to avoid nerve injury.
Intravenous (IV)Immediate100% bioavailability; requires strict aseptic technique and continuous monitoring for rapid adverse reactions.
Topical / TransdermalVariableClean skin thoroughly and rotate sites to prevent local irritation or erratic absorption.

Documentation and Reporting Standards

An unrecorded medication is legally considered an unadministered medication. Documentation must occur immediately after administration, capturing the exact drug name, dose, time, route, and nurse's signature. Charting a medication before giving it creates a false clinical record and invites catastrophic errors if the task gets interrupted. If a patient refuses a drug or experiences an adverse reaction, this must be detailed clearly in the nursing notes, alongside the steps taken and the physician notified. Accurate records protect the legal standing of the nurse and ensure continuity of care across different shift handovers in busy clinical units.

Prevention and Management of Medication Errors

Despite rigorous safety nets, medication errors can occur due to fatigue, illegible handwriting, similar drug names (look-alike/sound-alike), or calculation slips. When an error happens, the immediate priority shifts to patient safety: assess the patient's clinical condition, monitor vital signs, and notify the attending physician immediately. Transparency supersedes blame. Incident reports must be completed objectively, focusing on system flaws rather than punitive shaming, which only encourages a culture of hiding mistakes. Reviewing root causes helps healthcare teams redesign workflows to prevent identical errors from recurring in future clinical shifts.
