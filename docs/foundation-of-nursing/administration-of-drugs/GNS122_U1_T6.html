Intradermal Injection

Introduction and Purpose of Intradermal Injections

An intradermal (ID) injection deposits medication directly into the dermal layer of the skin, situated just beneath the epidermis. Because blood supply in the dermis is extremely limited and drug absorption occurs very slowly, this route is rarely used for delivering standard systemic doses. Instead, healthcare providers rely on intradermal injections primarily for diagnostic testing, such as tuberculin skin testing (Mantoux test for tuberculosis) and various allergy hypersensitivity tests. Local anesthesia testing prior to minor procedures also utilizes this route. A key clinical observation to keep in mind is that the slow absorption rate is precisely what allows the body to mount a localized immune response without triggering a widespread systemic reaction.

Anatomical Sites and Skin Layers

The skin consists of three main layers: the outer epidermis, the middle dermis, and the deep subcutaneous tissue. Intradermal injections target the middle layer, the dermis. Common anatomical sites chosen for this route include the inner surface of the forearm and the upper back, as these areas are relatively hairless, contain little subcutaneous fat, and allow easy visual inspection of skin reactions. The forearm is typically the preferred site for routine tuberculosis screening because nurses can easily access and observe the test area.

Equipment and Material Selection

Administering an intradermal injection requires specific, precise equipment to ensure the medication goes into the correct skin layer.

  • Syringe: A tuberculin syringe with a capacity of 1 mL, calibrated in hundredths of a milliliter, is standard.
  • Needle gauge: A fine gauge needle, typically 25 to 27 gauge, minimizes patient discomfort and tissue trauma.
  • Needle length: A short needle measuring between one-quarter and one-half inch is used.
  • Additional supplies: Alcohol swabs, clean gloves, and a puncture-resistant sharps container are required for infection control.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administration

  1. Perform hand hygiene and put on gloves: Clean your hands thoroughly and assemble your equipment to maintain asepsis.
  2. Select and clean the site: Choose a hairless area on the forearm and clean it with an alcohol swab using a circular motion outward. Allow the skin to dry completely.
  3. Position the needle: Hold the syringe with the needle bevel facing upward at a 5 to 15-degree angle to the skin.
  4. Insert the needle: Pull the skin taut with your non-dominant hand and advance the needle tip about 3 millimeters into the dermis. You should be able to see the outline of the needle beneath the skin.
  5. Inject the medication: Slowly push the plunger. A distinct bleb or small blister should form on the skin surface. If no bleb appears, the medication likely went into the subcutaneous tissue.
  6. Withdraw the needle: Pull the needle out at the same angle it entered and activate the safety device without recapping.

Reading and Interpreting Results

Reading an intradermal diagnostic test requires precision and timing. For a tuberculosis skin test, the nurse must evaluate the site 48 to 72 hours after administration. Assessment focuses on measuring the induration (the hard, raised area), not the surrounding redness or erythema. The nurse uses a specialized ruler to measure the diameter of the induration across the forearm. A positive result depends on the patient's specific health risks and clinical background rather than a single universal measurement.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although intradermal injections are generally safe, complications can occur if technique is flawed. If the needle enters too deeply into the subcutaneous fat, the medication will absorb too quickly, invalidating diagnostic tests like allergy or TB screenings. Patients may also experience local pain, bleeding, or mild bruising at the site. In rare cases, severe allergic hypersensitivity reactions can develop, so nurses must always monitor the patient for systemic signs of distress, such as shortness of breath or dizziness, and ensure emergency equipment is nearby.

Documentation and Patient Education

Accurate documentation is critical for tracking diagnostic tests. The nurse must record the medication name, exact dose, date, time, and specific anatomical site used. Additionally, the patient must be instructed not to scratch, rub, or wash off any ink marks used to outline the test site. Clear instructions on when to return for result interpretation, especially for tuberculosis screenings, prevent missed follow-up appointments and delayed medical care.
