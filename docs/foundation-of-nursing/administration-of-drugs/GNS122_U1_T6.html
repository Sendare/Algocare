Intradermal Injection

Introduction and Purpose of Intradermal Injections

An intradermal injection involves depositing medication or diagnostic substances directly into the dermal layer of the skin, situated just beneath the epidermis. Because this skin layer contains a dense network of blood vessels and nerve endings, absorption of the substance occurs very slowly, making this route ideal for local testing rather than systemic treatment. Clinical applications typically include: - **Tuberculin skin testing (Mantoux test)**: Evaluating exposure to Mycobacterium tuberculosis. - **Allergy skin testing**: Identifying specific hypersensitivities to environmental allergens or medications. - **Local anesthesia**: Desensitizing a small skin area prior to deeper procedures or venous cannulation.

Anatomical Sites and Skin Layers

Selecting the correct site is critical for accurate reading and interpretation of intradermal tests. The chosen area must be free of lesions, rashes, tattoos, hair, and excessive scarring, as these can obscure or alter the skin's local reaction. - **Volar surface of the forearm**: The traditional and most common site due to its sparse hair, light pigmentation, and high visibility for reading induration. - **Upper back**: Frequently utilized for extensive panels of allergy skin testing when multiple sites are required simultaneously. When inspecting the skin, nurses must differentiate the thin epidermis from the dermis. The dermis contains collagen and elastin fibers, which provide the resistance felt when the needle enters correctly.

Equipment and Material Selection

Administering an intradermal injection requires specialized equipment designed for precision, as volumes are exceptionally small (usually 0.1 mL to 0.5 mL). - **Syringe**: A tuberculin syringe calibrated in tenths and hundredths of a milliliter ensures accurate measurement. - **Needle gauge**: Fine-gauge needles, typically 25 to 27 gauge, minimize tissue trauma and discomfort. - **Needle length**: Short lengths ranging from one-quarter to one-half inch prevent accidental subcutaneous delivery. - **Antiseptic wipes**: Alcohol swabs for skin cleansing prior to puncture.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administration

Executing an intradermal injection demands steady hands and precise technique. 1. Perform hand hygiene and put on non-sterile gloves. 2. Cleanse the chosen skin site with an antiseptic swab using a circular motion outward, allowing it to dry completely. 3. Hold the patient's forearm and gently pull the skin taut with your non-dominant hand to create a firm surface. 4. Position the syringe with the needle bevel facing upward. 5. Insert the needle at a **5 to 15-degree angle** to the skin surface, advancing it only about 3 millimeters so the bevel is visible just beneath the epidermis. 6. Slowly inject the medication without aspirating; a distinct, pale, raised blister or **bleb** (resembling a mosquito bite) should form immediately. 7. Withdraw the needle swiftly at the same angle of insertion and do not massage or recap the needle.

Reading and Interpreting Results

Evaluating diagnostic skin tests requires careful timing and standardized measurement techniques. For tuberculin skin tests, the reaction must be assessed between **48 and 72 hours** after administration. If a patient fails to return within this window, the test is generally considered invalid, requiring a repeat procedure. Assessment focuses on measuring **induration** (hard, dense, raised tissue) rather than simple redness or bruising (erythema). Using a specialized ruler or caliper, the nurse measures the diameter of induration *transverse* to the long axis of the arm. Palpating with fingertips helps define the borders of the indurated area when visual inspection is inconclusive.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although intradermal injections are generally safe, minor complications can occur during or after administration. - **Infection**: Prevented through strict aseptic technique and skin cleansing. - **Necrosis or ulceration**: Rare, but can occur if highly irritating substances are improperly deposited or if strong hypersensitivity reactions develop. - **Systemic allergic reactions**: Rarely, patients with severe hypersensitivities may experience anaphylaxis during allergy testing; emergency equipment and epinephrine must be readily accessible. - **Failure to form a bleb**: If fluid leaks onto the skin surface or enters subcutaneous tissue, the test is invalid and must be repeated at a new site several centimeters away.

Documentation and Patient Education

Accurate documentation ensures continuity of care and provides a legal record of the procedure. The nursing record must include the specific medication or antigen administered, dosage, exact anatomical site, date and time, and the nurse's signature. Patient education is vital, particularly for diagnostic tests requiring a return visit. The patient must be instructed not to scratch, rub, or apply lotions to the injection site. Clear instructions regarding the mandatory return appointment time for reading the result prevent missed evaluations and repeated testing cycles.
