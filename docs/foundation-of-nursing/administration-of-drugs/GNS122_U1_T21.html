Dilution of Suspensions

Understanding Liquid Suspensions in Nursing Practice

A suspension is a biphasic liquid dosage form where finely divided, insoluble solid drug particles are dispersed throughout a liquid medium. Because the active ingredients do not truly dissolve, gravity inevitably causes them to separate over time, leading to caking at the bottom of the container if left undisturbed.

Recognizing this physical behavior is vital when administering medications in busy clinical environments across Nigeria, such as paediatric wards where oral liquid antibiotics are frequently prescribed. If a nurse pours a dose straight from an unmixed bottle, the initial fraction may contain virtually no active drug, while the final remaining volume delivers a dangerous, highly concentrated overdose. Thorough agitation before every single dose is therefore non-negotiable.

Indications and Rationale for Dilution

Diluting liquid suspensions is occasionally required in clinical practice, though it demands careful consideration. The primary rationale involves paediatric or neonatal dosing, where the prescribed dose volume is too small to measure accurately with standard oral syringes, or where the concentration is simply too harsh for delicate gastric mucosa.

Adding a vehicle must be done strictly according to pharmaceutical guidelines. Arbitrarily thinning a medication can destabilize the suspension system, alter the preservative efficacy, or change the palatability to the point where a child refuses the entire dose. Whenever secondary dilution is considered, pharmacists should be consulted to verify compatibility.

Mathematical Calculations and Ratios

Accurate dose calculation prevents both therapeutic failure and toxicity. When working with suspensions, orders are typically written in milligrams (mg) or micrograms (mcg), while the available stock bottle is labeled in mg per specific millilitre volume (e.g., 125 mg in 5 mL).

  • Desired Over Have Formula: $\frac{\text{Desired Dose}}{\text{Have Strength}} \times \text{Stock Volume} = \text{Volume to Administer}$
  • Proportional Analysis: Setting up ratios to solve for an unknown volume X when dealing with reconstituted powders.

Always double-check calculations with a colleague, particularly when dealing with potent antimicrobial agents or narrow therapeutic index drugs in children.

Step-by-Step Preparation and Reconstitution

Many dry powder suspensions require initial reconstitution with sterile or distilled water before any dilution or administration can take place.

  1. Tap the Bottle: Loosen the tightly packed powder at the base by firmly tapping the container against a hard surface.
  2. Add Water in Portions: Add about half the required volume of water first, cap tightly, and shake vigorously to wet all the powder without forming clumps.
  3. Complete the Volume: Add the remaining water up to the calibration mark on the label, then shake again thoroughly.
  4. Inspect: Check the bottom of the bottle to ensure no dry powder cake remains stuck at the base.

Equipment, Asepsis, and Measurement Accuracy

Using the correct measuring device is just as important as performing the right mathematical calculation. Household spoons vary wildly in capacity and must never be used in clinical or home settings for liquid medication delivery.

Measuring DeviceAccuracy LevelClinical Suitability
Household TeaspoonLow / VariableUnacceptable
Graduated Medicine CupModerateSuitable for large volumes (>10 mL)
Oral SyringeHighIdeal for infants and small doses

When measuring, view the meniscus at eye level on a flat surface. For oral suspensions, use oral syringes rather than hypodermic injection syringes to prevent accidental parenteral administration.

Safety, Storage, and Stability Guidelines

Once reconstituted, many oral suspensions have a limited shelf life and require specific environmental storage conditions to maintain potency. Antibiotic suspensions, for example, often mandate refrigeration, whereas others remain stable at room temperature.

Always check the manufacturer guidelines printed on the label for expiration parameters after reconstitution. A common clinical error is dispensing an antibiotic suspension that has exceeded its 7-to-14-day post-reconstitution window, resulting in sub-therapeutic dosing and treatment failure. Discard any unused portions past the designated expiry date.
