Dilution of Suspensions

Introduction to Suspensions in Nursing

A suspension is a liquid preparation containing solid drug particles dispersed throughout a liquid vehicle. Because these particles do not fully dissolve, they tend to settle at the bottom of the container over time.

In clinical practice, nurses most frequently handle oral suspensions for pediatric and geriatric patients who cannot swallow solid tablets. However, some suspensions are also prepared for intramuscular or subcutaneous injection. The defining characteristic of a suspension is its instability; the active drug settles rapidly. If a nurse administers a suspension without proper mixing, the patient may receive a weak dose initially, followed by a dangerous, highly concentrated dose at the bottom of the bottle.

Purpose and Indications for Diluting Suspensions

Diluting a drug suspension involves adding a compatible liquid vehicle, such as sterile water or a specific diluent, to reduce the concentration of the active ingredient or to adjust the viscosity for safe administration.

  • Ease of Swallowing: Thick suspensions can be difficult for young children or stroke patients to swallow safely.
  • Enteral Tube Patency: Viscous liquids easily clog nasogastric or gastrostomy tubes; dilution prevents tube obstruction.
  • Pediatric Dosing Accuracy: Very small volume doses are hard to measure precisely; diluting allows for a larger, safer measuring volume.
  • Reducing Local Irritation: Some concentrated liquid formulations irritate the mucosal lining of the esophagus or stomach.

Calculating Doses and Dilution Ratios

Accurate dose calculation is essential before altering any medication concentration. Nurses must verify the ordered dose against the available stock concentration using standard formulas.

$$\text{Desired Dose} \div \text{Stock Strength} \times \text{Volume} = \text{Amount to Administer}$$

When adding a diluent, calculate the final volume carefully to ensure the concentration per milliliter remains clear and easy to compute. Always double-check calculations with a colleague, especially when dealing with high-alert pediatric medications.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Diluting Suspensions

  1. Hand Hygiene: Wash hands thoroughly and put on clean gloves to prevent contamination.
  2. Inspect the Medication: Check the label, expiration date, and clarity of the diluent.
  3. Agitate the Bottle: Shake the suspension vigorously to resuspend any settled particles.
  4. Measure the Diluent: Use a calibrated oral syringe or measuring cup to draw the exact amount of approved diluent.
  5. Combine: Add the diluent to the suspension (or vice versa, depending on pharmacy instructions).
  6. Mix Thoroughly: Invert or swirl the container gently until a uniform mixture is achieved.
  7. Label: Attach a new label with the new concentration, date, time, and nurse initials if it will be stored.

Safety Precautions and Aseptic Techniques

Even though oral suspensions are not sterile products, maintaining clean techniques is vital to prevent microbial growth, especially in vulnerable populations.

Never touch the tip of the dispensing dropper or syringe to the patient's mouth or to contaminated surfaces. If preparing multidose suspensions, use single-use measuring devices when possible to avoid cross-contamination between patients. Always verify compatibility; mixing certain suspensions with incompatible juices or water can cause the active drug to precipitate or degrade rapidly.

Administration and Patient Education

Administering diluted suspensions requires patience and clear communication. For infants, place the oral syringe in the side of the mouth between the cheek and gum to prevent choking.

Instruct patients and caregivers to always shake the bottle before every single dose, not just when first opened. Explain that remaining residue in the cup can be rinsed with a small amount of water and swallowed to ensure the full dose is received. Emphasize storing the medication according to package instructions, as reconstituted suspensions often require refrigeration.

Storage, Stability, and Disposal

Once a dry powder suspension is reconstituted with a liquid diluent, its shelf life decreases dramatically. Many require refrigeration between 2°C and 8°C and must be discarded after 7 to 14 days.

  • Check Expiry: Always write the discard date clearly on the bottle label immediately upon mixing.
  • Monitor Appearance: Watch for changes in color, odor, or caking that cannot be resuspended.
  • Safe Disposal: Never pour leftover suspensions down hospital sinks if local environmental regulations prohibit it; follow facility protocols for pharmaceutical waste.
