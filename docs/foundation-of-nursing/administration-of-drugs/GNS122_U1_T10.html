Instillation of Drops

Introduction and Purpose of Medication Instillation

Medication instillation involves placing liquid drops into body cavities, most commonly the eyes, ears, and nose. This route allows for direct local treatment of infections, inflammation, and chronic conditions like glaucoma, minimizing widespread side effects across the body. Nurses must understand that even though these medications act locally, a portion can enter the bloodstream through mucous membranes, leading to systemic effects if the dose is too high.

Anatomical Considerations for Eye, Ear, and Nose Drops

Each target organ has unique anatomical features that dictate how drops must be administered:

  • Ophthalmic: The conjunctival sac is a small pocket formed by pulling down the lower eyelid. It holds only a single drop at a time; excess liquid simply spills over the cheek.
  • Otic: The ear canal contains sensitive skin and structures. For adults, pulling the pinna upward and backward straightens the canal. For children under three, pulling the pinna downward and straight back is necessary due to a shorter, flatter canal.
  • Nasal: The nasal passages lead directly into the sinuses and respiratory tract. Positioning the patient properly ensures the drops reach the intended turbinates rather than just running down the throat.

General Nursing Principles and Safety Measures

Patient safety governs every medication administration procedure. Nurses strictly follow the rights of medication administration, verifying the prescription, patient identity, and expiration dates. Cross-contamination is a major hazard; droppers must never touch eyelashes, ear canals, or nasal mucosa. If a dropper touches a contaminated surface, the entire bottle may need to be discarded to prevent spreading infection to the other eye or ear.

Equipment Preparation and Patient Assessment

Before starting the procedure, the nurse gathers the prescribed medication, clean gloves, tissues, and a cotton ball or pad. Checking the medication label against the patient chart three times prevents administration errors. Assessment involves checking the target area for redness, swelling, drainage, or pain. For ear drops, the nurse verifies that the tympanic membrane is intact, as instilling drops through a ruptured eardrum can cause severe pain and inner ear damage.

Procedure for Ophthalmic Drop Instillation

  1. Positioning: Ask the patient to tilt their head back or lie flat, looking up at the ceiling.
  2. Exposure: Gently pull down the lower eyelid with a clean tissue or gloved finger to expose the conjunctival sac.
  3. Application: Hold the dropper about one to two centimeters above the sac and drop the prescribed number of drops into the pocket.
  4. Occlusion: Ask the patient to close their eye gently and press lightly on the nasolacrimal duct for one to two minutes to stop the drug from draining into the throat and causing systemic side effects.

Procedure for Otic Drop Instillation

Otic medications should be warmed to room temperature before use, as cold drops can stimulate the inner ear and cause severe dizziness, vertigo, and nausea. The patient should lie on their side with the affected ear facing up. After pulling the pinna in the correct direction, instill the drops along the inside edge of the ear canal rather than directly onto the eardrum. Have the patient remain on their side for five minutes and gently press on the tragus to help the liquid move down.

Procedure for Nasal Drop Instillation

Positioning is critical for nasal drops; the patient should tilt their head back or lie down with a pillow under their shoulders so the drops flow into the posterior pharynx or sinuses depending on the clinical goal. Instruct the patient to breathe through their mouth during the procedure. The dropper is inserted about one centimeter into the nares, aiming away from the center nasal septum to protect delicate tissues. The patient should remain in the tilted position for a few minutes after instillation.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although medication instillation is routine, complications can occur. Local irritation, burning, or allergic reactions may manifest as sudden redness, itching, or swelling. Systemic toxicity can happen if potent drops are absorbed too quickly. If a patient experiences severe burning, vision changes, or difficulty breathing, the nurse must stop the medication immediately, wash the area if appropriate, and notify the physician.

Documentation and Patient Education

Accurate documentation in the patient chart must include the medication name, exact dose, time, and which eye, ear, or nostril received the drops, along with any patient responses. Patient education ensures safety at home. Nurses teach patients how to wash their hands, avoid touching dropper tips, maintain proper head positions, and complete the full course of treatment even if symptoms improve.
