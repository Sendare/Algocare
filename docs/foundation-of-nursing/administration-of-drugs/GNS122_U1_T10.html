Instillation of Drops

Introduction and Purpose of Medication Instillation

Medication instillation involves introducing a liquid medication into a body cavity, mucous membrane, or orifice, most commonly the eyes, ears, and nose. This route provides localized treatment for conditions like conjunctivitis, otitis externa, or allergic rhinitis, minimizing systemic side effects compared to oral or intravenous administration. In some instances, such as mydriatic drops for eye examinations, instillation achieves diagnostic purposes. Understanding the therapeutic goals ensures that the nurse evaluates the treatment's effectiveness accurately during follow-up assessments.

Anatomical Considerations for Eye, Ear, and Nose Drops

Each target organ possesses unique anatomical structures that dictate safe handling and administration techniques. - **Ophthalmic (Eye)**: The conjunctival sac is a delicate membrane that holds only a small drop of fluid; excess liquid spills over onto the face, risking systemic absorption through the nasolacrimal duct. - **Otic (Ear)**: The external auditory canal in adults curves upward and backward, requiring manual manipulation to straighten it for proper fluid descent toward the tympanic membrane. - **Nasal**: The nasal cavity communicates directly with paranasal sinuses and the nasopharynx, meaning liquid drops can easily run down into the throat if the patient's head position is incorrect.

General Nursing Principles and Safety Measures

Adhering to standard infection control and medication safety protocols prevents cross-contamination and adverse drug events. Always verify the medication order against the patient's identification band using standard safety checks. Perform hand hygiene and wear clean gloves before touching mucosal membranes or delivery devices. Never allow the dropper tip to touch any surface, including eyelashes, fingers, or the outer ear, to maintain sterility throughout the medication container's lifespan.

Equipment Preparation and Patient Assessment

Successful medication administration begins with thorough preparation and verification. Gather the prescribed medication, clean tissues or gauze, cotton-tipped applicators, and any required personal protective equipment. Assess the target area for inflammation, discharge, structural integrity, or pain. Check the medication label three times against the prescription sheet and inspect the expiration date. Ensure the medication is at room temperature unless specifically ordered otherwise, as cold drops placed in the ear can trigger severe vertigo and nausea.

Procedure for Ophthalmic (Eye) Drop Instillation

1. **Positioning**: Ask the patient to tilt their head backward or lie supine, focusing their gaze upward toward the ceiling. 2. **Exposure**: Gently pull down the lower eyelid with your non-dominant hand using a tissue or sterile gauze to expose the conjunctival sac. 3. **Instillation**: Hold the dropper 1 to 2 centimeters above the sac and release the prescribed number of drops without touching ocular tissues. 4. **Occlusion**: Release the eyelid and ask the patient to gently close their eyes. Apply gentle pressure to the nasolacrimal duct with a clean tissue for one to two minutes to prevent systemic drug absorption.

Procedure for Otic (Ear) Drop Instillation

1. **Positioning**: Have the patient sit or lie with the affected ear facing upward. 2. **Cleaning**: Remove any crusting or excessive discharge from the outer ear canal using a moist cotton-tipped applicator, ensuring you do not push debris deeper inward. 3. **Straightening**: Pull the pinna upward and backward for adults, or downward and backward for children under three years of age. 4. **Instillation**: Hold the dropper close to the ear canal opening without sealing it and instill the exact drop count along the side of the canal. 5. **Settling**: Release the pinna and gently press on the tragus a few times to help work the medication down, then have the patient remain side-lying for about five minutes.

Procedure for Nasal Drop Instillation

1. **Positioning**: Position the patient upright with the head tilted back, or supine with a small pillow placed under their shoulders to allow proper gravitational flow into the posterior nasal passages. 2. **Insertion**: Insert the dropper tip approximately one centimeter into the nares without touching the nasal septum. 3. **Instillation**: Direct the drops toward the midline of the superior concha to ensure contact with mucosal tissue. 4. **Resting**: Instruct the patient to keep their head tilted back for several minutes and advise them to breathe through their mouth to prevent sneezing or expelling the liquid.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although drop instillation is routine, complications can arise from incorrect technique or patient sensitivity. Local irritation, allergic reactions, or systemic toxicity from excessive drug absorption are notable risks. If a patient experiences burning, redness, or swelling after instillation, immediately stop the procedure, rinse the area with normal saline if appropriate, and notify the prescriber. For otic drops, ensure the tympanic membrane is intact before administration; instilling fluids through a perforated eardrum can introduce pathogens directly into the middle and inner ear, causing severe otitis media.

Documentation and Patient Education

Accurate charting completes the medication administration process. Document the medication name, dose, exact time, route, and the specific eye, ear, or nostril treated in the patient's record, along with any pre-administration assessments or adverse reactions. Patient education is equally critical for ensuring therapeutic compliance at home. Teach patients how to perform hand hygiene, check expiration dates, tilt their head correctly, and avoid contaminating dropper tips. Reinforce that they should never share multi-dose eye or ear drops with family members to prevent spreading infections.
