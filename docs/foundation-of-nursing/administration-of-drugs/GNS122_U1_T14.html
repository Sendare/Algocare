Oral Drug Administration Procedure

Introduction and Core Principles of Oral Medication

Giving medications by mouth, known as the oral route, is the most common, convenient, and usually the safest way for patients to take drugs. Nurses must understand that even though this route seems simple, it requires careful thought. Drugs given orally pass through the digestive system and the liver before entering the bloodstream, a process called first-pass metabolism. This means the liver changes some of the drug before it reaches the rest of the body.

Oral medications come in many forms, including tablets, capsules, liquids, and powders. Each form behaves differently in the body. For example, an enteric-coated tablet has a special outer layer that stops it from dissolving in the stomach acid, protecting the stomach lining or keeping the drug safe from being destroyed. Nurses must know these differences because crushing the wrong pill can cause harm or change how the drug works.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before giving any oral drug, the nurse must check the patient's condition to make sure it is safe to proceed. Start by reviewing the medication administration record (MAR) and checking for known allergies. Next, assess the patient's ability to swallow safely, known as the swallowing reflex. If a patient has a history of choking or neurological problems, giving pills can lead to aspiration, where food or liquid enters the airway.

Check vital signs if the drug requires it, such as taking a pulse rate before giving digoxin or checking blood pressure before giving antihypertensives. Position the patient sitting upright at a 90-degree angle, or as close to it as possible. This upright posture uses gravity to help the pill travel down the esophagus smoothly and lowers the risk of choking.

The Rights of Medication Administration

Following the rights of medication administration protects patients from harmful medical errors. Nurses must check these basic rules every single time they prepare and give drugs:

  • Right Patient: Use at least two identifiers, like checking the hospital wristband and asking the patient's name.
  • Right Medication: Compare the label on the drug package with the MAR three separate times.
  • Right Dose: Double-check math calculations and measure liquid doses using a proper measuring device.
  • Right Time: Give the drug within the prescribed window, usually 30 to 60 minutes before or after the scheduled hour.
  • Right Route: Confirm the drug is ordered to be swallowed, not given another way.
  • Right Documentation: Record the medication immediately after giving it, never before.
  • Right Reason: Ensure the drug matches the patient's current diagnosis and clinical needs.

Equipment and Medication Dispensing Techniques

Using the right tools keeps medication preparation clean and accurate. When dispensing solid pills, tap tablets or capsules directly from the container into the bottle cap first, then slide them into a small paper medication cup. Touching pills with bare hands introduces bacteria and violates basic infection control rules.

For liquid medications, use a calibrated oral syringe or a clear plastic measuring cup designed for medicine. Never use ordinary household kitchen spoons, as their sizes vary wildly and lead to inaccurate dosing. When reading a liquid dose in a cup, place the cup on a flat surface and look at the fluid level at eye level, reading the bottom of the curved liquid line, known as the meniscus.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Oral Medications

  1. Perform hand hygiene: Wash hands thoroughly or use alcohol rub before touching any supplies.
  2. Gather supplies: Bring the MAR and medications to the preparation area, checking each drug against the sheet.
  3. Prepare drugs: Pour pills into cups or measure liquids carefully without touching the items directly.
  4. Identify the patient: Enter the room and verify identity using two unique identifiers, such as checking the wristband and asking for their birth date.
  5. Explain the procedure: Tell the patient what medication they are taking and what it is for, answering any questions.
  6. Administer the drug: Hand the pills to the patient or assist them with the water cup. Stay close until every pill is swallowed.
  7. Document: Record the time, dose, and drug name immediately on the chart.

Special Considerations for Diverse Patient Populations

Different age groups and clinical conditions require changes to how oral drugs are given. Pediatric patients often need liquid formulations because they cannot swallow large pills. If crushing is necessary, nurses must check with a pharmacist to ensure the drug is safe to alter. Mixing crushed medicine in a small amount of food like applesauce helps, but avoid mixing it in staple foods like milk or formula, as the child might refuse that food later.

Older adults may have physiological changes, such as decreased saliva production or slower stomach emptying, making swallowing difficult. Conditions like stroke or dementia also increase the risk of aspiration. For these patients, checking the mouth after administration ensures no pills are trapped in the cheek pockets, a habit known as pouching.

Post-Administration Monitoring, Evaluation, and Documentation

The medication process does not end when the patient swallows the pill. Nurses must return later to evaluate if the drug worked as intended. For example, if a patient took a pain reliever, the nurse should check back in thirty to forty-five minutes to see if their pain level dropped.

Watch closely for any unexpected side effects or adverse drug reactions, such as rashes, nausea, or dizziness. If an error happens—like giving the wrong dose or missing a time slot—report it immediately to the charge nurse and the doctor, and fill out an incident report. Complete, honest documentation keeps the healthcare team informed and protects the patient.
