Initiating Intravenous Therapy

Introduction to Intravenous Therapy and Indications

Intravenous (IV) therapy involves delivering fluids, medications, blood products, or nutrients directly into a patient's venous system. This route provides immediate systemic bioavailability, making it the preferred method in emergency situations when rapid onset of action is critical. Unlike oral medications, which must pass through the digestive tract and the liver before reaching systemic circulation, IV administration bypasses first-pass metabolism entirely.

Common clinical indications for initiating IV access include:

  • Fluid replacement: Restoring intravascular volume in dehydration, burns, or hemorrhage.
  • Electrolyte correction: Administering specific ions like potassium or sodium when oral correction is too slow or impossible.
  • Medication administration: Giving drugs that are irritating to muscle tissue or poorly absorbed via the gastrointestinal tract.
  • Blood component therapy: Transfusing packed red blood cells, platelets, or plasma.
  • Nutritional support: Delivering total parenteral nutrition (TPN) when the gastrointestinal tract is non-functional.

Anatomy and Physiology of Peripheral Veins

Selecting an appropriate vein requires a solid understanding of upper extremity vascular anatomy. Peripheral IV cannulation is most commonly performed on the veins of the hand and forearm. The ideal vein should feel bouncy, straight, and refill quickly when released after palpation.

The primary veins used for peripheral cannulation include:

  • Metacarpal veins: Located on the back of the hand; easily visible but have thin walls and roll easily.
  • Cephalic vein: Runs up the lateral side of the forearm and arm; large and sturdy, excellent for most infusions.
  • Basilic vein: Runs up the medial side of the forearm; large caliber, but close to nerves and the brachial artery.
  • Median cubital vein: Located in the antecubital fossa; large and easy to cannulate, but typically reserved for blood draws or short-term emergency access because bending the elbow can occlude or kink the catheter.

Vein walls consist of three layers: the tunica intima (inner smooth lining), tunica media (middle muscular and elastic layer), and tunica adventitia (outer supportive connective tissue). Protecting the tunica intima during cannulation is vital to prevent inflammation and clot formation.

Equipment, Supplies, and Infusion Solutions

Gathering the correct supplies before entering the patient's room ensures a smooth, efficient cannulation process and minimizes patient discomfort. Standard equipment includes non-sterile gloves, a tourniquet, skin antiseptic (such as chlorhexidine or alcohol), an over-the-needle catheter (cannula), transparent dressing, tape, an extension set or gravity tubing, and a saline flush.

Intravenous solutions are categorized by their tonicity relative to blood plasma:

  • Isotonic solutions: Examples include Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl) and Lactated Ringer's. They have the same osmolarity as blood, causing no fluid shift into or out of cells, and are used for volume expansion.
  • Hypotonic solutions: Examples include 0.45% NaCl. They have a lower osmolarity than serum, causing fluid to shift out of blood vessels and into cells. They are used for cellular dehydration.
  • Hypertonic solutions: Examples include 3% NaCl or D5W in Normal Saline. They have a higher osmolarity, drawing fluid out of cells and into the vascular space to reduce cellular swelling.

Patient Assessment and Preparation

Preparing the patient physically and psychologically reduces anxiety and improves cannulation success. Begin by verifying the medical order and checking the patient's identity using two unique identifiers. Assess the patient's medical history, including allergies to latex, adhesives, or antiseptics, and check for past surgeries or conditions affecting the upper limbs, such as a prior mastectomy or dialysis fistula.

Examine both arms to select the most appropriate site. Start with the most distal veins on the non-dominant hand or forearm, preserving proximal sites for future use if complications arise. Avoid sites that show signs of infection, bruising, phlebitis, or areas near recent surgical incisions. Explain the procedure simply to the patient, encouraging them to report any sharp pain or burning sensations immediately.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Cannulation

Successful cannulation requires sterile technique, steady hand-eye coordination, and patience. Follow these standard procedural steps:

  1. Hand hygiene and PPE: Perform hand hygiene and put on non-sterile gloves.
  2. Apply tourniquet: Place the tourniquet 3 to 4 inches above the chosen insertion site to engorge the vein.
  3. Cleanse skin: Scrub the site vigorously with chlorhexidine or alcohol for 30 seconds and let it air-dry completely.
  4. Anchor the vein: Pull the skin taut below the insertion site with your non-dominant hand to prevent the vein from rolling.
  5. Insert needle: Hold the catheter at a 15- to 30-degree angle with the bevel facing up and pierce the skin and vein wall.
  6. Check for flashback: Look for a flash of blood in the chamber, indicating successful entry into the lumen.
  7. Advance catheter: Lower the angle nearly parallel to the skin and advance the plastic catheter slightly further into the vein while sliding it off the needle.
  8. Release tourniquet and secure: Release the tourniquet, apply gentle pressure above the insertion site to stop blood flow, remove the needle safely into a sharps container, and attach the extension set or IV tubing.

Calculating and Regulating Flow Rates

Ensuring the patient receives the correct volume of fluid over the prescribed time requires accurate calculation of infusion rates. When using gravity sets, nurses calculate the flow rate in drops per minute (gtt/min) using the drop factor of the tubing, which is printed on the package (e.g., 10, 15, or 20 gtt/mL for regular sets, or 60 gtt/mL for microdrip sets).

The standard formula for gravity infusion is: $$\text{Flow Rate (gtt/min)} = \frac{\text{Total Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Time in Minutes}}$$

When using an electronic infusion pump, calculations are straightforward because the pump programs directly in milliliters per hour (mL/hr). Nurses must routinely check gravity drips to ensure the rate has not drifted due to patient movement or changes in arm position.

Complications of Intravenous Therapy and Prevention

Intravenous therapy carries potential local and systemic complications that require vigilant monitoring. Early detection prevents minor irritation from escalating into severe tissue damage or bloodstream infection.

Common complications include:

  • Infiltration: The IV fluid enters the surrounding subcutaneous tissue instead of the vein. Signs include cool skin, swelling, and discomfort around the site. Stop the infusion and remove the catheter immediately.
  • Extravasation: Similar to infiltration, but involves a vesicant medication that causes blistering and tissue necrosis. Stop the infusion immediately and follow facility protocols for specific antidote administration.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall caused by mechanical, chemical, or bacterial irritation. Signs include redness, warmth, swelling, and a palpable cord along the vein.
  • Fluid Overload: Excess fluid volume in the circulatory system, leading to hypertension, shortness of breath, and crackles in the lungs.
  • Air Embolism: Air entering the circulatory system through the tubing, causing chest pain and respiratory distress. Keep the patient on their left side in Trendelenburg position and notify the provider.

Maintenance, Site Care, and Discontinuation

Maintaining an IV line involves regular assessment, dressing changes, and site care to prevent infection and maintain patency. Inspect the insertion site through the transparent dressing during every shift assessment. Change the transparent dressing if it becomes loose, soiled, or damp. Flush intermittent extension sets with normal saline according to facility policy to maintain patency and prevent blood clotting inside the catheter lumen.

When the IV therapy is complete or if a complication develops, discontinue the catheter promptly:

  1. Perform hand hygiene and put on gloves.
  2. Turn off the infusion pump or clamp the gravity tubing.
  3. Gently remove the transparent dressing and tape while holding the catheter steady.
  4. Place sterile gauze or a cotton ball lightly over the insertion site without applying pressure yet.
  5. Withdraw the catheter smoothly along the angle of insertion.
  6. Apply firm, direct pressure to the site with gauze for 2 to 3 minutes until bleeding stops, then apply a clean bandage.
