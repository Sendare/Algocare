Initiating Intravenous Therapy

Introduction to Intravenous Therapy and Indications

Intravenous therapy involves the direct administration of fluids, electrolytes, blood products, or medications into a patient's venous circulation. This route bypasses the gastrointestinal tract and hepatic metabolism, making it the fastest method to achieve therapeutic blood concentrations. In acute clinical settings across Nigeria, such as emergency rooms and busy medical wards, IV access is a vital cornerstone of patient management.

Common clinical indications include:

  • Fluid and electrolyte resuscitation: Correcting severe dehydration from gastroenteritis or acute blood loss.
  • Medication administration: Delivering antibiotics, analgesics, or emergency vasopressors when rapid onset is required.
  • Nutritional support: Administering total parenteral nutrition for patients unable to tolerate oral intake.
  • Blood transfusion: Restoring oxygen-carrying capacity in severe anemia or hemorrhage.

Anatomy and Physiology of Peripheral Veins

Successful cannulation requires a firm grasp of upper extremity venous anatomy. Peripheral veins are superficial, located in the hypodermis between the skin and deep fascia. They feature thin, compliant walls and contain bicuspid valves that prevent backflow and assist venous return against gravity.

The most commonly utilized veins for peripheral cannulation in adults include:

  1. Metacarpal veins: Found on the dorsal surface of the hand; easily visible but have small diameters and are prone to movement.
  2. Cephalic vein: Runs up the lateral aspect of the arm; provides a straight, robust pathway suitable for larger catheters.
  3. Basilic vein: Traveled along the medial side of the arm; larger in caliber but lies close to major nerves and arteries, requiring careful palpation.
  4. Median cubital vein: Located in the antecubital fossa; wide and stable, making it the preferred site for rapid fluid boluses and emergency access, though flexion of the elbow can kink catheters placed here.

Equipment, Supplies, and Infusion Solutions

Gathering appropriate supplies before starting a procedure prevents breaks in asepsis and reduces patient anxiety. Standard equipment includes an intravenous cannula (over-the-needle catheter), tourniquet, skin antiseptic (such as chlorhexidine or alcohol swabs), transparent dressing, extension set or infusion tubing, and normal saline flush.

Infusion solutions are categorized tonicity relative to human plasma:

  • Isotonic solutions: Examples include 0.9% Normal Saline and Hartmann's solution (Ringer's Lactate). They expand extracellular fluid volume without causing fluid shifts into or out of cells.
  • Hypotonic solutions: Examples include 0.45% Sodium Chloride. They shift fluid out of blood vessels and into cells, used primarily for cellular hydration.
  • Hypertonic solutions: Examples include 10% Dextrose in Water. They draw fluid out of intracellular spaces and into the vascular compartment, requiring careful monitoring to prevent circulatory overload.

Patient Assessment and Preparation

Before attempting cannulation, a thorough clinical evaluation ensures patient safety and selects the optimal insertion site. Review the medical record for drug allergies, ordered infusion rates, and specific clinical limitations such as a history of mastectomy, lymphedema, or a contralateral dialysis fistula.

Communicate the procedure clearly to the patient, explaining the temporary sting of the needle and the expected duration of treatment to secure their cooperation. Inspect both arms carefully, choosing the most distal viable site first to preserve proximal venous real estate for future access. Avoid sites with signs of infection, phlebitis, bruising, or compromised circulation.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Cannulation

Performing safe cannulation requires precise manual dexterity and strict adherence to aseptic technique.

  1. Preparation: Position the patient comfortably, apply the tourniquet 10 to 15 cm above the intended site, and palpate for a bouncy, unobstructed vein.
  2. Antisepsis: Clean the site with an antiseptic swab using a friction scrub and allow it to air dry completely to ensure maximum antimicrobial action.
  3. Insertion: Anchor the skin gently below the site, hold the cannula bevel up at a 15 to 30-degree angle, and pierce the skin and vein wall.
  4. Confirmation: Observe for flashback of dark red blood in the chamber, lower the catheter almost parallel to the skin, and advance it slightly further.
  5. Advancement and Securing: Slide the plastic catheter off the needle into the lumen while withdrawing the needle. Release the tourniquet, connect the extension set or IV tubing, flush with saline, and secure the device with a transparent dressing.

Calculating and Regulating Flow Rates

Ensuring a patient receives the precise volume of prescribed fluid requires accurate mathematical calculation and mechanical regulation. Infusions can be controlled manually using gravity tubing with a roller clamp or electronically via an infusion pump.

To calculate gravity drop rates, the nurse uses the drop factor of the administration set, measured in drops per milliliter (gtt/mL). Standard sets include macro-drip sets (typically 10, 15, or 20 gtt/mL) and micro-drip sets (60 gtt/mL), which are utilized for pediatric patients or fluid restriction.

$$\text{Flow Rate (gtt/min)} = \frac{\text{Total Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Time in Minutes}}$$

Complications of Intravenous Therapy and Prevention

Intravenous therapy carries potential local and systemic risks that demand vigilant nursing surveillance. Prompt identification minimizes patient discomfort and prevents severe morbidity.

  • Infiltration: Occurs when the catheter dislodges, allowing fluid to enter surrounding subcutaneous tissue. Manifests as swelling, coolness, and pallor around the site.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall caused by chemical, mechanical, or bacterial irritation. Characterized by pain, erythema, warmth, and a palpable cord along the vein.
  • Fluid Overload: Results from excessive infusion volume, leading to elevated central venous pressure, dyspnea, crackles in lung bases, and peripheral edema.
  • Air Embolism: A rare but life-threatening complication where air enters the vascular system, causing sudden respiratory distress, cyanosis, and hypotension.

Maintenance, Site Care, and Discontinuation

Maintaining an intravenous site requires routine assessment and adherence to institutional infection control protocols. Dressings must remain clean, dry, and intact; any dressing that lifts or becomes soiled requires immediate sterile replacement. Assess the insertion site every shift for early signs of complications.

When therapy is no longer required or a complication arises, discontinue the catheter promptly. Perform hand hygiene, put on gloves, turn off the infusion clamp, and gently remove the tape and transparent dressing. Withdraw the cannula smoothly along the axis of insertion, apply direct pressure with a clean gauze pad for several minutes until hemostasis is achieved, and apply a sterile adhesive bandage.
