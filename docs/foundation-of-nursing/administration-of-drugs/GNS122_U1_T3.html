Intramuscular Injection (IM)

Introduction and Principles of Intramuscular Injections

Intramuscular injection involves delivering medication directly into the deep muscle tissue, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract and superficial skin layers for rapid systemic absorption. Muscle tissue possesses a rich vascular network, allowing medications to enter the bloodstream faster than subcutaneous routes. This route is typically chosen when rapid onset is required, when drugs irritate subcutaneous tissue, or when a patient cannot tolerate oral medications. Therapeutic efficacy relies heavily on selecting a muscle mass large enough to absorb the volume safely without damaging underlying nerves, blood vessels, or bone.

Anatomical Landmarks and Site Selection

Choosing the correct injection site prevents severe nerve and vascular injury. Four primary sites are standard in clinical practice:

  • Ventrogluteal: Considered the safest site because it lacks major nerves and blood vessels, overlying the gluteus medius.
  • Vastus Lateralis: Located on the anterior-lateral thigh, making it the preferred and most accessible site for infants and toddlers.
  • Deltoid: Located on the lateral upper arm; limited to small volume injections (usually 1 mL or less) due to its small muscle mass.
  • Dorsogluteal: Historically popular but largely discouraged due to its close proximity to the sciatic nerve and superior gluteal artery.

Needle Selection and Equipment Parameters

Successful delivery depends on matching equipment specifications to patient physical characteristics and medication properties. Using a needle that is too short deposits medication into subcutaneous fat instead of muscle, while an excessively long needle risks hitting bone.

Patient Positioning and Psychological Preparation

Proper positioning relaxes the target muscle and minimizes patient anxiety. Tense muscle fibers make needle insertion painful and impede medication absorption. For ventrogluteal injections, positioning the patient in a lateral recumbent position with the upper leg flexed breaks muscle tension. Vastus lateralis injections work well when the patient sits or lies flat with the knee slightly bent. Clear communication regarding what the patient will feel—such as a sharp poke and a pressure sensation—builds trust and cooperation throughout the procedure.

The Z-Track Technique

The Z-track method is an injection technique used to prevent tracking of medication into the subcutaneous tissue, reducing skin staining and localized irritation from irritating drugs like iron dextran. To perform this technique, pull the skin and subcutaneous tissue laterally about 2.5 to 3.5 centimeters away from the injection site using the non-dominant hand. Hold the skin taut, insert the needle at a 90-degree angle, aspirate if required by institutional policy, and inject the medication slowly. Wait ten seconds before smoothly withdrawing the needle, then release the displaced skin. This maneuver creates a zigzag path that seals the needle track shut as tissues return to their natural alignment.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

  1. Hand Hygiene and Asepsis: Perform hand hygiene and apply non-sterile gloves. Cleanse the selected site with an antiseptic swab in a circular motion, moving from the center outward.
  2. Needle Insertion: Remove the needle cap, pull the skin taut (or use Z-track), and pierce the skin quickly and firmly at a 90-degree angle.
  3. Medication Delivery: Support the syringe barrel with your dominant hand and depress the plunger slowly—allow roughly 10 seconds per milliliter of fluid to prevent tissue tearing.
  4. Withdrawal: Withdraw the needle smoothly along the exact insertion angle, then activate the safety device immediately without recapping the dirty needle.

Complications and Safety Measures

Although routine, intramuscular injections carry risks if protocols are missed. Nerve injury results from poor site selection, leading to chronic pain, numbness, or paralysis if the sciatic nerve is struck. Abscess formation or sterile granulomas occur due to poor aseptic technique or irritating chemical properties of certain formulations. Accidental intravenous injection poses a severe risk of rapid, toxic drug reactions. To maintain safety, always verify patient identity using two identifiers, inspect the skin for prior lesions, and never recap needles after use to prevent accidental sharps injuries.

Documentation and Post-Injection Assessment

Post-procedure care involves monitoring the patient and accurately recording the intervention. Observe the patient for 15 to 30 minutes following administration, particularly with first-time doses, to watch for acute allergic reactions or anaphylaxis. Document the procedure immediately in the patient record, noting the exact medication name, dose, route, specific anatomical site used, time of administration, and patient tolerance. Clear documentation provides a legal record and ensures safe continuity of care among multidisciplinary healthcare team members.
