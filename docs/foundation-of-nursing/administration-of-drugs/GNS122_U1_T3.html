Intramuscular Injection (IM)

Introduction and Purpose of Intramuscular Injections

An intramuscular (IM) injection delivers medication directly into the deep muscle tissue. Muscle tissue has a rich blood supply, which allows the body to absorb drugs much faster than through the subcutaneous layer. Nurses use this route when a patient cannot take medications by mouth, or when a drug would irritate or destroy the stomach lining. Because the needle goes deep into living tissue, choosing the right equipment and technique is vital to protect nerves, blood vessels, and bone.

Anatomical Landmarks and Safe Sites

Selecting a safe injection site prevents permanent nerve damage and accidental puncture of major blood vessels.

  • Ventrogluteal: The safest and preferred site for adults. It is free of major nerves and blood vessels, located by placing the palm on the greater trochanter and the index finger on the anterior superior iliac spine.
  • Vastus Lateralis: Located on the outer thigh. This site is thick and well-developed, making it the primary choice for infants and toddlers.
  • Deltoid: Located on the upper arm near the acromion process. It has a small muscle mass, so nurses only use it for small-volume injections, usually under one milliliter.
  • Dorsogluteal: Located in the upper outer quadrant of the buttock. This site is rarely used today because it sits too close to the sciatic nerve and superior gluteal artery.

Equipment Selection and Dosage Safety

Using the correct syringe size, needle length, and gauge ensures the medication reaches the muscle layer without causing unnecessary trauma. Needle gauges range from 18 to 27, where a higher number means a thinner needle. Viscous or thick oily medications require a wider gauge, such as 20 or 21, to flow smoothly.

Needle length depends on the patient's body size and the chosen site, typically ranging from one to one-and-a-half inches for adults. For most IM injections, the maximum volume for an adult single site is three milliliters; anything larger should be split into two separate injections.

Patient Preparation and Positioning

Proper positioning relaxes the target muscle and reduces anxiety. A tense muscle makes needle insertion difficult and increases pain. For the ventrogluteal site, place the patient on their side with the top leg flexed. For the vastus lateralis, have the patient sit or lie flat with the leg relaxed. Provide privacy and explain each step before touching the patient. Assess the skin over the site for bruising, inflammation, lumps, or scars, and never inject into compromised tissue.

The Z-Track Technique

The Z-track technique is a method used during IM injections to prevent irritating medication from leaking back into the subcutaneous tissue and skin.

To perform this technique, pull the skin and subcutaneous tissue laterally about one to one-and-a-half inches away from the injection site using the non-dominant hand. Hold the skin taut, insert the needle at a ninety-degree angle, inject the medication slowly, wait ten seconds, and withdraw the needle before releasing the skin. The tissue snaps back, sealing the needle track like a zig-zag path.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Perform hand hygiene and put on clean gloves. Clean the injection site with an alcohol swab using a firm circular motion outward from the center, and let it dry completely. A wet alcohol strip stings when the needle enters.

Dart the needle into the muscle quickly at a ninety-degree angle. Support the syringe barrel, aspirate if required by institutional policy, and push the plunger slowly—about one milliliter every ten seconds. Leave the needle in place for ten seconds after injection to let the drug disperse into the muscle fibers, then withdraw smoothly along the insertion angle.

Potential Complications and Safety Measures

Complications can arise if proper technique is neglected. Hitting the sciatic nerve causes immediate burning pain down the leg, requiring immediate needle withdrawal. Accidental intravascular injection means the drug enters a blood vessel instead of muscle; always follow current clinical guidelines regarding aspiration.

Needlestick injuries are a major hazard to healthcare workers. Never recap used needles with two hands. Engage safety devices immediately after withdrawal and dispose of the syringe directly into a rigid sharps container.

Documentation and Post-Injection Assessment

Accurate documentation protects the patient and provides a legal record of care. Record the medication name, exact dose, route, time given, and specific anatomical site used. Note the patient's tolerance of the procedure and any immediate adverse reactions. Return to check the injection site within fifteen to thirty minutes to monitor for local reactions, swelling, or bleeding, especially when administering vaccines or new medications.
