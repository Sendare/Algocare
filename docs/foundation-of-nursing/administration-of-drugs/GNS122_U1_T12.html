Eye Irrigation

Introduction and Purpose of Eye Irrigation

Eye irrigation is a fundamental emergency and routine ophthalmic procedure designed to flush the conjunctival sac continuously. The primary goal is the mechanical removal of chemical splashes, foreign bodies, inflammatory exudates, or irritating particulate matter that could otherwise cause severe corneal ulceration or chemical burns. Speed is often the absolute determinant of visual preservation when dealing with alkali or acid exposures. By introducing a gentle, controlled stream of fluid across the ocular surface, the pH of the conjunctiva and cornea can be restored to normal physiological levels before deep tissue penetration occurs.

Indications and Contraindications

Clinical situations requiring ocular flushing demand quick triage. Clear indications include: - **Chemical splashes**: Acids, alkalis, or industrial solvents contacting the ocular surface. - **Foreign bodies**: Sand, dust, metal shavings, or glass fragments trapped in the conjunctival fornices. - **Mucopurulent discharge**: Dense exudates in severe infections that do not respond to simple wiping. - **Pre-surgical cleansing**: Preparing the operative field prior to minor ophthalmic procedures. Conversely, certain presentations call for a halt to routine irrigation. **Penetrating globe injuries** or open globes are absolute contraindications; introducing fluid pressure through a compromised sclera or cornea can force intraocular contents outward, leading to permanent vision loss. In these scenarios, a protective rigid shield is applied immediately without flushing.

Equipment and Solution Selection

Selecting the correct fluids and apparatus ensures both patient safety and effective decontamination. Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl) or Lactated Ringer's solution are the standard choices because they are isotonic to the tear film, preventing osmotic shifts across the corneal epithelium. Essential equipment includes: - Sterile irrigation solution warmed to room temperature. - Sterile IV tubing and bag, or a specialized eye irrigation lens (such as a Morgan lens). - Kidney basin or emesis basin to catch fluid runoff. - Waterproof drapes and towels to keep the patient dry. - Personal protective equipment including gloves and goggles.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before beginning the flush, a rapid visual acuity assessment should be performed if the chemical exposure allows, establishing a baseline record. Assess the degree of eyelid swelling, check for embedded particulate matter under the upper lid via eversion, and evaluate the conjunctiva for pallor or injection. Explain the procedural steps clearly to the patient, as anxiety runs high during eye emergencies. Position the patient comfortably, ideally reclined or sitting with the head tilted slightly toward the affected side. This positioning ensures that contaminated runoff flows away from the uninjured eye, preventing cross-contamination.

Step-by-Step Procedure and Execution

1. **Perform hand hygiene** and put on clean gloves and protective face gear. 2. **Position the patient** with a waterproof drape and hold the emesis basin snugly against the cheek on the affected side. 3. **Anesthetize the eye** with prescribed topical anesthetic drops if ordered, to relieve severe blepharospasm and pain. 4. **Expose the conjunctival sac** by gently retracting the lower lid downward with one gloved thumb while stabilizing the brow. 5. **Direct the flow** from the inner canthus toward the outer canthus, holding the nozzle or tubing approximately 2 to 3 inches away from the eye. 6. **Instruct the patient** to blink periodically to help wash out debris trapped behind the eyelids. 7. **Continue flushing** for the prescribed duration—often 15 to 30 minutes for mild debris, or up to several hours continuously for severe chemical burns until neutral pH is verified.

Post-Procedure Care and Evaluation

Once irrigation is complete, dry the surrounding facial skin gently with a clean towel. Recheck visual acuity and inspect the conjunctival tissues for residual debris or ongoing irritation. If a chemical injury occurred, perform pH test strips on the residual tear film to verify that the pH has returned to the normal range of approximately 7.0 to 7.3. Provide comfort measures, apply prescribed antibiotic ointments or patches if ordered, and document the total volume of fluid used, solution type, duration, and patient tolerance in the clinical record.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although eye irrigation is generally safe, adverse events can occur, particularly during emergency or prolonged flushing. Corneal abrasion can happen if the irrigation nozzle touches the eyeball directly or if particulate matter is dragged across the epithelium forcefully. Fluid overload or corneal waterlogging (edema) can result from excessively prolonged exposure to hypotonic fluids. If the patient reports increasing severe pain, sudden vision loss, or worsening redness after irrigation, stop the procedure immediately, inspect for trauma, and notify the attending physician or ophthalmologist for prompt re-evaluation.
