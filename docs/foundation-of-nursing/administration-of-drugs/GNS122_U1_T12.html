Eye Irrigation

Introduction and Purpose of Eye Irrigation

Eye irrigation is an urgent or routine nursing procedure designed to flush the conjunctival sac with a continuous stream of fluid. The primary purpose is to remove foreign bodies, chemical splashes, irritants, or excessive discharge that could potentially damage delicate ocular structures. Quick and effective washing can prevent permanent vision loss, especially when hazardous chemicals make contact with the cornea. Nurses frequently perform this procedure in emergency departments and occupational health settings. Time is the most critical factor during chemical exposures; delaying irrigation to search for specific neutralizing agents worsens tissue injury. Therefore, plain water or normal saline must be applied immediately to dilute and remove the offending substance.

Indications and Contraindications

Knowing when to wash the eye and when to hold back protects the patient from further harm.

  • Indications: Chemical splashes from acids or alkalis, loose particulate matter like dust or metal shavings, thick purulent discharge, and exposure to biological or noxious fumes.
  • Contraindications: Penetrating globe injuries, severe lacerations of the eyeball, and suspected rupture of the globe.

A common mistake is applying pressure or fluid when a sharp object has pierced the eyeball. Forcing fluid into a ruptured eye pushes contaminants inside the globe and forces out internal eye structures.

Equipment and Solution Selection

Using the correct supplies ensures a safe and efficient wash. Normal saline is the gold standard solution because it is isotonic to eye tissues, meaning it will not cause fluid shifts across the corneal cells. If normal saline is unavailable, sterile water serves as an acceptable alternative during emergency chemical decontamination.

Essential equipment includes:

  • Sterile basin or kidney dish to catch runoff fluid
  • Waterproof drape to keep the patient's clothing dry
  • Irrigation tubing, a Morgan lens, or a sterile syringe
  • pH indicator paper for chemical burn cases
  • Personal protective equipment such as gloves and goggles

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before starting the flow of fluid, the nurse must quickly assess the eye and prepare the patient. Check visual acuity if the patient is stable enough. Inspect the eyelids, eyelashes, and surrounding skin for trauma or embedded objects. If chemical exposure occurred, test the tear pH using indicator paper to establish a baseline.

Explain every step of the procedure clearly, even if the patient is panicked. Reassurance helps lower anxiety and reduces involuntary squeezing of the eyelids, which makes opening the eye difficult. Position the patient comfortably with their head tilted slightly toward the affected side so runoff flows away from the unaffected eye, preventing cross-contamination.

Step-by-Step Procedure

Executing the irrigation correctly guarantees thorough cleaning without injuring the eye.

  1. Perform hand hygiene and put on clean gloves and protective eyewear.
  2. Position the patient and place a waterproof towel and an emesis basin beneath the affected eye.
  3. Gently separate the eyelids using your thumb and index finger, resting your hand on the cheekbone never on the eyeball itself.
  4. Hold the irrigation nozzle or tubing about one inch away from the eye.
  5. Direct the stream of fluid starting at the inner canthus (near the nose) and let it flow outward toward the outer canthus.
  6. Encourage the patient to blink periodically to help wash out debris trapped behind the eyelids.
  7. Continue irrigation for the prescribed duration, which may range from fifteen minutes for mild debris to hours for severe alkali burns.

Post-Procedure Care and Evaluation

Once the irrigation is complete, dry the skin around the eye gently with clean gauze or a towel. Remove used equipment, discard disposables properly, and perform hand hygiene. Re-assess the patient's visual acuity and examine the conjunctiva and cornea for lingering irritation or damage. If a chemical burn was treated, recheck the tear pH to ensure it has returned to normal neutral levels. Document the procedure thoroughly in the nursing notes, recording the solution used, the volume or duration of the wash, patient tolerance, and any changes in eye appearance or vision.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although eye irrigation is generally safe, complications can occur if technique is poor or if the injury is severe. Potential risks include corneal abrasion from abrasive debris or rough equipment nozzles, secondary infection, and tissue edema from prolonged fluid exposure. If the patient complains of worsening eye pain, blurry vision, or increased redness after irrigation, stop the procedure immediately and notify the healthcare provider. For persistent chemical burns, ophthalmology consultation is mandatory because deeper layers of the eye may require specialized surgical or medical management.
