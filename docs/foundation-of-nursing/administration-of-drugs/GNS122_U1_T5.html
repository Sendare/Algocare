Rectal Route of Drug Administration

Introduction and Definition of the Rectal Route

The rectal route of drug administration involves inserting a medication into the rectum, where it dissolves or melts and is absorbed through the rectal mucosa. This route serves as an alternative when oral administration is impossible due to severe nausea, vomiting, or impaired swallowing reflexes. The rectum possesses a rich vascular network, allowing drugs to exert either local effects—such as relieving constipation or hemorrhoids—or systemic effects when absorption into the bloodstream is desired.

Indications and Contraindications

Clinical situations requiring rectal medication include active seizures where oral intake is unsafe, pediatric palliative care, and localized bowel conditions. Certain clinical findings completely bar this route due to high risk of injury or systemic complications.

  • Active Rectal Bleeding: Direct trauma or irritation from a suppository can worsen hemorrhage.
  • Recent Rectal or Bowel Surgery: Insertion disrupts healing surgical anastomoses and suture lines.
  • Severe Diarrhea: The bowel contents expel the medication before systemic or local absorption occurs.
  • Intestinal Obstruction: Pressure and lack of motility prevent proper drug distribution.

Types of Rectal Dosage Forms

Two primary formulations dominate rectal therapy: suppositories and enemas.

Suppositories are solid, bullet-shaped medications formulated with a base—such as hydrogenated vegetable oils or polyethylene glycol—that melts at body temperature. They must be stored correctly, often in a refrigerator, to prevent premature softening.

Enemas are liquid preparations instilled into the rectum and colon. They function either as retention enemas, where the patient holds the liquid for a specified time to allow systemic or local drug action, or evacuation enemas, which distend the bowel and stimulate rapid peristalsis to clear stool.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Preparation

Before touching a patient for a rectal procedure, nurses perform targeted assessments. Check the patient's medical record for recent rectal surgeries, bleeding disorders, or severe diarrhea. Inspect the perianal area for hemorrhoids, fissures, or skin breakdown, noting any pain reported by the patient.

Explain the procedure clearly to reduce anxiety and secure informed consent. Gather supplies: clean gloves, water-soluble lubricant, the medication, and privacy screens. Positioning is critical; place the patient in the left lateral Sims position, which aligns with the natural downward curve of the sigmoid colon and eases insertion.

Step-by-Step Procedure

  1. Perform Hand Hygiene and Don Gloves: Protect against infection by wearing clean gloves throughout the procedure.
  2. Position the Patient: Help the patient assume the left lateral Sims position and drape them to maintain privacy and dignity.
  3. Prepare the Medication: Unwrap the suppository and apply a generous amount of water-soluble lubricant to the rounded tip. Avoid petroleum jelly, as it can delay drug release.
  4. Insert the Suppository: Instruct the patient to take slow, deep breaths to relax the anal sphincter. Gently insert the suppository past the internal anal sphincter—about 1 inch in adults—using the index finger.
  5. Post-Insertion Care: Withdraw your finger, wipe the perianal area, remove gloves, and perform hand hygiene. Instruct the patient to remain lying down for 15 to 20 minutes to prevent expulsion.

Potential Complications and Nursing Management

Although generally safe, rectal administration carries specific risks. The primary complication is the premature expulsion of the suppository or liquid enema, which invalidates the dose. If this happens within a few minutes, inspect the expelled medication and notify the prescriber before repeating the dose.

Local trauma, irritation, or bleeding can occur, especially if the patient has underlying hemorrhoids or if force is used during insertion. Vagal nerve stimulation is another rare complication; stretching the rectal wall can trigger bradycardia in susceptible patients, making gentle technique vital. If the patient reports severe cramping or rectal pain, stop the procedure immediately.

Patient Education and Documentation

Clear communication empowers patients and ensures safety once they resume self-administration or understand hospital routines. Instruct ambulatory patients on proper hand hygiene, storage requirements—such as keeping heat-sensitive suppositories cool—and the correct technique for unwrapping and inserting the medication. Emphasize the importance of resting in bed for at least 15 minutes post-insertion.

Accurate documentation forms an essential legal and clinical record. Chart the medication name, dose, route, exact time of administration, patient tolerance, and any local adverse reactions like bleeding or expulsion. If the medication was an evacuation enema, record the results, including stool consistency, color, and approximate amount.
