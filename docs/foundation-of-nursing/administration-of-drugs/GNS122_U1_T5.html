Rectal Route of Drug Administration

Introduction and Definition of the Rectal Route

The rectal route of drug administration involves inserting a medication directly into the rectum. This method is primarily used when oral medication is impossible, unreliable, or clinically inappropriate. The lower gastrointestinal tract provides a viable pathway for both local and systemic drug action. While sometimes viewed as unpleasant by patients, it remains a reliable alternative in emergency settings, pediatric care, and palliative medicine.

Indications and Contraindications

Rectal administration is indicated when oral intake is compromised by severe vomiting, nausea, or decreased levels of consciousness. It is also frequently utilized for localized conditions like hemorrhoids or distal bowel inflammation, and for fever reduction in young children using antipyretic suppositories.

However, this route must be avoided in specific clinical situations:

  • Recent rectal or bowel surgery: Insertion can disrupt fragile suture lines and cause trauma.
  • Severe diarrhea or rectal bleeding: The drug may be expelled immediately or worsen existing mucosal damage.
  • Thrombocytopenia or severe bleeding disorders: Manipulation of the rectal mucosa risks severe hemorrhage.
  • Active rectal pathology: Severe inflammation, fistulas, or prolapsed hemorrhoids make insertion painful and unsafe.

Pharmacokinetic Considerations: Absorption and First-Pass Metabolism

Drugs absorbed in the lower rectum via the inferior and middle rectal veins enter directly into the systemic circulation, bypassing the portal system and the liver's first-pass metabolism. This allows a higher concentration of the active drug to reach systemic circulation quickly. Conversely, drugs that travel higher into the superior rectal vein enter the inferior mesenteric vein and the portal system, subjecting them to hepatic first-pass metabolism. Because fecal masses in the rectum can physically block the drug from contacting the mucosal wall, absorption is frequently variable.

Types of Rectal Dosage Forms

Rectal medications are formulated primarily as suppositories or enemas. Suppositories are small, cone-shaped or bullet-shaped solid masses containing a medication mixed with a base material—such as cocoa butter or synthetic glycerides—that melts at body temperature. Enemas are liquid preparations introduced into the rectum and colon to promote defecation or deliver local and systemic medications, such as anti-inflammatory agents.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before administering a rectal medication, the nurse must verify the medical order, review the patient's allergy history, and assess for any contraindications like rectal bleeding or recent bowel surgery. Check the patient's chart for recent bowel movements, as a full rectum impedes absorption. Explain the procedure clearly to secure patient cooperation and preserve their dignity. Gather all necessary supplies, including clean gloves, water-soluble lubricant, and tissues, before approaching the bedside.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Medications

  1. Perform hand hygiene and put on clean gloves: Protects both patient and nurse from cross-contamination.
  2. Position the patient: Assist the patient into Sims' position (left lateral with the upper leg flexed forward), which naturally aligns with the sigmoid colon.
  3. Expose the buttocks and inspect: Lift the upper buttock gently to view the anus, checking for hemorrhoids, rashes, or bleeding.
  4. Unwrap and lubricate: Remove the suppository wrapper and apply a water-soluble lubricant to the rounded tip.
  5. Insert the medication: Ask the patient to take a deep breath to relax the anal sphincter. Gently insert the suppository past the internal anal sphincter (about 1 inch in adults, less in infants).
  6. Clean and position: Remove gloves, perform hand hygiene, and instruct the patient to remain lying down for 15 to 20 minutes to prevent premature expulsion.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

While generally safe, rectal administration can present specific complications. Rectal irritation, mucosal tearing, or bleeding can occur, especially if lubricants are omitted or if the patient has underlying friable tissue. Premature expulsion of the suppository is common if inserted into a stool mass or if the patient bears down prematurely. If the patient expels the medication within a few minutes, inspect the stool, retrieve it if necessary, and notify the prescriber to evaluate if a repeat dose is required.

Patient Education and Documentation

Proper documentation is vital for legal and clinical continuity, requiring the nurse to record the medication name, dose, date, time, and route immediately after administration. Document any patient reactions, local discomfort, or expulsion of the medication. Educate the patient or caregiver on the rationale for the medication, proper retention techniques, and signs of adverse reactions to watch for, such as persistent bleeding or severe cramping.
