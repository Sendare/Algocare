Administration of Blood

Introduction and Principles of Blood Transfusion

Blood transfusion is a vital therapeutic intervention utilized in nursing practice to restore circulating blood volume, improve oxygen-carrying capacity, and correct coagulation defects in patients experiencing acute hemorrhage, severe anemia, or major surgical procedures. As nursing students in Nigeria, understanding the foundational principles of blood transfusion is crucial for patient safety and clinical competence. The primary goal is to safely deliver blood components—such as packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, platelets, or cryoprecipitate—while minimizing the risk of adverse physiological responses. Strict adherence to institutional protocols and international standards is mandatory at every stage of the transfusion process. Nurses act as the final safeguard in the medication administration chain, bearing the ultimate bedside responsibility to verify patient identity, check product details, and monitor for immediate or delayed complications. Ethical and legal considerations also apply, including obtaining informed consent from the patient or legal guardian prior to initiating the procedure, except in life-threatening emergency situations where consent is implied. Cultural or religious beliefs, such as those held by Jehovah's Witnesses, must be respected, and alternative bloodless management strategies explored where applicable. Interprofessional communication between the nursing staff, blood bank personnel, and the prescribing physician forms the bedrock of a successful and safe transfusion therapy program within any healthcare facility.

Blood Types, Crossmatching, and Compatibility

A thorough understanding of blood types, crossmatching, and compatibility is essential to prevent catastrophic hemolytic transfusion reactions. The human blood system is categorized primarily by the ABO system and the Rhesus (Rh) factor. The ABO system classifies blood into types A, B, AB, and O based on the presence or absence of specific antigens on the surface of red blood cells and corresponding antibodies in the plasma. Type O is historically known as the universal red cell donor, while type AB is considered the universal plasma donor. Furthermore, the Rhesus factor determines whether blood is positive or negative. Before any blood product is released for transfusion, rigorous pre-transfusion testing must be conducted by the blood bank. This includes ABO and Rh typing, antibody screening, and crossmatching. Crossmatching involves mixing the recipient's serum with donor red blood cells to detect any agglutination or adverse immune response, serving as the ultimate compatibility test. In emergency situations where there is no time for crossmatching, type O negative packed red blood cells are typically issued for females of childbearing potential, and type O positive or negative for others, to minimize immediate immune destruction. Nursing students must meticulously verify that the blood type on the donor bag matches the patient's exact blood group on the crossmatch report and hospital wristband to avert fatal transfusion errors.

Pre-Transfusion Assessment and Preparation

Pre-transfusion assessment and preparation set the stage for a safe and uneventful blood administration process. The nurse must begin by evaluating the patient's baseline vital signs—including temperature, pulse rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rate—within 30 minutes prior to initiation. This establishes a baseline against which any subsequent changes or febrile reactions can be measured. A comprehensive clinical assessment should include checking for a history of previous blood transfusions, known allergies, fluid overload risks (such as heart failure or chronic kidney disease), and current medications. The nurse must also inspect the physical integrity of the blood product from the blood bank. This involves examining the bag for any signs of abnormal discoloration, cloudiness, gas bubbles, clumps, or leakage, and ensuring that the expiration date has not passed. Checking institutional refrigerator logs or transport times is equally important to confirm that the blood has been stored at the correct temperature (typically between 1°C and 6°C for packed cells). Finally, the nurse must ensure that all required equipment—such as a dedicated intravenous catheter, normal saline 0.9% infusion, blood administration tubing, and personal protective equipment—is readily available at the bedside before retrieving the blood unit from the laboratory.

Equipment, IV Access, and Blood Administration Sets

Administering blood products requires specialized equipment designed to maintain product integrity and patient safety. The intravenous access must be adequate; a peripheral catheter of at least 18 to 20 gauge is generally recommended for adults to facilitate rapid infusion and prevent hemolysis of red blood cells caused by excessive pressure through a narrow lumen. If central venous access is used, standard institutional guidelines must be followed. The blood administration set is a specialized Y-type tubing system featuring an integral inline mesh filter. This filter is specifically designed to trap micro-aggregates, cellular debris, and fibrin clots that form during storage, preventing them from entering the patient's circulatory system. It is critical to note that only isotonic 0.9% Sodium Chloride (Normal Saline) should be used to prime the blood administration set and flush the line before and after blood administration. Hypotonic solutions like 5% Dextrose in Water (D5W) can cause hemolysis of red blood cells, while Lactated Ringer's solution contains calcium which may interact with citrate anticoagulant in the blood bag and cause micro-clotting. Furthermore, blood components should never be mixed with medications or other intravenous solutions through the same tubing. If blood warming is necessary due to rapid infusion or the risk of hypothermia, only approved, monitored blood warming devices equipped with visual and audible temperature alarms must be utilized.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Blood

The step-by-step procedure for administering blood demands rigorous attention to detail and adherence to patient safety protocols. The process begins with the mandatory bedside verification of the blood product. Two qualified healthcare professionals (such as two registered nurses, or a nurse and a doctor, depending on hospital policy) must independently verify the patient's identification band against the blood bag label, blood bank compatibility form, and hospital chart. They must check the patient's full name, hospital number, blood group (ABO and Rh), unique blood unit number, and expiration date. Once verification is complete and documented, hand hygiene is performed, and non-sterile gloves are donned. The nurse primes the Y-tubing with 0.9% Normal Saline, ensuring the filter is completely covered, and then clamps the saline side. The blood bag is gently inverted a few times to mix the cells, the blood port is spiked, and the blood clamp is opened while keeping the saline clamp closed. The infusion is started slowly—usually no faster than 2 mL/min or 50-100 mL/hr—for the first 15 minutes. The nurse must remain at the bedside during this initial period, as most severe acute hemolytic or anaphylactic reactions manifest within the first 50 mL of blood infusion. After 15 minutes, if the patient exhibits no adverse signs or symptoms, the flow rate is adjusted to the prescribed level, ensuring the entire unit of packed red blood cells is infused within a maximum timeframe of 4 hours to prevent bacterial proliferation.

Patient Monitoring and Observation during Transfusion

Patient monitoring and observation during a blood transfusion are critical nursing responsibilities designed to ensure early detection and intervention should complications arise. After the initial 15-minute observation period at the bedside, the nurse must recheck and document the patient's vital signs. Throughout the duration of the transfusion, vital signs should be monitored and recorded according to institutional policy—commonly every 30 minutes for the first hour, and hourly thereafter until the infusion is completed and for at least one hour post-transfusion. The nurse should actively communicate with the patient, instructing them to immediately report any unusual symptoms such as chills, fever, itching, chest tightness, back or flank pain, dyspnea, nausea, or feelings of impending doom. Additionally, the nurse must periodically inspect the intravenous insertion site for signs of infiltration, phlebitis, or localized swelling, and ensure the infusion rate remains on schedule. Special vigilance is required when monitoring vulnerable populations, such as elderly patients, infants, and individuals with pre-existing cardiac or renal compromise, who are at heightened risk for transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO). If any parameters deviate significantly from baseline or if the patient reports distressing symptoms, the nurse must be prepared to act instantly.

Recognition and Management of Transfusion Reactions

The recognition and management of transfusion reactions require swift, decisive nursing intervention. Transfusion reactions can be acute (occurring during or within 24 hours of the transfusion) or delayed. Acute complications include acute hemolytic reactions (due to ABO incompatibility), febrile non-hemolytic reactions, allergic reactions, anaphylaxis, septicemia from bacterial contamination, and transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO). The immediate nursing action upon suspecting any reaction is to stop the blood transfusion immediately. The nurse must disconnect the blood tubing and maintain intravenous access using a new infusion set primed with fresh 0.9% Normal Saline to keep the vein open and support blood pressure. The prescribing physician and the blood bank must be notified without delay. The remaining blood bag, along with the administration set, infusion records, and a freshly collected post-reaction blood sample and urine sample, must be sent back to the blood bank for laboratory investigation. The nurse must administer emergency medications as ordered by the physician—such as antihistamines, corticosteroids, antipyretics, bronchodilators, or vasopressors—and provide supportive care like oxygen therapy. Finally, comprehensive documentation of the reaction symptoms, vital sign changes, nursing actions taken, and physician notifications must be meticulously recorded in the patient's medical record and reported through institutional incident reporting channels.

Post-Transfusion Care and Documentation

Post-transfusion care and documentation mark the final responsibilities in managing a blood transfusion. Once the blood product has completely infused, the nurse flushes the remaining blood from the administration set using the attached 0.9% Normal Saline until the tubing is clear. The IV line can then be maintained for ongoing intravenous therapy or flushed and locked according to clinical orders and facility protocol. The empty blood bag must be handled safely; it should be disposed of in accordance with hospital biohazard waste policies, unless institutional guidelines require returning empty bags to the blood bank for a specified period. The patient's vital signs must be measured and recorded once again, typically one hour post-transfusion, to ensure stability and verify the absence of delayed adverse reactions. Thorough documentation in the nursing notes and fluid balance chart is legally and clinically mandatory. Entries must accurately reflect the start and completion times, the unique blood product identification number, total volume infused, baseline and post-transfusion vital signs, the patient's tolerance of the procedure, and any nurse or physician communications. Proper documentation provides a legal record of care, ensures continuity among healthcare team members, and fulfills regulatory standards within Nigerian healthcare settings.
