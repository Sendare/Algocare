Administration of Blood

Introduction and Principles of Blood Transfusion

Blood transfusion is a life-saving therapy used to restore circulating blood volume, improve oxygen-carrying capacity, and correct bleeding disorders. Whole blood is rarely given today; instead, blood is separated into specific components like packed red blood cells, platelets, fresh frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate. Each component targets a distinct patient need.

Strict safety protocols govern every step of the process because blood products are living biological tissue carrying serious risks of infection, immune reactions, and circulatory overload. The fundamental rule of transfusion safety is absolute patient identification. A single clerical error can trigger a fatal incompatible transfusion.

Blood Types, Crossmatching, and Compatibility

The ABO system classifies blood based on the presence or absence of A and B antigens on red blood cells, alongside naturally occurring antibodies in the plasma. The Rhesus (Rh) factor determines whether blood is positive or negative.

  • Type O negative: Considered the universal red cell donor because red cells lack A, B, and Rh antigens, minimizing immediate immune attack.
  • Type AB positive: Considered the universal plasma donor and universal recipient for red cells because their blood contains no antibodies against major antigens.
  • Crossmatching: Laboratory testing that mixes donor red cells with recipient serum to check for hidden antibodies and ensure true compatibility before infusion begins.

Pre-Transfusion Assessment and Preparation

Before requesting blood, nurses must confirm a valid medical prescription, signed informed consent from the patient, and a type and crossmatch sample drawn within the allowable timeframe (typically 72 hours). Baseline vital signs—temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure—must be recorded immediately before starting the infusion so any reaction-induced changes are easily spotted.

Preparation also involves checking equipment and the unit itself. Two licensed nurses must independently verify the blood bag against the patient's identification band, blood bank tag, and transfusion form. They verify patient name, hospital number, blood group, unit serial number, and expiration date.

Equipment, IV Access, and Blood Administration Sets

Blood administration requires specific gear to protect fragile blood cells and filter out clots or debris.

  • IV Access: A large-bore peripheral cannula, ideally 18-gauge or 20-gauge, is preferred. Smaller needles can shear red blood cells, causing hemolysis.
  • Blood Tubing: Must feature an integrated inline filter designed to trap microaggregates and clots. Regular IV tubing without a filter must never be used.
  • Solution: Normal Saline (0.9% Sodium Chloride) is the only intravenous fluid compatible with blood products. Dextrose solutions cause red cell clumping (hemolysis), and Ringer's lactate contains calcium which can trigger clotting in the tubing.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Blood

Begin by washing hands and putting on gloves. Prime the Y-type blood administration set with 0.9% normal saline, ensuring the lower filter is completely covered so air bubbles do not enter the line. Close the saline clamp and open the blood bag clamp to allow blood to flow past the filter.

Start the transfusion slowly, running the first 15 minutes at a slow rate (no more than 2 milliliters per minute or about 50-60 drops per minute). The nurse must remain at the bedside during this initial window because most severe acute hemolytic reactions occur quickly with small volumes of blood. If no reaction occurs, adjust the flow rate to the prescribed speed so the entire unit finishes within a maximum of 4 hours.

Patient Monitoring and Observation during Transfusion

Continuous observation is the nurse's primary safeguard during a blood transfusion. Vital signs must be measured before starting, 15 minutes after starting (at the bedside), hourly until the transfusion completes, and once more upon completion.

Instruct the patient to report unusual symptoms immediately. Red flag warning signs include chills, fever, itching, chest tightness, lower back pain, restlessness, or unusual shortness of breath. Nurses should never leave the room unattended during the crucial first 15 minutes of any new blood product unit.

Recognition and Management of Transfusion Reactions

Transfusion reactions range from mild allergic rashes to life-threatening hemolytic or septic emergencies.

  • Acute Hemolytic Reaction: Caused by ABO incompatibility. Symptoms include fever, chest pain, flank pain, and dark urine. Stop blood, infuse normal saline with new tubing, and notify the doctor and blood bank.
  • Allergic Reaction: Manifests as hives, itching, and flushing. May be treated with antihistamines if ordered.
  • Circulatory Overload (TACO): Occurs when blood infuses too fast for the heart to handle. Symptoms include coughing, severe dyspnea, and pulmonary crackles.
  • Febrile Non-Hemolytic Reaction: Characterized by sudden fever and chills without hemolysis, often managed with antipyretics.

Post-Transfusion Care and Documentation

Once the blood bag is empty, disconnect the administration set and flush the line with normal saline. Discard the blood bag and tubing according to hospital infection control policies, usually in biohazard waste containers. Record final vital signs and compare them against pre-transfusion baselines.

Documentation in the patient chart must be thorough and timely. Record the blood product serial number, type of product, start and stop times, total volume infused, baseline and post-transfusion vital signs, nurse signatures, and any patient reactions or lack thereof.
