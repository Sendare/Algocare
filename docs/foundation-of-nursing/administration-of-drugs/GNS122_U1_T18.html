Administration of Blood

Introduction and Principles of Blood Transfusion

Blood transfusion is a specialized therapeutic intervention involving the intravenous delivery of whole blood or specific blood components to restore circulating volume, enhance oxygen-carrying capacity, or correct coagulation deficits. Every unit represents a potential allogeneic transplant, demanding rigorous safety protocols from collection to infusion. Clinical indications range from acute massive hemorrhage following trauma or obstetric complications to chronic anemia unresponsive to standard therapies.

Ethical and legal frameworks mandate informed consent prior to administration, except in life-threatening emergencies where consent is implied. In resource-constrained settings across Nigeria, ensuring a safe blood supply relies heavily on voluntary non-remunerated donors, mandatory screening for transfusion-transmissible infections like HIV, Hepatitis B and C, and Syphilis, and maintaining strict cold chain integrity. The primary objective remains maximizing therapeutic benefit while minimizing the inherent immunological and infectious risks.

Blood Types, Crossmatching, and Compatibility

Immunohematology dictates that successful transfusions depend on matching the recipient's immune profile with donor red blood cell antigens. The ABO system categorizes blood into groups A, B, AB, and O based on inherited surface carbohydrates, while the Rhesus (Rh) factor denotes the presence or absence of the D antigen.

  • Major Crossmatch: Laboratory testing mixing donor red blood cells with recipient serum to detect pre-formed antibodies, preventing acute hemolytic reactions.
  • Universal Donor: Type O negative red blood cells lack A, B, and Rh antigens, minimizing immediate destruction in most recipients.
  • Universal Recipient: Type AB positive individuals possess no naturally occurring anti-A or anti-B antibodies in their plasma.

In emergency situations where crossmatched blood is unavailable, uncrossed O negative or type-specific uncrossmatched blood may be released by the blood bank following strict institutional protocols.

Pre-Transfusion Assessment and Preparation

Thorough pre-transfusion assessment establishes a baseline and prevents clerical errors, which remain the leading cause of fatal transfusion reactions. Baseline vital signs—temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure—must be recorded within thirty minutes prior to starting the infusion. Any baseline fever should be investigated, as an elevated pre-existing temperature can mask or mimic a transfusion reaction.

The nurse must personally verify the patient's identity using at least two independent identifiers, comparing the hospital wristband against the blood bank compatibility report and the blood bag label. Two qualified clinicians must independently perform this bedside check. Ensuring the IV site is patent and the appropriate equipment is gathered before retrieving the blood unit from the blood bank minimizes warm storage time and bacterial proliferation risks.

Equipment, IV Access, and Blood Administration Sets

Blood administration requires specialized equipment designed to protect delicate blood cells and filter micro-aggregates. A standard Y-type blood administration set features an integrated mesh filter designed to trap cellular debris and fibrin clots while allowing red blood cells to pass smoothly.

  • IV Cannula Size: A minimum of an 18-gauge or 20-gauge peripheral cannula is preferred for adults to prevent mechanical hemolysis caused by forcing viscous fluid through a narrow lumen.
  • Fluid Compatibility: Normal saline (0.9% Sodium Chloride) is the only intravenous fluid permitted for priming and co-infusing with blood products. Solutions containing calcium, such as Ringer's Lactate, or hypotonic solutions like 5% Dextrose in water, cause clotting or hemolysis.
  • Blood Warmers: Specialized, temperature-regulated blood warmers are reserved for massive transfusions, rapid infusion protocols, or patients with clinically significant cold agglutinins to prevent hypothermia.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Blood

Executing a blood transfusion safely requires methodical adherence to institutional steps. Begin by priming the Y-tubing with 0.9% normal saline, ensuring the main clamp is closed and the saline covers the filter completely. Invert the blood bag gently to mix cellular components without causing hemolysis.

  1. Initial Rate: Start the infusion slowly, maintaining a rate of no more than 2 milliliters per minute (or roughly 20-30 drops per minute) for the first fifteen minutes.
  2. Close Observation: The nurse must remain at the bedside during this initial window, as most acute, severe immune reactions manifest immediately upon exposure.
  3. Adjustment: If no adverse signs or symptoms occur after fifteen minutes, increase the infusion rate to the prescribed therapeutic level, ensuring the entire unit is completed within a maximum of four hours to discourage bacterial proliferation.

Patient Monitoring and Observation during Transfusion

Continuous vigilance forms the core of safe transfusion nursing. Vital signs must be reassessed at the fifteen-minute mark following the rate increase, hourly until the transfusion concludes, and once more an hour post-infusion. Beyond numerical parameters, the nurse evaluates subjective and objective indicators of patient comfort and safety.

Instruct the patient to report any sudden sensations of warmth along the vein, tightness in the chest, lumbar or flank pain, chills, nausea, or localized itching immediately. Monitor respiratory effort and urine output, particularly in vulnerable populations such as the elderly, pediatric clients, or individuals with pre-existing cardiac or renal compromise, where fluid overload can precipitate acute pulmonary edema.

Recognition and Management of Transfusion Reactions

Transfusion reactions are classified based on their immunological mechanism and timing. Acute reactions occur during or within 24 hours of the infusion, while delayed reactions manifest days to weeks later.

  • Acute Hemolytic Reaction: Caused by ABO incompatibility, presenting with fever, chills, hypotension, hemoglobinuria, and severe flank pain. Requires immediate cessation, infusing new saline through new tubing, and notifying the blood bank.
  • Febrile Non-Hemolytic Reaction: Driven by recipient antibodies reacting against donor white blood cells; characterized by isolated temperature spikes without hemodynamic collapse.
  • Allergic/Anaphylactic Reaction: Ranging from localized urticaria to severe bronchospasm and shock, often managed with antihistamines or epinephrine.
  • Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO): Fluid overload presenting with dyspnea, hypertension, and orthopnea, managed with diuretics and upright positioning.

Post-Transfusion Care and Documentation

Comprehensive post-transfusion care ensures safe closure of the procedure and accurate medical record keeping. Once the blood bag is empty, flush the administration set with normal saline to deliver any residual blood cells remaining in the tubing. Discard the blood bag and administration set according to institutional infectious waste management protocols, placing them in biohazard containers.

Record the completion time, total volume infused, final vital signs, and the patient's post-transfusion tolerance in the clinical chart. If a transfusion reaction occurred, retain the blood bag and tubing for return to the blood bank alongside a post-reaction blood sample and freshly voided urine specimen to aid investigation.
