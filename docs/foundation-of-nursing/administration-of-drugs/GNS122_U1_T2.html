Oral Route of Drug Administration

Introduction and Principles of the Oral Route

The oral route involves taking medication by mouth, where it is swallowed and enters the gastrointestinal tract. From the stomach and intestines, the drug is absorbed into the bloodstream and carried to the liver via the portal circulation before reaching the rest of the body. This passage through the liver is known as the first-pass effect, which can significantly reduce the active concentration of certain drugs before they reach systemic circulation. Nurses must understand this pharmacokinetic journey because it dictates why oral doses are often higher than intravenous doses of the same medication.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Oral Medications

Giving medication by mouth remains the most common method because it is convenient, economical, and generally accepted by patients without causing physical pain. However, this route has notable drawbacks. Absorption rates vary widely depending on stomach contents, metabolism, and gut motility. Furthermore, oral drugs cannot be used safely if a patient is vomiting, unconscious, or experiencing severe swallowing difficulties due to the high risk of aspiration.

Common Forms of Oral Medications

Oral medications come in diverse preparations designed for different absorption speeds and patient needs.

  • Tablets: Compressed solid medication powders that may be scored for splitting.
  • Capsules: Gelatin shells enclosing powdered or liquid medication for quick release in the stomach.
  • Liquids: Solutions, syrups, or elixirs that are absorbed faster and ideal for patients who cannot swallow solid pills.
  • Enteric-coated: Special coatings that resist stomach acid, preventing the drug from dissolving until it reaches the alkaline environment of the small intestine.
  • Sustained-release: Formulations designed to dissolve slowly over an extended period, maintaining steady blood levels.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Preparation

Before administering any oral medication, the nurse must verify the physician's order against the patient's medication administration record. Assessing the patient's level of consciousness, ability to swallow, and presence of active nausea or bowel sounds is critical. The nurse should also check vital signs if the drug impacts blood pressure or heart rate. When preparing medications, the nurse must calculate dosages accurately and check expiration dates, keeping medicines in their unit-dose packaging until right at the bedside.

The Rights of Medication Administration

Safe medication delivery relies on strictly adhering to the standard safety checks known as the rights of administration. While traditional lists vary between five and ten rights, the core principles remain constant. The nurse must confirm the right patient, right medication, right dose, right route, right time, right documentation, and right reason. Verifying the patient's identity using at least two unique identifiers, such as checking their wristband and asking their name, is mandatory before handing over any medication.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Oral Medications

Administering oral medication requires a methodical approach to ensure comfort and compliance. Position the patient in an upright sitting position, or at least a 45-degree angle, to ease swallowing. Provide a glass of water or preferred fluid unless fluid restrictions apply. Hand solid pills to the patient in a small cup so they can place them directly into their mouth. Stay at the bedside until every pill or sip of liquid has been completely swallowed. Never leave medication unattended on a bedside table.

Special Considerations for Vulnerable Patient Groups

Vulnerable populations, such as pediatric and geriatric patients, require special adaptations during oral drug administration. Older adults often experience physiological changes like reduced saliva production, altered gastrointestinal absorption, and polypharmacy, which increase their risk of adverse drug reactions. Children often require flavored liquid formulations calculated strictly by body weight, and may need gentle reassurance or creative administration techniques from parents or nurses to ensure compliance.

Documentation and Post-Administration Monitoring

Documentation must occur immediately after medication administration, never before. The nurse records the exact drug name, dose, time, route, and any relevant patient assessments like pre-administration pain scores or blood pressure readings. Post-administration monitoring is equally vital to check for therapeutic effectiveness and watch for adverse allergic reactions or side effects. If a patient experiences an unexpected reaction, the nurse must report it promptly to the attending physician.
