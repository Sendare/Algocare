Administration of Intravenous Fluids

Introduction and Fluid Classifications

Intravenous fluid therapy is a fundamental nursing intervention used to restore or maintain fluid and electrolyte balance in clients who cannot take adequate oral nourishment. Fluids are broadly divided into crystalloids and colloids. Crystalloids contain small molecules that easily pass through semipermeable membranes, moving freely between the blood vessels and the body tissues. Colloids contain larger, insoluble molecules like proteins or starches that stay inside the bloodstream and pull fluid into the vessels through high osmotic pressure.

Crystalloids are further broken down into three categories based on how they affect body cells:

  • Isotonic fluids: Have the same concentration of dissolved particles as blood plasma. They stay mainly in the extracellular space to expand vascular volume without causing water to shift in or out of cells. Examples include 0.9% Normal Saline and Ringer's Lactate.
  • Hypotonic fluids: Have a lower particle concentration than serum, causing fluid to move out of blood vessels and swell the cells. 0.45% Normal Saline is commonly used to treat cellular dehydration.
  • Hypertonic fluids: Have a higher particle concentration than serum, drawing water out of the cells and into the bloodstream to shrink them. 3% Normal Saline is an example used for severe sodium depletion.

Equipment and Pre-Administration Assessment

Before starting any intravenous infusion, the nurse must gather appropriate supplies and perform a thorough client assessment. Essential equipment includes the correct IV fluid bag, an administration set (either primary gravity tubing or secondary tubing), an IV cannula of the proper gauge, an extension set, transparent dressings, an antiseptic swab like chlorhexidine, a tourniquet, non-sterile gloves, and tape.

Assessment begins by verifying the medical order against the client's identification band using two unique identifiers. Nurses must check the fluid bag for clarity, expiration date, and absence of leaks. A baseline assessment of the client's vital signs, hydration status, and lung sounds is vital, especially for clients with a history of heart failure or kidney disease, who face high risks of fluid overload. Always inspect the proposed limb for signs of poor circulation, injury, or previous surgical procedures like a mastectomy or fistula creation.

Procedure for IV Cannulation

Successful peripheral cannulation requires patience, good anatomical knowledge, and proper technique.

  1. Preparation: Explain the procedure, wash hands, and apply a tourniquet roughly four to six inches above the chosen site to engorge the veins.
  2. Vein Selection: Palpate for a bouncy, straight vein. Avoid areas with valves, joints, scars, or signs of infection.
  3. Skin Antisepsis: Clean the site with an antiseptic swab using a friction motion and let it air dry completely.
  4. Insertion: Hold the skin taut below the site, insert the needle bevel-up at a 15-to-30-degree angle, and watch for a flash of blood in the flashback chamber.
  5. Advancement: Lower the catheter parallel to the skin and slide it off the needle into the vein.
  6. Securing: Release the tourniquet, press gently above the insertion tip, remove the needle safely into a sharps container, attach the primed tubing or saline lock, and secure it with a transparent dressing.

Calculating and Regulating Infusion Rates

Nurses must accurately calculate and regulate intravenous infusion rates to ensure clients receive the exact prescribed dose without fluid overload. When an electronic infusion pump is unavailable, gravity tubing is used, requiring manual drop rate calculations.

The drop factor, located on the administration set packaging, represents the number of drops per milliliter delivered by that specific tubing. Standard drop sets deliver 10, 15, or 20 drops per milliliter, while pediatric sets or blood administration sets often deliver 60 drops per milliliter (micro-drip).

The standard formula is: $$\text{Flow Rate (gtt/min)} = \frac{\text{Total Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Time in Minutes}}$$

Nurses should count the drops falling into the drip chamber over a full minute using a wristwatch. Frequent checks ensure the roller clamp has not slipped and altered the rate.

Monitoring and Maintenance

Routine care of an IV site prevents complications and keeps the access line functioning smoothly. Nurses must assess the site and surrounding skin at least once every shift, or more frequently for vulnerable clients like older adults or those receiving vesicant medications.

Key monitoring duties include checking the date on the dressing to ensure it is within hospital policy limits, usually every 7 days for transparent dressings unless soiled or loose. The tubing must be labeled with the date and time of change, typically every 96 hours for continuous crystalloid infusions. Always check that all connections are secure, the clamp is open, and the fluid is infusing at the correct rate. Encourage the client to report any unusual dampness, cool sensations, or aching around the insertion site immediately.

Complications and Nursing Actions

Intravenous therapy carries several potential complications that require prompt recognition and intervention by the nursing team.

  • Infiltration: Fluid leaks into surrounding subcutaneous tissue. The site becomes cool, pale, and swollen. Nursing action: Stop the infusion, remove the catheter, elevate the limb, and apply a warm or cold compress depending on the fluid type.
  • Extravasation: Similar to infiltration, but the leaked fluid is a vesicant medication that causes tissue necrosis. Nursing action: Stop the infusion immediately, leave the catheter in place briefly to aspirate remaining drug if possible, notify the provider, and administer an antidote.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall causing pain, redness along the vein path, and warmth. Nursing action: Stop the infusion, remove the device, and apply a warm compress.
  • Fluid Overload: Excess fluid volume in the vascular system leading to shortness of breath, crackles in the lungs, and high blood pressure. Nursing action: Slow down the infusion, elevate the head of the bed, and notify the physician.
  • Air Embolism: Air bubbles enter the circulatory system. Nursing action: Clamp the tubing, place the client on their left side in the Trendelenburg position, and administer oxygen.

Discontinuation of IV Therapy

Discontinuing an intravenous line is a straightforward procedure performed when therapy is complete, the client is discharged, or a complication arises.

Gather clean gloves, gauze, and tape. Explain the procedure to the client. Clamp the tubing or turn off the electronic infusion pump. Gently peel away the transparent dressing and tape while stabilizing the catheter hub. Place clean gauze lightly over the insertion site without applying pressure down on the needle. Withdraw the catheter smoothly, keeping it parallel to the skin to avoid trauma. Inspect the catheter tip to ensure it is intact. Apply firm, direct pressure to the puncture site with the gauze for at least one to two minutes until bleeding stops, longer if the client has a bleeding disorder. Secure the gauze with a clean adhesive bandage and document the time, site condition, and client tolerance.
