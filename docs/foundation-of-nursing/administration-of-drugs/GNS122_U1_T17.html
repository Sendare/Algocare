Administration of Intravenous Fluids

Introduction to Intravenous Fluid Therapy

Intravenous fluid therapy involves the direct infusion of liquid substances into a vein. This intervention remains a cornerstone of acute and critical hospital care, allowing for rapid restoration of circulating blood volume, correction of electrolyte disturbances, and continuous delivery of medications. Unlike oral routes, intravenous administration bypasses the digestive system, guaranteeing immediate systemic bioavailability. Understanding the physiological distribution of body water between intracellular and extracellular compartments is essential for predicting how infused fluids will behave once inside the vascular space.

Types and Classifications of IV Fluids

Intravenous solutions are categorized by their tonicity relative to normal human blood plasma. Selecting the correct fluid depends entirely on the underlying fluid or electrolyte deficit. - **Crystalloids**: Aqueous solutions of mineral salts or other water-soluble molecules. They move freely across capillary membranes. - *Isotonic*: Examples include Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl) and Ringers Lactate. They expand the extracellular volume without causing fluid shifts into or out of cells. - *Hypotonic*: Examples include 0.45% Sodium Chloride. They shift fluid out of the intravascular space and into cells, useful for cellular dehydration. - *Hypertonic*: Examples include 3% Sodium Chloride. They draw water out of the cells and into the vascular space, requiring careful monitoring to avoid circulatory overload. - **Colloids**: Large molecular weight substances like albumin or starches that remain in the intravascular space and increase oncotic pressure.

Equipment and Supplies for IV Administration

Gathering appropriate supplies before initiating therapy minimizes patient discomfort and reduces infection risks. Standard equipment includes: - **IV Cannula (Catheter over needle)**: Sized by gauge; larger gauge numbers indicate smaller diameters (e.g., 18G for rapid fluid resuscitation, 22G or 24G for fragile veins or slower infusions). - **Infusion Administration Set**: Macro-drip sets (usually 10, 15, or 20 drops/mL) for routine adult infusions, and micro-drip sets (60 drops/mL) for precise volume control in pediatric or delicate patients. - **Tourniquet**: Used to distend veins prior to cannulation. - **Antiseptic Swab**: Chlorhexidine or alcohol for rigorous skin disinfection. - **Transparent Dressing**: To secure the site and allow visual inspection. - **Extension Set and IV Pole**: To manage lines and hang solutions safely.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Thorough preparation prevents clinical errors and builds therapeutic rapport. Verify the medical prescription against the patient's identity band, checking the specific fluid type, additive medications, total volume, and prescribed rate. Assess the patient's medical history for conditions that contraindicate large fluid volumes, such as heart failure or chronic kidney disease. Inspect the proposed limb for signs of compromised circulation, local skin infections, or healed arteriovenous fistulas. Finally, explain the procedure to the patient, addressing anxieties and securing informed cooperation.

Step-by-Step Procedure for IV Cannulation and Infusion

1. **Hand Hygiene and Gloves**: Perform strict handwashing and don clean gloves. 2. **Vein Selection**: Choose a distal vein on the upper extremities first, preferably the metacognitive or cephalic veins, moving proximally if future insertions are needed. Avoid areas of flexion. 3. **Application**: Apply the tourniquet 4 to 6 inches above the selected site. Palpate for a bouncy, unobstructed vein. 4. **Skin Antisepsis**: Cleanse the site using friction in a back-and-forth motion or concentric circles, allowing it to dry completely. 5. **Insertion**: Hold the skin taut below the site. Insert the needle at a 15 to 30-degree angle with the bevel up. Watch for flashback of blood in the chamber. 6. **Advancement**: Lower the angle and advance the catheter slightly off the needle, then slide the catheter fully into the vein while withdrawing the needle. 7. **Securing**: Release the tourniquet, attach the primed infusion tubing, secure with a transparent dressing, and regulate the flow.

Calculating and Regulating Infusion Rates

Accurate calculation ensures the patient receives the prescribed volume without dangerous surges or delays. When an electronic infusion pump is unavailable, manual calculation using the drip factor is required. $$\text{Flow Rate (drops/min)} = \frac{\text{Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Time (minutes)}}$$ Always double-check calculations before starting the infusion. Count drops by watching the drip chamber for a full minute using a watch with a second hand. Nurses must also inspect gravity-fed lines periodically because patient movement or roller clamp shifting can alter the flow rate.

Monitoring and Maintenance of IV Sites

Routine care preserves access and prevents complications. Inspect the insertion site every shift for pain, redness, swelling, or coolness. Check that the dressing remains clean, dry, and intact. Change peripheral dressings according to facility protocol, typically every 7 days or immediately if compromised. Flush intermittent lines with sterile normal saline before and after medication administration to maintain patency and prevent drug incompatibilities. Document site condition, fluid volume infused, and any patient complaints clearly in the nursing notes.

Complications of IV Therapy and Nursing Interventions

Intravenous therapy carries risks that demand prompt recognition and intervention: - **Infiltration**: Fluid enters surrounding subcutaneous tissue due to catheter displacement. The site appears swollen, cool to the touch, and pale. *Action*: Stop infusion, remove catheter, elevate the limb, and apply warm or cold compresses based on the infused solution. - **Phlebitis**: Inflammation of the vein wall caused by mechanical, chemical, or bacterial irritation. Characterized by pain, erythema, and a palpable streak along the vein. *Action*: Stop infusion, remove the device, and notify the prescriber. - **Fluid Overload**: Excess intravascular volume leading to dyspnea, crackles in lung bases, and elevated blood pressure. *Action*: Slow the infusion, raise the head of the bed, and administer oxygen or diuretics as ordered. - **Air Embolism**: Air entering the circulatory system through unprimed tubing. *Action*: Clamp tubing, place the patient in Left Trendelenburg position, and administer emergency support.

Discontinuation of Intravenous Therapy

Discontinuing an intravenous line is indicated when therapy is complete, complications arise, or transitioning to oral intake occurs. Explain the procedure to the patient. Perform hand hygiene and put on gloves. Turn off the infusion pump or roller clamp. Gently remove the dressing while stabilizing the catheter. Place sterile gauze over the site, withdraw the cannula smoothly along the axis of insertion, and apply immediate firm pressure for at least 2 to 3 minutes until hemostasis is achieved. Inspect the removed catheter tip to ensure it is intact, apply a clean adhesive bandage, and document the discontinuation time and site appearance.
