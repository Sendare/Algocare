Parenteral Drug Administration

Introduction and Principles of Parenteral Drug Administration

Parenteral administration refers to the delivery of medications via routes other than the alimentary canal, bypassing the gastrointestinal tract entirely. This method is primarily chosen when rapid therapeutic drug action is required, when the drug would be destroyed by digestive enzymes, or when the patient cannot take medications orally due to unconsciousness, severe vomiting, or swallowing difficulties. The foundational principle governing all parenteral procedures is the absolute maintenance of sterility. Because these routes bypass the body's primary mechanical barrier—the skin and mucous membranes—microorganisms are introduced directly into deep tissues, the bloodstream, or body cavities, carrying a high risk of severe systemic infection.

Safety, Aseptic Technique, and Infection Control

Infection control practices during drug preparation and delivery protect both the patient and the healthcare provider. Standard precautions dictate that clean gloves be worn for any procedure involving potential contact with body fluids, though sterile gloves are specific to certain central line insertions or surgical procedures.

  • Sharps Safety: Never recap used needles using a two-handed technique. Utilize built-in safety devices or immediate drop into a rigid, puncture-resistant sharps container located at the point of use.
  • Aseptic Handling: Clean injection ports and vial stoppers vigorously with 70% isopropyl alcohol using a friction motion for at least fifteen seconds, allowing them to dry completely before piercing.
  • Single-Dose vs. Multi-Dose Vials: Whenever possible, prefer single-dose vials. If multi-dose vials must be used, label them with the date, time, and nurse's initials upon opening, and discard them according to facility policy or after 28 days.

Intradermal and Subcutaneous Injections

Intradermal injections place small volumes of fluid—usually 0.1 to 0.5 mL—into the dermal layer just beneath the epidermis. This route is used primarily for diagnostic testing, such as tuberculin skin tests and allergy sensitivity screens, because systemic absorption from this layer is extremely slow. The needle is inserted bevel up at a shallow angle, creating a small wheal or bleb on the skin surface.

Subcutaneous injections deposit medication into the loose connective tissue beneath the dermis. Absorption is slower than through the intramuscular route but faster than oral administration. Common sites include the outer posterior aspect of the upper arms, the abdomen from below the costal margins to the iliac crests, and the anterior thigh areas. Pinching the skin fold helps ensure the needle enters subcutaneous fat rather than underlying muscle, particularly in leaner patients.

Intramuscular Injections and Landmark Identification

Intramuscular injections deliver medications directly into muscle tissue, which has a rich blood supply allowing for rapid systemic absorption. Precise anatomical landmarking is essential to prevent injury to major nerves and blood vessels.

  • Ventrogluteal Site: Considered the safest and most preferred site for adults, located by placing the palm on the greater trochanter and pointing the index finger toward the anterior superior iliac spine.
  • Vastus Lateralis Site: Located on the anterior-lateral thigh, excellent for infants and toddlers due to a well-developed muscle mass lacking major nerve trunks.
  • Deltoid Site: Located by finding the acromion process; limited to small volume injections (usually up to 1 mL) because of proximity to the radial nerve and brachial artery.

Intravenous Therapy and Access Management

Intravenous therapy involves the direct infusion of fluids, electrolytes, blood products, or medications into a systemic vein, providing 100% immediate bioavailability. Peripheral access sites are commonly established in the dorsal veins of the hand and forearm. When selecting a vein, choose a distal site first, progressing proximally with subsequent insertions if needed. Avoid areas of flexion, bruised limbs, or arms on the same side as a recent mastectomy or an active dialysis fistula.

Maintaining patency requires regular assessment of the insertion site for signs of infiltration, phlebitis, or infection. Tubing changes and dressing management must follow strict facility infection control policies to prevent catheter-related bloodstream infections.

Patient Assessment and Pre-Administration Checks

Before administering any parenteral medication, the nurse performs a rigorous clinical assessment to establish baseline parameters and verify safety. This includes checking the medication order against the patient's identity using at least two unique identifiers, reviewing documented allergies, and assessing current vital signs or lab values that impact drug clearance.

The classic safety framework involves executing the rights of medication administration: right patient, right medication, right dose, right route, right time, right documentation, and right reason. Additionally, the nurse must evaluate the localized skin integrity at the proposed injection or infusion site to ensure there is no inflammation, edema, or tissue breakdown.

Complications, Prevention, and Nursing Management

Parenteral therapy carries inherent risks of local and systemic complications that require prompt recognition and intervention.

  • Infiltration: Occurs when IV fluid enters subcutaneous tissue surrounding the vessel; characterized by cool, blanched, swollen skin. Stop infusion immediately and remove the catheter.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall marked by pain, erythema, and warmth along the course of the vein. Discontinue the catheter and apply warm compresses if ordered.
  • Air Embolism: Rare but serious IV complication where air enters the circulatory system. Place the patient in a left lateral Trendelenburg position to trap air in the right atrium and notify the provider immediately.
  • Anaphylaxis: Severe systemic allergic reaction requiring immediate cessation of the offending agent, maintenance of a patent airway, and administration of emergency epinephrine.

Documentation and Post-Administration Evaluation

Accurate charting completes the medication administration cycle, ensuring continuity of care and legal accountability. Documentation must occur immediately after administration—never before—and should include the exact medication name, dose, route, site used, date, time, and the nurse's signature or electronic stamp.

Post-administration evaluation is equally vital. The nurse must return to assess the patient within an appropriate timeframe based on the drug's pharmacokinetics to evaluate therapeutic efficacy and monitor for delayed adverse reactions. Any refusal of medication, adverse event, or unexpected clinical response must be clearly noted in the medical record and reported to the primary healthcare team.
