Dilution of Injections

Introduction and Principles of Dilution of Injections

Dilution of injections is a foundational clinical skill that involves adding a sterile liquid solvent to a concentrated medication. This process lowers the drug concentration to a level safe for administration via intravenous, intramuscular, or subcutaneous routes. Concentrated parenteral medications can cause severe local tissue necrosis, intense pain, or rapid, life-threatening systemic toxicities if pushed directly into the circulation. In many Nigerian hospital wards, medications supplied in dry powder form or highly concentrated ampoules must be appropriately manipulated before delivery to guarantee patient safety and therapeutic efficacy.

Indications and Rationale for Diluting Medications

Clinicians dilute parenteral drugs for several distinct clinical reasons. Preventing chemical phlebitis is paramount when administering vesicant agents that irritate peripheral endothelium. Volume adjustments are also critical in pediatric nursing and neonatology, where tiny dosages require vehicle expansion for accurate measurement. Furthermore, slow intravenous push medications often require volume expansion to ensure the dose is delivered over the prescribed number of minutes rather than seconds.

Understanding Diluents and Compatibility

Selecting the correct diluent is just as important as measuring the correct drug dose. Using an incompatible liquid can cause precipitation, cloudiness, or complete inactivation of the active pharmaceutical ingredient. Common diluents include:

  • Sterile Water for Injection: Lacks antimicrobial agents and solutes; must be used promptly once opened.
  • 0.9% Sodium Chloride (Normal Saline): Isotonic solution commonly used for general drug reconstitution.
  • 5% Dextrose in Water (D5W): Nutritive diluent often specified for acid-labile or specific chemotherapy agents.

Equipment Needed and Preparation of the Sterile Field

Before touching any medication vial or ampoule, gathering the correct equipment prevents procedural delays and contamination. Essential items include syringes of appropriate sizes, blunt fill needles or safety filter needles, alcohol swabs, the medication, and the correct diluent. The work surface must be wiped down with a disinfectant and allowed to dry. Maintaining a sterile field ensures that microorganisms from the environment or hands do not transfer into the medication pathways.

Calculating Drug Dosages and Dilution Ratios

Precise mathematical calculation is a core competency for safe drug delivery. Nurses use formulas like (Desired Dose / Stock Dose) × Stock Volume to determine the correct amount of concentrated drug to extract. When reconstituting powders, the displacement volume of the powder itself must be factored in if high precision is required, though standard clinical practice often relies on manufacturer-specified dilution charts found on vial inserts.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Reconstitution and Dilution

  1. Inspect Vials: Check drug labels, expiration dates, and clarity.
  2. Cleanse Rubbers: Scrub the rubber septum of both the diluent and medication vials with an alcohol swab.
  3. Inject Diluent: Draw the required volume of diluent and inject it into the powder vial.
  4. Mix Gently: Roll or swirl the vial between your palms until the powder is completely dissolved. Do not shake vigorously to avoid foaming.
  5. Withdraw Solution: Invert the vial and withdraw the reconstituted mixture into the syringe.

Safety Protocols, Aseptic Techniques, and Preventing Errors

Medication errors during dilution can lead to catastrophic patient outcomes. Always perform the three medication checks: when retrieving the drug, when preparing the drug, and before administration. Never leave a prepared syringe unattended at a bedside table or medication trolley. Adhering to strict aseptic non-touch technique (ANTT) protects sterile parts from contamination by unwashed hands or unsterile surfaces.

Documentation and Post-Administration Monitoring

Accurate recording completes the medication administration cycle. Immediately after giving the diluted injection, document the drug name, exact dose, route, time, and your signature in the patient chart. Monitor the insertion site for signs of infiltration, redness, or swelling. Observe the patient for expected therapeutic responses as well as adverse systemic reactions such as allergic anaphylaxis or hypotension.
