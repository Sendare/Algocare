Dilution of Injections

Principles and Indications for Diluting Injections

Dilution of injections involves mixing a concentrated medication with a sterile liquid, known as a diluent, before administration. Nurses perform this procedure to achieve specific clinical goals that protect the patient and ensure accurate dosing.

  • Tissue Protection: Highly concentrated medications can irritate blood vessels or subcutaneous tissue, causing pain, phlebitis, or necrosis. Dilution reduces this localized irritation.
  • Rate Control: Some powerful drugs must be given very slowly. Diluting them in a larger fluid volume allows the nurse to regulate the infusion rate safely.
  • Reconstitution: Medications supplied as dry powders lack stability in liquid form. They must be diluted into a liquid state before they can be drawn into a syringe or added to an IV bag.
  • Pediatric Safety: Infants and small children require extremely small doses. Dilution makes it possible to measure these tiny amounts accurately without risking massive overdose from a concentrated stock solution.

Understanding Diluents and Drug Compatibility

Choosing the correct liquid to dilute a drug is just as important as measuring the right dose. Using the wrong fluid can cause the medication to precipitate, meaning it forms solid particles that can block blood vessels and harm the patient.

Common diluents include sterile water for injection, 0.9% sodium chloride (normal saline), and 5% dextrose in water. Each drug monograph specifies which diluents are safe to use.

Drug compatibility refers to how different substances react when mixed together. Physical incompatibility is visible, such as cloudiness, color changes, or crystals forming in the syringe. Chemical incompatibility is invisible and happens when one drug breaks down or inactivates another. Nurses must consult a drug reference guide or pharmacist before mixing any medications in the same syringe or IV line.

Calculating Doses and Dilution Ratios

Accurate calculation is vital during drug dilution. Nurses use simple proportions and formulas to find the exact amount of stock drug and diluent needed.

The standard formula is: $$\frac{ ext{Desired Dose}}{ ext{Stock Dose}} \times \text{Stock Volume} = ext{Amount to Administer}$$

When reconstituting a powder, the nurse must also account for displacement volume. Some powders take up physical space inside the vial once liquid is added. For example, adding 5 mL of diluent to a vial might result in a total final volume of 5.5 mL because the powder itself adds volume. Ignoring displacement volume leads to an unintended lower concentration and a weaker dose than prescribed.

Equipment and Aseptic Preparation

Preparing injections requires a clean workspace and strict adherence to aseptic technique to prevent microbial contamination.

  1. Syringes and Needles: Select the correct size syringe to ensure measurement accuracy. Use blunt fill needles or filter needles when drawing from glass ampoules to keep glass shards out of the patient.
  2. Alcohol Swabs: Vials and ampoule necks must be scrubbed vigorously with an alcohol swab and allowed to dry completely before puncture.
  3. Diluent Vials: Inspect all vials and ampoules for cracks, particles, or expiration dates before opening.

A common mistake is touching the sterile needle or the tip of the syringe plunger during handling. Once a sterile item touches a non-sterile surface, it is contaminated and must be discarded immediately.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Reconstitution and Dilution

Executing the dilution process requires a steady, methodical approach to ensure patient safety and medication stability.

  • Check Orders: Verify the medication order against the patient's chart and check for allergies.
  • Clean the Vial: Remove the plastic cap and scrub the rubber stopper of both the drug vial and the diluent vial with an antiseptic swab.
  • Draw Diluent: Pull back the syringe plunger to the volume of diluent required, inject air into the diluent vial, and withdraw the exact amount of liquid.
  • Mix the Drug: Inject the diluent slowly into the powder vial. Do not force the plunger.
  • Dissolve Completely: Gently roll or swirl the vial between your hands until the powder dissolves fully. Avoid vigorous shaking, as this creates foam and bubbles that make accurate measurement difficult.

Safety Protocols, Labeling, and Post-Administration Monitoring

The dilution process is incomplete until the prepared medication is properly labeled and the patient is monitored for adverse reactions.

Prepared syringes or IV bags containing diluted medications must be labeled immediately with the patient's name, drug name, dose, date, time, and the nurse's initials. Unlabeled medications left at a bedside create a severe risk of medication errors.

After administering the diluted injection, the nurse remains alert for signs of an allergic reaction, such as rash, itching, or difficulty breathing, especially with new medications. For intravenous dilutions, monitor the injection site closely for swelling, pain, or coolness, which indicate infiltration or extravasation.
