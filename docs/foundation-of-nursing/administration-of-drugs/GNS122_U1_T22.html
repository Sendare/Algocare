Dilution of Injections

Principles and Indications for Diluting Injections

Dilution of injections involves mixing a concentrated medication with a sterile liquid, known as a diluent, before administration. Nurses perform this procedure to achieve specific clinical goals.

  • Reduced tissue irritation: Highly concentrated drugs can cause severe pain, inflammation, or necrosis at the injection site.
  • Accurate dosing: Small volume requirements for potent drugs are difficult to measure accurately without dilution.
  • Controlled infusion rates: Intravenous medications often require dilution to allow safe administration over a set period.
  • Enhanced solubility: Some powdered medications, particularly antibiotics, are unstable in liquid form and must be stored as powders until immediately before use.

Choosing Diluents and Ensuring Drug Compatibility

Selecting the correct diluent is critical for patient safety and drug efficacy. Manufacturers specify approved diluents, commonly Sterile Water for Injection, 0.9% Sodium Chloride (Normal Saline), or 5% Dextrose in Water.

Mixing incompatible drugs or using the wrong diluent can cause precipitation, where solid particles form in the solution, or chemical inactivation of the active ingredient. Nurses must check the drug manufacturer's guidelines every time a new medication is prepared. Visual inspection of the reconstituted solution for discoloration, cloudiness, or particulate matter is mandatory before administration.

Equipment and Aseptic Technique

Preparation requires meticulous attention to infection prevention. The nurse must gather a sterile syringe, appropriate gauge needles or blunt fill needles, an alcohol swab, the medication vial, and the correct diluent.

The work surface must be clean and uncluttered. Aseptic technique prevents microbial contamination of the sterile pathways. The nurse must scrub the rubber septum of both the medication vial and the diluent vial with an antiseptic swab and allow it to air dry completely before needle insertion. Touching the needle or the syringe plunger tip breaks sterility and mandates immediate replacement of the equipment.

Calculating Volumes and Dilution Ratios

Accurate dose calculation prevents medication errors. When dealing with powdered medications in vials, the nurse must account for the displacement volume—the space occupied by the powder itself once liquid is added.

To find the final concentration, use the formula: $$\text{Concentration} = \frac{\text{Total Mass of Drug}}{\text{Total Volume of Diluent + Powder Displacement}}$

For example, if a 1-gram vial of medication requires 9 mL of diluent to yield a total volume of 10 mL, the final concentration is 100 mg/mL. Failing to account for powder displacement leads to administering a weaker dose than prescribed.

Step-by-Step Reconstitution Procedure

  1. Check the order: Verify the medication name, dose, patient identity, and diluent type against the prescription chart.
  2. Cleanse vials: Wipe the rubber tops of both the diluent and medication vials with an alcohol swab.
  3. Draw air: Pull an amount of air into the syringe equal to the required volume of diluent.
  4. Inject air: Insert the needle into the diluent vial and inject the air, then withdraw the precise volume of diluent.
  5. Reconstitute: Inject the diluent into the powdered medication vial.
  6. Mix gently: Roll or swirl the vial between your hands until the powder is fully dissolved. Do not shake vigorously, as this creates air bubbles and can degrade certain proteins.

Safety Protocols and Error Prevention

Medication preparation errors during dilution can lead to severe patient harm. Nurses must practice the rights of medication administration every time a drug is prepared.

  • Labeling: Every prepared syringe or IV bag must be labeled immediately with the patient's name, drug name, dose, concentration, date, and time of preparation. Unlabeled syringes present a severe hazard.
  • Double-checking: High-risk medications, such as concentrated electrolytes or pediatric doses, require an independent double-check by a second nurse.
  • Needle safety: Never recap needles using two hands. Use safety devices or a single-handed scoop technique to prevent needle-stick injuries.

Documentation and Post-Administration Monitoring

Once the diluted injection is administered, prompt documentation is required. Record the medication name, exact dose, route, site of injection, time, and the nurse's signature in the patient's chart.

Post-administration monitoring is essential to catch early signs of adverse reactions, such as allergic responses, phlebitis, or infiltration at the site. For IV medications, monitor the infusion site frequently for swelling, coolness, or pain. Educate the patient to report any unusual sensations, itching, or difficulty breathing immediately.
