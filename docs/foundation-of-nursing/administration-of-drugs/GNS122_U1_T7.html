Subcutaneous Injection

Introduction and Purpose of Subcutaneous Injections

A subcutaneous (SC) injection is administered into the layer of fat and connective tissue located directly beneath the dermis and above the muscle layer. Because adipose tissue has a poor blood supply compared to muscle, medications injected here are absorbed slowly and at a sustained rate. This makes the route ideal for drugs that require a slow, steady release rather than an immediate systemic rush.

Common medications given via the subcutaneous route include insulin for diabetes management, low molecular weight heparins like enoxaparin for blood clot prevention, and certain vaccines. Nurses must always verify the medication order and confirm that the drug is specifically formulated for subcutaneous delivery, as giving an intramuscular or intravenous drug into the fat layer can lead to poor absorption, tissue damage, or severe adverse reactions.

Anatomy of Subcutaneous Tissue

The subcutaneous layer, also called the hypodermis, consists primarily of adipose and loose connective tissue. This layer acts as a cushion for the body, provides thermal insulation, and stores energy.

  • Blood supply: Minimal blood vessels, which explains the slower absorption rate of medications.
  • Sensory nerve endings: Present in moderate amounts, meaning injections can cause discomfort or stinging, though generally less painful than deep intramuscular routes.
  • Thickness variation: The depth of this layer varies wildly depending on the patient's age, nutritional status, and specific body site. A thin elderly patient has a very shallow fat layer compared to a well-nourished adult, directly dictating needle length and insertion angle choices.

Selecting and Assessing Injection Sites

Choosing the right site ensures proper drug absorption and minimizes localized tissue damage. Common sites include the outer posterior aspect of the upper arms, the abdomen (avoiding a two-inch circle around the umbilicus), the anterior thighs, and the scapular areas of the upper back.

Rotation of sites is critical, especially for patients requiring daily injections like insulin. Repeatedly injecting into the exact same spot leads to lipohypertrophy (thickening of the fat) or lipoatrophy (breakdown of fat), both of which ruin reliable drug absorption. When assessing a chosen site, the nurse must inspect the skin for bruising, inflammation, scarring, edema, or lumps before cleaning it.

Equipment and Medication Preparation

Gathering the correct equipment before entering the patient's room saves time and maintains strict asepsis. Standard supplies include a prescribed medication vial or pre-filled pen, a syringe sized appropriately for the dose (usually 1 mL to 3 mL), and a needle sized for subcutaneous use.

  • Needle gauge: Typically 25 to 30 gauge.
  • Needle length: Usually 3/8 inch to 5/8 inch.
  • Alcohol swabs: For disinfecting the vial top and skin.

When withdrawing medication from a vial, the nurse cleans the rubber stopper with alcohol, injects an amount of air equal to the dose into the vial to prevent a vacuum, and then inverts the vial to draw out the exact liquid volume. Always check the medication label three times against the medication administration record to prevent errors.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Executing the injection smoothly reduces patient anxiety and discomfort. Begin by performing hand hygiene and putting on clean gloves. Clean the selected skin site with an alcohol swab using a firm circular motion, moving from the center outward, and let it dry completely.

  1. Pinch the skin: Gently pinch or bunch a fold of skin and fat using your non-dominant thumb and forefinger to elevate the tissue away from the muscle.
  2. Insert the needle: Hold the syringe like a dart and insert the needle quickly at a 45-degree angle (for a 5/8-inch needle in a thin person) or a 90-degree angle (for a 3/8-inch needle or in an ample skin fold).
  3. Deliver the medication: Release the pinched skin once the needle is in, stabilize the syringe, and push the plunger slowly to inject the drug.
  4. Withdraw and dispose: Pull the needle out swiftly at the same angle it entered, engage the safety device immediately without recapping, and discard it into a sharps container.

Patient Safety and Infection Control

Patient safety centers on preventing medication errors and healthcare-associated infections. Always perform the standard three medication checks and verify the patient's identity using two unique identifiers before administration.

Aseptic technique must be maintained throughout. Never touch the sterile needle or the rubber stopper of the vial after it has been cleansed. If a needle touches any unsterile surface, discard it immediately. Additionally, never recap a used needle by hand, as this is a primary cause of accidental needle-stick injuries in clinical settings. Utilize engineered sharps safety devices provided by your facility.

Complications and Post-Injection Monitoring

Although subcutaneous injections are generally safe, various complications can occur if proper technique is neglected. Common issues include localized pain, bruising, erythema, and hard lumps from poor site rotation. More serious risks include sterile abscesses, nerve injury, or allergic reactions.

After administration, the nurse must monitor the patient for adverse drug reactions, especially when giving new medications, insulin, or anticoagulants. Instruct the patient to report signs of excessive bleeding, severe burning, rash, or unusual swelling at the site. Document the procedure accurately in the patient chart, including the exact site used, medication name, dose, time, and the patient's physiological response.
