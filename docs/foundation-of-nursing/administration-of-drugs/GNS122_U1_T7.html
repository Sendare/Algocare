Subcutaneous Injection

Introduction and Purpose of Subcutaneous Injections

A subcutaneous (SC) injection is administered into the layer of connective tissue directly beneath the dermis and above the muscle layer. Because adipose tissue has relatively few blood vessels compared to muscle, medication absorption is slower and more sustained, making this route ideal for drugs that require a prolonged, steady release. Common medications delivered subcutaneously include insulin for glycemic control, low molecular weight heparins like enoxaparin for thrombosis prevention, and certain immunizations. Clinical trials and pharmacokinetic studies consistently demonstrate that SC absorption rates remain remarkably stable over several hours, minimizing peaks and troughs in systemic drug concentration. In busy Nigerian hospital wards and community health outreaches, mastering this route is essential for chronic disease management, particularly diabetes mellitus, where patients often learn self-administration before discharge.

Anatomy and Physiology of Subcutaneous Tissue

The subcutaneous layer, or hypodermis, sits immediately beneath the dermis and consists primarily of loose connective tissue and adipose cells. This layer acts as an effective heat insulator and a mechanical shock absorber for underlying structures. Fat cells store energy, while the interwoven network of collagen and elastin fibers provides structural elasticity. Blood supply to the hypodermis is sparse relative to muscle and skin, which directly dictates how injected drugs behave pharmacokinetically. Small capillaries permeate the fat lobules, slowly taking up water-soluble molecules. Nerve endings are present but less densely packed than in the overlying dermis, meaning SC injections are generally less painful than intradermal pricks, provided the needle does not breach the underlying muscle fascia.

Selecting and Assessing Injection Sites

- **Abdomen**: The preferred site for insulin administration because of rapid, consistent absorption; keep at least two inches away from the umbilicus. - **Outer posterior arms**: Offers a reliable fat layer, useful when abdominal sites are unavailable or rotated. - **Anterior and lateral thighs**: Frequently utilized for self-injection or in pediatric populations where arm and abdominal fat may be minimal. - **Scapular area of the upper back**: Sometimes used for palliative or continuous subcutaneous infusions. Site rotation is paramount to prevent lipodystrophy—either lipohypertrophy (fat accumulation) or lipoatrophy (fat wasting)—which severely impairs insulin absorption. Nurses must inspect and palpate sites before every injection, checking for edema, bruising, scars, inflammation, or hardened lumps.

Equipment Selection and Medication Preparation

Proper equipment ensures accurate dosing and minimizes tissue trauma. Standard subcutaneous syringes range from 0.5 mL to 3 mL, with insulin syringes calibrated specifically in international units (Units). Needle selection depends on the patient's body habitus, age, and injection site: typically, a gauge of 25 to 30 and a length between 3/8 inch (9 mm) and 5/8 inch (16 mm) are standard. Shorter needles, such as 4 mm or 5 mm pen needles, are widely favored because they reliably reach the subcutaneous layer while virtually eliminating the risk of accidental intramuscular injection. When preparing medications, verify the drug order against the patient chart three times. Cleanse multidose vial rubber stoppers with an alcohol swab and allow them to air dry before piercing. If mixing two types of insulin, always draw up the clear (regular) insulin before the cloudy (NPH) insulin to avoid contaminating the short-acting vial with intermediate-acting suspension.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

1. **Perform hand hygiene and don clean gloves**: Essential for breaking the chain of infection before handling sterile equipment. 2. **Cleanse the selected site**: Use an antiseptic wipe in a circular motion, moving from the center outward for about 5 cm, and let it dry completely. 3. **Grasp or pinch the tissue**: Form a skin fold using the thumb and forefinger to lift subcutaneous fat away from underlying muscle, particularly in leaner patients. 4. **Insert the needle**: Dart the needle quickly at a 45-degree angle for a 1-inch fold, or a 90-degree angle if a 2-inch fold can be grasped or a very short needle is used. 5. **Inject the medication slowly**: Depress the plunger steadily without aspirating (as aspiration is no longer recommended for routine subcutaneous injections). 6. **Withdraw the needle smoothly**: Pull out at the same angle it entered, releasing the skin fold, and apply gentle pressure with a dry swab if needed.

Patient Safety, Comfort, and Infection Control

Safety measures protect both the patient and the healthcare provider. Never recap used needles using a two-handed technique; instead, use a one-handed scoop method or drop the unrecapped needle directly into a puncture-resistant sharps container located at the point of use. Minimizing discomfort builds patient trust and adherence. Ensure alcohol on the skin has dried completely before puncture, as wet antiseptic entering the tissue causes a stinging sensation. Inserting the needle with a quick, dart-like motion reduces pain fiber stimulation. Engaging the patient in conversation or asking them to take a deep breath during insertion can also distract from procedural anxiety.

Complications and Post-Injection Monitoring

Although subcutaneous injections are generally safe, complications can arise if technique or site management is flawed. Localized reactions include bruising, hematoma formation, sterile abscesses, and skin necrosis. Systemic complications, though rare, involve allergic hypersensitivity or accidental intravenous administration resulting in rapid, untoward drug effects. Post-injection monitoring requires observing the patient for therapeutic response and adverse effects, especially when administering high-alert medications like insulin or anticoagulants. Check injection sites during subsequent shifts for signs of inflammation, induration, or bleeding. For anticoagulants, instruct patients to report unusual bruising, bleeding gums, or dark tarry stools.
