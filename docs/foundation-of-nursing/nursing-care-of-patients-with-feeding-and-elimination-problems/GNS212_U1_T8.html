Faecal Incontinence

Introduction and Definition of Faecal Incontinence

Faecal incontinence is the inability to control bowel movements, resulting in the involuntary passing of stool or gas. It ranges from occasional leakage of liquid stool during flatulence to a complete loss of bowel control. While often viewed as a normal part of aging by some patients, it is a medical symptom that profoundly disrupts daily living, social interactions, and personal dignity. In many local communities, patients hide the condition out of shame, delaying early diagnosis and appropriate care.

Anatomy and Physiology of Normal Bowel Control

Normal bowel control relies on a complex coordination of muscles, nerves, and sensory feedback loops. The bowel stores stool in the rectum until defecation is socially convenient.

  • Internal Anal Sphincter: An involuntary smooth muscle that maintains resting closure of the anus.
  • External Anal Sphincter: A voluntary skeletal muscle that provides extra closure strength, especially during sudden increases in intra-abdominal pressure.
  • Puborectalis Muscle: Forms a sling around the rectum, creating an acute anorectal angle that helps maintain fecal continence.
  • Rectal Compliance: The ability of the rectum to stretch and accommodate stool without triggering an immediate urgent need to defecate.

Etiology and Common Causes in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Faecal incontinence stems from various physical and neurological disruptions. In Nigerian clinical practice, specific local and regional etiologies frequently present in hospital wards and outpatient clinics.

  • Obstetric Trauma: Perineal tears during difficult or unassisted home deliveries, including severe obstetric fistulas.
  • Neurological Impairment: Stroke, spinal cord injuries, and diabetic neuropathy affecting nerve signals to the bowel.
  • Severe Diarrheal Illnesses: Infections causing urgent, watery diarrhea that overwhelm a normal sphincter mechanism.
  • Pelvic Surgeries: Previous rectal or anal operations that inadvertently damage the sphincter muscles.

Pathophysiology and Types of Faecal Incontinence

Understanding why leakage occurs requires looking at the two main functional mechanisms: urge incontinence and passive incontinence.

Urge incontinence happens when the patient feels the normal urge to defecate but cannot reach the toilet in time due to weak sphincters or severe diarrhea. Passive incontinence occurs without any prior warning or sensation; the patient leaks stool or mucus simply because the sphincter is loose or damaged, and the rectal nerves fail to register the presence of faeces.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

An accurate nursing assessment forms the foundation of effective care. Nurses must take a detailed, sensitive bowel history, asking about stool consistency, frequency, and leakage episodes. Using a validated bowel diary helps track patterns over several days. Physical assessment involves inspecting the perianal area for skin breakdown, scars, or gaping of the anus, followed by assessing voluntary anal squeeze pressure during a digital rectal examination.

Comprehensive Nursing Care and Bowel Training Management

Managing faecal incontinence requires structured routines aimed at restoring predictability. Bowel training programs encourage regular evacuation times, usually after meals when gastrocolic reflexes are strongest. Dietary modifications are equally important; increasing soluble fiber helps bulk the stool, while identifying and avoiding individual food triggers reduces loose stools. Nurses also assist with scheduling toileting times and ensuring easy access to commodes.

Skin Care and Prevention of Perineal Dermatitis

Stool contains digestive enzymes and bacteria that quickly break down delicate perineal skin. Perineal dermatitis is a painful complication that causes redness, burning, and skin breakdown. Nursing care focuses on prompt hygiene: gently cleansing the area with mild soap and warm water after each episode, patting dry rather than rubbing, and applying a moisture barrier cream to protect the skin from direct contact with moisture and faecal matter.

Psychosocial Support and Complications Management

The emotional toll of faecal incontinence is heavy, often leading to anxiety, depression, and social isolation. Patients frequently worry about odor and sudden accidents in public. Nurses provide empathetic listening, reassure patients that they are not alone, and introduce practical coping strategies such as using discreet incontinence pads and carrying a change of clothing. Involving family caregivers with the patient's consent helps build a strong support system at home.
