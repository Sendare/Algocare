Insertion of Flatus Tube

Introduction, Purpose, and Anatomy

A flatus tube, also known as a rectal tube, is a soft, flexible catheter inserted through the anus and rectum to relieve severe abdominal distention caused by trapped gas and flatus. When patients cannot expel gas naturally—often following abdominal surgery, due to paralytic ileus, or from severe constipation—pressure builds up in the lower bowel, causing sharp cramping and discomfort.

The lower gastrointestinal tract plays a direct role here. The rectum stores fecal matter and gas before elimination, and the anal canal is guarded by the internal and external anal sphincters. Inserting the tube safely requires navigating these sphincters and following the natural curve of the rectosigmoid junction. If trapped gas cannot escape on its own, a temporary conduit like a flatus tube provides immediate mechanical relief by bridging the anal sphincters and allowing gas to vent outward into a collection bag or bedpan.

Indications, Contraindications, and Equipment

Flatus tubes are reserved for situations where non-invasive measures—such as early ambulation, repositioning, or simple gas-expulsion exercises—have failed. Common indications include postoperative gaseous distension, flatulence associated with paralytic ileus, and severe abdominal cramping due to trapped wind.

However, this procedure is not safe for everyone. Contraindications include recent rectal or anal surgery, severe hemorrhoids, rectal bleeding, or inflammatory bowel conditions where mucosal tissue is fragile and prone to tearing.

Before beginning, gather the necessary supplies:

  • Clean gloves and a disposable waterproof pad
  • Water-soluble lubricant
  • The appropriate size flatus tube (usually soft rubber or silicone)
  • Adhesive tape or securing device
  • Bedpan or a collection bag

Patient Preparation and Communication

Because the procedure involves a private and sensitive area of the body, clear communication is essential to ease patient anxiety and secure cooperation. Explain the steps of the procedure simply, emphasizing that while it may feel uncomfortable or awkward, it should not cause sharp pain. Assure the patient that privacy will be maintained throughout.

Position the patient comfortably on their left side in the Sims' position, with the top leg flexed toward the chest. This position aligns with the natural downward curve of the sigmoid colon and rectum, making insertion smoother and less traumatic. Provide a protective waterproof pad under the patient's buttocks to catch any accidental leakage during the procedure.

Step-by-Step Insertion Procedure

  1. Hand Hygiene and Gloves: Perform thorough hand hygiene and put on clean gloves.
  2. Lubrication: Generously lubricate the tip of the flatus tube with a water-soluble lubricant to minimize friction and prevent mucosal trauma.
  3. Insertion: Gently insert the lubricated tip through the anus, directing it toward the umbilicus approximately 7 to 10 centimeters (3 to 4 inches) in an adult. Never force the tube against resistance.
  4. Securing and Venting: Secure the outer end of the tube with tape to the patient's thigh or let it drain into an appropriate collection container.
  5. Duration: Leave the tube in place only as long as necessary, typically no longer than 20 to 30 minutes, to prevent pressure necrosis of the rectal mucosa.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Patient Comfort

Once the flatus tube is removed, clean the anal area gently with warm soapy water and pat it dry to prevent skin irritation from moisture or fecal residue. Reposition the patient into a comfortable posture and encourage deep breathing or mild movement if permitted.

Monitor the patient closely for the return of normal bowel sounds and assess whether abdominal distention and pain have decreased. If gas continues to trap, consult the primary care provider before attempting another insertion. Keep in mind that leaving a rectal tube in place for too long can impair the natural sphincter tone and damage the blood supply to the lining of the rectum.

Complications and Documentation

Although helpful, flatus tube insertion carries risks if performed incorrectly. Common complications include rectal mucosal trauma, bleeding, and accidental perforation. Prolonged use can also lead to loss of sphincter control and ulceration from continuous pressure.

Accurate documentation is a vital legal and clinical responsibility. Record the following details in the patient's chart:

  • Date and time of the procedure
  • Reason for insertion and patient response
  • Size of the tube used and depth of insertion
  • Amount and type of gas or stool expelled
  • Patient tolerance and condition of the perianal skin upon removal
