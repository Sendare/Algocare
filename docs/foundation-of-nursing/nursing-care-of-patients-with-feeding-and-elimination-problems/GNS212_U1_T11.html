Insertion of Flatus Tube

Introduction and Purpose of Flatus Tube Insertion

The insertion of a flatus tube, also referred to as a rectal tube, is a specialized nursing procedure designed to relieve acute gaseous distension in the lower bowel. When trapped gas cannot be expelled naturally due to postoperative ileus, sluggish peristalsis, or weakened anal sphincter tone, the tube provides a direct conduit for flatus to escape into an appropriate receptacle or collection bag. This mechanical decompression prevents painful abdominal cramping, reduces diaphragmatic elevation that can otherwise compromise respiratory status, and protects surgical suture lines from excessive intraluminal pressure. Unlike a standard enema tube, which is used for fluid instillation, a flatus tube remains in place for a strictly limited duration—usually no longer than twenty to thirty minutes—to prevent mucosal ischemia, necrosis, or loss of normal sphincter responsiveness.

Relevant Anatomy and Physiology of the Lower Gastrointestinal Tract

Successful execution of this procedure requires a firm grasp of rectosigmoid anatomy. The large intestine terminates in the rectum, a fifteen-centimeter muscular tube that follows the curve of the sacrum and coccyx. As the rectum approaches the pelvic floor, it narrows into the anal canal, which is guarded by two distinct muscular rings: the internal anal sphincter (involuntary smooth muscle) and the external anal sphincter (voluntary skeletal muscle).

When gas accumulates in the sigmoid colon and upper rectum, normal defecation reflexes are triggered. However, if neuromuscular coordination fails, gas becomes trapped behind the closed sphincters. The clinician must navigate two notable physiological bends—the rectal valves of Houston—during insertion, advancing the tube gently following the natural posterior curvature of the spinal column to avoid traumatic perforation of the anterior rectal wall.

Indications and Contraindications

  • Indications: Severe abdominal distension secondary to postoperative flatus accumulation, failure to pass flatus following abdominal surgery, or excessive gas retention causing patient distress.
  • Contraindications: Recent rectal or colonic anastomoses, severe hemorrhoidal disease with active bleeding, rectal prolapse, inflammatory bowel disease flares, and neutropenic or thrombocytopenic states where mucosal trauma could trigger uncontrollable hemorrhage or severe infection.

Required Equipment and Materials

Gathering appropriate supplies before approaching the bedside ensures an efficient, sterile, and uninterrupted procedure.

  1. Flatus tube: Typically a rubber or silicone French catheter (sizes 22 to 30 Fr for adults).
  2. Water-soluble lubricant: Essential to minimize friction and prevent trauma to the delicate anal mucosa.
  3. Clean gloves: Worn throughout the procedure to maintain standard precautions.
  4. Waterproof pad: Placed under the patient's buttocks to protect bed linen from accidental leakage of stool or mucus.
  5. Container or collection bag: To catch escaping gas, liquid stool, or mucus.
  6. Tissue paper or wipes: For cleaning the perianal area upon tube removal.

Patient Preparation and Communication

Because rectal procedures induce anxiety and vulnerability, clear communication is paramount. Explain the rationale for the intervention, what sensations to expect (such as a feeling of fullness or an urgent urge to defecate), and how the patient can assist by taking slow, deep breaths to relax the anal sphincters. Position the patient in a left lateral Sims position, which naturally aligns with the downward curve of the sigmoid colon and promotes relaxation of the gluteal muscles. Provide adequate privacy with screens or curtains, and uncover only the immediate perianal region.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Flatus Tube Insertion

Perform hand hygiene and don clean gloves. Generously lubricate the distal 7 to 10 centimeters of the flatus tube. Separate the patient's buttocks with your non-dominant hand to clearly visualize the anal opening. Ask the patient to take a deep breath, and gently insert the lubricated tip through the anus, aiming toward the umbilicus initially for about 2 to 3 centimeters, then redirecting the tube slightly backward along the curve of the rectum. Advance the tube slowly for approximately 10 to 15 centimeters in adults. Never use force; if resistance is met, stop immediately. Secure the outer end of the tube to the patient's thigh with tape or direct it into a collection container, leaving it in place for no longer than twenty minutes.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Patient Comfort

Monitor the patient continuously while the tube is in place, observing for the expulsion of gas, the sound of escaping air, or the presence of fecal drainage in the collection container. Check the patient's abdomen periodically for a decrease in distension and softness upon palpation. Once the designated time has elapsed, gently remove the tube, wrap the soiled end within a paper towel, and discard it safely. Cleanse the perianal area thoroughly and apply a protective barrier cream if skin irritation is present. Assist the patient into a comfortable position, check vital signs, and encourage early ambulation if permitted to stimulate natural peristalsis.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although generally safe when performed correctly, flatus tube insertion carries specific risks. Rectal mucosal trauma and bleeding can occur from rough handling or inadequate lubrication. Prolonged retention can lead to pressure necrosis of the bowel wall, ulceration, or loss of normal sphincter tone. Vagal stimulation resulting from rectal manipulation may occasionally trigger bradycardia or a vasovagal syncopal episode. If the patient reports dizziness, diaphoresis, or feels faint during the procedure, remove the tube immediately, lower the head of the bed, and monitor vital signs closely.

Documentation and Reporting in Nursing Practice

Accurate documentation in the clinical record provides legal protection and continuity of care. Record the exact date and time of insertion, the size of the tube used, the depth of insertion, the patient's tolerance of the procedure, and the character and approximate volume of gas or fecal matter expelled. Document the duration the tube remained in place and the condition of the perianal skin upon removal. Report any unusual findings immediately to the attending physician, such as frank rectal bleeding, severe unrelieved pain, or signs of a vasovagal reaction.
