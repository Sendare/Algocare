Diarrhoea

Introduction and Definition of Diarrhoea

Diarrhoea is a common gastrointestinal condition characterized by an increase in the frequency, fluidity, or volume of bowel movements. Clinically, it involves passing three or more loose or watery stools per day. While many people experience short-term bouts after eating contaminated food, persistent changes in bowel habits warrant a closer look.

The condition is broadly categorized by its duration. Acute diarrhoea lasts for less than fourteen days and is usually caused by viral, bacterial, or parasitic infections. Persistent diarrhoea lasts between fourteen and thirty days, whereas chronic diarrhoea extends beyond four weeks and typically points to non-infectious, underlying systemic disorders such as inflammatory bowel disease or malabsorption syndromes.

Pathophysiology and Mechanisms of Diarrhoea

To understand why watery stools occur, you must look at how the intestines handle fluid. Normally, the small and large intestines absorb most of the water we ingest or secrete through digestive juices. Diarrhoea happens when this delicate balance shifts toward net fluid loss.

  • Secretory diarrhoea: Active ion secretion by intestinal epithelial cells pulls water into the lumen, often caused by bacterial toxins like cholera.
  • Osmotic diarrhoea: Unabsorbed solutes remain in the bowel lumen, holding onto water through osmotic pressure, seen in lactose intolerance.
  • Exudative diarrhoea: Inflammation and ulceration of the intestinal mucosa cause weeping of blood, mucus, and protein-rich fluids into the gut.
  • Motility-related diarrhoea: Rapid transit time through the intestines prevents adequate water reabsorption, often driven by neuromuscular disorders or laxative abuse.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Patients presenting with diarrhoea display a range of signs and symptoms depending on the underlying cause and severity of fluid loss. The most obvious sign is frequent watery or loose stools, but nurses must look out for accompanying systemic indicators. Abdominal cramping, nausea, vomiting, and low-grade fever frequently accompany infectious etiologies.

Assessment must focus heavily on detecting dehydration early. Nurses should evaluate skin turgor, mucous membrane dryness, sunken eyes, capillary refill time, and changes in mental status. Monitoring daily weight and measuring exact intake and output are vital nursing actions. A patient who is dizzy upon standing or passing very dark, concentrated urine is signaling significant intravascular volume depletion.

Diagnostic Investigations

When diarrhoea is persistent, bloody, or accompanied by systemic illness like high fever, diagnostic workup becomes necessary. Laboratory tests help pinpoint whether the origin is infectious, inflammatory, or metabolic.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management of diarrhoea centers on treating the root cause, correcting electrolyte imbalances, and halting excessive bowel movements when appropriate. For mild acute cases, antibiotics are usually avoided because many viral infections are self-limiting. However, confirmed bacterial or parasitic infections require targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Antidiarrheal agents, such as loperamide, slow intestinal motility and decrease stool frequency. A common clinical mistake is giving motility inhibitors to patients with invasive bacterial infections or C. difficile, as trapping the toxins can lead to toxic megacolon. Therefore, antimotility drugs should be reserved for non-inflammatory watery diarrhoea. Bismuth subsalicylate can also help reduce secretions and provide mild antimicrobial action in traveler's diarrhoea.

Nursing Care and Fluid Replacement Therapy

The cornerstone of nursing care for a patient with diarrhoea is aggressive prevention and treatment of dehydration. Oral rehydration therapy (ORT) containing precise ratios of salts and glucose is the most effective and physiological method for replacing lost fluids and electrolytes.

Nurses must monitor intravenous fluid infusions closely if the patient is severely dehydrated or unable to take fluids by mouth. Perineal skin breakdown is a frequent complication due to the frequent passage of acidic, watery stools. Nursing interventions must include gentle cleansing of the perianal area with warm water, patting dry, and applying protective barrier creams. Encouraging small, frequent sips of fluid rather than large volumes at once helps prevent triggering further nausea and vomiting.

Complications and Patient Education

Unmanaged or severe diarrhoea can lead to life-threatening complications that require vigilant nursing observation. The most common acute danger is severe dehydration leading to hypovolemic shock and acute kidney injury. Additionally, profound loss of bicarbonate-rich intestinal fluids can trigger metabolic acidosis, presenting with rapid, deep breathing known as Kussmaul respirations.

Patient education plays a vital role in preventing recurrence and managing recovery at home. Nurses should instruct patients on proper hand hygiene, safe food handling, and drinking safe, treated water. During recovery, patients should gradually reintroduce bland foods like bananas, rice, applesauce, and toast while avoiding dairy, caffeine, and fatty foods that can irritate the healing gut.
