Diarrhoea

Introduction and Definition of Diarrhoea

Diarrhoea is clinically defined as the passage of three or more loose or liquid stools per day, or a frequency that is abnormal for the individual. In local Nigerian clinical settings, distinguishing between acute and chronic presentations is vital during initial patient triage. Acute diarrhoea lasts less than fourteen days, while chronic diarrhoea persists beyond four weeks. The consistency of the stool matters more than the actual bulk; water content dictates stool form. Understanding this baseline definition helps healthcare providers quickly separate self-limiting viral gastroenteritis from persistent malabsorptive disorders that require intensive investigation.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The disruption of normal intestinal fluid and electrolyte transport drives the pathophysiology of diarrhoea. The bowel normally absorbs the vast majority of fluid entering the tract, but various pathological mechanisms overwhelm this capacity:

  • Osmotic Diarrhoea: Unabsorbed solutes draw water into the intestinal lumen, ceasing when the offending substance is withheld.
  • Secretory Diarrhoea: Active ion secretion by intestinal epithelial cells continues despite fasting, driven by bacterial enterotoxins.
  • Exudative Diarrhoea: Mucosal inflammation and ulceration lead to weeping of plasma, blood, and mucus into the gut.
  • Motility-related Diarrhoea: Rapid transit time prevents adequate water reabsorption from the fecal mass.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

A thorough patient assessment moves beyond counting stool frequency to evaluating systemic hydration status and identifying dangerous alarm features. Clinicians must actively search for signs of volume depletion such as sunken eyes, dry mucous membranes, poor skin turgor, and altered mental status. In pediatric patients across community health posts in Nigeria, the WHO skin pinch test remains a reliable indicator of severe dehydration. Furthermore, healthcare workers should inspect stool characteristics—watery stools suggest viral or enterotoxigenic bacterial agents, while blood and mucus point toward invasive bacterial or parasitic pathogens like Shigella or Entamoeba histolytica.

Diagnostic Investigations

While mild acute diarrhoea is often managed empirically without laboratory testing, targeted investigations are necessary for persistent symptoms, systemic toxicity, or suspected outbreaks like cholera. Stool microscopy, culture, and sensitivity tests identify specific bacterial or parasitic culprits. Electrolyte panels and renal function tests uncover dangerous imbalances in sodium, potassium, and serum creatinine resulting from severe fluid loss. Complete blood counts help differentiate viral infections from severe bacterial inflammation marked by leukocytosis.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological interventions target the underlying etiology and alleviate distressing symptoms while avoiding inappropriate drug use. Antimicrobial therapy is reserved for confirmed bacterial infections, protozoal infestations, or severe cholera cases, as indiscriminate antibiotic use promotes resistance and prolongs the carrier state for organisms like Salmonella. Antidiarrheal agents such as motility inhibitors are strictly contraindicated in invasive bacterial infections because slowing gut motility traps toxins and worsens mucosal invasion. Zinc supplementation plays a proven therapeutic role in pediatric patients, shortening illness duration and protecting against future episodes.

Nursing Care and Fluid Replacement Therapy

The cornerstone of nursing management in diarrhoea is aggressive fluid resuscitation to prevent hypovolemic shock. Oral Rehydration Salts remain the most effective intervention for mild to moderate dehydration, utilizing coupled sodium-glucose transport mechanisms in the intestinal mucosa. Nurses must accurately calculate fluid deficits, monitor intake and output charts meticulously, and recognize when intravenous access is required for severe dehydration or intractable vomiting. Perianal skin care is equally critical; frequent acidic stools cause painful excoriation, demanding regular cleansing, barrier creams, and prompt linen changes.

Complications and Patient Education

Uncontrolled fluid loss precipitates severe complications, most notably hypovolemic shock, severe electrolyte imbalances such as hypokalemia and hyponatremia, and acute kidney injury. Patient and caregiver education forms the final barrier against recurrent episodes, focusing on safe drinking water practices, proper handwashing technique with soap, and hygienic food preparation. Nurses must teach families how to recognize early warning signs that necessitate immediate return to a healthcare facility, transforming patients and caregivers into active partners in recovery.
