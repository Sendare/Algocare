Colostomy Care

Introduction and Indications for Colostomy

A colostomy is a surgical procedure that brings one end of the large intestine out through the abdominal wall. This creates an opening, called a stoma, through which waste material leaves the body. The faeces are then collected in an external pouch attached to the abdomen.

Surgeons perform this operation when a portion of the bowel must be bypassed or removed due to disease, trauma, or congenital anomalies. Common conditions requiring a colostomy include colorectal cancer, severe diverticulitis, inflammatory bowel disease, bowel obstruction, and abdominal injuries from accidents. Understanding why a patient needs this diversion helps nurses prepare them psychologically and physically for life with a stoma.

Types of Colostomies and Anatomical Considerations

The characteristics of stoma output depend heavily on the anatomical site where the colon is divided.

  • Ascending colostomy: Located on the right side of the abdomen. Output is liquid to pasty because very little water has been absorbed by the large intestine yet. This type is relatively rare.
  • Transverse colostomy: Located across the upper abdomen. Output is usually mushy to semi-formed, as food has passed through part of the colon. These can be loop or double-barrel stomas.
  • Descending or Sigmoid colostomy: Located on the left lower abdomen. This is the most common type. Because stool has traveled through almost the entire colon, the output is firm and formed, closely resembling normal bowel movements.

Stoma and Peristomal Skin Assessment

Regular assessment of the stoma and the surrounding skin is a vital nursing duty. A healthy stoma should appear pink to brick-red, moist, and slightly raised above the skin surface. A pale, dusky, or purple stoma indicates poor blood supply and requires immediate medical notification.

The skin around the stoma, known as the peristomal skin, must remain intact and free from irritation. Digestive enzymes in stool can easily cause painful chemical dermatitis if the pouching seal leaks. Nurses should check for redness, rashes, ulcerations, or skin breakdown every time the appliance is changed.

Colostomy Appliance Selection and Pouching Technique

Selecting the correct pouching system ensures comfort, prevents leaks, and protects the skin. Appliances can be one-piece units where the skin barrier and bag are fused together, or two-piece units where the pouch snaps onto a separate flange.

When applying a new appliance, measure the stoma carefully using a guide. Cut the opening on the skin barrier about 1 to 2 millimeters larger than the stoma base to avoid cutting the delicate tissue while preventing raw skin exposure to stool. Clean the peristomal skin gently with warm water, pat dry completely, and press the barrier firmly in place to create a secure seal.

Nutritional Considerations and Dietary Guidelines

Living with a colostomy does not usually require a permanent restrictive diet, but certain foods can cause problems like excessive gas, odor, or blockage. Patients learn through trial and error which items affect them personally.

  • Odor-producing foods: Eggs, garlic, onions, fish, and beans.
  • Gas-forming foods: Carbonated drinks, beer, cabbage, beans, and cucumbers.
  • Blockage risks: Corn, nuts, popcorn, celery, and raw fruits with tough skins or seeds.

Adequate fluid intake is also essential, especially for patients with ascending or transverse colostomies who lose more water through their output.

Identifying and Managing Colostomy Complications

Early recognition of complications helps prevent long-term health issues. Common physical complications include:

  1. Stomal Necrosis: Death of stoma tissue caused by interrupted blood supply, appearing dark or black.
  2. Stomal Retraction: The stoma sinks below the level of the skin surface, causing severe leakage and skin breakdown.
  3. Parastomal Hernia: Bulging of the intestine through the weakened abdominal muscle around the stoma site.
  4. Prolapse: The bowel telescopes out, causing the stoma to lengthen abnormally.

Nurses educate patients to watch for severe pain, unusual bleeding, changes in stoma color, or persistent leaks, and to report these signs promptly.

Patient Education and Psychosocial Support

Adapting to a colostomy involves significant emotional and lifestyle adjustments. Body image disturbance, anxiety, and fear of social embarrassment are common among new ostomates. Nurses play a crucial role in providing a safe, empathetic environment for patients to express their feelings.

Education should begin early and progress at the patient's own pace. Teaching involves hands-on practice with emptying and changing the pouch while hospitalized so the patient gains confidence. Involving family members or caregivers also builds a strong support network before discharge home.
