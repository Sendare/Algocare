Colostomy Care

Introduction and Indications for Colostomy

A colostomy is a surgical procedure that brings a portion of the colon through the abdominal wall to the exterior, creating an artificial opening called a stoma. This diversion allows faeces and gas to bypass a diseased or injured section of the lower intestinal tract. In Nigerian surgical wards, this procedure is frequently performed as an emergency life-saving intervention for late-presented bowel obstructions, complicated typhoid perforations, and severe abdominal trauma from road traffic accidents or penetrating injuries.

Elected, non-emergent colostomies are commonly scheduled for malignant colorectal tumours, severe diverticular disease, and inflammatory bowel conditions that fail medical management. Depending on the underlying pathology and surgical goals, the diversion may be temporary, designed to allow distal bowel healing and re-anastomosis later, or permanent when the distal rectum or anus has been completely excised.

Types of Colostomies and Anatomical Considerations

The consistency and output volume of a colostomy depend heavily on its anatomical location along the large intestine. Ascending colostomies produce liquid to semi-liquid effluent that is highly corrosive to the skin due to active digestive enzymes. Descending and sigmoid colostomies yield formed, solid stool because much of the water has already been absorbed as chyme traveled through the proximal bowel.

Surgical construction methods vary based on clinical necessity:

  • End colostomy: Created from the proximal end of a divided bowel, typically permanent, following procedures like an abdominoperineal resection.
  • Loop colostomy: Formed by bringing a loop of the bowel to the surface and supporting it with a plastic bridge or rod, usually constructed quickly for emergency decompression.
  • Double-barrel colostomy: Involves dividing the bowel completely and bringing both the proximal active stoma and the distal mucus fistula to the abdominal wall separately.

Preoperative and Postoperative Nursing Assessment

Preoperative preparation centers on psychological support, marking the optimal stoma site, and baseline nutritional assessment. The stoma site must be chosen collaboratively with the patient while sitting, standing, and bending to ensure it avoids waistlines, skin folds, scars, and bony prominences, allowing the patient clear visibility for future self-care.

Postoperative monitoring requires frequent checks of the stoma every two to four hours for the first 24 to 48 hours. A healthy stoma appears beefy red, moist, and slightly elevated. Immediate reporting is vital if the stoma turns pale, dusky, dark purple, or black, as these color changes signify compromised arterial blood supply or venous congestion leading to ischemia and necrosis. Assess the surrounding abdominal dressing for excessive bleeding, serosanguinous drainage, or signs of surgical site infection.

Stoma and Peristomal Skin Assessment

Maintaining healthy peristomal skin is essential for pouch adherence and patient comfort. Peristomal skin refers to the skin within a 10-centimeter radius surrounding the stoma base. Inspect this area during every appliance change for signs of irritation, moisture-associated skin damage, fungal rashes, or mechanical trauma caused by improper pouch removal.

Enzymatic destruction of the skin occurs rapidly when liquid effluent leaks beneath the skin barrier wafer and contacts unprotected skin. The use of harsh chemical solvents, aggressive scrubbing, or frequent barrier changes can strip the epidermal layer, leading to painful excoriation. Always use warm water and mild, residue-free soap for cleansing, ensuring the skin is completely patted dry before applying any new barrier products.

Colostomy Appliance Selection and Pouching Technique

Selecting the correct pouching system depends on stoma output consistency, abdominal contours, and patient dexterity. Options include one-piece systems where the skin barrier and pouch are combined, and two-piece systems that allow the pouch to be detached from the skin barrier wafer without removing the adhesive base from the skin.

Executing a successful pouch change involves precise measurement and application steps:

  1. Measure: Trace and measure the stoma size accurately using a stoma guide to avoid leaving exposed skin.
  2. Cut: Cut the opening on the back of the skin barrier approximately 1 to 2 millimeters larger than the stoma to prevent frictional trauma.
  3. Clean: Cleanse the peristomal skin gently with warm water, patting it thoroughly dry.
  4. Apply: Center the barrier over the stoma, pressing firmly from the center outward to ensure a secure, crease-free seal.

Patient Education and Self-Care Management

Empowering patients to manage their colostomy independently requires structured, step-by-step education initiated early in the recovery process. Begin by encouraging the patient to simply look at the stoma, progressing gradually to touching the appliance, emptying the bag, and eventually performing complete pouch changes without assistance.

Involving family caregivers or close relatives is crucial, especially in regions where community support groups are scarce and home care relies heavily on familial networks. Address common psychosocial concerns such as body image disturbance, fear of odor in public spaces, and anxiety regarding sexual intimacy. Reassure patients that modern pouches are odor-proof and flat enough to remain concealed beneath normal clothing.

Nutritional Considerations and Dietary Guidelines

Dietary management for colostomy patients aims to promote regular, manageable stool consistency and minimize excessive gas or offensive odors. In the immediate post-operative period, patients advance progressively from clear liquids to a low-residue diet that reduces fecal bulk while the bowel heals. Long-term dietary restrictions are generally minimal for descending or sigmoid colostomies, though patients learn to identify foods that trigger specific responses.

Certain foods produce excess gas, including beans, cabbage, carbonated beverages, onions, and garlic. Odor-producing foods include fish, eggs, and asparagus, while foods like nuts, corn, and raw vegetable skins carry a high risk of causing mechanical stoma obstruction if not chewed thoroughly. Encourage adequate fluid intake of at least two liters daily to prevent dehydration, especially in patients with ascending or transverse colostomies who experience higher liquid output volumes.

Identifying and Managing Colostomy Complications

Early recognition of colostomy complications prevents minor issues from escalating into surgical emergencies.

ComplicationClinical ManifestationNursing or Medical Management
ProlapseTelescoping or excessive protrusion of the bowel through the stoma siteRest in recumbent position, apply warm moist compresses; surgical revision if blood supply is threatened.
Parastomal HerniaBulging of the bowel through the weakened abdominal muscle wall around the stomaProvide abdominal support binder, educate on lifting precautions, monitor for bowel obstruction.
Stomal StenosisNarrowing or stricture of the stoma lumen at the skin or fascial levelGentle digital dilation by trained clinical staff, dietary modification, surgical revision if severe.
RetractionStoma sinks below the level of the surrounding skin surfaceUse convex pouching systems and specialized barrier rings to achieve an effective seal.
