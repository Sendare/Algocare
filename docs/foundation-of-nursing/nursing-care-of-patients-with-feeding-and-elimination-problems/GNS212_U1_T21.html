Indwelling Catheter

Introduction and Purpose of Indwelling Catheters

An indwelling urinary catheter, often referred to as a Foley catheter, is a flexible tube passed through the urethra into the bladder to drain urine continuously. Unlike intermittent catheters that are removed immediately after bladder emptying, an indwelling catheter is retained via an inflated balloon. Primary indications for placement include: - **Acute urinary retention** or bladder outlet obstruction - **Accurate measurement of hourly urine output** in critically ill patients - **Perioperative management** during prolonged surgical procedures or urological interventions - **Healing facilitation** of sacral or perineal pressure ulcers in incontinent clients - **Terminal illness care** where bed linen changes cause severe discomfort

Anatomical Considerations for Catheterization

Successful catheter insertion requires a firm grasp of pelvic anatomy. In biological females, the urethra is relatively short, measuring about 4 centimeters, and opens into the vestibule between the clitoris and the vaginal opening. This short pathway increases vulnerability to bacterial migration from the perineum into the bladder. Biological males possess a much longer urethra, approximately 20 centimeters, divided into prostatic, membranous, and spongy segments. The male tract features two natural anatomical curves and a narrow external meatus. Passing a catheter through the male urethra demands patience and gentle manipulation, particularly when navigating the prostate gland, to avoid creating false passages or urethral trauma.

Types, Sizes, and Materials of Urinary Catheters

Catheters are manufactured from various materials suited to the expected duration of use. Standard sizing follows the **French (Fr)** scale, where each unit equals one-third of a millimeter in external diameter. | Material | Typical Duration | Key Characteristics | | --- | --- | --- | | **Latex** | Short-term (under 3 weeks) | Flexible, but prone to encrustation and latex allergy risks | | **Silicone** | Long-term (up to 3 months) | Biocompatible, less tissue irritation, rigid wall design | | **Teflon-coated** | Short to medium term | Resists urethral mucosal friction better than plain latex | Standard adult sizes range from **14Fr to 18Fr**. Selecting a larger size than necessary increases pressure on the urethral wall, potentially causing ischemia and stricture formation.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Preparation

Before opening any sterile supplies, thorough clinical evaluation prevents avoidable complications. Verify the medical order and assess the patient's baseline bladder fullness through gentle palpation or bedside bladder scanning. Inquire specifically about any history of urethral strictures, previous difficult catheterizations, or known latex and iodine sensitivities. Explain the procedural steps clearly to secure patient cooperation and reduce anxiety. Gather all necessary equipment—including a sterile catheterization tray, appropriate catheter size, sterile water, water-soluble lubricant, and securement device—ensuring adequate lighting and an adjustable working height at the bedside.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Indwelling Catheter Insertion

Catheter insertion is a sterile procedure requiring strict adherence to aseptic technique to prevent introducing pathogens into the urinary tract. 1. **Positioning**: Place the female patient in a dorsal recumbent position with knees flexed and hips externally rotated (frog-leg); place the male patient in a supine position with legs extended. 2. **Perineal Hygiene**: Don clean gloves, wash the meatal area with antiseptic solution using downward strokes, then remove gloves and perform hand hygiene. 3. **Sterile Setup**: Open the sterile catheterization kit, don sterile gloves, and arrange the drape, antiseptic swabs, lubricant, and syringe. 4. **Lubrication**: Liberally lubricate the catheter tip (the distal 2.5 to 5 cm for females, 15 to 17 cm for males). 5. **Insertion**: Advance the catheter smoothly through the urethra until urine flashback appears, then advance an additional 2 to 5 inches to ensure the balloon is entirely inside the bladder. 6. **Balloon Inflation**: Slowly instill the specified volume of sterile water (usually 10 mL) into the balloon port. Pull back gently until resistance is felt to seat the balloon. 7. **Securement and Drainage**: Connect the catheter to the drainage system and secure the tubing to the thigh or lower abdomen to eliminate traction.

Ongoing Nursing Care and Maintenance

Maintaining an indwelling catheter requires diligent daily hygiene and system oversight. Clean the perineal area and the first few inches of the catheter tubing daily with soap and water during routine bathing; avoid applying antiseptic ointments or powders to the meatus, as these can promote bacterial growth. The drainage bag must remain **constantly positioned below the level of the bladder** to prevent gravity-dependent backflow of urine, which introduces bacteria. Ensure the tubing is free of kinks, loops, or dependent coils that obstruct continuous drainage. Empty the collection bag regularly using a clean measuring container for each patient, ensuring the spigot never touches the contaminated receptacle.

Prevention of Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections (CAUTI)

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections represent one of the most common healthcare-associated infections worldwide. Bacteria ascend the outer or inner surfaces of the catheter lumen from contaminated collection bags or the perineum. Core prevention strategies include: - **Inserting catheters only for established clinical indications** and removing them as soon as clinically feasible. - **Maintaining a closed drainage system**; breaking the junction between the catheter and tubing contaminates the entire apparatus. - **Performing hand hygiene** before and after handling the catheter or drainage system. - **Securing the catheter** securely to minimize micro-motions in the urethra that draw microbes inward. - **Using aseptic technique** during initial placement.

Troubleshooting Common Catheter Complications

When a patient with an indwelling catheter experiences pain, bladder spasms, or a sudden cessation of urine output, systematic troubleshooting is required. First, inspect the tubing for obvious kinks, compression from body weight, or dependent loops trapping urine. If the tubing is patent and unkinked, assess the lower abdomen for bladder distension. Low output or leakage around the catheter (bypassing) is frequently caused by bladder spasms or catheter obstruction from sediment or blood clots. Irrigation should only be performed with a specific medical order and if a clot obstruction is suspected, using strict aseptic technique to avoid forcing debris further upward.

Catheter Removal Procedure and Post-Removal Monitoring

Catheter removal should occur as soon as the patient no longer meets criteria for retention. Gather a clean disposal bag, a disposable pad, and a syringe matching the balloon inflation volume (typically 10 mL). Explain the procedure; patients often fear pain, but removal is generally quick and only causes minor urethral tickling. Insert the syringe into the balloon port and allow the water to withdraw passively. Never cut the balloon port tubing with scissors, as this can trap a portion of the channel or leave a jagged edge. Once the balloon is fully deflated, instruct the patient to take a deep breath and gently withdraw the catheter. Following removal, monitor the time and volume of the first voiding. Urinary frequency, dysuria, or complete inability to void (post-catheter retention) must be reported promptly.
