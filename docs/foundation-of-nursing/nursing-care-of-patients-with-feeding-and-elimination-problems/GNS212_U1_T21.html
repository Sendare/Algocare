Indwelling Catheter

Introduction and Purpose of Indwelling Catheters

An indwelling urinary catheter, often called a Foley catheter, is a flexible tube inserted through the urethra into the bladder to drain urine continuously. A small balloon inflated near the tip keeps the tube securely inside the bladder. Nurses use indwelling catheters for specific clinical indications rather than routine convenience.

  • Acute urinary retention: Inability to pass urine due to an obstruction or nerve problem.
  • Accurate output monitoring: Measuring exact hourly urine volumes in critically ill patients.
  • Surgical support: Keeping the bladder empty during prolonged operations or urological procedures.
  • Wound healing: Protecting open pressure ulcers or perineal wounds from urine contamination.
  • Comfort at end of life: Managing incontinence when moving the patient causes severe pain.

Anatomical Considerations for Catheterization

Successful catheter insertion requires a clear understanding of male and female pelvic anatomy. The female urethra is short, measuring about 4 centimeters, and sits directly posterior to the clitoris and anterior to the vaginal opening. This short distance increases the risk of accidentally slipping the catheter into the vagina; if this happens, leave the misplaced catheter in place temporarily as a landmark and use a fresh, sterile catheter for the urethral attempt.

The male urethra is significantly longer, measuring about 20 centimeters, and features natural curves at the penoscrotal junction and the prostate gland. When advancing a male catheter, stretching the penis perpendicular to the body straightens the pendulous urethra and reduces resistance. Nurses must also navigate the prostate gland, which can enlarge with age and compress the urethral canal, causing friction or difficulty during advancement.

Types, Sizes, and Materials of Urinary Catheters

Urinary catheters vary in material composition, lumen count, and sizing to match specific patient needs. Catheter size is measured using the French (Fr) scale, where each unit equals one-third of a millimeter in diameter. For most adult patients, sizes 14 Fr to 16 Fr provide adequate drainage without overstretching the urethral tissues.

Material selection impacts how long a catheter can remain in place. Standard latex catheters can cause allergic reactions and are typically used for short-term needs under two weeks. Silicone or hydrogel-coated catheters resist encrustation better and are safer for long-term indwelling use.

Lumen configurations include single-lumen tubes for intermittent drainage, double-lumen tubes for continuous drainage and balloon inflation, and triple-lumen tubes that add an extra channel for continuous bladder irrigation after prostate surgery.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Preparation

Before opening any sterile supplies, the nurse performs a focused assessment to confirm the clinical order, check for latex or iodine allergies, and evaluate the patient's bladder fullness through palpation or portable bladder scanning. Explaining the procedure in simple, reassuring language helps reduce anxiety and secures patient cooperation.

Gathering all necessary equipment beforehand prevents breaks in sterile technique. The nurse prepares a well-lit environment, positions the bed to a comfortable working height, and ensures adequate privacy. Proper positioning is vital: women lie in the dorsal recumbent position with knees flexed and hips rotated outward, while men lie supine with legs extended flat.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Indwelling Catheter Insertion

Inserting an indwelling catheter is a strict aseptic procedure. The nurse performs hand hygiene, opens the sterile catheter kit on a clean surface, and dons sterile gloves. Using the non-dominant hand to expose the urethral meatus—which then becomes contaminated—the nurse cleans the area with antiseptic swabs using a single downward stroke per swab, always moving from front to back or from the center outward.

With the dominant sterile hand, the nurse picks up the lubricated catheter and gently advances it into the meatus until urine begins to flow, then advances it another 2 to 5 centimeters to ensure the balloon is fully inside the bladder before inflation. Injecting sterile water into the balloon should never cause patient pain; if resistance or pain occurs, stop immediately, deflate the balloon if injected, and reassess placement.

Ongoing Nursing Care and Maintenance

Once an indwelling catheter is in place, daily maintenance focuses on hygiene and gravity drainage. The perineal area requires gentle cleansing with mild soap and water daily and after bowel movements, avoiding harsh scrubbing of the catheter tubing. The drainage bag must always hang below the level of the bladder to prevent backflow of urine, and the tubing should be secured to the patient's thigh or lower abdomen to minimize urethral traction and tissue irritation.

Prevention of Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections (CAUTI)

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections represent one of the most common healthcare-associated infections. Bacteria migrate into the bladder through the lumen or along the outer surface of the catheter sheath. Preventive protocols require maintaining a closed urinary drainage system at all times; disconnecting the tubing breaks this sterile seal and introduces pathogens.

Additional preventive measures include emptying the collection bag regularly using a clean, dedicated measuring container for each patient, avoiding dependent loops in the drainage tubing, and removing the catheter as soon as clinical indications resolve.

Troubleshooting Common Catheter Complications

When urine stops flowing into the collection bag, the nurse must troubleshoot systematically before assuming kidney failure. Common issues include external kinking of the tubing, patient positioning that blocks drainage, or internal obstruction from blood clots and sediment.

The nurse checks the entire length of the tubing from the meatus to the bag for twists, kinks, or compression under the patient's body weight. If the tubing is patent and straight, gentle milking or stripping of the tube may dislodge small clots. If these steps fail and the bladder is distended, a physician's order may be required to flush the system or replace the blocked catheter.

Catheter Removal Procedure and Post-Removal Monitoring

Removing an indwelling catheter is a straightforward procedure that requires clinical follow-up. The nurse attaches a syringe to the balloon inflation port and completely withdraws all sterile water; relying on passive drainage alone leaves the balloon partially inflated and causes severe urethral trauma during extraction.

After balloon deflation, the nurse instructs the patient to take a deep breath and gently pulls the catheter out in a smooth, continuous motion. Post-removal nursing care focuses on monitoring the patient's first spontaneous voiding, typically within 6 to 8 hours. The nurse documents the time, volume, and character of the urine, and observes for complications like urinary retention, burning, or incontinence.
