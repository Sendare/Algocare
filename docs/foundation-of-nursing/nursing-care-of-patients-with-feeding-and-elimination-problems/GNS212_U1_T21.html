Indwelling Catheter

Introduction and Purpose of Indwelling Catheters

An indwelling urinary catheter, commonly known as a Foley catheter, is a flexible tube passed through the urethra into the bladder to continuously drain urine. In Nigerian clinical practice, understanding the precise indications for catheterization is vital for patient safety and minimizing hospital-acquired complications. The primary purposes of an indwelling catheter include managing acute or chronic urinary retention, monitoring precise hourly urine output in critically ill patients, facilitating healing of urological or gynecological surgeries, managing severe hematuria via continuous bladder irrigation, and providing comfort for terminally ill patients where bed linen changes cause severe distress. Nurses must remember that a catheter is not a substitute for nursing care in incontinent patients; inappropriate use drastically increases the risk of urinary tract infections. Each insertion requires a valid medical prescription and continuous clinical justification. Regular reassessment ensures that the catheter is removed as soon as it is clinically indicated, aligning with global best practices taught in nursing education across West Africa.

Anatomical Considerations for Catheterization

Successful and safe catheterization requires a thorough understanding of male and female pelvic anatomy. In female patients, the urethra is relatively short, measuring about 3.8 to 5 centimeters, and is located anterior to the vaginal opening and posterior to the clitoris. The shorter female urethra increases the risk of bacterial migration from the perineum into the bladder, necessitating meticulous antiseptic technique. When performing female catheterization, visualization can sometimes be challenging due to anatomical variations, obesity, or childbirth changes, making proper positioning crucial. In contrast, the male urethra is significantly longer, measuring approximately 20 centimeters, and traverses the prostate gland and the penis. Male catheterization involves navigating anatomical curves, including the pendulous urethra and the bulbous urethra, as well as passing through the prostate gland, which may be enlarged in older men with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Encountering resistance at the internal urethral sphincter or prostate requires the nurse to pause, avoid forced advancement, and seek senior assistance or medical review to prevent traumatic false passages or urethral rupture.

Types, Sizes, and Materials of Urinary Catheters

Urinary catheters vary by type, French (Fr) size, and material composition, and selecting the correct catheter is a critical nursing responsibility. Standard indwelling catheters are typically double-lumen (one channel for drainage, one for balloon inflation) or triple-lumen (used for continuous bladder irrigation). Catheter sizes are measured in the French scale, where each unit equals 0.33 millimeters in diameter. For most adult patients, sizes 14 Fr to 16 Fr are standard; smaller sizes (e.g., 10-12 Fr) are used for pediatric or delicate patients, while larger sizes (e.g., 18-24 Fr) are reserved for hematuria with blood clots or viscous fluids. Material selection affects indwelling duration and patient comfort. Latex catheters are flexible and economical but can provoke latex allergies in sensitive patients. Silicone catheters are hypoallergenic, cause less tissue irritation, and can remain in situ for longer periods (up to 12 weeks). Hydrophilic-coated and silver alloy-impregnated catheters are also available to reduce friction and minimize bacterial colonization, offering advanced infection control benefits in resource-conscious healthcare settings.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Preparation

Before inserting an indwelling urinary catheter, the nurse must conduct a comprehensive pre-procedure assessment and prepare all necessary equipment. The nurse begins by verifying the medical order, checking the patient's identity, assessing allergies (particularly to latex, iodine, or chlorhexidine), and evaluating the patient's clinical status, bladder distension via palpation or bladder scanner, and baseline renal function. Explaining the procedure to the patient and securing informed consent is essential to reduce anxiety and promote cooperation. Environmental preparation involves ensuring adequate lighting, privacy screens, and a comfortable working height. The nurse gathers a sterile catheterization tray, the correct size and material of the catheter, sterile gloves, appropriate antiseptic cleansing solution, a sterile water-filled syringe for balloon inflation, a secured drainage bag, and a waterproof pad. Proper hand hygiene is performed before assembling equipment. Organizing the sterile field meticulously ensures asepsis throughout the procedure, safeguarding the patient against introducing nosocomial pathogens into the urinary tract.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Indwelling Catheter Insertion

Inserting an indwelling urinary catheter requires strict adherence to aseptic technique to prevent iatrogenic infection. The nurse positions the patient correctly—dorsal recumbent with knees flexed for females, and supine with legs extended for males. After performing hand hygiene and donning sterile gloves, the nurse sets up the sterile field and prepares the equipment, testing the retention balloon and lubricating the distal catheter tip. For female patients, the non-dominant hand separates the labia minora to expose the meatus, while the dominant hand cleanses the area from clitoris toward the anus using separate antiseptic swabs. For males, the penis is held upright at a 90-degree angle, the foreskin retracted if uncircumcised, and the glans cleansed in a circular motion from the meatus outward. The catheter is gently advanced until urine flows, and then advanced an additional 2 to 5 centimeters to ensure the balloon is fully within the bladder before inflation. Sterile water is injected according to the manufacturer's specification. Finally, the catheter is gently pulled until resistance is felt to seat the balloon, connected to a closed drainage system, and secured to the thigh or lower abdomen to prevent urethral traction.

Ongoing Nursing Care and Maintenance

Ongoing nursing care and maintenance of an indwelling catheter are vital to prevent complications and ensure optimal functioning. The nurse must perform routine daily perineal hygiene using mild soap and water, gently cleaning along the length of the catheter away from the body to avoid pushing bacteria into the urethra. The closed drainage system must remain intact at all times; breaking the junction between the catheter and drainage tubing introduces pathogens and invites catheter-associated urinary tract infections. The drainage bag must be maintained below the level of bladder height at all times to prevent the backflow of urine into the bladder, yet it must never touch the floor to avoid contamination of the spigot. Tubing should be secured properly to the patient's thigh (females) or lower abdomen (males) using a securement device to eliminate tension and prevent urethral erosion. Nurses should regularly assess urine output volume, color, clarity, and odor, documenting abnormal findings immediately. Routine irrigation or prophylactic use of antiseptics is not recommended unless specifically ordered for clot evacuation.

Prevention of Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections (CAUTI)

Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infections (CAUTI) represent one of the most common healthcare-associated infections globally, making prevention a top priority for nursing professionals. CAUTI occurs when microorganisms enter the urinary tract via the catheter lumen, drainage bag, or the mucosal space around the insertion site. Core preventative strategies center on the bundle approach: inserting catheters only when strictly necessary, using aseptic technique during insertion, maintaining an uncompromised closed drainage system, and removing catheters as promptly as clinical criteria allow. Daily assessment of the continued need for the catheter is arguably the single most effective intervention to lower infection rates. Additionally, nurses must ensure that the drainage bag is never raised above the bladder, urine is emptied regularly using a clean container for each patient to prevent cross-contamination, and urine sample collection ports are disinfected properly before aspirating specimens. Antibiotic prophylaxis is generally not recommended for preventing CAUTI, as it promotes resistant bacterial strains.

Troubleshooting Common Catheter Complications

Nurses frequently encounter and must expertly troubleshoot common indwelling catheter complications. One frequent issue is urinary leakage around the catheter (bypass), which can be caused by bladder spasms, catheter obstruction from blood clots or sediment, constipation putting pressure on the bladder, or a catheter size that is too small. Management involves checking the patency of the tubing for kinks, administering prescribed antispasmodics, and assessing bowel function. Another serious complication is acute catheter blockage and retention; if no urine drains for several hours and the patient reports lower abdominal pain or restlessness, the nurse must inspect the tubing. If the tubing is patent and flushing is ordered, gentle irrigation can be performed using aseptic technique. Accidental or premature removal by confused or restless patients is also common; nurses must avoid forcibly pulling a catheter with an inflated balloon, as this causes severe urethral trauma. Physical restraints should be a last resort, prioritizing behavioral interventions, mittens, or abdominal binders where appropriate to protect the line.

Catheter Removal Procedure and Post-Removal Monitoring

The safe removal of an indwelling catheter and subsequent post-removal monitoring are critical concluding steps in urological nursing care. The nurse reviews the medical order for removal, explains the procedure to the patient, and gathers a clean collection container and absorbent pad. After performing hand hygiene and donning non-sterile gloves, the nurse places a waterproof pad under the patient, attaches a syringe to the inflation valve, and completely aspirates all sterile water from the retention balloon, ensuring the full volume is withdrawn by watching for passive recoil. The patient is instructed to take a deep breath to relax the pelvic floor muscles, and the catheter is smoothly and gently withdrawn. The nurse cleanses the perineal area and informs the patient that mild burning or frequency may occur during the first few voids. Post-removal monitoring requires tracking the time and volume of the patient's first spontaneous void, typically expected within 6 to 8 hours. The nurse assesses for urinary retention, overflow incontinence, or dysuria, reporting any inability to void or significant bladder distension promptly to the physician.
