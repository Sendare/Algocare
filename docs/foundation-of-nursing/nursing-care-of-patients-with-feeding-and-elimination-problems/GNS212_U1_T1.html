Passage and Care of Nasogastric Tube

Introduction and Indications for Nasogastric Intubation

A nasogastric (NG) tube is a flexible plastic conduit passed through the nasopharynx, down the esophagus, and into the stomach. Nurses use this intervention for various clinical goals when the gastrointestinal tract needs decompression, drainage, or direct access for nutrition. Recognizing why a patient requires an NG tube helps guide monitoring priorities and patient preparation.

  • Gastric Decompression: Removal of accumulated air, gastric secretions, or blood from the stomach, commonly used in bowel obstruction or paralytic ileus.
  • Enteral Nutrition: Delivering liquid feeds and water directly to the stomach when a patient cannot swallow safely.
  • Medication Administration: Giving oral medications in liquid or crushed form to patients unable to take them by mouth.
  • Diagnostic Evaluation: Sampling gastric contents to test for bleeding or chemical composition.

Contraindications and Potential Complications

Before attempting NG tube insertion, nurses must evaluate the patient for conditions that make the procedure unsafe. Forcing a tube past structural blocks or severe trauma zones can cause catastrophic injury.

Absolute and relative contraindications include severe facial trauma, basilar skull fractures, recent nasal surgery, and esophageal strictures or burns. In patients with severe mid-face fractures, a misplaced tube can accidentally enter the intracranial vault through a fractured cribriform plate.

Potential complications range from mild discomfort to life-threatening events:

  • Aspiration: Stomach contents entering the airway, often leading to aspiration pneumonia.
  • Mucosal Damage: Nasal or esophageal ulceration, necrosis, or bleeding from prolonged pressure.
  • Tube Misplacement: Accidental entry into the trachea or lungs during insertion.
  • Fluid and Electrolyte Imbalance: Excessive loss of gastric juices leading to metabolic alkalosis.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Equipment Preparation

Thorough preparation ensures safety and reduces patient anxiety. Start by explaining the procedure clearly, noting that swallowing water during passage helps guide the tube down.

Assess the patient's nasal passages for patency, septal deviation, polyps, or previous trauma, choosing the clearer nostril. Check the patient's level of consciousness, gag reflex, and ability to cooperate.

Gather your equipment before entering the room:

  • Appropriate size NG tube (such as Levin or Salem sump)
  • Water-soluble lubricant
  • 50-mL syringe (often catheter-tip)
  • Stethoscope
  • pH indicator strips
  • Emesis basin and tissues
  • Hypoallergenic tape or securement device
  • Glass of water with a straw (if fluids are not restricted)

Step-by-Step Procedure for Insertion

Insert the NG tube with precision and care to minimize discomfort. Position the conscious patient in a high Fowler's position to use gravity and ease swallowing. Stand on the patient's right side if you are right-handed.

Measure the correct insertion distance using the NEX method: measure from the tip of the Nose to the Earlobe, then down to the Xiphoid process. Mark this distance with tape or note the centimeter marking.

Curve the tip of the tube slightly downward, lubricate the first 5 to 10 centimeters, and gently insert it along the floor of the nasal passage toward the ear. When the tube reaches the nasopharynx, the patient may gag. Ask the patient to lower their chin to the chest and take small sips of water through a straw, advancing the tube with each swallow. If the patient shows signs of respiratory distress, coughing, or cyanosis, immediately withdraw the tube.

Methods for Verifying Nasogastric Tube Placement

Verifying correct placement is a vital safety step before using an NG tube for feeding or medication administration. Relying on a single verification method is dangerous; nurses must use a combination of checks.

  • Abdominal X-ray: The gold standard for confirming placement. Radiographic imaging definitively shows the tube tip resting within the stomach.
  • Aspirate pH Testing: Testing fluid withdrawn from the tube using pH strips. Stomach aspirates are typically acidic, with a pH of 5.5 or lower, whereas respiratory secretions are usually alkaline (pH 6.0 or higher).
  • Visual Assessment of Aspirate: Observing the color and consistency of fluid. Gastric fluid is often grassy green, tan, or clear with mucus.

Note that auscultation—injecting air through the tube while listening over the abdomen with a stethoscope—is no longer considered a reliable standalone method because air sounds similar whether the tube is in the stomach, esophagus, or lungs.

Routine Maintenance and Daily Care

Keeping an NG tube functioning smoothly requires consistent daily maintenance to prevent complications like skin breakdown, tube blockage, and mucosal irritation.

Secure the tube firmly to the bridge of the patient's nose using tape or a specialized bridging device to prevent excessive movement or accidental dislodgement. Clean the nares daily with warm water or normal saline to remove crusts and secretions. Apply water-soluble lubricant to the nostril if dryness or irritation occurs.

Flush the tube regularly with water—typically 30 mL before and after feedings or medication administration, and every 4 to 6 hours during continuous feedings—to maintain patency. Document the color, consistency, and amount of any gastric drainage accurately on the fluid balance chart.

Troubleshooting Common Tube-Related Problems

Even with meticulous care, nurses frequently encounter issues such as tube clogging, nausea, vomiting, or accidental dislodgement. Knowing how to troubleshoot these problems quickly restores patient comfort and safety.

  • Tube Clogged: Often caused by crushed medications or thick formula. Try flushing and aspirating the tube gently with warm water using a 30-mL or larger syringe. Avoid using small syringes, as they create high pressure that can rupture the tube.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Usually caused by delayed gastric emptying or rapid feeding infusion. Check residual volumes and pause the feeding if residuals are high.
  • Accidental Pull-Out: Never attempt to reinsert a partially pulled-out NG tube through the nose without checking guidelines, as it can coil or enter the airway. Always verify placement before restarting feeds.

Removal of the Nasogastric Tube

When the patient's gastrointestinal function recovers or enteral support is no longer needed, the physician orders the removal of the NG tube. Explain the procedure to the patient, reassuring them that removal is quick and much more comfortable than insertion.

Gather tissues and an emesis basin. Turn off any active suction or enteral feeding pump. Put on clean gloves and detach the tube from the patient's gown and nose. Ask the patient to take a deep breath and hold it—this closes the glottis and prevents accidental aspiration of any residual secretions during withdrawal. Gently and steadily pull the tube out in one smooth motion, wrapping it in a towel or pad as it comes out. Provide oral hygiene and facial wipes to clean the patient, then document the time, reason for removal, and patient tolerance.
