Passage and Care of Nasogastric Tube

Introduction and Indications for Nasogastric Intubation

Nasogastric (NG) intubation is a common clinical procedure in nursing practice that involves passing a flexible plastic tube through the nasopharynx, down the esophagus, and into the stomach. As nursing students in Nigeria preparing for clinical rotations and professional exams, understanding the indications for this procedure is vital. An NG tube is primarily used for enteral nutrition and hydration when a patient is unable to swallow safely, such as those experiencing severe dysphagia due to a cerebrovascular accident (stroke), altered level of consciousness, or head and neck trauma. Additionally, NG tubes are indicated for gastric decompression to relieve pressure and remove accumulated secretions or gas in conditions like bowel obstruction, paralytic ileus, or severe postoperative nausea and vomiting. Nurses also utilize NG tubes for gastric lavage to remove toxic ingestions or blood from the stomach following an upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and for administering specific medications directly to patients who cannot take them orally. Recognizing these indications promptly allows the nurse to anticipate provider orders, prepare necessary equipment, and provide clear explanations to conscious patients or their relatives, thereby reducing anxiety and promoting cooperation throughout the procedure.

Contraindications and Potential Complications

Before proceeding with nasogastric intubation, nurses must meticulously evaluate contraindications to prevent severe patient harm. Absolute and relative contraindications include severe facial trauma, basilar skull fractures, nasal polyps, severe coagulopathies, and recent nasal or esophageal surgery. In patients with facial or basilar skull fractures, an improperly placed tube could mistakenly enter the cranial vault rather than the stomach, leading to catastrophic neurological damage. Esophageal strictures, caustic ingestions, and recent esophageal variceal repair also pose high risks of perforation and hemorrhage. Potential complications during and after the procedure range from minor discomfort to life-threatening events. Traumatic insertion can cause mucosal irritation, epistaxis, and pharyngeal or esophageal perforation. Aspiration of gastric contents is a major risk, especially in sedated or impaired patients, which can precipitate aspiration pneumonia. Other common local complications include nasal necrosis from pressure, sinusitis, and sore throat. Furthermore, long-term use can lead to fluid and electrolyte imbalances or metabolic disturbances if feeding regimens are not carefully monitored. As patient advocates, nurses must weigh these risks against the clinical benefits and collaborate with the medical team to ensure safety.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Equipment Preparation

Thorough pre-procedure assessment and accurate equipment preparation are the hallmarks of safe, professional nursing care. The nurse must begin by reviewing the patient's medical history for any contraindications, confirming the physician's order, and checking the patient's identity using standard institutional protocols. A comprehensive physical assessment should include evaluating the patency of both nares, inspecting the nasal mucosa for inflammation or deviation, assessing the abdominal status, and checking the patient's level of consciousness and ability to swallow. Establishing a simple communication sign, such as raising a hand to signal distress during the procedure, helps reassure the patient. Equipment preparation requires gathering a clean workspace and all necessary supplies within arm's reach. Essential items include the appropriate size and type of nasogastric tube (such as a Levin or Ryle's tube), a water-soluble lubricant to ease passage, a 50ml catheter-tip syringe, a stethoscope, pH indicator strips, adhesive tape for securing the tube, a glass of water with a straw (if fluid restriction is not present), clean gloves, a waste basin, and a towel or disposable waterproof pad to protect the patient's clothing and bed linen.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Nasogastric Tube Insertion

Executing a nasogastric tube insertion requires precision, patience, and strict adherence to infection control principles. Begin by performing hand hygiene and donning clean gloves. Position the patient in a high-Fowler's position, unless contraindicated, to facilitate swallowing and leverage gravity. Stand on the patient's right side if you are right-handed (or left side if left-handed). Measure the correct insertion length using the traditional NEX method: measure from the tip of the nose to the earlobe, and then down to the xiphoid process; mark this distance with tape or note the centimeter marking. Lubricate the distal 5 to 10 cm of the tube with a water-soluble lubricant. Instruct the patient to slightly hyperextend the neck, and gently insert the tube through the selected naris, directing it downward and backward toward the ear. As the tube reaches the nasopharynx, the patient may gag; instruct them to relax, breathe through the mouth, and sip water through a straw if permitted. As the patient swallows, advance the tube steadily down the esophagus toward the stomach until the predetermined mark is reached. Never force the tube; if resistance is met or the patient shows signs of respiratory distress (coughing, cyanosis, gasping), withdraw the tube immediately. Once successfully passed, temporarily anchor the tube to the nose with tape before confirming placement.

Methods for Verifying Nasogastric Tube Placement

Verifying the correct placement of a nasogastric tube is a critical nursing responsibility that must be performed immediately after insertion and prior to every feed or medication administration. Incorrect placement into the respiratory tract can lead to accidental administration of feeds into the lungs, causing aspiration, pneumonia, or pneumothorax. The gold standard and most reliable method for verifying placement is obtaining an abdominal X-ray, which clearly visualizes the tip of the tube within the stomach. However, nurses also utilize bedside verification techniques. Aspiration of gastric contents is highly effective; gastric fluid is typically cloudy, green, tan, bloody, or brown. Testing the pH of the aspirated fluid using pH indicator strips is another evidence-based method: gastric aspirates usually have an acidic pH of 1 to 4 (or up to 5.5 if the patient is receiving acid-inhibiting medications), whereas respiratory secretions are generally more alkaline (pH 6 or higher). Auscultation—pushing a small bolus of air through the tube while listening with a stethoscope over the epigastric region—is historically taught but is no longer considered a reliable standalone method because air injected into the pharynx or upper airway can produce a similar swoishing sound. Therefore, nurses must rely on a combination of pH testing, visual inspection of aspirate, and definitive X-ray confirmation.

Routine Maintenance and Daily Care of the Patient

Routine maintenance and daily care of the patient with a nasogastric tube are essential for preventing complications, ensuring patient comfort, and maintaining tube patency. Once placement is secured, the nurse must anchor the tube securely to the bridge of the nose using hypoallergenic tape, and pin the distal end of the tube to the patient's hospital gown to prevent accidental dislodgement caused by pulling. Daily skin assessment is paramount; the nurse must inspect the nasal mucosa and skin around the nares for pressure ulcers, necrosis, or irritation, and clean the area gently with warm water and mild soap. Oral hygiene is frequently neglected but crucial: unconscious or NPO patients require frequent mouth care, tooth brushing, and lip moisturization every 2 to 4 hours to prevent dry mouth, parotitis, and stomatitis. To maintain tube patency and prevent occlusion from viscous formulas or medications, the nurse should routinely flush the tube with 30 ml of sterile or clean water before and after intermittent feedings, medication administration, and at least every 4 to 6 hours during continuous feedings. Monitoring the patient's fluid balance, tracking intake and output, and observing for abdominal distension, nausea, or vomiting will ensure early detection of intolerance or complications.

Administration of Enteral Feedings and Medications

Administering enteral feedings and medications safely via a nasogastric tube requires meticulous technique and adherence to pharmacological and nutritional guidelines. Before initiating any feed or medication delivery, the nurse must verify tube placement, assess bowel sounds, and check residual gastric volume if indicated by hospital protocol. The patient should be maintained in a semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's position (at least 30 to 45 degrees elevated) during the procedure and for at least 30 to 60 minutes afterward to minimize the risk of aspiration. For enteral feedings, formulas should be administered at room temperature. Whether using gravity bags or an electronic feeding pump, the nurse must regulate the flow rate carefully to prevent cramping, diarrhea, and dumping syndrome. When administering medications, the nurse should ensure that medications are available in liquid form whenever possible. If pills must be crushed, they should be finely pulverized and thoroughly dissolved in warm water separately. Tablets that are enteric-coated, sustained-release, or sublingual should never be crushed. The nurse must flush the tube with 15 to 30 ml of water before and after administering each medication, and flush between different medications to prevent chemical reactions, clumping, and tube clogging.

Troubleshooting Common Tube-Related Problems

Even with optimal care, nurses frequently encounter tube-related problems that require prompt troubleshooting and clinical problem-solving. One common complication is tube occlusion or clogging, often caused by inadequate flushing, improper medication crushing, or formula coagulation. If a tube clogs, the nurse should first attempt to troubleshoot by gently flushing and aspirating the tube using warm water and a large 50ml syringe (avoiding small syringes which generate excessive pressure and can rupture the tube). If water fails, pancreatic enzyme solutions or specialized declogging kits may be used per institutional protocol. Another common issue is tube displacement, which may occur due to coughing, vomiting, or accidental pulling. If displacement is suspected, the nurse must verify placement using reliable methods and notify the physician; a displaced tube must not be advanced blindly back into the stomach if it has been partially pulled out and exposed to unsterile air. Patients may also experience nausea, vomiting, or abdominal cramping, often indicating rapid formula infusion, delayed gastric emptying, or formula intolerance; in such cases, pausing the feed, checking residual volumes, and consulting the dietitian or physician is necessary. Diarrhea is another frequent issue linked to rapid infusion rates, cold formula, or formula contamination.

Removal of the Nasogastric Tube

The removal of a nasogastric tube is performed when the patient's gastrointestinal function returns to normal, the underlying condition resolves, or enteral access is no longer clinically indicated. The nurse begins by reviewing the physician's order and explaining the procedure to the patient. Although removal is generally quick and less uncomfortable than insertion, patients often feel apprehensive. The nurse should gather necessary equipment, including clean gloves, tissues, a waste disposal bag, and a waterproof pad. Position the patient in a semi-Fowler's position to comfort them and reduce aspiration risks. Place a towel or pad across the patient's chest and provide tissues. Put on clean gloves, detach the tube from the patient's gown, and gently remove the adhesive tape securing the tube to the nose. Instruct the patient to take a deep breath and hold it (or perform the Valsalva maneuver), which closes the glottis and prevents accidental aspiration of any residual secretions in the tube during withdrawal. With a smooth, steady, and swift motion, withdraw the tube while wrapping it in the disposable pad or towel. Dispose of the equipment according to infection control standards. Immediately offer oral hygiene, clean the patient's nose and face, and document the procedure, noting the time of removal, the intact condition of the tube, and the patient's tolerance.
