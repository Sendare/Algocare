Passage and Care of Nasogastric Tube

Indications and Clinical Uses for Nasogastric Intubation

Nasogastric intubation involves passing a flexible tube through the nasopharynx down into the stomach. This intervention serves multiple diagnostic and therapeutic purposes in acute and chronic clinical settings. Understanding why a tube is ordered helps prioritize nursing observations and ensures alignment with the patient's overall treatment plan. - **Gastric Decompression**: Removal of accumulated air and fluid, commonly utilized post-operatively or in cases of mechanical bowel obstruction and paralytic ileus to prevent nausea, vomiting, and aspiration. - **Enteral Nutrition**: Delivery of liquid nutrients, calories, and fluids directly into the gastrointestinal tract for patients with intact intestinal function who cannot swallow safely. - **Medication Administration**: Providing a reliable route for essential oral drugs when patients have swallowing difficulties, altered consciousness, or facial trauma. - **Gastric Lavage**: Flushing the stomach and removing toxic ingestions following acute drug overdoses or poisoning.

Contraindications and Potential Complications

While nasogastric tubes are routine medical devices, improper insertion or inappropriate patient selection can lead to severe adverse events. Recognizing absolute and relative contraindications protects vulnerable patients from harm. Absolute contraindications include severe facial trauma, basilar skull fractures, and massive midface instability, where a misplaced tube could inadvertently enter the cranial vault. Relative contraindications involve esophageal strictures, recent esophageal or gastric surgery, caustic ingestions, and severe coagulation disorders. Complications range from mild mucosal irritation to life-threatening events: - **Aspiration**: Inhalation of gastric contents or tube feeds into the respiratory tree. - **Tube Misplacement**: Inadvertent entry into the trachea or lungs. - **Mucosal Necrosis**: Pressure damage to the nasal mucosa, nasal septum, or pharynx. - **Fluid and Electrolyte Imbalances**: Dehydration or electrolyte shifts associated with unchecked gastric suctioning.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Equipment Preparation

Thorough preparation reduces procedural anxiety and minimizes clinical complications. Before approaching the bedside, review the medical chart to confirm the physician's order and verify that no contraindications exist. Assess the patient's nasal passages by inspecting each nostril for deviated septa, polyps, or previous surgical alterations, and choose the clearer side. Gather all necessary supplies to maintain efficiency and sterile integrity during insertion: - Appropriate size and type of nasogastric tube (e.g., Levin or Salem sump) - Water-soluble lubricating jelly - Clean gloves and personal protective equipment - Tongue depressor and penlight - 50-mL catheter-tipped syringe - Stethoscope - Hypoallergenic adhesive tape or specialized securement device - Glass of water with a straw (if patient condition permits swallowing) - Emesis basin and tissues

Step-by-Step Procedure for Nasogastric Tube Insertion

Insertion requires clear communication, patient cooperation, and precise anatomical measurement. Position the conscious patient in a high Fowler's position to facilitate swallowing and leverage gravity. 1. **Measure the Insertion Distance**: Extend the tube tip from the tip of the nose, to the earlobe, and down to the xiphoid process (NEX measurement). Mark this length with tape or note the centimeter marking. 2. **Lubricate**: Apply generous water-soluble lubricant to the distal 10 to 15 centimeters of the tube. 3. **Insert and Advance**: Gently guide the tube through the chosen nostril, directing it backward and downward toward the ear. When resistance is felt at the nasopharynx, encourage the patient to sip water through a straw and swallow, advancing the tube with each swallow. 4. **Monitor Tolerance**: Stop immediately and withdraw the tube slightly if the patient experiences severe coughing, cyanosis, or significant respiratory distress, as these indicate tracheal placement.

Methods for Verifying Nasogastric Tube Placement

Ensuring correct tube placement before administering any feeds, flushes, or medications is a critical safety responsibility. Relying on outdated methods, such as auscultating an air bolus over the epigastrium, is unreliable and hazardous. - **Abdominal Radiography**: The gold standard and most definitive method. An X-ray confirms the exact anatomical location of the tube tip before initial use. - **Aspirate pH Testing**: Testing fluid withdrawn from the tube using pH indicator paper. Gastric fluid is typically acidic (pH between 1 and 4), whereas respiratory secretions are generally alkaline (pH 6 or higher). - **Visual Assessment of Aspirate**: Observing characteristics of fluid withdrawn from the tube. Stomach aspirate often appears green, tan, bloody, or cloudy brown.

Routine Maintenance and Daily Care of the Patient

Maintaining a nasogastric tube requires diligent daily care to prevent complications such as skin breakdown, tube displacement, and infection. The external length of the tube should be documented and checked against the initial insertion measurement during every shift change. Skin care around the insertion site is paramount. Clean the nares daily with warm water or mild soap, removing crusts and secretions. Rotate the anchor tape or change the securement device regularly to relieve constant pressure on the nasal mucosa and prevent pressure ulcers. Lubricate the lips and provide frequent oral hygiene, as patients with nasogastric tubes often breathe through their mouths, leading to extreme mucosal dryness and discomfort.

Administration of Enteral Feedings and Medications

Delivering nutrition and drugs via a nasogastric tube requires strict adherence to infection control, timing, and flushing protocols. Elevate the head of the bed to at least 30 to 45 degrees during feeding and keep it elevated for 30 to 60 minutes afterward to prevent aspiration. Always flush the tube with 15 to 30 mL of sterile water before and after administering medications or intermittent feedings, and every 4 to 6 hours during continuous feeds to prevent clogging. Medications should be thoroughly crushed to a fine powder (if appropriate for the drug formulation) and dissolved in warm water, administering each medication separately and flushing between each drug.

Troubleshooting Common Tube-Related Problems

Clinical challenges frequently arise during long-term nasogastric intubation. Recognizing early signs of obstruction or irritation prevents treatment delays. - **Tube Clogging**: The most common issue. Prevent by flushing regularly. If clogged, attempt to flush gently with warm water using a 30-mL or larger syringe. Never use small syringes, as high pressure can rupture the tube. - **Nausea and Vomiting**: Often caused by delayed gastric emptying or tube displacement. Check placement, assess bowel sounds, and pause feeds as ordered. - **Diarrhea**: Frequently linked to rapid feeding infusion rates, medication additives, or formula contamination. Slowing the rate or consulting a dietitian usually resolves the issue.

Safe Removal of the Nasogastric Tube

Removal of a nasogastric tube occurs when the patient's underlying condition resolves, bowel function returns, or oral intake is safely re-established. Verify the physician's discontinuation order before proceeding. Explain the procedure to the patient, assuring them that removal is quick. Turn off any active suction or enteral feeds. Place a disposable towel or waterproof pad across the patient's chest and provide tissues. Put on clean gloves, detach the securement tape from the nose, and ask the patient to take a deep breath and hold it. Holding the breath closes the glottis, preventing accidental aspiration during withdrawal. Smoothly and steadily withdraw the tube in one continuous motion. Provide oral hygiene and dispose of equipment according to hospital waste protocols.
