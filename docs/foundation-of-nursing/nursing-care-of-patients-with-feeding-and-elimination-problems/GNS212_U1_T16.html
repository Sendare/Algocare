Urinary Retention

Definition and Overview of Urinary Retention

Urinary retention is the inability to completely empty the bladder during micturition. Unlike urinary incontinence, where urine leaks involuntarily, retention causes urine to accumulate, stretching the detrusor muscle past its normal capacity. This condition can present as an inability to void any urine at all or as a persistent post-void residual volume. In Nigerian clinical settings, male patients presenting with acute retention frequently have a history of untreated or poorly managed benign prostatic hyperplasia, whereas postpartum women may experience retention following prolonged labor or regional anesthesia.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The mechanisms leading to urinary retention generally involve either mechanical obstruction of the lower urinary tract or impaired detrusor muscle contractility (neurological or myogenic failure). When an obstruction blocks the urethral outlet, the bladder muscle must generate increasingly higher pressures to expel urine, eventually fatiguing the muscle fibers.

  • Mechanical Obstruction: Benign prostatic hyperplasia, urethral strictures, bladder calculi, and pelvic tumors.
  • Neurological Impairment: Spinal cord injuries, diabetic neuropathy, stroke, and multiple sclerosis that disrupt nerve signals to the bladder.
  • Pharmacological Agents: Anticholinergic drugs, tricyclic antidepressants, and sympathomimetics that impair detrusor contraction or increase sphincter tone.

Acute versus Chronic Urinary Retention

Clinical presentation splits retention into two distinct temporal patterns that dictate how urgently care must be delivered.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Assessing a patient with suspected retention requires a focused physical examination and history. Inspection often reveals a noticeable lower abdominal swelling. Palpation and percussion of the suprapubic region will yield a dull note and a tense, smooth, dome-shaped mass if the bladder is distended.

Ask about the timeline of symptoms, recent medication changes, and any history of prostate enlargement or urological surgeries. In cases of chronic retention, observe for signs of overflow incontinence, where the high intravesical pressure forces small amounts of urine past a fatigued sphincter, misleadingly suggesting frequent urination.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Confirming retention relies on objective measurement tools rather than physical exam alone. A bedside portable ultrasound bladder scanner is the non-invasive gold standard for quickly estimating post-void residual volume without introducing infection risks. Catheterization may also serve as a diagnostic tool if ultrasound equipment is unavailable.

Blood tests such as serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen are essential to evaluate for post-renal acute kidney injury caused by backpressure from the obstructed bladder. Urinalysis and urine culture help rule out or identify secondary urinary tract infections that frequently accompany static urine.

Medical and Interventional Management

Resolving the immediate retention episode requires prompt bladder decompression. For acute retention, immediate urethral catheterization is mandatory. If urethral passage is blocked by an enlarged prostate or stricture, a suprapubic catheter may be placed by a physician.

Long-term medical management depends on the underlying etiology. Alpha-adrenergic blockers relax smooth muscle in the prostate and bladder neck, while 5-alpha-reductase inhibitors gradually shrink prostate volume over months. Surgical options, such as transurethral resection of the prostate, address the anatomical obstruction definitively.

Nursing Care and Catheterization Procedures

Performing catheterization demands strict aseptic technique to prevent iatrogenic urinary tract infections. When draining a massively distended bladder, nurses must be vigilant about rapid decompression risks. Draining more than 500 to 1000 mL of urine too quickly can cause sudden drop in intra-abdominal pressure, leading to hematuria from mucosal bleeding or post-decompression hypotension.

Clamp the catheter intermittently if large volumes must be drained, following facility protocols. Secure the tubing properly to prevent urethral traction, maintain a closed drainage system below bladder level, and monitor urine output closely.

Complications and Patient Education

Unmanaged or recurrent urinary retention paves the way for severe downstream complications, including chronic urinary tract infections, bladder stones, hydronephrosis, and irreversible renal failure.

Discharge teaching should empower patients to manage their devices or recognize warning signs. Educate individuals with indwelling or intermittent catheters on proper hygiene, signs of infection like cloudy or foul-smelling urine, and when to seek medical help. Emphasize compliance with prescribed medications and scheduled follow-up visits with the urology clinic.
