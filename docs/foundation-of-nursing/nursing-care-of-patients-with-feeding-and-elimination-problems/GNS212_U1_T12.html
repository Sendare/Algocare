Gastric Lavage

Introduction and Definition of Gastric Lavage

Gastric lavage, often called stomach pumping or washing, is a medical procedure where a large-bore tube is passed through the mouth or nose down into the stomach. Sterile water, normal saline, or tap water is repeatedly instilled and then removed to wash out the stomach contents. This process aims to remove ingested poisons, drugs, or toxic substances before they pass into the small intestines where absorption into the bloodstream accelerates.

Timing is everything with gastric lavage. It is generally only effective if performed within one to two hours of ingestion, because most substances move quickly past the stomach pylorus into the duodenum. Once a poison leaves the stomach, lavage becomes useless for removal and puts the patient through unnecessary discomfort. Furthermore, healthcare teams must weigh the real benefits of removing toxins against the serious risks of the procedure itself, such as mechanical injury or aspiration.

Indications and Contraindications

Deciding who gets a gastric lavage requires careful clinical judgment. Nurses and doctors reserve this invasive procedure for life-threatening poisoning cases where the ingested substance is toxic, non-corrosive, and consumed very recently. Activated charcoal is often preferred nowadays, but lavage remains useful when charcoal does not bind the toxin or when dealing with massive, lethal overdoses.

However, strict boundaries protect patients from harm:

  • Corrosive substances: Strong acids or alkalis cause chemical burns; passing a tube risks perforating a compromised esophagus or stomach wall.
  • Hydrocarbons: Substances like kerosene carry a high risk of chemical pneumonia if they enter the lungs.
  • Unprotected airway: Patients who are comatose or actively seizing cannot protect their airway unless they are intubated first.
  • Recent gastrointestinal surgery: The mechanical stress of tube insertion and fluid pressure can burst fresh suture lines.

Required Equipment and Materials

Gathering the correct tools before starting ensures the procedure runs smoothly without dangerous delays. Because the stomach needs to be evacuated quickly and thoroughly, standard small-bore nasogastric tubes are insufficient; instead, clinicians use large-bore tubes.

  • Ewald or Orringer tube: Large-bore, stiff rubber or plastic tubes with multiple large drainage eyes at the tip to prevent clogging by food particles or pill fragments.
  • Lavage fluid: Warmed sterile water, normal saline, or tap water, usually kept at room temperature or body temperature to prevent hypothermia.
  • Funnel or large catheter-tip syringe: Used for instilling the wash solution into the tube.
  • Collection container or bucket: Placed at a lower level than the patient to allow gravity drainage of stomach contents.
  • Suction apparatus: Functional wall or portable suction ready as a backup if gravity drainage fails or if the patient vomits.
  • Personal protective equipment: Gowns, gloves, masks, and face shields because splashing of toxic gastric contents is common.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before any tube enters a patient, baseline clinical data must be recorded. Nurses measure vital signs, assess neurological status using the Glasgow Coma Scale, and check oxygen saturation levels. Establishing an accurate baseline helps the team spot sudden deterioration during the procedure, such as dangerous heart arrhythmias triggered by vagal nerve stimulation or absorbed toxins.

If the patient is conscious, explain the steps clearly and reassure them, as anxiety makes the procedure harder to tolerate. Consent must be obtained from the patient or legal surrogate, depending on their mental state. Most importantly, airway protection must be confirmed. If the patient has a depressed level of consciousness, an endotracheal tube with an inflated cuff must be placed prior to lavage to block fluid from entering the trachea.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Gastric Lavage

Executing a gastric lavage demands precision and teamwork. First, place the patient in the left lateral decubitus position with the head tilted slightly downward. This gravity-friendly position slows down gastric emptying into the small bowel and helps any accidental overflow drain out of the mouth instead of into the lungs.

Measure the tube distance from the tip of the nose to the earlobe and down to the xiphoid process, then lubricate the tip with water-soluble jelly. Gently advance the tube through the mouth or nose into the stomach. Confirm correct placement by listening for a rush of air over the stomach with a stethoscope or by aspirating gastric contents.

Once placement is verified, instill a measured small volume of warm fluid (usually 200 to 300 mL for adults, less for children) into the stomach via funnel or syringe. Lower the funnel or tube below the stomach level to let the fluid siphon back out into the collection container. Repeat this fill-and-drain cycle continuously until the returned fluid runs completely clear and shows no sign of pill fragments or foreign material.

Monitoring and Post-Procedure Nursing Care

The job is not finished when the tube comes out. After removing the lavage tube, continuous monitoring of the patient remains vital. Check vital signs frequently, watch closely for signs of respiratory distress, and monitor oxygen saturation levels. Patients can aspirate fluid even after the procedure if residual liquid remains in the oropharynx.

Inspect the final output for any remaining particulate matter. Often, activated charcoal is administered directly through the tube just before removal to adsorb any remaining molecules of the toxin left behind in the GI tract. Provide oral hygiene since the procedure leaves an unpleasant taste and mouth dryness. Document the total volume of fluid instilled, the total volume recovered, the clarity of the return, and the patient's overall tolerance.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Gastric lavage is a harsh intervention that carries notable risks. Nurses must stay alert to spot complications early and act fast. Aspiration pneumonia stands out as a major danger, happening when fluid or stomach contents enter the trachea and lungs. If the patient coughs, turns cyanotic, or experiences a sudden drop in oxygen saturation, stop the procedure immediately, suction the airway, and give supplemental oxygen.

Mechanical trauma is another hazard. Forceful tube advancement can tear the esophageal mucosa or perforate the stomach wall, often signaled by bright red blood in the return fluid or sudden, severe chest and abdominal pain. Electrolyte imbalances can also occur, especially in children, if large amounts of plain tap water are used instead of normal saline, leading to hyponatremia. Finally, irritation of the vagus nerve can cause sudden bradycardia or dangerous heart arrhythmias.
