Gastric Lavage

Introduction and Definition of Gastric Lavage

Gastric lavage, commonly known as stomach pumping or gastric irrigation, is an emergency medical procedure involving the instillation of fluid into the stomach followed by immediate removal of the gastric contents. The primary goal is the mechanical removal of ingested toxic substances, poisons, or drug overdoses before systemic absorption occurs. While historically routine in toxicology, modern evidence-based protocols have significantly narrowed its use because rapid gastric emptying can sometimes push toxins further into the pylorus and small intestine. In Nigerian emergency departments, it remains a critical intervention for specific acute poisoning cases, particularly when patients present within a narrow window after ingestion and when regional access to specialized antidotes or activated charcoal is delayed.

Indications and Contraindications

Selecting the right candidate for gastric lavage dictates whether the intervention saves a life or causes severe harm. Time elapsed since ingestion is the single most critical factor, typically limited to within one hour of ingestion for most toxic substances. - **Indications**: Life-threatening ingestion of a toxic substance, massive tricyclic antidepressant overdose, or presentation of a cooperative patient within sixty minutes of poison consumption. - **Contraindications**: Ingestion of hydrocarbons due to high aspiration pneumonia risk, strong corrosive or caustic acid/base ingestion, unprotected airway in an unconscious patient, and late presentation exceeding the absorption window.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before passing any tube into the stomach, baseline hemodynamic stability must be established through vital signs and neurological checks using the Glasgow Coma Scale. If the patient's level of consciousness is depressed or if protective airway reflexes are absent, rapid sequence intubation with a cuffed endotracheal tube must be performed first to prevent lethal pulmonary aspiration. Explain the procedure clearly to a conscious patient, securing their verbal consent where applicable, or obtaining emergency consent from relatives if the patient is incapacitated. Position the patient in a left lateral decubitus position with the head lowered slightly; this gravity-assisted posture utilizes the anatomical lie of the stomach to trap particulate matter away from the gastroesophageal junction.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Gastric Lavage

1. **Preparation**: Put on appropriate personal protective equipment and ensure working suction is immediately accessible at the bedside. 2. **Measurement and Insertion**: Measure the tube distance from the tip of the nose to the earlobe, and down to the xiphoid process. Lubricate the tip and gently pass the orogastric tube through the mouth into the stomach. 3. **Confirmation**: Confirm correct tube placement by auscultating air injected via a syringe over the epigastric region, or by aspirating gastric contents. 4. **Instillation**: Pour a small, measured aliquot of warmed isotonic fluid (typically 150 to 200 mL for adults, less for children) into the funnel or syringe attached to the tube. 5. **Evacuation**: Allow the fluid to flow in, then lower the funnel or use gentle suction to siphon the fluid back out into a collection container. Repeat this cycle continually until the returned effluent runs completely clear and is free of pill fragments or particulate matter.

Monitoring and Post-Procedure Nursing Care

Continuous observation does not end when the lavage tube is removed. Closely monitor vital signs—especially heart rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation—because systemic absorption of toxins can still progress or cause secondary organ damage. Measure and record the exact volume of fluid instilled versus the volume retrieved; a net fluid deficit or excessive retention can lead to water intoxication or electrolyte imbalances, particularly in pediatric or elderly clients. Inspect all returned aspirates for blood, noting any signs of mucosal trauma or gastrointestinal bleeding. Provide oral hygiene and comfort measures, as the procedure leaves a dry, irritated mouth and throat.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Gastric lavage is an invasive procedure carrying significant risks that demand vigilance from the nursing team. Aspiration pneumonia remains a leading hazard if airway protection fails during fluid exchange. Mechanical injury, ranging from minor mucosal abrasions to esophageal or gastric perforation, can result from forceful tube advancement. Electrolyte disturbances, such as hyponatremia, develop when large volumes of hypotonic fluids are inadvertently absorbed. If resistance is felt during tube insertion, stop immediately and report to the physician to avoid forcing a false passage. Keep emergency suction and bag-valve-mask ventilation ready at the bedside throughout the entire intervention.
