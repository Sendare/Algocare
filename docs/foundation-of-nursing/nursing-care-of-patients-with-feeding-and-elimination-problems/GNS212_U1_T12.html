Gastric Lavage

Introduction and Definition of Gastric Lavage

Gastric lavage, commonly referred to as stomach pumping or gastric irrigation, is an emergency medical procedure involving the introduction of fluid into the stomach followed by the evacuation of gastric contents through a large-bore orogastric or nasogastric tube. In the Nigerian clinical context and global emergency nursing practice, this technique is primarily utilized for gastrointestinal decontamination in cases of acute, life-threatening oral poisoning or drug overdoses. The core clinical objective of gastric lavage is to physically remove unabsorbed poisons or toxic substances before they transit from the stomach into the small intestines, where systemic absorption accelerates significantly. Additionally, it may be indicated to evacuate retained blood or clots from the stomach during acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding or to clear the stomach prior to emergency endoscopic procedures. However, the procedure is invasive and carries inherent clinical risks, making prompt timing critical. Evidence suggests that gastric lavage is most effective when performed within one hour of ingestion of the toxic agent. Beyond this critical timeframe, gastric emptying has typically occurred, rendering the procedure of minimal benefit while exposing the patient to unnecessary procedural trauma and aspiration risks. As nursing students training within the Nigerian healthcare system, understanding the precise definition and physiological rationale of gastric lavage establishes a solid foundation for safe, evidence-based emergency nursing interventions.

Indications and Contraindications

A thorough understanding of indications and contraindications is paramount for nurses performing or assisting with gastric lavage to ensure patient safety and avoid iatrogenic harm. Indications include acute ingestion of potentially lethal amounts of a poison or drug, provided the patient presents within the critical one-hour window. It may also be considered for massive ingestion of sustained-release or enteric-coated preparations that delay drug absorption, or for substances that do not bind effectively to activated charcoal, such as lithium, iron, alcohols, and heavy metals. Conversely, contraindications represent clinical situations where the procedure poses a severe, unacceptable danger to the patient. Absolute contraindications include ingestion of corrosive substances such as strong acids or alkalis, because re-introducing the tube or inducing emesis can cause esophageal or gastric perforation, worsening chemical burns. Hydrocarbon ingestion is also generally contraindicated due to the extreme risk of pulmonary aspiration and chemical pneumonitis. Furthermore, lavage is strictly contraindicated in patients with an unprotected airway, altered mental status, or impaired gag reflex unless endotracheal intubation with a cuffed tube has been successfully performed first. Other relative contraindications involve patients with recent gastrointestinal surgery, esophageal strictures, varices, or uncorrected bleeding disorders where mechanical trauma from the tube could trigger massive hemorrhage. Nigerian nursing students must rigorously evaluate these criteria before preparing any patient for the procedure.

Required Equipment and Materials

Executing gastric lavage safely requires gathering and organizing specific emergency equipment at the bedside before initiating the procedure. The nurse must ensure all materials are readily available to minimize delays during a crisis. Essential equipment includes a large-bore orogastric or nasogastric tube (typically Ewald tube for adults, ranging from 36 to 42 French, to allow particulate matter to pass). Water-soluble lubricant is required to facilitate smooth, trauma-free insertion through the oropharynx or nasopharynx. Personal protective equipment (PPE) such as gloves, gowns, masks, and protective eyewear are mandatory for the nursing team to guard against splashes of toxic gastric contents or body fluids. A reliable suction apparatus with a Yankauer catheter must be fully functional and tested at the bedside to clear the airway immediately if vomiting or aspiration occurs. Irrigation fluid, usually warmed normal saline or sterile water (approximately 200-300 mL per cycle for adults), should be prepared, along with a measuring container or basin to collect and quantify returned effluent. A large syringe (50 mL catheter-tip) or a funnel system is needed to instill the lavage fluid. Additionally, tongue depressors, a penlight, and a stethoscope are necessary to verify tube placement. If activated charcoal is ordered to follow the lavage, it should be staged nearby. Having these materials systematically checked and arranged demonstrates high standards of emergency preparedness expected of professional nurses in Nigeria.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Patient Preparation

Comprehensive pre-procedure assessment and meticulous patient preparation are vital responsibilities of the nurse before gastric lavage commences. The nurse must first verify the patient's identity, review the exact history of ingestion (time, substance, and estimated quantity), and assess the current level of consciousness and baseline vital signs, including blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation. Neurological status and airway patency must be evaluated continuously. If the patient is comatose, semi-conscious, or lacks a gag reflex, the nurse must ensure that endotracheal intubation with an inflated cuff has been performed by the medical team to protect the airway from aspiration before any tube insertion begins. Informed consent should be obtained from the conscious patient or legal surrogate, keeping in mind emergency exception protocols in cases of acute life-threatening poisoning. The nurse prepares the patient psychologically by explaining the procedure calmly, even if the patient is confused, as reassuring tones help reduce anxiety. Physically, the patient is positioned appropriately, typically in the left lateral decubitus position with the head of the bed lowered slightly (Trendelenburg position). This specific positioning utilizes gravity to minimize the risk of gastric contents flowing past the gastroesophageal junction into the trachea, should vomiting occur. Dentures or removable dental appliances must be removed from the oral cavity to prevent airway obstruction or damage during tube insertion. Completing these preparatory steps safeguards the patient against preventable complications.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Gastric Lavage

Executing the step-by-step procedure for gastric lavage requires precision, clinical skill, and seamless teamwork. First, measure the insertion distance of the orogastric tube from the tip of the nose to the earlobe, and then down to the xiphisternum, marking the tube if necessary. Lubricate the tip of the tube with a water-soluble lubricant. Gently and steadily pass the tube through the mouth (or nose, though orogastric is preferred for larger tubes) into the stomach, encouraging the conscious patient to swallow as the tube advances. Once inserted to the predetermined depth, verify correct placement. Placement is confirmed by aspirating gastric contents (testing pH with litmus paper if needed) or by auscultating over the epigastric region while injecting a small bolus of air through the tube with a syringe to listen for a characteristic swishing sound. Secure the tube temporarily if necessary. Next, connect the funnel or syringe system, and instill a measured aliquot of warmed lavage fluid (typically 150-200 mL for pediatric patients or 200-300 mL for adults). Avoid using excessive volumes, as large volumes can force the toxic substance past the pylorus into the small intestine. Lower the funnel or aspirate to allow gravity or gentle suction to siphon the fluid and stomach contents back out into a collection container. Repeat this instillation and drainage cycle continuously until the returned effluent runs completely clear and is free of particulate matter or pill fragments. Document the total volume instilled and retrieved meticulously. Finally, if prescribed, instill activated charcoal through the tube before withdrawing it smoothly and rapidly to minimize pharyngeal irritation.

Monitoring and Post-Procedure Nursing Care

Monitoring and post-procedure nursing care are critical phases that ensure the patient stabilizes safely after gastric lavage. Immediately following tube removal, the nurse must clean the patient's face and mouth, provide oral hygiene, and reposition them into a comfortable, safe position (such as semi-Fowler's, provided they are fully awake and their airway is secure) to promote respiratory expansion. Continuous monitoring of vital signs—including temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation—must be maintained every 15 to 30 minutes initially, then hourly as stability is confirmed. The nurse must closely observe the patient for delayed signs of respiratory distress, tachypnea, coughing, or decreased oxygen saturation, which may indicate silent pulmonary aspiration of gastric contents or lavage fluid. Neurological status must also be assessed regularly for any changes in consciousness or signs of systemic toxicity from unabsorbed poison. The nurse must accurately measure, record, and compare the total fluid balance, noting the exact volume of fluid instilled versus the volume retrieved to detect any significant fluid retention or electrolyte imbalances, such as water intoxication or hyponatremia, especially when plain water is used. Abdominal assessment is performed to check for distension, tenderness, or rigidity. Finally, comprehensive documentation of the procedure, including the patient's tolerance, appearance of the lavage return, total volumes, and any untoward incidents, must be entered into the nursing notes promptly.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Despite careful technique, gastric lavage is an aggressive intervention that carries notable potential complications, requiring vigilant nursing observation and prompt interventions. One of the most severe complications is pulmonary aspiration of gastric contents or lavage fluid, which can lead to chemical pneumonitis, severe hypoxemia, or secondary bacterial pneumonia. If aspiration is suspected, the nurse must immediately stop the procedure, suction the airway, administer supplemental oxygen, and notify the physician. Mechanical trauma to the gastrointestinal tract is another risk; forceful or improper tube insertion can cause abrasions, bleeding, or perforation of the esophagus or stomach. Nurses must monitor for hematemesis, severe chest or abdominal pain, and rigid abdomen, which indicate mucosal injury or perforation, and report these findings instantly. Fluid and electrolyte disturbances, such as hyponatremia or hypothermia (if cold lavage fluids are used), can also develop, requiring strict thermal regulation of irrigation fluids and electrolyte monitoring. Cardiac dysrhythmias can occasionally be triggered by vagal nerve stimulation during tube passage, particularly in patients who have ingested cardiotoxic drugs like tricyclic antidepressants or digoxin; continuous cardiac monitoring is therefore mandatory. By recognizing these complications early, Nigerian nurses play a life-saving role in stabilizing vulnerable emergency patients.
