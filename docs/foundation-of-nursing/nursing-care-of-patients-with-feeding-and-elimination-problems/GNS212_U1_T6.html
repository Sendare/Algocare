Constipation

Definition and Normal Bowel Physiology

Constipation is defined clinically by infrequent bowel movements, hard stool consistency, excessive straining, and a sensation of incomplete evacuation. Normal bowel elimination relies on a coordinated physiological process involving the colon, rectum, and anal sphincters. The proximal colon absorbs water and electrolytes from liquid chyme, transforming it into formed stool. Segmental contractions mix the contents, while high-amplitude propagating contractions sweep fecal mass toward the rectum.

When feces enter the rectum, rectal distension triggers the rectoanal inhibitory reflex, relaxing the internal anal sphincter and creating the conscious urge to defecate. Voluntary relaxation of the puborectalis muscle and external anal sphincter, combined with an increase in intra-abdominal pressure via the Valsalva maneuver, allows successful evacuation. Disruption at any point along this neuromuscular and absorptive pathway leads to functional delay.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

Constipation arises from three primary pathophysiological mechanisms: slow transit time, pelvic floor dyssynergia, and normal transit with functional disturbance. Slow transit constipation involves impaired colonic neuromuscular activity, often linked to myenteric plexus degeneration or neuroendocrine abnormalities. Pelvic floor dyssynergia, or defecatory disorders, occurs when the abdominal wall, puborectalis, and anal sphincters fail to coordinate harmoniously, resulting in paradoxical contraction during attempted evacuation.

Secondary causes are widespread in clinical practice and must be systematically ruled out:

  • Metabolic and Endocrine: Hypothyroidism slows systemic metabolism, diabetes mellitus induces autonomic neuropathy, and hypercalcemia impairs smooth muscle contractility.
  • Pharmacological Agents: Opioids bind to enteric receptors reducing motility, while iron supplements, calcium channel blockers, and aluminum-containing antacids frequently harden stool.
  • Lifestyle Factors: Inadequate fluid intake, low dietary fiber consumption, and ignoring the natural defecation urge blunt rectal sensory feedback.

Clinical Manifestations and Diagnostic Assessment

Patients presenting with constipation describe a constellation of subjective complaints that require objective verification using the Rome IV criteria, which mandate symptoms lasting for at least three months. Typical features include straining during more than a quarter of defecations, lumpy or hard stools, a feeling of incomplete evacuation, and manual maneuvers required to facilitate passage.

A thorough nursing and medical history focuses on onset, diet, medication review, and red flag symptoms such as rectal bleeding, unexplained weight loss, iron-deficiency anemia, and a family history of colon cancer. Physical examination includes abdominal inspection, auscultation, and a mandatory digital rectal examination to assess sphincter tone, identify fecal impaction, and detect masses or blood.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological treatment follows a stepped approach, starting with simple agents and advancing if lifestyle modifications prove insufficient.

Nursing Interventions and Dietary Approaches

Non-pharmacological strategies form the foundation of nursing care for constipated patients. Dietary management centers on a gradual increase in soluble and insoluble fiber intake paired with adequate hydration of at least 1.5 to 2 liters of water daily, unless fluid restriction applies. Sudden fiber increases without adequate fluids worsen gas and cramping.

Establishing regular bowel habits is vital. Encourage patients to attempt evacuation 20 to 30 minutes after meals, utilizing the gastrocolic reflex. Elevating the feet on a small stool while sitting straightens the anorectal angle, mimicking a squatting posture that eases passage and reduces straining.

Complications and Preventive Care

Unmanaged chronic constipation leads to significant physical complications that degrade a patient's quality of life. Hard stools and excessive straining elevate intra-abdominal and venous pressures, precipitating hemorrhoids, anal fissures, and rectal prolapse. In vulnerable populations, particularly older adults, accumulated fecal masses can harden into a fecal impaction, causing paradoxical diarrhea where liquid stool leaks around the blockage.

Severe impactions can lead to stercoral ulceration, bowel ischemia, or perforation. Prevention focuses on early mobilization, proactive bowel regimens for patients taking opioids, hydration monitoring, and avoiding the habit of ignoring the defecation urge.
