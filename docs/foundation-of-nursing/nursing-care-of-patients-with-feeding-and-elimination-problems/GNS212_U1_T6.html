Constipation

Normal Bowel Physiology and Definition

Constipation is formally defined as infrequent bowel movements, difficulty passing stool, or a feeling of incomplete emptying after a trip to the toilet. Medically, having fewer than three bowel movements a week usually flags a problem, but every person has their own regular rhythm.

Normal bowel function relies on a smooth, coordinated process:

  • Digestion: Food breaks down and moves through the small intestine as liquid waste.
  • Water Absorption: As waste enters the large intestine (colon), the body absorbs most of the water and salts.
  • Peristalsis: Wave-like muscle contractions push the remaining solid waste toward the rectum.
  • Defecation Reflex: When the rectum fills with stool, nerve signals create the urge to pass it, involving both voluntary control of the external anal sphincter and involuntary relaxation of the internal sphincter.

Causes and Classification of Constipation

Constipation is usually grouped into three main types based on how food moves through the gut: normal-transit constipation, slow-transit constipation, and pelvic floor dysfunction.

  • Normal-Transit Constipation: Stool moves through the colon at a normal speed, but the patient feels constipated, often due to a low-fiber diet or mild abdominal discomfort.
  • Slow-Transit Constipation: The colon's muscular contractions are sluggish, delaying the journey of waste from one end to the other.
  • Pelvic Floor Dysfunction: The patient has trouble coordinating the muscles of the pelvic floor and anal sphincters, making it physically hard to push stool out.

Common underlying causes include a lack of dietary fiber, not drinking enough water, ignoring the urge to defecate, lack of physical exercise, and certain drugs like opioid pain relievers, iron supplements, and aluminum-containing antacids.

Clinical Manifestations and Complications

When a patient is constipated, they show several distinct signs beyond just missing a trip to the toilet. The stool is typically hard, dry, and small, resembling pellets. Passing it requires a lot of straining, and the patient often feels bloated, crampy, and heavy in the abdomen.

If left unchecked, chronic constipation can lead to painful local problems and serious systemic issues:

  • Hemorrhoids: Swollen veins around the anus caused by heavy straining.
  • Anal Fissures: Small tears in the delicate lining of the anal canal from passing hard, dry stools.
  • Rectal Prolapse: A condition where part of the rectum slips outside the anus due to prolonged pushing.
  • Fecal Impaction: A large, hard mass of stool gets stuck in the rectum and cannot be pushed out, sometimes causing liquid stool to leak around it.

Nursing Assessment and History Taking

Assessing a patient with constipation starts with a detailed health history. The nurse asks about the patient's usual bowel pattern, the consistency of recent stools, how long the problem has lasted, and whether they need to strain or use manual help to pass stool.

Next, physical examination of the abdomen is essential. The nurse inspects the belly for distention, auscultates all four quadrants for bowel sounds, and gently palpates the abdomen to check for tenderness, firmness, or a palpable stool mass in the lower left quadrant.

A common clinical mistake is assuming all liquid stool leakage means diarrhea; in elderly or immobile patients, liquid leaking around a hard mass actually points to fecal impaction.

Pharmacological Management

When lifestyle changes are not enough, doctors prescribe various laxatives depending on the patient's needs. Each group works differently in the gut:

  • Bulk-Forming Laxatives: Psyllium or methylcellulose absorb water in the intestine, making the stool bulkier and softer. They need to be taken with plenty of water.
  • Osmotic Laxatives: Polyethylene glycol or magnesium hydroxide draw water into the stool from surrounding tissues, making it easier to pass.
  • Stimulant Laxatives: Bisacodyl or senna irritate the bowel wall directly, triggering rhythmic contractions to move waste along quickly.
  • Stool Softeners: Docusate sodium lets water and fats mix into the stool, softening it so less straining is required.

Laxatives should be used carefully; long-term use of stimulants can make the bowel lazy, dulling its natural reflex to move waste.

Nursing Interventions and Prevention

Nursing care focuses heavily on non-drug measures to restore normal bowel habits. Increasing dietary fiber with whole grains, fresh fruits, and vegetables adds bulk to the stool. Patients should also drink adequate fluids, aiming for at least six to eight glasses of water daily unless restricted by a medical condition.

Encouraging physical activity helps stimulate natural bowel motility, especially in bedridden or elderly patients. Nurses should also teach patients not to ignore the natural urge to defecate and to establish a consistent daily toilet routine, ideally after breakfast when the gastrocolic reflex is most active.
