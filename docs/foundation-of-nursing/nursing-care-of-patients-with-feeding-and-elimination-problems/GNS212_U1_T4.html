Gastrostomy Feeding

Introduction to Gastrostomy Feeding and Indications

Gastrostomy feeding involves delivering liquid nutrition, medications, and water directly into the stomach through a surgically or endoscopically placed tube. This method bypasses the oral cavity and esophagus entirely, ensuring that individuals who cannot swallow safely still receive adequate calories and hydration.

Clinicians typically recommend a gastrostomy when oral intake is inadequate or unsafe for an extended period, usually defined as longer than four to six weeks. Common reasons include severe neurological impairment from strokes or dementia, head and neck cancers, severe facial trauma, or advanced degenerative conditions like motor neuron disease. Compared to nasogastric tubes, gastrostomy tubes are more comfortable, less visible, and carry a lower risk of long-term nasal mucosal damage or accidental removal.

Types of Gastrostomy Tubes

Gastrostomy tubes come in various designs to suit individual patient needs, lifestyles, and healing stages.

  • Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy (PEG) tube: Placed initially using an endoscope, featuring a long external tubing length and an internal retention bolster or balloon.
  • Low-profile gastrostomy device (skin-level button): Sits flush against the abdomen with a valve instead of long tubing, ideal for active patients and children, requiring a special extension set for feeding.
  • Transjejunal tube: Extends past the stomach into the jejunum, used when gastric feeding is poorly tolerated or carries a high aspiration risk.

Pre-Procedural Assessment and Preparation

Before initiating any gastrostomy feeding, nurses must perform a thorough physical and clinical assessment. Verify correct tube placement through documentation, visual inspection of external length markings, and checking residual volumes if ordered by the physician. Assess bowel sounds in all four quadrants and palpate the abdomen for distension, tenderness, or pain.

Elevate the head of the bed to at least 30 to 45 degrees. This position must be maintained throughout the feeding and for at least 30 to 60 minutes afterward to prevent gastric reflux and pulmonary aspiration. Check the expiration date of the formula and ensure it is at room temperature to prevent abdominal cramping.

Administration Methods: Bolus, Intermittent, and Continuous

Gastrostomy feedings can be delivered through different methods depending on patient tolerance, nutritional needs, and clinical setting.

  1. Bolus feeding: Formula is delivered quickly via a syringe several times a day, mimicking normal meal times but potentially causing nausea if given too fast.
  2. Intermittent feeding: Formula is gravity-fed via a bag over 20 to 60 minutes several times daily, offering a slower and better-tolerated alternative to rapid bolus delivery.
  3. Continuous feeding: Formula infuses slowly over 16 to 24 hours using an electronic pump, best suited for patients with compromised digestive function or high nutritional demands.

Stoma Site Care and Maintenance

Daily maintenance of the gastrostomy stoma site prevents skin breakdown, bacterial growth, and localized infection. Inspect the skin around the insertion site daily for redness, swelling, warmth, foul-smelling drainage, or leakage of gastric contents. Clean the area gently with mild soap and warm water, rinsing thoroughly and patting completely dry.

Avoid applying tight dressings or hydrogen peroxide unless specifically ordered, as harsh chemicals delay healing. Rotate a new PEG tube slightly each day according to institutional policy to prevent the internal bolster from embedding into the stomach wall, a condition known as buried bumper syndrome.

Medication Administration via Gastrostomy Tube

Administering medications through a gastrostomy tube requires careful technique to prevent tube clogging and ensure proper drug absorption. Whenever possible, request liquid medication formulations from the pharmacist. If crushing pills is necessary, crush them into a fine powder and dissolve them thoroughly in warm water.

Never mix different medications together in the same syringe. Flush the tube with 15 to 30 mL of warm water before the first medication, flush between each individual drug, and flush again after the final medication. Stop any continuous feeding formula and clamp the tube during medication delivery if required by the specific drug instructions.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Patients with gastrostomy tubes can experience various mechanical and physiological complications that require prompt nursing intervention.

  • Tube clogging: Caused by inadequate flushing or crushed medications; prevented by flushing before and after feeds and using warm water to clear blocks.
  • Aspiration: Occurs when formula enters the lungs; managed by keeping the head of bed elevated and checking gastric residuals.
  • Diarrhea or cramping: Often triggered by cold formula, high infusion rates, or formula contamination; managed by slowing the rate or warming formula.
  • Dislodgement: Accidental tube removal is a medical emergency; cover the site with a sterile dressing and notify the provider immediately before the tract closes.

Patient and Caregiver Education for Home Care

Successful long-term tube feeding depends on empowering patients and their family caregivers with comprehensive home care knowledge. Teach caregivers strict hand hygiene before touching the tube, equipment, or patient. Provide clear written instructions covering daily flushing schedules, skin cleaning techniques, and troubleshooting steps for clogs or leaks.

Emphasize the signs that require urgent medical attention, such as persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, inability to flush the tube, or sudden tube dislodgement. Ensure caregivers demonstrate return competency in setting up feeds, administering flushes, and caring for the stoma site before hospital discharge.
