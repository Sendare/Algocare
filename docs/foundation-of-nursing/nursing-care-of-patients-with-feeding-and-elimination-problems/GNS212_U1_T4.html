Gastrostomy Feeding

Introduction to Gastrostomy Feeding and Indications

Gastrostomy feeding involves delivering liquid nutrition, medications, and water directly into the stomach through a surgically or endoscopically placed tube. This route bypasses the oral cavity and esophagus entirely. Clinicians select this intervention when the gastrointestinal tract remains functional, yet oral intake is unsafe, inadequate, or impossible for an extended duration.

Common clinical scenarios requiring this support include advanced neurological disorders like stroke or amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, severe facial trauma, obstructive esophageal malignancies, and prolonged unconsciousness. Whenever functional gut integrity persists, enteral nutrition through a gastrostomy is universally preferred over total parenteral nutrition because it preserves intestinal mucosal immunity, maintains gut barrier function, and carries a significantly lower risk of systemic infection.

Types of Gastrostomy Tubes

  • Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy (PEG) Tube: Inserted via endoscopy with a retention bolster inside the stomach and an external bumper holding it against the skin.
  • Low-Profile Gastrostomy Device (Button Device): Sits flush against the abdominal skin, featuring a skin disk and a valve mechanism, making it highly desirable for active pediatric and adult patients.
  • Surgically Placed Gastrostomy: Performed via open laparotomy or laparoscopy when endoscopy is contraindicated or fails due to anatomical obstructions.

Pre-Procedural Assessment and Preparation

Before initiating a gastrostomy feed, the nurse must systematically evaluate the patient's baseline clinical status. Inspect the abdominal skin surrounding the insertion site for signs of infection, leakage, tissue breakdown, or granulation tissue. Auscultate bowel sounds to confirm gastrointestinal motility. Verify tube placement using facility-approved protocols, which may involve checking external length markers against documentation, measuring gastric residual volume when ordered, or testing pH levels of aspirated gastric contents.

Elevate the head of the bed between 30 and 45 degrees. This critical positioning must remain maintained throughout the entire feeding and for at least 30 to 60 minutes afterward to prevent gastric reflux and subsequent pulmonary aspiration.

Administration Techniques: Bolus vs. Continuous Feeding

Enteral feeding schedules vary depending on patient tolerance, metabolic needs, and clinical settings.

  • Bolus Feeding: Involves delivering a measured volume of formula (typically 250 to 400 mL) over 15 to 20 minutes via a syringe or gravity bag, mimicking normal meal times.
  • Continuous Feeding: Administered slowly and steadily over 8 to 24 hours using an electric infusion pump, ideal for patients with compromised gastrointestinal absorption or high aspiration risk.

Stoma Site Care and Maintenance

Routine stoma hygiene prevents localized skin breakdown and infection. Clean the stoma site daily using warm water and mild soap, then pat the area completely dry. Avoid hydrogen peroxide or harsh antiseptic solutions unless specifically prescribed, as they can damage delicate healing tissues and delay epithelialization. Rotate the tube gently 360 degrees daily (if ordered and healed) to prevent the retention bolster from embedding into the gastric mucosa.

Flush the tube with 30 to 50 mL of sterile or clean tap water before and after every feeding and medication administration. Regular flushing prevents formula residue from accumulating inside the lumen, which is the primary cause of mechanical tube occlusion.

Medication Administration via Gastrostomy Tube

Administering medications through a gastrostomy tube requires careful preparation to prevent tube occlusion. Whenever possible, request liquid medication formulations from the pharmacist. If solid pills must be used, crush them into a fine powder and dissolve them thoroughly in warm water. Never crush enteric-coated, sublingual, or sustained-release medications, as altering their physical form destroys their pharmacokinetic properties.

Flush the tube with at least 15 to 30 mL of water before giving medications, administer each medication separately, and flush with 5 to 10 mL of water between individual drugs. Finish with a final 30 to 50 mL water flush. Never mix medications directly into the feeding formula bag, as chemical interactions can cause the formula to curdle and instantly clog the tube.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Gastrostomy tubes can present acute and chronic complications.

  • Clogged Tube: Caused by inadequate flushing or inappropriate medication administration. Clear blockages using warm water flushes and gentle back-and-forth motion with a syringe; avoid using rigid guide wires.
  • Aspiration: Manifests as coughing, dyspnea, or decreased oxygen saturation during or after feeds. Immediately stop the feeding, suction the airway, and position the patient on their side.
  • Stoma Infection and Leakage: Redness, foul odor, or purulent drainage indicates infection, requiring swab cultures and topical care. Leakage around the site often stems from an oversized tract or high gastric pressure.

Patient and Caregiver Education for Home Care

Transitioning a patient home with a gastrostomy tube requires thorough, structured education for family caregivers. Teach caregivers how to recognize red-flag symptoms that necessitate prompt medical notification, such as severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, unexplained fever, sudden leakage of gastric contents around the stoma, or accidental tube dislodgement. Emphasize that if a tube falls out, the stoma tract can begin closing within hours; therefore, the caregiver must insert a clean backup tube or notify the healthcare provider immediately.

Ensure caregivers demonstrate a complete return-demonstration of hand hygiene, formula preparation, pump operation or gravity feeding technique, flushing protocols, and daily stoma site cleaning before hospital discharge.
