Gastrostomy Feeding

Introduction to Gastrostomy Feeding and Indications

Gastrostomy feeding is a vital nutritional support method used when patients are unable to ingest food orally due to anatomical, neurological, or pathological conditions. It involves the surgical, endoscopic, or radiologic placement of a feeding tube directly into the stomach through the abdominal wall. In nursing practice across Nigeria and globally, recognizing the appropriate indications for this procedure ensures timely intervention, prevents severe malnutrition, and promotes patient recovery. Common indications include severe dysphagia secondary to cerebrovascular accidents (strokes), head and neck cancers, severe facial trauma, advanced neurological degenerative diseases such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and prolonged coma or unconsciousness. Unlike nasogastric tubes which are typically temporary and carry risks of nasal mucosal erosion, sinusitis, and accidental removal, gastrostomy tubes are designed for long-term enteral nutrition lasting longer than four weeks. As nursing students, understanding these indications helps you identify patients who would benefit from enteral access, collaborate effectively with the multidisciplinary healthcare team, and address nutritional deficiencies before they lead to compromised wound healing, increased infection susceptibility, and prolonged hospital stays. Proper assessment of the patient's underlying disease process and swallowing capability dictates the transition from oral feeding to gastrostomy support.

Anatomy and Types of Gastrostomy Tubes

A gastrostomy tube is placed directly through the abdominal wall into the gastric lumen, bypassing the oral cavity and esophagus. Understanding the anatomical landmarks, such as the stomach's position relative to the anterior abdominal wall and the peritoneal cavity, is crucial for safe nursing care. There are several types of gastrostomy tubes used in clinical settings. Percutaneous Endoscopic Gastrostomy (PEG) tubes are the most common, inserted using an endoscope and held in place by an internal retention bolster and an external bumper. Low-profile gastrostomy devices, commonly known as 'button' devices, are skin-level replacements preferred by mobile or pediatric patients because they are discreet, less prone to accidental pulling, and comfortable under clothing. They require a special connecting tube (extension set) for feedings. Another variation includes surgically placed Foley or standard replacement tubes if a PEG needs to be changed urgently. Each tube type has specific manufacturer guidelines regarding balloon inflation volumes (for balloon-retained buttons or tubes), cleansing frequency, and extension set replacement. Nurses must correctly identify the specific tube type a patient has to ensure safe administration of feeds, proper flushing, and troubleshooting of mechanical issues such as leaks or clogs.

Pre-Procedural Assessment and Preparation

Pre-procedural assessment and preparation are foundational responsibilities of the nurse when caring for a patient scheduled for gastrostomy tube placement or initiating a new feeding regimen. The nurse must perform a comprehensive baseline assessment, including the patient's nutritional status, weight, hydration level, baseline vital signs, and gastrointestinal function such as bowel sounds and abdominal distension. Allergies, baseline lab values (like electrolytes and coagulation profiles), and medical history must be meticulously reviewed. For newly placed tubes, the nurse must verify initial tube placement through documentation of X-ray or medical imaging confirmation before any feed or fluid is instilled. For existing tubes, the nurse checks the external tube length measurement against the initial insertion record to detect migration, inspects the peristomal skin for signs of infection, breakdown, leakage, or hypergranulation tissue, and assesses tube patency. Psychological preparation is equally important; explaining the procedure or feeding process to the conscious patient and family helps reduce anxiety and fosters cooperation. Ensuring the patient is in the correct position—typically head of the bed elevated at least 30 to 45 degrees—is mandatory before beginning any enteral nutrition to prevent aspiration. Proper preparation sets the standard for safe, individualized, and effective patient care.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administering Gastrostomy Feedings

Administering gastrostomy feedings requires strict adherence to aseptic technique, patient positioning, and clinical protocols to ensure safety and tolerance. The nurse begins by verifying the medical prescription for the specific type of formula, volume, and rate of administration. Hand hygiene is performed, and personal protective equipment is donned as necessary. The patient's head of the bed must be elevated to at least 30 to 45 degrees during the feeding and maintained at this angle for 30 to 60 minutes post-feeding to minimize the risk of aspiration. Before feeding, the nurse checks residual gastric volume if ordered by protocol, returning the contents unless the volume exceeds facility limits, which could indicate delayed gastric emptying. The tube is then flushed with 30 milliliters of warm sterile or clean tap water to ensure patency. Feedings can be administered via gravity bag, syringe bolus, or an electronic enteral feeding pump. The formula should be at room temperature to prevent abdominal cramping. Throughout the procedure, the nurse monitors the patient for tolerance, watching for nausea, vomiting, abdominal fullness, or discomfort. After the feeding is complete, the tube is flushed again with 30 to 50 milliliters of water to prevent formula accumulation and microbial growth within the lumen. Documenting the volume infused, residual amount, and patient tolerance completes the procedure.

Stoma Site Care and Maintenance

Proper stoma site care and maintenance are essential nursing responsibilities to prevent skin breakdown, local infections, and tube dislodgement. The gastrostomy stoma should be assessed at least daily for redness, swelling, warmth, tenderness, purulent or foul-smelling drainage, and hypergranulation tissue. For a newly created stoma, site care is typically performed using sterile technique according to institutional policy, often with sterile saline or mild soap and water, followed by careful drying. Once the site is fully healed and mature (usually after a few weeks), soap and water cleansing during routine bathing is generally sufficient. The skin around the stoma must be kept clean and dry to prevent maceration caused by gastric juice leakage. Gauze dressings under the external bumper are managed based on drainage levels; some protocols encourage an open-air technique once drainage stops, while others use split-gauze dressings. The external bolster or bumper should be adjusted to allow a small space (about the width of a coin or 0.5 centimeters) between it and the skin to prevent pressure necrosis, but loose enough to avoid excessive tube movement. Rotating the tube slightly (if permitted by the physician) prevents the internal bolster from adhering to the gastric wall. Regular monitoring and meticulous hygiene protect skin integrity and promote patient comfort.

Medication Administration via Gastrostomy Tube

Administering medications via a gastrostomy tube is a common nursing procedure that requires strict adherence to pharmacological safety to prevent tube occlusion and ensure therapeutic drug efficacy. Whenever possible, liquid medications are the preferred dosage form. If solid medications must be used, the nurse must verify with a pharmacist that the tablet can be safely crushed. Enteric-coated, sustained-release, sublingual, and buccal medications must never be crushed or administered through a feeding tube, as altering their form can lead to toxicity, erratic drug absorption, or destruction of the tube lumen. Medications should be prepared individually; they must not be mixed together in the same syringe or added directly into the hanging feeding formula. Before giving any medication, the nurse flushes the tube with at least 15 to 30 milliliters of warm water. Each medication is administered separately, followed by a 5 to 10 milliliter water flush between different drugs. After the final medication is given, a post-medication flush of 30 to 50 milliliters of water is required to clear the tube completely. If the patient is receiving continuous tube feedings, the feeding must be paused before administering incompatible medications, and the tube must be flushed as outlined. Precise documentation of drug administration ensures patient safety and maintains continuity of care.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Gastrostomy feeding is associated with several potential complications that require vigilant nursing monitoring and prompt intervention. Common early and late complications include tube dislodgement, tube occlusion (clogging), stoma site infections, leakage around the site, aspiration, and gastrointestinal symptoms like diarrhea, nausea, cramping, and constipation. Tube occlusion is frequently caused by inadequate flushing after feedings or medication administration; if a clog occurs, the nurse should attempt to clear it using warm water and gentle back-and-forth motion with a feeding syringe, avoiding excessive pressure or stiff wires. If a tube becomes accidentally dislodged, this is a medical emergency—especially in newly placed tracts—because the stoma can close rapidly within hours. The nurse must immediately notify the physician, cover the site with a sterile dressing, and never attempt to blindly force a new tube back into a fresh tract. Aspiration is another serious risk managed by maintaining proper head elevation, checking residuals, and monitoring for respiratory distress. Diarrhea may result from rapid infusion rates, cold formula, or medication side effects, requiring adjustments in administration speed or formula composition. Early identification of these complications allows nurses to provide life-saving interventions and prevent further morbidity.

Patient and Caregiver Education for Home Care

Comprehensive patient and caregiver education is paramount for safe and successful long-term home care following gastrostomy tube placement. Before hospital discharge, nurses must ensure that patients and their families demonstrate competence in all aspects of tube management through return demonstrations. Key educational topics include proper hand hygiene, formula storage and preparation, techniques for administering intermittent or continuous feeds, and the correct execution of water flushes. Caregivers must be thoroughly taught how to perform daily stoma site cleaning, inspect the skin for signs of infection or breakdown, and check external tube length measurements to monitor for migration. Additionally, education must cover troubleshooting skills, such as what to do if the tube clogs or accidentally falls out, emphasizing emergency contact numbers and the importance of never attempting blind re-insertion into a fresh tract. Warning signs that require immediate medical attention—such as persistent fever, severe abdominal pain, excessive leakage, bleeding, or respiratory difficulty—must be clearly articulated in both verbal explanations and written discharge instructions. Empowering patients and caregivers with this knowledge promotes independence, enhances confidence, and drastically reduces the incidence of home-based complications.
