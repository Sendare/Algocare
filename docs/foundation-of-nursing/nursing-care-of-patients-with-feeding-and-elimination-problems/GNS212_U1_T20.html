Catheters and Catheterisation

Introduction to Catheters and Urinary Elimination

Urinary elimination is a basic human need that removes waste and keeps body fluids in balance. Sometimes, disease, surgery, or injury makes natural urination impossible. When this happens, a urinary catheter is used. A catheter is a hollow, flexible tube passed through the urethra into the bladder to drain urine. As nurses, our goal is to maintain or restore normal elimination while protecting the patient from infection and trauma. Understanding when and how to use a catheter safely is a core nursing skill.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Urinary Tract

Safe catheterisation requires a clear mental picture of the lower urinary tract. Urine flows from the kidneys down the ureters into the muscular storage sac called the bladder. From the bladder, urine exits the body through the urethra.

  • Female Urethra: Approximately 3.5 to 5 cm long, straight, and located close to the vagina and anus, which increases the risk of contamination.
  • Male Urethra: Approximately 18 to 20 cm long, curved, and passes through the prostate gland and penis.

A common mistake for beginners is confusing the female urethral opening with the vaginal opening. The urethral meatus sits just below the clitoris and above the vagina.

Types of Urinary Catheters and Equipment

Choosing the right equipment ensures patient comfort and safety. Catheters come in various materials, sizes, and designs depending on the clinical need.

Catheter sizes are measured in the French (Fr) scale, where a larger number means a wider tube. Most adult sizes range from 14Fr to 16Fr. Common materials include latex, silicone, and teflon-coated rubber.

Indications for Catheterisation

A urinary catheter is a medical intervention that requires a clear clinical justification. Nurses must verify the doctor's order and confirm an appropriate indication before proceeding.

Common valid reasons for catheterisation include acute urinary retention or bladder outlet obstruction, accurate measurement of hourly urine output in critically ill patients, assistance with healing open sacral or perineal wounds in incontinent patients, and specific urological surgeries. Using a catheter merely for staff convenience or patient incontinence without wounds is considered poor practice and increases infection risks unnecessarily.

Pre-Procedural Assessment and Preparation

Before touching the patient, the nurse must perform a thorough assessment. Check the patient's medical history for any previous urethral surgeries, strictures, or latex allergies. Assess the bladder fullness by palpating and inspecting the lower abdomen for distension. Explain the procedure clearly to the patient to gain cooperation and reduce anxiety. Gather all necessary supplies—such as the catheter kit, sterile gloves, antiseptic solution, and securement device—and ensure adequate lighting and privacy in the room.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Male and Female Catheterisation

Catheter insertion is a strictly sterile procedure designed to protect the patient from introducing pathogens into the urinary tract.

  1. Positioning: Place the female in a dorsal recumbent position with knees flexed, and the male in a supine position with legs extended.
  2. Perineal Care: Clean the area thoroughly using antiseptic wipes, wiping from front to back in females, and in a circular motion from the meatus outward in males.
  3. Insertion: Lubricate the catheter tip. Gently insert the tube into the urethral meatus until urine begins to flow, then advance it slightly further (especially in males to ensure the balloon is fully inside the bladder before inflation).
  4. Inflation and Securing: Inflate the retention balloon with sterile water according to the manufacturer's volume, gently pull back to feel resistance, and secure the tubing to the patient's thigh.

Ongoing Catheter Care and Maintenance

Once a catheter is in place, daily maintenance prevents infection and blockage. The drainage bag must always be kept below the level of the bladder to prevent backflow of urine. Ensure the tubing has no kinks or loops that could trap urine. Perform daily perineal hygiene using mild soap and water, wiping away from the insertion site. Never disconnect the catheter from the tubing unless an irrigation order is present, as breaking the closed system invites bacteria.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Catheters carry risks that require vigilant nursing oversight. The most common complication is a urinary tract infection (UTI), marked by fever, cloudy urine, foul odor, or lower back pain. Other issues include urethral trauma from forceful insertion, bladder spasms causing leakage around the tube, and catheter blockage from blood clots or sediment. If urine stops flowing, the nurse should first check the tubing for kinks before suspecting an internal blockage.

Catheter Removal Protocol

Removing a catheter should happen as soon as it is medically unnecessary to reduce infection risks. Check the doctor's order, explain the process to the patient, and prepare a clean collection container. Attach a syringe to the inflation port and completely withdraw all sterile water from the retention balloon—never cut the port tubing. Gently pull the catheter out, encouraging the patient to take slow, deep breaths to relax the pelvic muscles. Document the removal time and monitor the patient's first spontaneous voiding afterwards.
