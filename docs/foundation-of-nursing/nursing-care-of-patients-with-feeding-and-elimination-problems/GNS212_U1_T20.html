Catheters and Catheterisation

Anatomy and Physiology of the Urinary Tract

Successful urinary catheterisation demands a working spatial map of the lower urinary tract. Urine collects in the muscular reservoir known as the bladder, which transitions inferiorly into the internal urethral sphincter under involuntary control. The pathway then traverses the urethra, a fibromuscular tube carrying urine to the exterior body surface.

Significant anatomical divergence exists between sexes. The female urethra measures a brief 3 to 5 centimetres, running straight from the bladder neck to the external meatus situated anterior to the vaginal opening. This short track drastically lowers the mechanical barrier against ascending microorganisms, elevating baseline female susceptibility to urinary tract infections during manipulation. Conversely, the male urethra spans roughly 18 to 20 centimetres, featuring distinct prostatic, membranous, and spongy segments alongside two natural anatomical curves. Negotiating these curves and the resistance of the external urethral sphincter requires precise procedural technique to avoid urethral trauma in male patients.

Types of Urinary Catheters and Equipment

Selecting the correct tubing and retention device dictates procedural success and patient comfort. Catheters vary by material, lumen count, and tip configuration.

  • Intermittent Catheters (Nelaton): Single-lumen, rigid or flexible tubes designed for immediate drainage, removed instantly once the bladder empties.
  • Indwelling Retention Catheters (Foley): Double or triple-lumen tubes featuring an inflatable balloon near the tip to anchor the catheter inside the bladder.
  • Coudé Catheters: Feature a curved, slightly angulated tip specifically engineered to bypass an enlarged prostate gland in male patients.
  • Materials: Latex serves for short-term use, while silicone or Teflon-coated alternatives reduce tissue irritation and encrustation for long-term indwelling needs.

Indications for Catheterisation

Placing an indwelling or intermittent drainage device requires clear clinical justification. Unnecessary catheterisation introduces severe infection risks without therapeutic benefit.

  • Acute Urinary Retention: Inability to void caused by mechanical blockage or neurological shock.
  • Accurate Input and Output Monitoring: Critical care management for unstable patients requiring precise fluid balance tracking.
  • Urological Surgeries: Maintaining a dry surgical field or protecting healing anastomoses in the pelvic region.
  • Sacral Pressure Injury Healing: Diverting urine away from unhealed wounds in incontinent, immobile patients to prevent skin breakdown.
  • Palliative Comfort: Managing end-stage incontinence when moving the patient causes intractable pain.

Pre-Procedural Assessment and Preparation

Before opening any sterile pack, evaluate the patient's baseline clinical status. Check for known allergies to latex, adhesives, or antiseptic solutions like chlorhexidine. Review past medical history for urethral strictures, previous pelvic surgeries, or radical prostatectomies that alter pelvic anatomy.

Explain the procedural steps clearly to secure informed consent and reduce patient anxiety. Gather all necessary equipment at the bedside, ensuring adequate lighting and proper ergonomic bed height to protect the clinician's posture. Verify that the patient has not voided recently if a residual urine volume check is the primary goal, or encourage an attempt if sterility is the sole focus.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Male and Female Catheterisation

Maintaining absolute sterile technique is non-negotiable during catheter insertion. Perform hand hygiene, establish a sterile field, and don sterile gloves. Position the patient appropriately: dorsal recumbent with knees bent for females, and supine with legs extended for males.

Cleanse the periurethral area using antiseptic swabs, wiping from cleanest to dirtiest—from the clitoris downward for females, and in a circular motion from the meatus outward for males. Lubricate the catheter tip generously. Gently insert the tube until urine flows, then advance it a few additional centimetres to ensure the balloon resides entirely within the bladder before inflation. Inflate the retention balloon with sterile water strictly according to manufacturer specifications, then pull back gently until resistance is felt to seat the balloon at the bladder neck.

Ongoing Catheter Care and Maintenance

Once in place, an indwelling catheter demands diligent daily maintenance to thwart ascending infections and mechanical blockages. Cleanse the insertion site daily with mild soap and water during routine hygiene, avoiding harsh antiseptics or powders that foster bacterial growth.

Keep the drainage bag continuously below the level of the bladder at all times to prevent backflow of contaminated urine. Ensure the tubing remains free of kinks, coils, or patient compression that obstructs drainage. Empty the collection bag regularly using a clean container for each patient, preventing the spigot from touching contaminated surfaces. Maintain a closed drainage system; breaking the junction between the catheter and tubing invites immediate bacterial colonization.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Catheterisation carries inherent physical risks that require vigilant monitoring and swift clinical intervention.

  • Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection (CAUTI): Manifests as fever, foul-smelling cloudy urine, or suprapubic tenderness; managed by reviewing the ongoing necessity of the catheter and obtaining urine cultures.
  • Urethral Trauma and Hematuria: Caused by traumatic insertion or excessive traction; managed by securing the tubing properly to the thigh or abdomen.
  • Bladder Spasms: Painful cramping triggered by balloon irritation; managed by checking for mechanical obstructions before administering prescribed antispasmodics.
  • Encrustation and Blockage: Mineral buildup that halts drainage; managed by irrigating or replacing the catheter per protocol.

Catheter Removal Protocol

Removing an indwelling catheter requires a valid medical order and careful monitoring of post-removal voiding function. Gather a clean syringe sized to match the volume marked on the inflation valve. Insert the syringe hub fully and allow the sterile water to withdraw passively; do not force withdrawal if water fails to return.

Explain to the patient that removal causes minimal discomfort. Gently withdraw the catheter along the natural urethral axis. Document the exact time of removal and instruct the patient to report their first spontaneous void. Monitor for urinary retention by palpating the suprapubic region or scanning bladder volume if the patient fails to void within six to eight hours post-removal.
