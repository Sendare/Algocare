Rectal Washout

Introduction and Definition of Rectal Washout

A rectal washout, sometimes called a colonic irrigation or rectal lavage, is a therapeutic nursing procedure involving the introduction of fluid into the rectum and lower colon to cleanse the bowel. Unlike a standard enema designed primarily to stimulate peristalsis and relieve acute constipation, a rectal washout often uses larger volumes of fluid administered in cycles to thoroughly remove impacted stool, mucus, blood, or toxic substances from the distal bowel.

In specialized surgical units, particularly pediatric surgery, rectal washouts are frequently performed to decompress the bowel in conditions like Hirschsprung's disease before definitive surgical correction. Understanding the fundamental goal—clearing the lower bowel completely rather than just triggering a single bowel movement—helps nurses explain the procedure clearly to patients and manage equipment appropriately.

Clinical Indications for Rectal Washout

Rectal washouts are prescribed across various clinical specialties, requiring nurses to recognize why a particular patient needs the intervention.

  • Hirschsprung's Disease: Used in infants to relieve severe bowel obstruction and wash out accumulated stool before corrective pull-through surgery.
  • Lower Bowel Preparation: Utilized when oral bowel preparations are contraindicated, ineffective, or when a clean distal segment is specifically required for diagnostic tests.
  • Toxic Ingestion: Occasionally employed to remove locally absorbed poisons or contrast media trapped in the lower bowel.
  • Fecal Impaction: Indicated for stubborn, rock-hard fecal masses unresponsive to standard digital disimpaction or conventional enemas.

Contraindications and Associated Risks

Because introducing fluid into a compromised colon carries significant hazards, nurses must carefully screen patients before the procedure begins. Performing a washout blindly without checking for recent bowel surgery can lead to catastrophic outcomes.

  • Recent Bowel Anastomosis: Fresh surgical joins in the colon can easily rupture under fluid pressure, leading to peritonitis.
  • Severe Bowel Infarction or Ischemia: Weakened, dying intestinal walls have a high perforation risk.
  • Active Gastrointestinal Bleeding: Introducing fluids may obscure the bleeding source or worsen hemorrhage.
  • Fluid and Electrolyte Imbalance: Large volumes of tap water absorbed through the rectal mucosa can cause water intoxication and severe hyponatremia, especially in infants.

Equipment Preparation and Patient Positioning

Proper preparation ensures safety, comfort, and efficiency during the washout procedure. Nurses must gather all supplies beforehand and create a private, warm environment.

  1. Solution: Prepare prescribed warm fluid, typically normal saline to prevent dangerous electrolyte shifts, warmed to body temperature.
  2. Tubing and Container: Irrigating container, connecting tubing, and a flow clamp.
  3. Catheter: An appropriate size rectal tube or Foley catheter (without over-inflating the balloon, or avoiding balloons entirely in pediatrics).
  4. Protection: Waterproof underpads, bedpan, or a specialized washout collection system.
  5. Positioning: Place the adult patient in the Left lateral Sims' position, which aligns naturally with the anatomical curve of the sigmoid colon and rectum.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Performing a Rectal Washout

Executing a rectal washout requires sterile or clean technique depending on institutional policy, gentle handling, and constant observation of patient tolerance.

Explain every step to the conscious patient to reduce anxiety. Lubricate the rectal tube generously and insert it gently through the anal sphincter, never forcing resistance. Elevate the container no more than 30 to 45 centimeters above the patient's hips to maintain a safe, low-pressure gravity flow. Allow a predetermined small volume of fluid to flow inward, then lower the container or connect to a drainage bag to siphon the fluid and stool back out. Repeat these gentle cycles of instillation and drainage until the return fluid runs clear of stool and debris. Stop immediately if the patient complains of sharp abdominal cramping or if rectal bleeding appears.

Patient Monitoring and Post-Procedure Assessment

The nurse's responsibility does not end when the washout tubing is removed. Continuous clinical vigilance is required to catch delayed complications early.

Monitor vital signs closely, watching for signs of vagal stimulation—such as sudden bradycardia, dizziness, or sweating—during rectal manipulation. Assess the abdomen for any new distension, tenderness, or rigidity that might suggest bowel perforation. Measure and record the net fluid balance by comparing the total volume of fluid instilled against the total volume returned, ensuring that the patient is not retaining excessive free water that could trigger hyponatremia.

Documentation and Nursing Responsibilities

Accurate charting provides a legal and clinical record of the procedure's success and the patient's tolerance. Nurses must document the exact time, type, and volume of fluid instilled, along with the precise character and volume of the return effluent. Note the patient's subjective response, including comfort levels and any adverse symptoms like cramping or bleeding. Finally, report any abnormal findings—such as persistent bleeding, severe pain, or a large fluid deficit—to the attending physician promptly.
