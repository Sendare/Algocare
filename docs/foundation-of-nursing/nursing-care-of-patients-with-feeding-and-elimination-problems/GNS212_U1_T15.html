Care of Patients with Urinary Problems

Anatomy and Physiology of the Urinary System

The human urinary system consists of two kidneys, two ureters, a urinary bladder, and a urethra. The primary functional unit of the kidney is the nephron, responsible for filtering blood, reabsorbing vital nutrients and water, and excreting metabolic wastes as urine.

  • Kidneys: Maintain fluid and electrolyte balance, regulate blood pressure via the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, and produce erythropoietin for red blood cell synthesis.
  • Ureters: Muscular tubes that propel urine from the renal pelves to the bladder via peristaltic waves.
  • Bladder: A hollow, distensible muscular organ composed of detrusor muscle that stores urine until voluntary micturition occurs.
  • Urethra: Conveys urine from the bladder exteriorly; notably shorter in females, which predisposes them to ascending urinary tract infections.

Normal urine output averages about 30 to 50 milliliters per hour in an adult. Drop below this threshold for consecutive hours signals poor renal perfusion or obstruction, requiring immediate clinical attention.

Common Types of Urinary Dysfunction

Urinary disorders manifest in diverse ways, altering normal storage and elimination patterns. Recognizing these distinct presentations helps direct focused nursing interventions.

  • Urinary Retention: The inability to empty the bladder completely, often caused by benign prostatic hyperplasia, urethral strictures, or neuromuscular impairment.
  • Urinary Incontinence: Involuntary leakage of urine. Types include stress incontinence (leakage during coughing or sneezing), urge incontinence (sudden, intense compulsion to void), and overflow incontinence (dribbling from an overfilled bladder).
  • Dysuria: Painful or difficult urination, frequently signaling inflammation or infection within the lower urinary tract.
  • Hematuria: The presence of red blood cells in the urine, which can be macroscopic (visible) or microscopic, pointing toward trauma, stones, or malignancy.

In busy Nigerian clinical wards, prolonged untreated retention is a frequent precursor to chronic kidney disease and severe bladder atony.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough urological assessment begins with a detailed health history focusing on voiding patterns, pain characteristics, fluid intake, and previous urological surgeries. Physical examination centers on abdominal inspection, palpation for bladder distension, and percussion over the costovertebral angles to detect renal tenderness.

Diagnostic tests provide objective data regarding renal architecture and function:

  • Urinalysis and Culture: Detects infection, hematuria, glucosuria, and identifies specific causative pathogens.
  • Blood Tests: Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen measure renal clearance efficiency.
  • Ultrasonography: Non-invasive imaging used to visualize post-void residual urine volume and renal structural anomalies.
  • Cystoscopy: Direct endoscopic visualization of the urethral mucosa and bladder interior.

When checking for post-void residual volume via ultrasound or straight catheterization, perform the procedure within ten minutes of the patient attempting to void for accuracy.

Comprehensive Nursing Care for Urinary Conditions

Managing urinary alterations requires a systematic approach prioritizing patient comfort, infection control, and physiological stability. For patients experiencing acute retention, immediate relief via catheterization is warranted, but rapid decompression of an overdistended bladder must be avoided to prevent sudden hematuria and hypotension.

Monitor daily fluid balance meticulously. Maintain accurate intake and output records, ensuring the patient receives adequate hydration unless contraindicated by cardiac or renal failure. Administer prescribed medications such as alpha-blockers to relax smooth muscle in prostatic hyperplasia or anticholinergics to calm an overactive detrusor muscle.

Skin integrity is another major concern, particularly for patients with urinary incontinence. Moisture-associated skin damage can develop rapidly; therefore, implement prompt cleansing, barrier cream application, and scheduled toileting regimens.

Catheter Care and Infection Prevention

Indwelling urinary catheters are frequent sources of catheter-associated urinary tract infections, making rigorous aseptic technique during insertion and meticulous ongoing maintenance mandatory.

  • Maintain a closed drainage system at all times. Breaking the junction between the catheter and tubing introduces environmental pathogens.
  • Secure the catheter tubing to the patient's thigh or lower abdomen to minimize urethral traction and micro-tears.
  • Keep the collection bag positioned below the level of the bladder to prevent the backflow of urine.
  • Perform daily perineal hygiene using soap and water, cleaning away from the urethral meatus along the tubing.

Never elevate the drainage bag above the bladder level during patient transfers or stretcher transport. If a break in sterility or leakage occurs, the entire system must be replaced under sterile protocol.

Patient Education and Rehabilitation

Restoring or adapting to optimal urinary function requires structured patient education and supportive rehabilitation programs. Empowering patients with self-management skills decreases hospital readmissions and improves quality of life.

Teaching strategies incorporate several key components:

  • Pelvic Floor Muscle Training: Instructing patients to perform Kegel exercises to strengthen pubococcygeal muscles, improving support for stress incontinence.
  • Bladder Retraining: Establishing a scheduled voiding timetable that gradually increases intervals between voids to suppress urgency.
  • Hydration Guidelines: Advising consistent fluid intake spaced throughout the day while limiting fluids right before bedtime to reduce nocturia.
  • Intermittent Self-Catheterization: Teaching patients with neurogenic bladders how to perform clean intermittent catheterization safely at home.

Ensure teaching materials match the patient's literacy level and language preference, verifying understanding through a teach-back method before discharge.
