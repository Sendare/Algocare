Bladder Irrigation

Introduction and Purpose of Bladder Irrigation

Bladder irrigation is a specialized nursing procedure involving the instillation of sterile fluid into the urinary bladder. The primary objective is to maintain patency of an indwelling urinary catheter, remove blood clots, pus, or sediment, and administer local medication directly to the urothelium. In Nigerian surgical wards, this technique is frequently utilized post-prostatectomy, particularly after transurethral resection of the prostate, where hematuria and clot formation pose a constant risk of acute urinary retention.

Indications and Contraindications

Clinical application of bladder irrigation requires careful patient selection.

  • Indications: Gross hematuria with clot risk, presence of sediment or thick mucus, post-urological surgery, and the need for continuous or intermittent instillation of therapeutic antimicrobial or antineoplastic agents.
  • Contraindications: Recent trauma to the urinary tract that disrupts structural integrity, suspected or confirmed urethral tear, active radical prostatectomy with fresh anastomotic suture lines, and unexplained anuria.

Open versus Closed Irrigation Systems

Bladder irrigation can be performed using two distinct structural setups, each carrying different implications for infection control.

Required Equipment and Preparation

Proper preparation ensures both safety and efficiency during the procedure. Gather sterile irrigation fluid (typically normal saline), a calibrated IV pole, a dedicated irrigation tubing set or sterile syringe depending on the method, an antiseptic swab, a clean procedure tray, and a graduated collection container for accurate output measurement. Always check the medical prescription for the exact type, volume, and rate of the irrigant before touching the patient. Explain the procedural steps to the client to reduce anxiety and secure cooperation.

Continuous Bladder Irrigation Procedure

Continuous bladder irrigation (CBI) relies on a triple-lumen catheter: one lumen for balloon inflation, one for urine outflow, and a third for fluid inflow.

  1. Perform hand hygiene and put on non-sterile gloves for initial setup, transitioning to sterile technique when handling connection ports.
  2. Prime the irrigation tubing with sterile normal saline, expelling all air bubbles to prevent gas introduction into the bladder.
  3. Connect the primed tubing to the inflow port of the triple-lumen catheter.
  4. Adjust the roller clamp to regulate the drip rate according to the medical prescription or until the outflow runs clear of bright red blood and large clots.
  5. Monitor the drainage bag continuously, ensuring that the total output exceeds or equals the total fluid input.

Intermittent Bladder Irrigation Procedure

Intermittent irrigation is performed using an open system or a dual-lumen catheter equipped with a needleless injection port. The nurse draws a prescribed volume of sterile saline (usually 30 to 50 mL) into a sterile syringe. After cleansing the catheter port with an antiseptic swab, the nurse slowly and gently instills the fluid into the bladder. The syringe is then disconnected or opened to gravity drainage, allowing the instilled fluid and waste products to drain into a collection receptacle. Never force fluid against resistance, as this risks bladder rupture.

Monitoring, Complications, and Troubleshooting

Diligent monitoring prevents severe complications such as acute bladder distension, water intoxication, and severe hemorrhage. The cardinal rule of continuous irrigation is fluid balance tracking: input must be subtracted from total output to determine true urine volume. If the drainage stops, the nurse must immediately check for kinks in the tubing, verify roller clamp settings, and perform a manual flush if ordered. Neglecting an obstructed CBI line can lead to severe lower abdominal pain, autonomic dysreflexia in spinal cord injury patients, and suture line disruption.

Documentation and Nursing Responsibilities

Accurate documentation completes the clinical procedure and ensures continuity of care across nursing shifts. The nurse must record the exact start time, type and volume of irrigation fluid used, color and consistency of the return effluent, presence of any blood clots or debris, and net fluid balance at the end of every shift. Furthermore, reporting abnormal findings—such as persistent bright red bleeding, severe bladder spasms, or a sudden cessation of output—to the attending physician is a critical legal and professional responsibility.
