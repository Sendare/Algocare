Nasogastric Tube Feeding

Indications and Contraindications for Nasogastric Feeding

Nasogastric (NG) tube feeding provides nutritional support directly to the stomach via a flexible tube passed through the nasopharynx. It serves patients with a functional gastrointestinal tract who cannot take adequate nutrients orally.

  • Indications: Severe dysphagia from stroke, altered level of consciousness, facial trauma, severe protein-energy malnutrition, and esophageal obstruction by benign tumors.
  • Contraindications: Basilar skull fractures, severe midface trauma, esophageal strictures, recent esophageal or gastric surgery, and uncorrected coagulopathy.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Equipment Preparation

Preparation before inserting an NG tube ensures patient safety and procedural success. Begin by verifying the medical order and assessing the patient's nasal passages for septal deviation, polyps, or trauma. Check bowel sounds and abdominal distension.

Gather the necessary equipment at the bedside:

  • Appropriate sized NG tube (usually 14-16 Fr for adults)
  • Water-soluble lubricant
  • 50 mL catheter-tip syringe
  • Stethoscope
  • Hypoallergenic tape
  • Glass of water with a straw (if permitted)
  • Emesis basin and tissues

Step-by-Step Procedure for Tube Insertion

Insert the NG tube with the patient in a high Fowler's position to reduce aspiration risk. Measure the correct insertion length using the NEX method: measure from the Nose tip to the Earlobe, then down to the Xiphiid process. Mark this length with tape or note the centimeter markings.

Lubricate the distal 5 to 10 cm of the tube with water-soluble jelly. Instruct the patient to tilt their head slightly hyperextended as you gently pass the tube through the nostril aiming downward and backward. Once the tube reaches the nasopharynx, ask the patient to flex their head forward and swallow small sips of water to advance the tube down the esophagus with each swallow. Stop immediately and withdraw the tube if the patient experiences severe respiratory distress, cyanosis, or significant coughing.

Methods for Verifying Nasogastric Tube Placement

Verifying correct tube placement before every feed or medication administration prevents pulmonary aspiration of nutritional formulas.

  • X-ray imaging: The gold standard method that provides definitive visual confirmation of the tip inside the stomach.
  • pH testing: Aspirating gastric contents and testing acidity; a pH of 1 to 4 strongly indicates gastric placement.
  • Visual assessment: Inspecting the color of aspirated fluid, which should be grassy green, tan, or clear.

*A common misconception*: Auscultation—injecting air through the tube while listening over the epigastrium with a stethoscope—is unreliable and no longer recommended as a primary verification method because air sounds similar over the lungs, pharynx, and stomach.

Administering Enteral Feedings and Water Flushes

Enteral feedings can be given intermittently using a gravity bag or continuous infusion via an electronic pump. Always check gastric residual volume (GRV) before intermittent feedings; large residuals (usually over 250 to 500 mL depending on facility protocol) indicate delayed gastric emptying.

Keep the head of the bed elevated at least 30 to 45 degrees during feeding and for 30 to 60 minutes afterward to prevent aspiration. Flush the tube with 30 mL of sterile or warm tap water before and after feedings, and after medication administration, to maintain patency and prevent clogging.

Monitoring, Maintenance, and Prevention of Complications

Continuous care minimizes the risk of local tissue damage and systemic complications associated with enteral tubes.

  • Nasal skin breakdown: Clean the nostril daily, secure the tube to the cheek to avoid pressure on the nares, and rotate the taping point.
  • Tube occlusion: Flush regularly with warm water and crush medications thoroughly.
  • Diarrhea and cramping: Often caused by cold formula or rapid infusion rates; warm formula to room temperature and adjust the pump rate.
  • Aspiration pneumonia: Maintain head elevation and monitor for coughing, shortness of breath, or fever.

Documentation and Tube Removal

Accurate documentation ensures continuity of care and legal protection. Record the date and time of insertion, tube size and type, verification method used, patient tolerance, and the external length marking at the nostril.

Remove the NG tube when the patient resumes adequate oral intake or the tube is no longer needed. Explain the procedure, place a waterproof pad on the chest, clamp or kink the tube, ask the patient to take a deep breath and hold it, and smoothly withdraw the tube. Clean the nose and mouth, document the removal time, and note the condition of the removed tube.
