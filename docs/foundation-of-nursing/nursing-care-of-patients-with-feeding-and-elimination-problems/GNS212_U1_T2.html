Nasogastric Tube Feeding

Indications and Purpose of Nasogastric Tube Feeding

Nasogastric tube feeding, often called enteral nutrition, is a method used to deliver liquid nutrients directly into the stomach via a flexible tube passed through the nose. This intervention is lifesaver for patients who have a functional gastrointestinal tract but cannot swallow safely or take enough food by mouth.

Common reasons for placing a nasogastric tube include:

  • Severe dysphagia: Difficulty swallowing caused by a stroke, head injury, or advanced neurological disease.
  • Facial or esophageal trauma: Injuries that physically block normal oral intake or make chewing impossible.
  • Coma or altered consciousness: Patients who are unable to protect their airway during normal feeding.
  • High nutritional demands: Critically ill patients, such as those with severe burns, whose bodies need extra calories and protein to heal.

Providing nutrition this way helps maintain the structural integrity of the gut mucosa, preventing the bacteria translocation that often occurs when the bowels are left completely empty. It is always preferred over intravenous nutrition if the gut is working, because it preserves normal digestive function and has fewer systemic infection risks.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before inserting a nasogastric tube, a thorough physical assessment ensures the procedure is safe and reduces the risk of complications. You must inspect the patient's nasal passages for any polyps, a deviated septum, trauma, or bleeding disorders that make insertion difficult or dangerous. Always check if the patient has a history of nasal surgery or a basal skull fracture, as these are absolute contraindications for the nasal route.

Explain the entire procedure clearly to the conscious patient, even if they are anxious. Gaining cooperation makes insertion much smoother because the patient can swallow on cue, which guides the tube down the esophagus instead of the trachea. Establish a simple hand signal, like raising a finger, so the patient can pause you if they feel distressed. Position the patient in a high Fowler's position, sitting upright at a 45 to 90-degree angle, to align the upper airway and esophagus properly.

Equipment Needed for Insertion and Feeding

Gathering your supplies beforehand ensures a calm, efficient procedure. Missing an item halfway through insertion places unnecessary stress on both the nurse and the patient.

  • Nasogastric tube: Typically a Ryle's tube or Levin tube made of polyurethane or silicone, sized appropriately in French (Fr) gauges.
  • Water-soluble lubricant: Facilitates smooth passage and minimizes friction against delicate nasal mucosa.
  • Stethoscope: Used alongside an air bolus syringe to check tube placement.
  • pH testing strip: Measures the acidity of aspirated fluid to confirm stomach placement.
  • Hypoallergenic tape: Secures the external tube firmly to the bridge of the nose.
  • Emesis basin and tissues: Prepared nearby for expected coughing, gagging, or vomiting.
  • Glass of water with a straw: Helps the conscious patient swallow during advancement.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Insertion

Inserting a nasogastric tube requires steady hands, patience, and careful attention to the patient's reactions. First, estimate the correct insertion length using the NEX measurement: measure from the tip of the Nose, to the Earlobe, and down to the xiphoid process (X) of the sternum. Mark this length with a small piece of tape or note the centimeter numbers on the tube.

Lubricate the first 5 to 10 centimeters of the tube. Ask the patient to tilt their head slightly backward as you gently insert the tube through the nostril, aiming downward and backward toward the ear. Once you feel resistance at the back of the nasopharynx, instruct the patient to chin-to-chest and begin taking small sips of water through a straw if permitted. Advance the tube smoothly each time the patient swallows. If the patient begins coughing, gasps for air, or turns cyanotic, stop immediately and pull the tube back because it has likely entered the trachea.

Methods for Verifying Tube Placement

Verifying that a nasogastric tube is inside the stomach before feeding or giving medication is a critical nursing responsibility. Mistakes here can lead to fatal pulmonary aspiration.

  • X-ray confirmation: This is the gold standard. An abdominal X-ray must be performed after initial placement to visualize the tip of the tube clearly below the diaphragm.
  • Aspirate pH testing: Draw back stomach contents using a syringe. Stomach fluid is typically acidic, with a pH of 5.5 or lower. A high alkaline pH often means the tube is in the lungs or intestines.
  • Visual inspection of aspirate: Stomach contents often look grassy green, tan, or cloudy, whereas respiratory secretions are usually clear or mucus-tinged.

Note that the old method of auscultating by injecting air and listening for a whoosh over the stomach is unreliable and no longer accepted as a safe primary confirmation method because the sound can easily travel from the lungs or pharynx.

Administering Enteral Feedings and Water Flushes

Once placement is confirmed, administering the feeding safely requires careful infection control and flow management. Feedings can be given intermittently using a gravity bag or continuously using an electronic feeding pump.

Always check gastric residual volumes before intermittent feedings if required by facility policy. High residual volumes mean the stomach is not emptying properly, placing the patient at high risk for vomiting and aspiration. Flush the tube with 30 milliliters of warm water before and after every feeding or medication administration to keep the narrow lumen from clogging.

Keep the patient's head elevated at least 30 to 45 degrees during the entire feeding and for at least 30 to 60 minutes afterward. Never rush a gravity feeding; allow the formula to flow slowly by adjusting the roller clamp rather than squeezing the bag, which puts too much pressure on the stomach and causes cramping.

Monitoring, Maintenance, and Prevention of Complications

A patient with a nasogastric tube requires ongoing nursing vigilance to catch minor problems before they turn into severe medical emergencies.

  • Clogged tubes: Prevent blockages by flushing regularly and crushing medications thoroughly into a fine powder dissolved in warm water.
  • Nasal skin breakdown: The tape and tube press constantly against delicate skin. Clean the nostril daily, apply mild barrier creams, and re-tape the tube slightly to a different spot on the nose.
  • Diarrhea and cramping: Often caused by feeding formulas that are too cold or infused too rapidly. Warm formulas to room temperature and slow down the pump rate.
  • Fluid and electrolyte imbalances: Monitor daily weights and strict input and output records because high-protein formulas can cause dehydration if water flushes are inadequate.

Documentation and Nasogastric Tube Removal

When the patient is able to tolerate adequate nutrition by mouth or the medical treatment is complete, the physician will order the nasogastric tube removed.

Explain the removal process to the patient; reassure them that removal is much quicker and less uncomfortable than insertion. Put on clean gloves, detach the tape from the nose, and place a waterproof pad or towel across the patient's chest. Ask the patient to take a deep breath and hold it. Holding the breath closes the glottis, which prevents accidental aspiration of any secretions remaining in the tube as you smoothly and steadily pull it out.

Inspect the tip of the tube to ensure it is intact. Offer mouthwash, oral hygiene, and tissues to clean the nose and mouth. Finally, document the procedure thoroughly in the nursing notes, recording the date, time, reason for removal, appearance of the tip, and how the patient tolerated the procedure.
