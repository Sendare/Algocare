Nasogastric Tube Feeding

Indications and Clinical Uses for Nasogastric Tube Feeding

Nasogastric tube feeding is a specialized method of providing nutritional support directly into the stomach via a flexible tube passed through the nasopharynx. This intervention is clinically indicated whenever a patient has a functioning gastrointestinal tract but is unable to ingest adequate oral nutrients to meet metabolic demands. - **Neurological Impairment**: Conditions such as cerebrovascular accidents, severe head trauma, or advanced dementia impair swallowing coordination and elevate aspiration risks. - **Structural or Mechanical Obstruction**: Facial trauma, severe burns, or obstructive tumors of the esophagus prevent safe mastication and passage of food. - **Severe Debility or Malnutrition**: Catabolic states, prolonged hypermetabolic stress, or severe anorexia nervosa where voluntary oral intake remains critically insufficient. - **Gastrointestinal Rest and Decompression**: Post-operative states or acute bowel obstruction requiring removal of gastric secretions.

Pre-Procedure Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before attempting insertion, a thorough clinical evaluation prevents avoidable complications such as nasal trauma, misplacement, or aspiration. Assess the patency of both nares by asking the patient to occlude one nostril at a time and breathe through the other, noting any septal deviation, polyps, or previous trauma. Evaluate the patient's level of consciousness and ability to cooperate, as an alert patient can assist swallowing during passage. Inspect the oral cavity to ensure the pharynx is clear. Explain the procedural steps clearly to gain patient cooperation and reduce anxiety. Establish a simple communication signal, such as raising a hand, to indicate the need to pause during insertion.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Insertion

Inserting a nasogastric tube requires precision and adherence to sterile or clean technique to minimize patient discomfort and procedural risk. 1. **Positioning**: Place the patient in a high Fowler's position to use gravity and facilitate anatomical alignment. 2. **Measurement**: Measure the distance from the tip of the nose to the earlobe, and down to the xiphoid process, marking the length with tape. 3. **Lubrication**: Apply a water-soluble lubricant to the distal 5 to 10 centimeters of the tube to ease mucosal passage. 4. **Insertion**: Gently insert the tube through the chosen nare, aiming downward and backward toward the ear. When resistance is felt at the nasopharynx, encourage the patient to swallow. 5. **Advancement**: Continue advancing the tube with each swallow until the pre-measured mark is reached. 6. **Securing**: Temporarily anchor the tube to the nose with tape until placement is definitively verified.

Methods for Verifying Tube Placement

Confirming correct gastric placement before initiating any feed or medication administration is a critical safety checkpoint. Relying on a single verification method is insufficient in clinical practice. | Verification Method | Clinical Description and Criteria | |---|---| | **Radiographic Imaging** | The gold standard. An abdominal X-ray confirms the exact terminal tip location in the stomach. | | **Aspirate Characteristics** | Visualizing gastric contents, which typically appear grassy green, tan, or cloudy off-white. | | **pH Testing** | Testing the acidity of gastric aspirate using pH paper. A reading between 1.0 and 4.0 strongly indicates gastric placement. | *Note*: The outdated practice of auscultating air auscultation over the epigastrium while pushing air through the tube is unreliable and no longer recommended by safety authorities.

Administering Enteral Feedings and Water Flushes

Once correct placement is verified, feeding can proceed safely. Enteral nutrition can be delivered via intermittent bolus using a syringe or continuously using an electronic feeding pump. Maintain the head of the bed elevated at a minimum of 30 to 45 degrees during feeding and for at least 30 to 60 minutes afterward to prevent pulmonary aspiration. Check residual gastric volume before bolus feedings, as large volumes indicate delayed gastric emptying. Flush the tube with 30 milliliters of water before and after intermittent feeds, and every 4 hours during continuous feeding, to maintain patency and prevent lumen occlusion.

Monitoring, Maintenance, and Complications

Continuous vigilance is necessary to manage potential complications associated with enteral tube feeding. Common mechanical problems include tube clogging, local skin irritation at the nostril, and accidental dislodgement. Metabolic and gastrointestinal issues such as diarrhea, nausea, cramping, and hyperglycemia can also occur, particularly when formulas are initiated too rapidly. Inspect the nasal mucosa daily for signs of pressure necrosis caused by tape friction. Rotate the securement tape position gently and provide meticulous nasal and oral hygiene to maintain mucosal integrity and patient comfort.

Documentation and Tube Removal

Accurate clinical documentation ensures continuity of care and legal accountability. Record the date, time, tube size, type, naris used, verification methods employed, patient tolerance, and residual volumes. When the patient is ready to resume oral nutrition or the tube is no longer clinically required, removal is ordered. Explain the procedure, place a waterproof pad under the chin, and fold the tube over itself near the exit site to prevent backflow of fluid. Instruct the patient to take a deep breath and hold it. Smoothly and steadily withdraw the tube in one continuous motion. Inspect the removed tube tip to ensure it is intact, provide oral hygiene, and document the removal details.
