Drainage

Introduction to Drainage Systems in Nursing

Drainage systems are essential medical devices used to remove unwanted fluids, blood, pus, or air from body cavities, wounds, or organs. In clinical practice across Nigerian health facilities, understanding how these systems work helps prevent complications such as fluid accumulation, tissue pressure, and severe infection. A drainage system can be as simple as a passive rubber strip or as complex as a regulated suction apparatus attached to a closed collection chamber. The primary physiological goal is to restore normal tissue apposition, promote wound healing, and monitor output volume and characteristics accurately.

Purposes and Clinical Indications for Drainage

Drains are deployed across various medical and surgical specialties based on specific clinical needs. Prophylactic placement happens during surgery to prevent anticipated fluid build-up in dead spaces. Therapeutic placement addresses existing collections such as abscesses, pleural effusions, or urinary retention.

  • Prophylactic drainage: Placed after clean-contaminated surgeries where serosanguineous fluid pooling is expected.
  • Therapeutic drainage: Used to evacuate active infections, hematomas, or excessive air in the pleural space.
  • Diagnostic drainage: Occasionally used to collect fluid samples for laboratory analysis and culture testing.

Open versus Closed Drainage Systems

Drainage systems are broadly categorized by how they interact with the external environment. Open systems empty directly onto a dressing or into an unsealed receptacle, carrying a higher risk of retrograde bacterial contamination. Closed systems utilize a sealed tubing network connected to a collection bag or bottle, maintaining a barrier against external pathogens.

Common Gastrointestinal and Urinary Drainage Tubes

Nurses frequently manage specialized tubes designed for the gastrointestinal and genitourinary tracts.

  • Nasogastric (NG) Tube: Inserted through the nose into the stomach for decompression, feeding, or medication administration.
  • Indwelling Urinary (Foley) Catheter: Placed in the bladder to continuously drain urine, allowing precise output monitoring and relief of urinary retention.
  • T-Tube: Placed in the common bile duct after biliary surgery to ensure bile bypasses the operative site while healing occurs.
  • Gastrostomy Tube: Placed surgically directly into the stomach for long-term nutritional support.

Patient Assessment and Monitoring of Drainage Sites

Evaluating a drainage site requires a systematic physical inspection and output review. The nurse must check the insertion site for erythema, swelling, warmth, and purulent discharge, which indicate potential infection. The tubing must be assessed for kinks, loops, or dependent sagging that impedes flow. Output must be quantified at regular intervals, noting color, consistency, and odor. A sudden cessation of drainage or a sharp spike in bright red blood warrants immediate reporting to the surgical or medical team.

Routine Nursing Care and Maintenance of Drainage Devices

Maintaining patency and preventing infection form the cornerstone of daily drainage device care. Hand hygiene must be executed strictly before and after handling any tubing or collection chamber. Emptying output containers requires a clean measuring cylinder and proper infection control protocols to avoid cross-contamination. Suction devices like Jackson-Pratt bulbs or Hemovacs must be periodically compressed and plugged to restore negative pressure. Furthermore, securing the tubing to the patient's gown prevents accidental traction and painful dislodgement during movement.

Managing Complications and Troubleshooting Blockages

Drainage complications range from minor mechanical failures to severe physiological hazards. Tube obstruction caused by blood clots or thick fibrin strands can lead to internal fluid pooling and pressure necrosis. If a blockage is suspected, the nurse should gently milk or strip the tubing only if institutional policy permits and clinical orders allow. Accidental dislodgement requires immediate sterile dressing coverage and urgent medical notification. Never force fluid into a blocked surgical drain unless explicitly directed by a surgeon, as this can introduce infection or disrupt delicate anastomoses.

Patient Education and Safe Removal Procedures

Preparing a patient for drain removal and educating them on home care reduces anxiety and promotes recovery. Before removal, patients should be informed that they might feel a brief pulling or stinging sensation. Analgesics administered thirty minutes prior can greatly improve comfort. Once the retention sutures are cut and the drain is smoothly withdrawn, an occlusive dressing is applied to seal the tract and absorb residual leakage. For patients discharged with chronic drains, teaching proper hygiene, output logging, and red-flag symptoms prevents avoidable readmissions.
