Drainage

Introduction and Purposes of Clinical Drainage

Drainage systems are essential tools used in hospital wards to remove unwanted fluids or air from the human body. In nursing practice, managing a drain requires understanding why it was inserted and how gravity or suction keeps it working. Without proper drainage, fluid can collect inside closed spaces, leading to pressure, severe pain, and dangerous bacterial growth.

Clinicians place drainage tubes for several primary reasons:

  • Preventing fluid accumulation: Removing blood, serum, or pus from surgical wounds to stop hematomas and abscesses.
  • Decompressing hollow organs: Relieving pressure inside the stomach or bladder by draining built-up gas and liquid.
  • Restoring negative pressure: Re-expanding the lungs by removing air or fluid from the pleural space.
  • Monitoring output: Measuring the exact volume and assessing the physical characteristics of bodily discharges after operations.

Types of Drainage Systems: Open versus Closed

Understanding the difference between open and closed drainage systems helps nurses prevent hospital-acquired infections.

An open system drains fluid directly onto a sterile dressing or into an unsealed receptacle. A classic example is a Penrose drain, which is a flat, soft rubber tube. Because there is no barrier between the inside of the wound and the outside air, the risk of bacteria entering the body is much higher. Nurses managing open drains must change soiled dressings frequently to keep the surrounding skin healthy.

A closed system uses a sealed, sterile container or collection bag attached to the tubing. Examples include Jackson-Pratt drains and urinary catheter bags. These systems often use gentle suction—like a compressed bulb or wall vacuum—to pull fluid out. Because the pathway from the body to the collection chamber remains closed, bacteria find it much harder to travel backward into the patient.

Common Gastrointestinal and Urinary Drainage Tubes

Nurses frequently care for patients with tubes placed in the gastrointestinal tract or the urinary system. Each tube has a specific design suited to its anatomical location.

Nasogastric (NG) tubes pass through the nose into the stomach. They are used either to feed patients who cannot swallow or to suction out stomach contents and gas, a process called gastric decompression. Nurses must always check correct tube placement before feeding or giving medications.

Indwelling urinary catheters, commonly known as Foley catheters, drain urine directly from the bladder. They feature a small balloon near the tip that is inflated with sterile water inside the bladder to keep the tubing from slipping out. Maintaining a clear, unobstructed path for urine flow is vital to prevent painful bladder spasms and urinary tract infections.

Patient Assessment and Monitoring of Drainage Sites

Regular assessment is the cornerstone of safe drain management. The nurse must evaluate both the patient and the drainage equipment during every shift.

When checking the drainage site, inspect the skin around the insertion point for redness, swelling, unusual warmth, or foul-smelling discharge, which often signal developing infection. Ensure the stitches holding the tube in place are intact and that the tubing is not pulled too tight.

When evaluating the output, document the exact amount, color, consistency, and odor. A sudden drop in output can mean the tube is blocked or the patient is dehydrated. Conversely, a sudden rush of bright red blood usually means active bleeding that requires immediate medical notification.

Routine Nursing Care and Maintenance

Keeping drainage systems functional requires consistent, sterile maintenance routines.

Emptying collection devices demands strict infection control practices. The nurse must wear clean gloves, use a separate measuring container for each patient, and avoid letting the drainage spout touch the container to prevent cross-contamination. Always record the output accurately in the fluid balance chart.

Securing the tubing is equally crucial. Pins or specialized clips should fasten the drain to the patient's clothing or bed linens, ensuring enough slack so movement does not pull on the insertion site. Never lift a drainage bag above the level of the insertion site unless it is temporarily clamped, because gravity will cause fluid to flow backward into the body.

Managing Complications and Troubleshooting Blockages

Drainage problems can happen at any time. Recognizing issues early prevents severe complications like organ damage or severe infections.

  • Tubing obstruction: Clots, mucus plugs, or accidental kinking can stop flow. If allowed by hospital policy and doctor's orders, gentle milking or stripping of the tubing can help dislodge clots.
  • Accidental dislodgement: If a drain falls out completely, cover the site immediately with a sterile occlusive dressing and notify the medical team. Never try to push a partially slipped tube back inside, as this introduces severe bacteria.
  • Fluid leakage around the site: Often caused by a blockage in the main tube forcing fluid out the sides, or by an enlarged insertion opening. Reinforce the dressing and report the issue.

Patient Education and Safe Removal Procedures

When a patient no longer needs a drain, the medical team plans for its removal. Preparing both the patient and the environment ensures a smooth procedure.

Explain the removal process clearly in simple language. Inform the patient that they may feel a brief pulling or stinging sensation, but that it happens quickly. Giving pain medication about thirty minutes before removal helps keep the patient comfortable.

During removal, the nurse puts on clean gloves, removes any secure sutures, and instructs the patient to take a deep breath and hold it (the Valsalva maneuver). This increases chest pressure and prevents air from entering the wound. Pull the tube out smoothly and swiftly, then immediately apply a sterile airtight dressing over the site.
