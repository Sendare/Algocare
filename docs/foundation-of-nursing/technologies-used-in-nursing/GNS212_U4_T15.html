Ventilator

Introduction to Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving supportive therapy used when a patient's natural respiratory system fails to maintain adequate gas exchange. The ventilator machine delivers pressurized air and oxygen directly into the patient's airways, assisting or completely taking over the work of breathing. This intervention does not treat the underlying disease process; instead, it buys critical time for medical treatments to take effect and for the body to heal. Nurses must understand that ventilation alters normal physiological pressures inside the chest, which directly impacts cardiovascular function.

Indications and Clinical Purposes of Ventilation

Patients require mechanical support when they can no longer breathe effectively on their own. The clinical reasons generally fall into two categories: acute respiratory failure and severe physiological exhaustion.

  • Hypoxemic respiratory failure: Occurs when the lungs cannot oxygenate the blood properly, seen in severe pneumonia or ARDS.
  • Hypercapnic respiratory failure: Occurs when the body cannot eliminate carbon dioxide effectively, often due to drug overdose or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation.
  • Airway protection: Needed when a patient has a depressed level of consciousness from head trauma or general anesthesia and cannot protect their airway from aspiration.
  • Work of breathing: The respiratory muscles are so fatigued that the patient will soon stop breathing entirely.

Basic Components and Modes of Mechanical Ventilators

A mechanical ventilator setup consists of the main machine console, corrugated tubing circuits, a humidification system, and an artificial airway like an endotracheal tube. The ventilator operates through specific modes that dictate how much work the machine does compared to the patient.

  • Control Mode: The ventilator delivers breaths at a set rate and volume regardless of patient effort.
  • Assist-Control (AC): The machine delivers a set volume or pressure whenever the patient initiates a breath, but also backs them up with a minimum rate.
  • Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV): Delivers preset breaths at set times, but allows the patient to breathe spontaneously between those breaths without machine assistance.

Patient Assessment and Baseline Monitoring

Monitoring a ventilated patient requires a combination of clinical observation and machine data interpretation. Nurses must regularly assess chest rise symmetry, breath sounds, and vital signs. A sudden drop in oxygen saturation or unilateral chest movement points toward dangerous complications like an endotracheal tube slipping into the right mainstem bronchus or a tension pneumothorax. Always check the ventilator screen for high and low pressure alarms, as these indicate changes in lung compliance or airway resistance.

Nursing Care and Management of the Ventilated Patient

Comprehensive nursing care keeps the ventilated patient safe, comfortable, and free from secondary harm. This involves regular oral care with chlorhexidine to reduce bacteria in the mouth that can travel down the airway. Suctioning is performed only when clinical signs like visible secretions or coughing demand it, rather than on a rigid routine, to prevent trauma to the delicate bronchial mucosa. Sedation levels must be monitored closely so the patient is calm yet able to interact when needed.

Prevention of Ventilator-Associated Complications

Ventilator-associated complications pose serious threats to patient survival. Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP) is one of the most common hospital-acquired infections. Nurses use a collection of evidence-based interventions known as a care bundle to prevent VAP and other harms.

  • Head of bed elevation: Keep the bed elevated between 30 and 45 degrees to prevent aspiration of gastric contents.
  • Daily sedation vacation: Pause sedation daily to assess the patient's readiness to breathe on their own.
  • Peptic ulcer prophylaxis: Prevent stress ulcers caused by the physiological stress of critical illness.
  • Deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis: Use compression stockings or blood thinners since immobility increases clot risks.

Weaning Processes and Extubation

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilatory support back to spontaneous breathing. The medical team initiates weaning as soon as the underlying illness improves and the patient meets specific clinical criteria, such as stable vital signs and adequate oxygenation. During a Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT), the ventilator provides minimal or no support while nurses watch closely for signs of respiratory distress like sweating, rising heart rate, or falling oxygen levels. If the patient passes the trial, the endotracheal tube is removed in a procedure called extubation.
