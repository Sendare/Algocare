Ventilator

Introduction to Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation is a life-supporting medical intervention designed to artificially assist or completely replace spontaneous breathing when a patient's respiratory system fails to maintain adequate gas exchange. The primary goals are twofold: maintaining physiological **arterial blood gases** (oxygenation and carbon dioxide elimination) and reducing the metabolic work of breathing for exhausted respiratory muscles. In acute care settings across Nigeria, from university teaching hospitals to specialized emergency units, ventilators serve as a critical bridge while the underlying pathology—such as severe pneumonia, traumatic brain injury, or postoperative respiratory depression—is treated and resolved. Understanding this foundational mechanism allows the nurse to interpret alarm sounds and recognize whether the machine is doing the work of breathing or if the patient is triggering each breath.

Indications and Clinical Purposes of Ventilation

Patients require mechanical ventilation when spontaneous breathing becomes ineffective or impossible due to acute or chronic decompensation. Clinical indicators are broadly divided into acute respiratory failure, hemodynamic instability, and severe neurological impairment. - **Hypoxemic Respiratory Failure**: Occurs when the respiratory system fails to oxygenate blood adequately, often seen in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) or severe pulmonary edema. - **Hypercapnic Respiratory Failure**: Characterized by the inability to eliminate carbon dioxide, frequently caused by chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbations, drug overdoses, or neuromuscular weakness like myasthenia gravis. - **Airway Protection**: Comatose patients with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 8 or less, or those with severe upper airway burns, cannot safeguard their own tracheobronchial tree from aspiration. - **Prophylactic Support**: Anticipated prolonged procedures or severe postoperative fatigue following extensive cardiothoracic surgeries.

Basic Components and Modes of Mechanical Ventilators

Modern mechanical ventilators consist of a control unit, a gas source delivering compressed air and oxygen, and a patient circuit containing inspiratory and expiratory limbs. The ventilator interacts with the patient through specific modes that dictate how breaths are delivered: | Ventilator Mode | Description | Clinical Application | | --- | --- | --- | | **Control Mode (CMV/IPPV)** | Ventilator delivers breaths at a set rate and volume regardless of patient effort. | Heavily sedated or paralyzed patients. | | **Assist-Control (AC)** | Every breath is machine-assisted, whether triggered by the patient or time-cycled. | Initial phase of acute respiratory failure. | | **Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV)** | Delivers set mandatory breaths synchronized with patient effort, allowing spontaneous breaths between. | Weaning and intermediate support. | | **Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV)** | Provides pressure boost during spontaneous breaths to overcome circuit resistance. | Spontaneous breathing trials and weaning. |

Patient Assessment and Baseline Monitoring

Continuous surveillance of a mechanically ventilated patient prevents rapid deterioration. The nurse begins by checking the artificial airway: verifying tube placement depth at the lips or teeth, ensuring secure taping, and auscultating bilateral breath sounds to rule out mainstem intubation. Chest wall expansion must be symmetrical. Vital signs, pulse oximetry, and end-tidal carbon dioxide readings require ongoing observation. Ventilator parameters—such as peak inspiratory pressure, expired tidal volume, and respiratory rate—must match prescribed settings. When ventilator alarms sound, the nurse must never silence them blindly; the immediate priority is to check the patient first, and if the patient is unstable, disconnect the machine and manually ventilate using a bag-valve-mask device.

Nursing Care and Management of the Ventilated Patient

Managing a ventilated patient demands meticulous, multi-system nursing interventions. Oral hygiene using chlorhexidine solutions reduces bacterial colonization and lowers infection risks. Endotracheal suctioning is performed strictly on an as-needed basis rather than on a routine schedule, utilizing sterile technique and pre-oxygenation to prevent hypoxemia. Eye lubrication is essential for patients with sedation or impaired blinking to prevent corneal ulceration. Gastric decompression via a nasogastric or oro-gastric tube prevents abdominal distension and aspiration. Frequent position changes and pressure area care safeguard skin integrity, while physical therapy and passive range of motion exercises mitigate the rapid muscle wasting associated with critical illness.

Prevention of Ventilator-Associated Complications

Mechanical ventilation carries significant risks that require proactive bundle interventions. Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP) is mitigated by maintaining the head of the bed elevated between 30 and 45 degrees, performing daily sedative interruption trials to assess readiness for weaning, and ensuring subglottic secretion drainage. Barotrauma, resulting in pneumothorax or subcutaneous emphysema, is minimized by keeping peak inspiratory and plateau pressures within safe limits. Oxygen toxicity is prevented by titrating the fraction of inspired oxygen to the lowest level required to maintain adequate oxygen saturation. Stress ulcers are guarded against using prescribed prophylactic proton pump inhibitors or H2-receptor antagonists.

Weaning Processes and Extubation

Weaning is the systematic transition from mechanical ventilatory support to spontaneous unassisted breathing. The process begins once the underlying cause of respiratory failure is resolved and the patient demonstrates adequate hemodynamic stability and gas exchange. Daily screening evaluates criteria such as spontaneous breathing parameters, cough strength, and mental status. A Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT) using minimal or no support via a T-piece or low pressure support is conducted for 30 to 120 minutes. If the patient maintains stable vital signs, normal blood gases, and no signs of respiratory distress, the clinician proceeds with extubation. Post-extubation care involves monitoring for upper airway stridor, hoarseness, and respiratory fatigue.
