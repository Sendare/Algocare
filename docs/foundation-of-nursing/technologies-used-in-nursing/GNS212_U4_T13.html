Automated External Defibrillator

Introduction and Indications for AED Use

An automated external defibrillator, commonly known as an AED, is a portable electronic device designed to detect life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias, specifically ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia. When these dangerous rhythms occur, the heart quivers instead of pumping blood effectively. The AED analyzes the heart's electrical activity and delivers a synchronized or unsynchronized electrical shock if indicated, aiming to reset the cardiac conduction system back to a normal rhythm. Time is critical during sudden cardiac arrest. For every minute defibrillation is delayed, a person's chance of survival drops significantly.

Indications for using an AED are straightforward and clear-cut. The device must be applied immediately whenever an individual experiences sudden collapse, is unresponsive to verbal or physical stimuli, and lacks normal breathing or has only occasional gasps. You will find AEDs mounted on walls in public spaces like airports, schools, and shopping centers, as well as in hospital wards and outpatient clinics. A common misconception among student nurses is that an AED should be used on any patient with chest pain. However, an AED is strictly for cardiac arrest where the patient is unresponsive and pulseless; it will not deliver a shock to a conscious person or someone with a beating heart, even if the device is accidentally attached.

Components and Anatomy of an AED Unit

  • Power Source: Typically a long-life lithium battery designed to last several years on standby or provide numerous shocks during an actual emergency.
  • Electrodes (Pads): Disposable adhesive pads equipped with conductive gel and embedded metal plates that stick to the patient's bare chest to sense rhythm and deliver the shock.
  • Connector Cable: A wire linking the electrode pads to the main computer unit of the device.
  • Analyzing System: A built-in computer processor that evaluates the heart rhythm captured by the pads.
  • Shock Button: A manual or automated button that triggers the delivery of electrical energy when prompted by the machine.
  • Speaker and Display Screen: Audio prompts and visual indicators that guide the rescuer step-by-step through the entire rescue process.

Step-by-Step Operation and Protocol

  1. Power On: Turn on the AED immediately by pressing the power button or opening the lid, which often powers the unit on automatically.
  2. Expose the Chest: Remove all clothing from the patient's chest. Wipe the skin dry if it is wet or sweaty.
  3. Apply the Pads: Peel the backing off the pads and place them firmly on the bare chest. Follow the placement pictures on the pads: one goes on the upper right chest below the collarbone, and the other goes on the lower left side of the ribs.
  4. Clear the Patient: Shout "Clear!" and ensure no one is touching the patient so the machine can analyze accurately without motion artifact.
  5. Deliver the Shock: If the AED advises a shock, ensure everyone is still clear, then press the flashing shock button. If no shock is advised, immediately resume chest compressions.

Integration of AED with Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

An AED is never a replacement for cardiopulmonary resuscitation; it is a powerful partner to it. High-quality chest compressions maintain a minimal flow of oxygenated blood to the brain and heart muscle until a normal rhythm returns. When you arrive with an AED during an ongoing resuscitation, do not stop compressions until the device is turned on and the pads are ready to be attached.

Modern AED protocols operate in cycles. After the device analyzes the rhythm and either delivers a shock or advises no shock, you must immediately resume chest compressions and rescue breaths without checking for a pulse. The machine will typically prompt you to perform CPR for two minutes before it pauses automatically to re-analyze the heart rhythm. Minimizing interruptions in chest compressions is vital for patient survival. Every pause you take to apply pads or listen to prompts reduces the blood pressure built up inside the coronary arteries, lowering the chance that the heart will successfully restart.

Special Considerations and Safety Precautions

Operating an AED in clinical or community environments requires environmental awareness to protect both the patient and the rescuers. Water is a strong conductor of electricity. If a patient is lying in a puddle or swimming pool, you must drag them onto a dry surface and wipe their chest dry before applying pads. Never apply AED pads directly over a metallic implanted pacemaker or defibrillator bulge, as this can block the shock energy; instead, place the pad at least an inch away from the device.

Medication patches on the chest, such as nitroglycerin or nicotine patches, can block energy transfer or cause skin burns. Peel off any visible patches and wipe the area clean before placing the AED pads. If the patient has a very hairy chest, the pads may fail to stick properly, trapping air pockets that cause electrical arcing. Use the razor usually found in the AED prep kit to quickly shave the pad placement sites if chest hair is thick.

Maintenance, Inspection, and Troubleshooting

An AED is only useful if it is fully functional when an emergency strikes. Healthcare facilities and public departments must establish routine inspection schedules, often checking units daily or weekly depending on institutional policy. Most modern AEDs perform automated self-tests daily, checking battery status, internal circuitry, and pad integrity. A blinking green status indicator light usually means the device is ready for use, while a red light or chirping sound signals a fault.

Nurses assigned to equipment checks must also inspect the expiration dates on the electrode pads and ensure spare batteries or pads are stored with the unit. Troubleshooting common equipment alerts is straightforward:

  • Low Battery Warning: Replace the battery pack immediately with a fresh manufacturer-approved unit.
  • Pad Connection Error: Check the cable plug to ensure it is firmly pushed into the AED socket.
  • Expired Pads: Discard outdated pads and replace them with unexpired ones immediately.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Documentation

Once the resuscitation effort concludes—whether the patient regains a pulse or is pronounced dead—the nurse's responsibilities shift to post-resuscitation care, equipment restocking, and detailed documentation. If the patient achieves return of spontaneous circulation, nursing care focuses on hemodynamic stabilization, monitoring vital signs, maintaining adequate oxygenation, and preparing for transfer to an intensive care unit.

From an equipment standpoint, the nurse must clean the AED according to manufacturer guidelines, restock used consumable supplies like razors and spare pads, and ensure the unit is returned to its designated charging or storage station. Documentation is a legal and clinical necessity. The nurse must record the exact time of collapse, the time the AED was applied, the number of shocks delivered, the patient's immediate response to treatment, and the names of team members involved in the code. Many modern AEDs feature internal memory cards that record event data and ECG tracings, which must be downloaded and attached to the permanent medical record for physician review.
