Pulse Oximeter

Introduction and Underlying Principles

Pulse oximetry provides a non-invasive method for monitoring a patient's arterial oxygen saturation, designated clinically as SpO2. Before widespread adoption, assessing tissue oxygenation required painful and delayed arterial blood gas sampling. This technology relies on two fundamental physiological principles: spectrophotometry and photoplethysmography.

  • Spectrophotometry: Oxygenated hemoglobin and deoxygenated hemoglobin absorb light at different wavelengths. Oxygenated hemoglobin absorbs more infrared light (around 940 nm), while deoxygenated hemoglobin absorbs more red light (around 660 nm).
  • Photoplethysmography: The device distinguishes pulsatile arterial blood flow from static tissues such as bone, skin, and venous blood by measuring light absorption changes during systole versus diastole.

Components and Mechanism of the Device

A standard pulse oximetry system consists of a microprocessor-based monitor and a sensor probe attached to the patient. The probe contains two light-emitting diodes (LEDs) on one side and a photodetector on the opposite side.

Transmission sensors are placed across a capillary bed, such as a finger, toe, or earlobe, where light passes through the tissue to the detector. Reflectance sensors, conversely, measure light bounced back from the tissue surface and are useful on flat areas like the forehead. The microprocessor filters out background noise, isolates the pulsatile arterial signal, and calculates the final SpO2 percentage displayed on the screen alongside the pulse rate.

Clinical Indications in Nigerian Healthcare

In resource-constrained environments across Nigeria, pulse oximeters serve as vital, rapid triage and monitoring tools. They reduce maternal and child mortality by enabling early detection of hypoxemia in busy emergency departments, labor wards, and pediatric units.

Key clinical scenarios requiring continuous or intermittent SpO2 monitoring include acute lower respiratory tract infections like pneumonia, severe malaria complications involving respiratory distress, perioperative anesthesia care, and emergency stabilization of trauma victims. Regular checks help clinicians administer supplemental oxygen judiciously, conserving scarce medical oxygen cylinders.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Accurate Readings

  1. Preparation: Explain the procedure to the patient and ensure the chosen monitoring site is clean, dry, and free of dark nail polish.
  2. Site Selection: Choose a finger with good capillary refill; warm cold hands gently before application to improve peripheral perfusion.
  3. Placement: Gently clip the probe onto the digit, ensuring proper alignment of the light emitter and receiver without excessive squeezing.
  4. Verification: Observe the plethysmographic waveform or pulse amplitude indicator to confirm a strong, steady pulse signal before recording the value.
  5. Documentation: Record both the SpO2 percentage and the corresponding pulse rate in the patient chart alongside the delivery method of any supplemental oxygen.

Interpreting Values and Clinical Correlates

A normal SpO2 reading in a healthy adult breathing room air typically ranges from 95% to 100%. Values between 90% and 94% suggest mild hypoxia, while levels dropping below 90% indicate significant hypoxemia requiring prompt clinical intervention. Patients with chronic respiratory conditions, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, often maintain lower baseline target ranges specified by their managing physician.

Always correlate numerical readouts with clinical signs of respiratory distress, such as tachypnea, nasal flaring, use of accessory muscles, and altered mental status.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Care

Effective use of pulse oximetry extends beyond attaching a probe. Nursing duties encompass rotating sensor sites every few hours to prevent pressure ulcers and skin breakdown, particularly in immobile or vulnerable patients. Clinicians must periodically verify that the device's displayed pulse rate matches the patient's actual palpated apical or radial pulse to confirm signal accuracy. Furthermore, alarms must be set to appropriate individual thresholds rather than disabled, ensuring patient safety without causing alarm fatigue.
