WHO Recommendations on Injection Safety

Introduction to Injection Safety

An injection is one of the most common healthcare procedures globally, used to prevent, diagnose, and treat various illnesses. However, when these procedures are performed with contaminated equipment or improper techniques, they pose severe health risks to both patients and healthcare providers. The World Health Organization defines a safe injection as one that does not harm the recipient, does not expose the healthcare worker to any avoidable risk, and does not result in waste that is dangerous for the community. Achieving this standard requires a systematic approach that combines proper training, availability of appropriate supplies, and strict adherence to established protocols.

Global Burden of Unsafe Injections

Unsafe injection practices have historically caused millions of new infections worldwide, particularly involving bloodborne pathogens. Reusing single-use syringes or needles without sterilization remains a primary driver of this burden in resource-constrained settings.

  • Hepatitis B Virus (HBV): Highly transmissible through contaminated needles, leading to chronic liver disease.
  • Hepatitis C Virus (HCV): Frequently spread through unsafe therapeutic injections, often remaining silent for years.
  • Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV): Transmitted when sharp devices contaminated with infected blood are reused.

Beyond viral infections, unhygienic practices lead to local bacterial infections, abscesses, and systemic sepsis.

Core Principles of WHO Guidelines

The WHO injection safety strategy rests on three fundamental pillars: preventing injury to the healthcare worker, protecting the patient, and preventing environmental hazards. To achieve this, healthcare facilities must ensure a reliable supply of injection devices, promote single-use practices, and manage waste responsibly. Every healthcare professional holds a personal and professional responsibility to question practices that deviate from these safety standards, regardless of resource shortages.

Standard Precautions and Hand Hygiene

Hand hygiene is the single most effective intervention to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Before preparing or administering any injection, nurses must perform proper hand hygiene using either an alcohol-based hand rub or soap and water. Clean gloves should be worn whenever there is a risk of exposure to blood or body fluids. Gloves do not replace hand hygiene; hands must still be cleaned before putting gloves on and immediately after removing them.

Use of Engineered Devices and Rejection of Reuse

Reusing syringes or needles—even on the same patient with a different medication vial—is strictly prohibited because it introduces microbial contamination into the fluid path. To protect workers, healthcare facilities should prioritize engineered devices such as auto-disable syringes, which automatically lock or break after a single use, and needles with retractable shields. Never attempt to recap used needles using a two-handed technique, as this is the leading cause of accidental punctures.

Safe Handling and Disposal of Sharps Waste

All used sharps, including needles, syringes with fixed needles, and lancets, must be disposed of immediately at the point of use into a puncture-resistant, leak-proof safety box. Safety boxes should never be filled beyond the designated fill line—usually three-quarters full—to prevent protruding needles from scratching passersby. Never shake, empty, or transfer contents from a safety box into another container, and ensure final disposal occurs via high-temperature incineration or safe encapsulation.

Prevention of Needle-Stick Injuries

A needle-stick injury exposes nurses and other healthcare workers to potentially life-threatening bloodborne pathogens. Prevention centers on eliminating unnecessary injections, adopting safe work practices, and utilizing safety-engineered devices. If an exposure incident occurs, the nurse must immediately wash the affected wound with soap and water—without scrubbing—encourage gentle bleeding, report the incident according to facility protocol, and seek prompt post-exposure evaluation.

Implementation and Monitoring in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Implementing injection safety guidelines in Nigerian healthcare facilities requires overcoming challenges such as supply chain disruptions, funding deficits, and variable training compliance. Hospital management must ensure consistent procurement of auto-disable syringes and safety boxes. Furthermore, continuous supportive supervision and regular nursing audits help reinforce safe habits among clinical staff, ensuring that national safety policies translate into everyday clinical reality.
