WHO Recommendations on Injection Safety

Introduction and Global Burden of Unsafe Injections

Unsafe medical injections present a silent crisis across many developing healthcare systems, transmitting serious bloodborne pathogens like hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Millions of injections are administered annually worldwide, yet a significant proportion occurs under hazardous conditions involving contaminated equipment, poor sterilisation, or improper disposal. In resource-constrained Nigerian clinical environments, erratic power supplies and procurement shortages can sometimes compromise sterile supply chains, making foundational injection safety knowledge critical for every practicing nurse.

Core Principles of WHO Injection Safety Guidelines

The World Health Organization defines a safe injection as one that does not harm the recipient, does not expose the provider to any avoidable risks, and does not result in waste that is dangerous for the community. Achieving this standard requires a systematic approach built upon three fundamental pillars:

  • Protective Practices: Eliminating unnecessary injections by prioritizing oral medications when clinically appropriate.
  • Equipment Integrity: Ensuring absolute sterility of every device, including single-use syringes and reconstitution needles.
  • Waste Management: Establishing secure handling, transport, and final destruction pathways for all sharps items.

Standard Precautions and Hand Hygiene

Infection prevention begins before touching any medication vial or patient skin. Hand hygiene is the single most effective intervention to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Nurses must perform hand hygiene using alcohol-based hand rub or soap and clean running water before and after every patient contact, as well as immediately before aseptic procedures like preparing an intravenous line or drawing up a medication.

Use of Engineered Devices and Rejection of Reuse

Standard disposable syringes can be re-used illicitly if supply chains fail or resource pressures mount. To combat this, the World Health Organization advocates for the global transition to Auto-Disable (AD) syringes for therapeutic and prophylactic injections. An AD syringe locks automatically or breaks after a single plunger depression, physically preventing a second use. Furthermore, nurses must never pre-fill multiple syringes for future use, as this breaches sterility and invites microbial contamination.

Safe Handling and Disposal of Sharps Waste

Proper waste management closes the circle of injection safety. Every clinical ward must keep a puncture-resistant safety box (sharps container) within arm's reach of the injection site. Containers should never be filled beyond the designated three-quarters line to prevent protruding needles from piercing staff. A common exam pitfall is believing that needles should be recapped after use; WHO guidelines mandate that needles must NEVER be recapped by hand before disposal.

Prevention of Needle-Stick Injuries in Nursing Practice

Needle-stick injuries (NSIs) expose nursing personnel to life-altering pathogens. Prevention requires vigilance at every stage of the procedure. Never point a naked needle toward any part of your body, and dispose of the entire syringe assembly immediately into the nearest safety box. If an NSI occurs, the nurse must immediately encourage bleeding under running water, wash the site thoroughly with soap and water—avoiding harsh chemical bleaches—and report the incident immediately for post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) assessment.

Implementation and Monitoring in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Translating global safety guidelines into busy Nigerian hospitals requires sustained institutional commitment, supportive supervision, and consistent material supply chains. Facility managers must ensure regular stocking of auto-disable syringes and safety boxes. For student and practicing nurses, advocacy, adherence to standard operating procedures, and participation in infection control committees help bridge the gap between policy and daily bedside practice.
