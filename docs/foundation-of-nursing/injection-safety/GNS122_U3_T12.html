Requisition of Injection Safety Commodities

Introduction to Injection Safety and Commodities

Injection safety covers all practices that protect patients, health workers, and the community from harm during the administration of injections. Unsafe injections transmit dangerous blood-borne pathogens like hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV. To prevent these infections, healthcare facilities must maintain a steady supply of injection safety commodities. These essential items include single-use disposable syringes, auto-disable syringes for immunizations, safety boxes for sharp disposal, and personal protective equipment. Without reliable access to these commodities, healthcare workers are forced to reuse equipment or administer injections without proper protection, endangering everyone in the clinical environment.

Types of Injection Safety Commodities and Equipment

An effective injection safety program relies on a specific set of tools designed to cover every stage of the injection process, from preparation to safe disposal.

  • Auto-Disable (AD) Syringes: These lock automatically after a single injection, making reuse physically impossible. They are the standard for immunization campaigns.
  • Disposable Synges: Standard syringes used for curative treatments where AD syringes are not mandated, requiring immediate discard after use.
  • Safety Boxes: Puncture-resistant and leak-proof containers used specifically for collecting used needles and syringes at the point of care.
  • Reconstitution Syringes: Larger volume syringes used strictly for mixing powdered medications with diluents before drawing them up for administration.
  • Alcohol Swabs: Single-use antiseptic pads used to clean the injection site skin before piercing.

Principles of Inventory Management and Stock Estimation

Ordering injection supplies requires careful calculation based on past patient attendance and service delivery data rather than simple guesswork. Nurses and facility managers use consumption rates to determine how many syringes and safety boxes are needed for a specific period, usually monthly or quarterly. Two key concepts guide this process: the maximum stock level, which is the highest amount of inventory a store should hold, and the minimum stock level, which serves as an alarm signal indicating that a new order must be placed immediately. Maintaining this balance ensures the facility never ties up too much money in excess goods while protecting against unexpected surges in patient volume.

The Requisition Process and Ordering Workflow

The requisition workflow moves from local clinical units up to central medical stores in an orderly, step-by-step sequence. First, ward nurses count existing stock and record the quantities consumed over the reporting period. Next, they fill out a standard requisition and issue form, detailing the exact quantities needed to bring stock back to maximum levels. The facility head or pharmacist must review and sign off on this form to verify its accuracy and authorization. Once approved, the document is submitted to the district or central medical store for packing and dispatch. Following this structured pathway prevents supply leaks, unauthorized orders, and internal discrepancies.

Documentation, Record Keeping, and Logistics Management

Good record keeping is the backbone of reliable medical supply chains. Every time injection commodities arrive, move between rooms, or get issued to a clinical department, staff must record the transaction in a stock ledger or bin card. A bin card hangs directly on the storage shelf next to the items, providing an immediate, running balance of stock movements. Modern facilities also utilize electronic Logistics Management Information Systems (LMIS) to track consumption trends across multiple sites in real time. Accurate documentation protects against theft, highlights mysterious inventory losses, and provides the hard data required for future forecasting.

Storage, Handling, and Quality Control of Commodities

Proper storage conditions protect injection safety commodities from environmental damage that could render them unsafe for clinical use. Syringes and safety boxes must be kept in clean, dry, well-ventilated rooms off the floor using pallets or metal shelving. Exposure to direct sunlight, excessive heat, or moisture can weaken cardboard safety boxes and degrade plastic syringe components, making them brittle or prone to leaking. Staff must also practice the FEFO (First-Expired, First-Out) principle, ensuring that batches with the earliest expiry dates are dispatched and used before newer stock. Routine quality checks during storage help identify crushed boxes, torn packaging, or compromised sterility seals before items reach patients.

Preventing Wastage and Managing Stockouts

Wastage and stockouts represent two sides of the same operational failure in healthcare supply chains. Stockouts leave clinics without vital syringes, halting immunization drives and treatment programs, whereas excessive wastage drains limited financial resources through expired or damaged goods. To prevent stockouts, facilities must maintain open communication channels with regional depots and report usage shifts promptly. To curb wastage, staff should avoid over-ordering, respect storage guidelines, and monitor expiry dates closely. When a stockout does occur, emergency borrowing protocols between nearby facilities can bridge the gap while regular shipments are en route.
