Ordering Commodities and Supplies Using Stock Cards

Introduction to Stock Cards and Inventory Control in Nursing

Inventory control forms a silent yet vital backbone of safe clinical care. Without reliable supplies on the ward, even the most skilled nursing interventions grind to a halt. A **stock card** is a physical or digital ledger kept alongside stored goods to track the exact quantity of a specific commodity available in a facility. In busy Nigerian primary health care centres and tertiary teaching hospitals alike, these cards serve as the frontline defense against running out of essential drugs, cotton wool, syringes, and intravenous fluids. Effective inventory control bridges the gap between clinical demand and central medical store supply chains, ensuring resources remain available when patients walk through the door.

Anatomy of a Standard Stock Card: Key Terms and Fields

Reading a stock card requires familiarity with a specific set of logistical terms. Each card represents a single item, strength, and presentation. - **Item Description**: The generic name, dosage form, and strength of the commodity. - **Receipts**: Quantities of new stock delivered into the ward from the pharmacy store or central warehouse. - **Issues**: Quantities of the item given out to patients, treatment rooms, or specific clinical units. - **Balance**: The running total of stock remaining after every receipt or issue transaction. - **Batch Number and Expiry Date**: Crucial tracking details used to rotate stock and implement a First-Expired, First-Out (FEFO) policy.

Calculating Stock Levels: Minimum, Maximum, and Reorder Levels

Stock control relies on mathematical thresholds that guide procurement decisions. Setting these levels incorrectly leads either to overstocking and expiration or sudden stockouts. | Stock Parameter | Definition | Clinical Significance | | --- | --- | --- | | **Minimum Level** | The safety buffer stock that must remain before emergency re-supply. | Prevents complete stockout during delivery delays. | | **Reorder Level** | The specific inventory threshold that triggers a new order. | Accounts for lead time needed for supplies to arrive. | | **Maximum Level** | The upper ceiling cap for inventory storage capacity. | Prevents overcrowding, storage damage, and expiration. | Calculating these numbers depends on average consumption rates and delivery lead times from the central medical stores.

Routine Monitoring and Recording Stock Movements

A stock card is only as accurate as the entries made upon it. Every single movement of goods—whether incoming from a supply truck or outgoing to a patient's bedside trolley—must be documented immediately. Delaying documentation invites errors, forgotten entries, and unaccounted discrepancies. Nurses assigned to inventory duties must perform physical counts regularly to verify that the numbers written on the paper or screen match the actual boxes sitting on the pharmacy shelves. In high-volume Nigerian health facilities, end-of-shift handovers should include a quick verification of critical emergency items like adrenaline and intravenous cannulae.

Identifying When and How to Place an Order

Ordering supplies should never be left to guesswork or memory. When the running balance on a stock card drops down to the pre-calculated **reorder level**, action is required. The nurse manager or inventory officer compiles a requisition form, cross-referencing current stock counts against consumption rates to justify the quantities requested. In public health sectors, requisitions travel through formal administrative channels to medical stores. Accurate quantification prevents over-ordering, which chokes limited storage space, and under-ordering, which courts imminent stockouts.

Receiving, Inspecting, and Updating Stock Records

The supply cycle completes a crucial loop when ordered goods arrive at the ward or clinic. Before signing any delivery note, the receiving nurse must conduct a rigorous inspection. Packages must be checked for physical damage, correct quantities, matching item descriptions, and acceptable expiry dates. Accepting compromised, short-dated, or incorrect items creates severe clinical hazards. Once verified, the new quantities are immediately added to the stock card, updating the running balance and recording the new batch details.

Preventing Stockouts, Wastage, and Discrepancies

Inventory management failures manifest as stockouts, expired drugs, and unexplained stock losses. A stockout halts emergency interventions, forcing families to source medications externally under distress. Conversely, poor rotation leads to piles of expired commodities that must be written off, draining scarce healthcare budgets. Routine physical stock audits help reconcile discrepancies between paper records and physical reality, uncovering recording errors, theft, or breakage early. Diligent stock card maintenance safeguards both institutional resources and patient wellbeing.
