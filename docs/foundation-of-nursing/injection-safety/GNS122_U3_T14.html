Ordering Commodities and Supplies Using Stock Cards

Introduction to Stock Cards and Inventory Control in Nursing

A stock card is a physical or digital ledger used in healthcare facilities to track the exact quantity of a specific item, drug, or supply on hand. In clinical settings, inventory control is not just an administrative task for storekeepers; it is a core nursing responsibility. Ward nurses frequently manage floor stock, ranging from everyday consumables like gauze and syringes to critical emergency medications. Without accurate tracking, supplies run out unexpectedly, putting patient safety at direct risk during emergencies. Conversely, hoarding excess supplies ties up valuable hospital capital and leads to expiration before use. Stock cards bridge this gap by providing a real-time historical record of every item received, used, or discarded on the ward.

Anatomy of a Standard Stock Card: Key Terms and Fields

Reading and maintaining a stock card requires familiarity with standard fields found across healthcare facilities. Every card is dedicated to a single item description, including its dosage strength and unit of issue.

  • Date: The exact day a transaction occurred.
  • Reference / Source: Where items came from (e.g., central medical store) or where they went (e.g., Treatment Room).
  • Quantity Received: The number of units newly added to the inventory.
  • Quantity Issued: The number of units taken out for patient care or ward use.
  • Balance: The running total of remaining stock after each transaction.
  • Signature: The initials or name of the staff member who performed or verified the entry.

Calculating Stock Levels: Minimum, Maximum, and Reorder Levels

Effective inventory management relies on setting clear numerical thresholds on stock cards. These limits prevent both shortages and overstocking.

  • Minimum Level (Safety Stock): The absolute lowest threshold. If stock drops to this point, you are at high risk of a stockout before new supplies arrive.
  • Maximum Level: The upper limit of stock the ward should hold. Exceeding this leads to congestion and potential wastage from expiration.
  • Reorder Level: A predetermined trigger point above the minimum level. When the balance hits this number, it is time to submit a new requisition.
  • Lead Time: The duration it takes from submitting an order to receiving the items on the ward.

Routine Monitoring and Recording Stock Movements

A stock card is only useful if it reflects reality. Every single time items are removed from the storage cupboard for patient use, the stock card must be updated immediately. Waiting until the end of the shift or the end of the week introduces memory lapses and missing entries. Common errors include forgetting to record broken items, failing to document returned unused supplies, or making simple arithmetic mistakes when subtracting issues from the balance. Regular physical audits—such as counting high-risk or fast-moving items at the change of each shift—help catch these discrepancies early before they snowball into major inventory losses.

Placing an Order and Preventing Stockouts

When the stock balance reaches the reorder level, the nurse must prepare a requisition form. This process involves calculating the exact quantity needed to bring the stock back up to the maximum level without exceeding it. Good ordering practice considers seasonal disease patterns, such as ordering more anti-malaria medications or oral rehydration salts ahead of the rainy season. A stockout occurs when an item is completely unavailable when needed for patient care. Preventing stockouts requires discipline, accurate record-keeping, and respecting lead times so that orders arrive before safety buffers are exhausted.

Receiving, Inspecting, and Updating Stock Records

Receiving new stock is more than just dumping boxes onto the ward shelf. Every delivery must be carefully inspected against the delivery note and the original requisition. Nurses must check for damaged packaging, incorrect product strengths, and short shipments where fewer items arrived than were billed. Crucially, the nurse must check expiration dates on all incoming items; products nearing expiration should be rejected or flagged. Once inspection is complete, the new quantities are immediately added to the stock card, bringing the balance up and signing off on the receipt.
