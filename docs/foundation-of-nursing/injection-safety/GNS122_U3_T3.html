Diseases Transmitted by Unsafe Injection Practices

Introduction to Injection Safety in Nursing

An injection is one of the most common healthcare procedures worldwide, used to save lives, deliver medications, and administer vaccines. However, when procedures are rushed or supplies are scarce, injections can become a deadly vector for disease transmission. Injection safety refers to a set of measures taken to perform injections in a manner that does no harm to the recipient, does not expose the healthcare worker to avoidable risk, and results in waste that does not create any danger for the community. In many clinical settings, a single lapse in technique—such as reusing a syringe or multi-dosing medication vials—can expose patients to life-threatening pathogens. Nurses stand at the frontline of patient care, bearing the ethical and legal responsibility to ensure that every single injection follows strict aseptic principles. Understanding the scope of unsafe injection practices is the first step toward building a safe healthcare environment.

Common Pathogens Transmitted Through Unsafe Injections

Unsafe injection practices primarily transmit blood-borne pathogens that attack the liver or the immune system. The most significant infections linked to contaminated needles include:

  • Hepatitis B Virus (HBV): A resilient virus that attacks the liver, capable of surviving on environmental surfaces for at least seven days.
  • Hepatitis C Virus (HCV): A blood-borne virus that frequently leads to chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, and liver cancer.
  • Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV): A retrovirus that gradually destroys the immune system, leaving the body vulnerable to opportunistic infections.
  • Bacterial Infections: Pathogens like *Staphylococcus aureus* that cause severe local abscesses, sepsis, or toxic shock when introduced via non-sterile equipment.

Pathophysiology and Modes of Transmission

Transmission occurs when contaminated blood or body fluids enter the body of another person. The primary mechanisms include the reuse of disposable syringes, drawing medication from multi-dose vials with a used needle, and accidental needle-stick injuries among health workers. Once a pathogen enters the bloodstream, it targets specific cells. For instance, HIV targets CD4+ T-lymphocytes, compromising cell-mediated immunity. Hepatitis B and C viruses target hepatocytes, triggering an immune-mediated inflammatory response that damages liver tissue over time. The risk of infection after a single needle-stick exposure varies by pathogen; Hepatitis B carries the highest transmission risk, followed by Hepatitis C and HIV.

Clinical Manifestations of Blood-Borne Infections

Many infections transmitted through unsafe injections do not show immediate symptoms, allowing diseases to spread silently. When symptoms do appear, they often reflect the specific organ system targeted.

  • Acute Phase Symptoms: Fatigue, low-grade fever, nausea, right upper quadrant abdominal discomfort, and dark urine often signal acute viral hepatitis.
  • Jaundice: Yellowing of the skin and sclera occurs due to impaired bilirubin processing by the inflamed liver.
  • Chronic Complications: Long-term untreated infections lead to chronic fatigue, cirrhosis, ascites, and progressive immune failure characteristic of acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).
  • Local Signs: Pain, redness, swelling, and purulent drainage at the injection site point toward a secondary bacterial infection.

Risk Factors and System Challenges in Nigeria

Injection safety challenges vary globally, but resource-limited settings face distinct systemic hurdles. In Nigeria, high rates of malaria, typhoid, and routine infections often lead to a heavy reliance on parenteral drug therapy because patients and practitioners believe injections work faster than oral medications. Contributing factors include a shortage of single-use auto-disable syringes, irregular power supplies affecting sterilization, and the presence of informal or unregulated drug vendors. Additionally, cultural preferences favoring injections over tablets create a high-demand environment where overworked staff may cut corners. Addressing these challenges requires systemic supply chain strengthening, strict regulatory enforcement, and sustained public health campaigns.

Standard Precautions and Infection Control

Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) relies on the consistent application of standard precautions for every patient, regardless of their diagnosis. Key practices include performing hand hygiene before and after patient contact, wearing clean gloves when handling blood or body fluids, and maintaining a clean work area. Nurses must never recap used needles, as this action is a leading cause of accidental needle-stick injuries. If a needle must be recapped in an unavoidable emergency, a one-handed scoop technique is mandatory. Single-dose medication vials should be used whenever possible, and multi-dose vials must be dedicated to a single patient and kept away from patient treatment areas.

Proper Sharps Management and Safe Disposal

Safe management of sharps does not end when the injection is finished; proper disposal is critical to protect waste handlers and the community. Sharps containers must be puncture-resistant, leak-proof, and placed within arm's reach of the injection site. To prevent overfilling, containers must never be filled past the designated fill line—typically three-quarters full. Never attempt to bend, break, or force needles into an overfull container. Facilities must implement secure collection and final destruction methods, such as high-temperature incineration, to ensure contaminated sharps do not find their way into scavenged waste markets.

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis and Management

Despite rigorous precautions, accidental exposures still occur. Immediate post-exposure management can prevent infection establishment. First, wash the exposed site immediately with soap and water; do not squeeze or scrub the wound, and avoid using bleach or caustic agents. Report the incident immediately to the occupational health supervisor or infection control team. Assess the exposure source for HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C status. Administer Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV ideally within hours, but no later than 72 hours after exposure. Provide baseline serological testing and follow-up counseling for the affected healthcare worker.

Patient Advocacy and Community Education

Nurses act as educators and advocates, bridging the gap between clinical protocols and community understanding. In many communities, patients demand injections because of misconceptions regarding drug potency. Nurses must calmly educate clients about oral medication efficacy, explaining that pills are just as effective for many common conditions without the risks associated with broken skin barriers. Furthermore, nurses should empower patients to speak up if they see a provider reusing a syringe or failing to open a sterile pack in front of them. Community outreach programs help dispel myths and encourage health-seeking behaviors that prioritize safety over speed.
