Sigmoidoscopy

Introduction to Sigmoidoscopy and Indications

Sigmoidoscopy is a diagnostic and screening procedure that allows the clinician to examine the lining of the rectum and the sigmoid colon using a flexible or rigid illuminated scope. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice and board examinations in Nigeria, understanding the indications for this procedure is essential. Common indications include the evaluation of unexplained lower gastrointestinal bleeding, chronic diarrhea, persistent abdominal pain, and suspected inflammatory bowel disease such as ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease. Furthermore, it serves as a vital screening tool for colorectal cancer and polyps, particularly in individuals presenting with changes in bowel habits or a positive family history. Unlike a full colonoscopy which visualizes the entire large intestine, a sigmoidoscopy specifically targets the lower third of the colon. Nurses must accurately identify why a patient is ordered for this procedure to tailor their pre-procedural assessments, address patient anxiety, and ensure appropriate informed consent is obtained by the medical team. Recognizing these clinical indicators helps the nurse anticipate potential complications, educate the patient effectively, and collaborate efficiently with the multidisciplinary healthcare team to deliver safe, patient-centered care.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Sigmoid Colon and Rectum

A thorough knowledge of the anatomy and physiology of the sigmoid colon and rectum is fundamental for nursing students. The rectum is the final straight portion of the large intestine, measuring approximately 12 to 15 centimeters in length, terminating at the anal canal. Superior to the rectum lies the sigmoid colon, which is an S-shaped loop of the large intestine connecting the descending colon to the rectum. Its primary physiological function is to store fecal waste and absorb remaining water and electrolytes before elimination through defecation. The muscular walls of the sigmoid colon undergo powerful peristaltic contractions, creating high intraluminal pressure, which makes this region a common site for diverticula formation (diverticulosis) and inflammation. The blood supply to this area stems from the inferior mesenteric artery, and venous drainage occurs via the portal and systemic systems. Understanding these anatomical structures allows the nurse to anticipate where the scope will travel, recognize the physiological responses of the patient during the procedure, and comprehend why certain pathologies like polyps, tumors, and diverticular disease frequently manifest in this specific segment of the gastrointestinal tract.

Equipment and Instrumentation used in Sigmoidoscopy

The equipment and instrumentation used during a sigmoidoscopy must be meticulously prepared, inspected, and maintained by the nursing and procedural staff. Instruments generally include either a rigid sigmoidoscope or a flexible fiber-optic or video sigmoidoscope. A flexible sigmoidoscope is most commonly used today due to its increased patient comfort and broader field of view, featuring a light source, an air/water channel to clear the lens, and a biopsy channel for passing instruments. Auxiliary equipment includes insufflation tubing to inflate the bowel with air or carbon dioxide for better visualization, suction apparatus to clear secretions, biopsy forceps, and cytology brushes for tissue sampling. In addition, personal protective equipment (PPE), lubricating jelly, specimen containers, and cleaning solutions are required. Nurses are responsible for checking the functionality of the light source and suction before the procedure begins. Following the procedure, the nurse plays a critical role in infection prevention by adhering to strict sterilization and high-level disinfection protocols for reusable scopes to prevent cross-contamination and healthcare-associated infections.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Bowel Preparation

Pre-procedure nursing assessment and thorough bowel preparation are critical for ensuring a successful, safe sigmoidoscopy. The nurse must conduct a comprehensive baseline assessment, reviewing the patient's medical history, current medications (especially anticoagulants and antiplatelet drugs which may need to be withheld), allergies, and previous abdominal surgeries. Vital signs are recorded, and baseline anxiety is evaluated. Bowel preparation is essential to clear the colon of feces and allow clear visualization of the mucosal lining. This typically involves a low-residue diet a day prior, followed by prescribed laxatives and/or warm saline or phosphate enemas given a few hours before the procedure. The nurse must educate the patient on what to expect during the bowel prep, emphasizing the importance of staying hydrated with clear fluids and managing anticipated frequent bowel movements. Additionally, the nurse verifies that informed consent has been properly signed by the patient and the physician. Documenting baseline findings and ensuring the bowel is adequately cleansed minimizes procedural delays and reduces the risk of diagnostic errors or complications.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Positioning

During the sigmoidoscopy, the nurse plays a vital role in intra-procedure management, patient safety, and positioning. The standard patient position for a sigmoidoscopy is the left lateral Sims' position, with the patient lying on their left side, knees bent toward the chest. This position utilizes gravity and the anatomical curve of the sigmoid colon to facilitate safer advancement of the scope. The nurse ensures patient privacy and comfort by using drapes and positioning supportive pillows. Throughout the procedure, the nurse continuously monitors the patient's vital signs, oxygen saturation, pain levels, and emotional status, watching closely for signs of vagal response, severe cramping, or distress. Assisting the physician by handing instruments, managing insufflation or suction, and managing tissue specimens correctly are key technical responsibilities. The nurse also provides verbal reassurance, explains sensations the patient may experience (such as bloating, pressure, or cramping), and maintains a calm environment. Immediate recognition of distress allows the nurse to alert the clinician to pause or stop the procedure promptly, safeguarding the patient's well-being.

Post-Procedure Care, Monitoring, and Complication Prevention

Post-procedure care, monitoring, and complication prevention are critical nursing duties immediately following a sigmoidoscopy. Once the scope is withdrawn, the nurse assists the patient in cleaning the perianal area and helps them transition to a comfortable resting position. Vital signs are monitored at regular intervals until stable. Although sigmoidoscopy is generally safe, potential complications include bowel perforation, excessive bleeding from biopsy sites, severe abdominal pain, and vasovagal reactions. The nurse must instruct the patient to report symptoms such as severe abdominal distension, sharp or worsening abdominal pain, fever, chills, or rectal bleeding immediately. If biopsies were taken, small amounts of blood on the first bowel movement may be normal, but continuous or large volume bleeding is a medical emergency. The nurse also assesses for excessive gas and encourages the patient to pass flatus to relieve cramping and bloating caused by the air or carbon dioxide used during insufflation. Documenting the procedure outcome, patient tolerance, and any interventions completes the immediate post-procedural nursing record.

Patient Education, Discharge Instructions, and Follow-up

Comprehensive patient education, discharge instructions, and follow-up planning ensure safe recovery after a sigmoidoscopy. Before the patient leaves the healthcare facility, the nurse provides clear, verbal and written discharge instructions. If mild sedation was used, the nurse verifies that a responsible adult is present to accompany the patient home, as operating machinery or driving is strictly contraindicated for the rest of the day. Dietary instructions usually allow the patient to resume their normal diet immediately unless otherwise specified by the physician. The nurse reviews warning signs that necessitate urgent medical attention, including severe abdominal pain, high fever, dizziness, or heavy rectal bleeding. Furthermore, the nurse explains the plan for follow-up appointments to discuss biopsy results or further diagnostic recommendations, such as scheduling a full colonoscopy if abnormal findings or polyps were detected. Providing empathetic, culturally appropriate education empowers patients to manage their recovery at home and promotes adherence to ongoing healthcare regimens.
