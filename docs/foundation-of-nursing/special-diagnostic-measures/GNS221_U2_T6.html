Sigmoidoscopy

Introduction to Sigmoidoscopy and Indications

A sigmoidoscopy is a diagnostic procedure that allows a healthcare provider to examine the interior lining of the rectum and the sigmoid colon using a thin, flexible, or rigid tube equipped with a light and a camera. Unlike a full colonoscopy, which visualizes the entire large intestine, a sigmoidoscopy focuses strictly on the lower third of the colon. This makes it an efficient tool for specific diagnostic evaluations and routine screening.

Clinicians order a sigmoidoscopy for several clear reasons:

  • Rectal bleeding: Finding the source of bright red blood in the stool or on toilet paper.
  • Persistent bowel changes: Investigating unexplained chronic diarrhea, new-onset constipation, or alternating bowel habits.
  • Abdominal pain: Exploring lower left abdominal discomfort that lacks a clear diagnosis.
  • Cancer screening: Detecting early-stage colorectal cancer or pre-cancerous polyps in individuals without symptoms.
  • Monitoring disease: Assessing the extent and severity of inflammatory bowel diseases like ulcerative colitis.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Sigmoid Colon and Rectum

Understanding the anatomy of the lower gastrointestinal tract helps nurses anticipate patient sensations during the procedure. The sigmoid colon is an S-shaped loop of the large intestine located in the lower left quadrant of the abdomen. It connects the descending colon to the rectum. Because of its sharp curves and mobility, passing a scope through this region can cause distinct cramping or the sudden urge to have a bowel movement.

The rectum is the final straight section of the large intestine, measuring roughly twelve to fifteen centimeters long, terminating at the anal canal. Its primary physiological role is to temporarily store fecal waste before defecation. The rich network of blood vessels and sensitive mucosal lining in both the rectum and sigmoid colon make them common sites for inflammation, polyps, and bleeding.

Equipment, Instrumentation, and Preparation

A successful sigmoidoscopy relies on specialized equipment designed to illuminate, visualize, and sometimes treat abnormalities within the lower bowel.

  • Sigmoidoscope: Available as either rigid metal tubes or flexible fiber-optic or video scopes. Flexible scopes are most common because they cause less discomfort and allow better maneuverability.
  • Light source and camera: High-intensity light combined with a video processor projects a clear, magnified view of the mucosal lining onto a monitor.
  • Insufflation system: Pumps controlled amounts of air or carbon dioxide into the bowel to gently inflate the walls, clearing folds for better visualization.
  • Biopsy forceps and snares: Miniature instruments passed through the working channel of the scope to collect tissue samples or remove small polyps.
  • Suction apparatus: Clears away mucus, liquid stool, or blood that might obscure the camera lens during the procedure.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Bowel Preparation

Thorough preparation before a sigmoidoscopy ensures a clear field of vision for the physician and reduces procedural risks. Unlike a full colonoscopy, which requires drinking large volumes of laxatives the day before, a sigmoidoscopy often relies on simpler bowel cleansing methods.

Nursing responsibilities during the pre-procedure phase include:

  • Health history review: Check for bleeding disorders, current anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications, and known allergies.
  • Bowel preparation education: Instruct the patient on using one or two saline or tap water enemas administered rectally roughly one to two hours before the procedure. Some clinics may prescribe a mild oral laxative instead.
  • Consent verification: Confirm that the patient understands the risks, benefits, and alternatives, and that a signed informed consent form is in the chart.
  • Baseline vitals: Record blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation to establish a safe starting point.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Positioning

During the sigmoidoscopy, the nurse plays a vital role in maintaining patient comfort, monitoring vital signs, and assisting the physician. Because sedation is rarely used for standard sigmoidoscopies, the patient remains fully awake and conscious throughout.

Proper patient positioning is essential for success. The patient is typically placed in the left lateral position (Sims' position), lying on their left side with the right knee and hip bent upward toward the chest. This position aligns the sigmoid colon and rectum naturally with the path of the examining scope.

Nursing duties during the procedure involve:

  • Continuously monitoring the patient's facial expressions, verbal feedback, and comfort levels.
  • Offering reassurance and deep breathing coaching during moments of cramping.
  • Tracking vital signs, particularly if the patient experiences discomfort or anxiety.
  • Managing specimen containers and labeling tissue samples correctly as the doctor hands them off.

Post-Procedure Care, Monitoring, and Complication Prevention

Once the sigmoidoscopy is complete, immediate post-procedure care focuses on recovery and monitoring for rare but serious complications. Since most patients do not receive sedation, they usually recover quickly with minimal downtime.

Key post-procedure nursing actions include:

  • Assessing for flatulence and cramping: Air introduced during the procedure must be expelled. Encourage the patient to pass gas to relieve abdominal distension and discomfort.
  • Monitoring for warning signs: Watch closely for severe abdominal pain, high fever, chills, or significant rectal bleeding.
  • Checking vital signs: Ensure blood pressure and heart rate return to baseline levels.

Severe complications like bowel perforation or major hemorrhage are uncommon, but nurses must recognize early indicators. Perforation presents with sudden, severe, worsening abdominal pain, a rigid abdomen, and signs of peritonitis.

Patient Education, Discharge Instructions, and Follow-up

Before leaving the healthcare facility, the patient must receive clear, comprehensive discharge instructions to ensure a safe recovery at home. Providing both verbal and written guidance helps reinforce crucial safety points.

Essential discharge topics include:

  • Dietary resumption: Reassure the patient that they can typically return to their normal diet immediately, unless the physician gives specific alternative orders.
  • Normal expectations: Explain that mild cramping and minor streaks of blood on the first bowel movement are normal, especially if biopsies were taken.
  • Activity guidelines: Most patients can resume normal daily routines right away.
  • When to seek help: Outline clear red-flag symptoms that require contacting the clinic or returning to the emergency department, such as heavy bleeding (more than a few drops), severe pain, or fever.
  • Follow-up plan: Remind the patient when to expect pathology results and when their next appointment with the provider is scheduled.
