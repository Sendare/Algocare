Underwater Seal Drainage

Introduction to Underwater Seal Drainage

Underwater seal drainage is a medical technique used to re-establish normal negative pressure within the pleural cavity. When air, blood, or fluid accumulates in this potential space, the lungs cannot expand fully, leading to respiratory compromise. The system acts as a one-way valve: gases and liquids exit the chest during expiration and coughing, but atmospheric air cannot re-enter the pleural space. This restores the physiological gradient necessary for optimal lung ventilation. In many Nigerian clinical settings, managing these systems requires constant vigilance, especially when commercial disposable units are unavailable and modified glass bottle systems are utilized.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Pleural Space

The pleural cavity is a closed, serous membrane-lined compartment surrounding each lung. It consists of two continuous layers: the visceral pleura, which closely covers the lung surface, and the parietal pleura, which lines the inner chest wall. Normally, this space contains only a few milliliters of lubricating fluid, resulting in a continuous sub-atmospheric pressure ranging from -4 to -8 mmHg during the respiratory cycle. Disruptions to this delicate seal through trauma, surgery, or disease allow air or fluid to enter, immediately abolishing the negative pressure and causing lung collapse.

Indications for Chest Tube Insertion

  • Pneumothorax: Accumulation of air in the pleural space, either spontaneous or traumatic.
  • Hemothorax: Collection of blood resulting from chest trauma, thoracic surgery, or vascular rupture.
  • Pleural Effusion: Excessive serous fluid buildup due to heart failure, infection, or malignancy.
  • Empyema: Presence of purulent material or pus within the pleural cavity, requiring drainage and irrigation.
  • Chylothorax: Leakage of lymphatic fluid from the thoracic duct into the pleural space.

Components and Mechanism of the Drainage System

Traditional and modern chest drainage setups typically employ a multi-chamber design.

  1. Collection Chamber: Receives and measures fluid or air draining directly from the patient's chest tube.
  2. Water Seal Chamber: Contains sterile water kept at a specific depth (usually 2 cm) acting as a one-way valve. Bubbling here indicates air escaping from the patient, while fluid level fluctuations (tidaling) reflect normal pressure changes during breathing.
  3. Suction Control Chamber: Regulates the maximum level of negative suction applied to the system, governed by the height of water in the chamber rather than the wall regulator setting.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Regular evaluation of both the patient and the drainage apparatus is a core responsibility. Inspect the insertion site for subcutaneous emphysema, swelling, redness, or purulent drainage. Ensure all tubing connections are securely taped to prevent accidental disconnection. Check that the drainage system remains upright and positioned well below the level of the patient's chest at all times to prevent gravity-driven backflow. Document hourly or shift output volumes, noting color and consistency, particularly following surgical interventions.

Patient Management and Care Maintenance

Encouraging deep breathing and effective coughing exercises helps expand lung tissue and clears retained secretions. Assist the patient with frequent repositioning while safeguarding the tubing against kinks, loops, or dependent pooling. Ambulating the patient, when prescribed, requires disconnecting or carefully carrying the secure unit while maintaining the low position. Never routinely clamp a chest tube during transport or patient movement, as clamping can rapidly induce a tension pneumothorax by trapping accumulating air.

Complications and Emergency Management

Despite careful management, emergencies can occur. If the chest tube accidentally pulls out of the chest wall, immediately cover the wound with a sterile petrolatum gauze dressing taped on three sides to allow air escape while preventing large air entry. If the drainage unit fractures or disconnects from the tubing, submerge the distal end of the chest tube in a sterile bottle of water to maintain a temporary water seal. Watch intently for signs of tension pneumothorax—such as tracheal deviation, absent breath sounds, and hemodynamic instability—which demand urgent medical intervention.

Chest Tube Removal Procedure

Removal of a chest tube occurs once lung re-expansion is confirmed via chest radiography and air leaks or fluid drainage have ceased. Pre-medicate the patient for pain as ordered. Instruct the patient to take a deep breath, exhale fully, and perform the Valsalva maneuver (bearing down) during the actual removal pull, which increases intrathoracic pressure and prevents air from entering the pleura. The nurse or doctor rapidly withdraws the tube and immediately applies an airtight, occlusive petroleum jelly dressing over the insertion site to secure closure.
