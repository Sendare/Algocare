Underwater Seal Drainage

Understanding the Pleural Space and Chest Tube Purpose

The chest cavity houses the lungs inside a closed environment called the pleural space. Normally, this space contains a very thin layer of fluid that helps the lungs glide smoothly against the chest wall during breathing. Crucially, the pleural space operates under negative pressure, meaning the pressure inside is lower than the atmospheric pressure outside. This negative pressure acts like a vacuum, keeping the lungs fully expanded against the chest wall.

When air, blood, or excess fluid enters the pleural space, that vital negative pressure is lost. The lung can no longer expand properly and begins to collapse. To restore normal pressure and allow the lung to re-expand, a chest tube is inserted into the pleural space and connected to a drainage system. This setup removes the trapped air or fluid while preventing anything from flowing back into the chest.

Common Reasons for Chest Tube Insertion

Clinicians insert chest tubes to treat several acute conditions that threaten breathing and circulation. Recognizing these specific problems helps nurses anticipate patient needs and monitor drainage effectively.

  • Pneumothorax: Air accumulates in the pleural space, often due to chest trauma or a ruptured lung bleb, causing a partial or complete lung collapse.
  • Hemothorax: Blood collects in the pleural space, frequently following chest surgery, penetrating injuries, or severe blunt trauma.
  • Pleural Effusion: Excess serous fluid builds up due to heart failure, infection, or cancer, compressing the lung tissue.
  • Empyema: Purulent, infected fluid accumulates in the pleural space, requiring drainage and targeted antibiotic therapy.

How the Three-Chamber Drainage System Works

Modern underwater seal chest drainage units typically feature three interconnected chambers. Each chamber performs a distinct job to protect the patient while clearing air and fluid.

Chamber NamePrimary FunctionNormal Finding
Collection ChamberReceives and measures fluid draining from the patient.Gradual accumulation of blood or serous fluid.
Water Seal ChamberActs as a one-way valve allowing air out while stopping air from entering the patient.Tidaling (water level rises and falls with breathing).
Suction Control ChamberRegulates the amount of negative suction applied to the system.Gentle bubbling in wet systems or an orange indicator in dry systems.

Essential Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Caring for a patient with a chest tube requires frequent, systematic checks of both the patient and the equipment. Start by assessing the patient's respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, breath sounds, and pain levels. Diminished breath sounds on the affected side can indicate that the lung has not re-expanded or that the tube is blocked.

Next, inspect the entire drainage system. Ensure all tubing connections are securely taped to prevent accidental disconnections. Check that the tubing hangs in loose loops without kinks, dependent loops, or heavy bedding resting on it, as any obstruction can trap fluid in the chest. Always keep the drainage unit positioned safely below the level of the patient's chest to prevent gravity from pulling fluid backward.

Troubleshooting Air Leaks and System Disruptions

Air leaks are a frequent complication in chest drainage systems. When continuous bubbling appears in the water seal chamber, it usually indicates an air leak somewhere in the circuit. Nurses must quickly determine whether the leak is coming from the patient's chest or from loose external equipment.

To find the source, briefly use rubber-tipped clamps to pinch the tubing close to the patient's dressing. If the bubbling stops immediately, the air leak is inside the patient's chest or at the insertion site. If the bubbling continues, move the clamp down the tubing toward the drainage box, checking connections along the way. Never clamp a chest tube for long periods, as this can quickly cause a life-threatening tension pneumothorax.

Emergency Management and Tube Disconnection

Accidents happen, and nurses must know how to respond instantly if a chest tube is accidentally pulled out or if the drainage system breaks.

If the chest tube slips completely out of the patient's chest, cover the wound immediately with a sterile petroleum gauze dressing taped on three sides. This occlusive dressing seals the wound during inhalation while allowing trapped air to escape during exhalation, preventing a tension pneumothorax. If the drainage unit breaks or cracks, quickly submerge the open end of the plastic tubing into a sterile bottle of sterile water to maintain an emergency water seal until a new drainage system can be connected.

Assisting with Chest Tube Removal

When the underlying condition resolves, lung re-expansion is confirmed on an X-ray, and air drainage stops, the physician decides to remove the chest tube. The nurse prepares the patient by explaining the procedure and offering pain medication about thirty minutes beforehand to ensure comfort.

During removal, the nurse instructs the patient to take a deep breath, hold it, and bear down. This maneuver, known as the Valsalva maneuver, increases intrathoracic pressure and prevents air from rushing back into the pleural space as the tube is pulled out. The clinician quickly places an airtight petroleum gauze dressing over the site immediately after tube removal, and the nurse monitors the patient's vital signs and lung sounds closely afterward.
