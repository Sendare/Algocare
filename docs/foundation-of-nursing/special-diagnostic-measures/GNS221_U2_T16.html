Intravenous Pyelography

Introduction and Definition of Intravenous Pyelography

Intravenous pyelography, commonly known as IVP, is a specialized X-ray examination that uses an injected contrast medium to visualize the kidneys, ureters, and urinary bladder. As the iodinated contrast dye travels through the bloodstream, it is filtered out by the kidneys, outlining the entire urinary tract on sequential radiograph images. This diagnostic tool allows healthcare providers to assess the structural integrity, size, and shape of the renal system, as well as evaluate how efficiently the kidneys drain urine. While modern cross-sectional imaging like CT scans and ultrasound have largely replaced it for certain emergencies, IVP remains invaluable for seeing the actual plumbing of the urinary tract in real-time.

Indications and Diagnostic Purposes

  • Urolithiasis: Detecting the presence, size, and exact location of kidney stones or ureteral calculi.
  • Hematuria: Investigating the underlying structural cause of blood found in a patient's urine.
  • Flank Pain: Determining if chronic or acute lower back and side pain stems from an upper urinary obstruction.
  • Congenital Anomalies: Identifying structural birth defects such as duplex kidneys or horseshoe kidneys.
  • Trauma: Assessing extent of damage to the renal parenchyma following blunt or penetrating abdominal injuries.

Contraindications and Risk Factors

Before administering contrast dye, nurses must carefully screen patients for factors that make the procedure unsafe. A documented history of severe allergic reaction to iodine or shellfish is a strict contraindication, as the contrast medium can trigger life-threatening anaphylaxis. Patients with compromised renal function, indicated by an elevated serum creatinine or low glomerular filtration rate, are at high risk for contrast-induced nephropathy, which can worsen kidney damage. Additionally, pregnant women should avoid this procedure due to the harmful effects of ionizing radiation on the developing fetus. Metformin use must also be identified, as combining it with iodinated contrast increases the dangerous risk of lactic acidosis.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Preparation for an IVP begins well before the patient enters the radiology department. The nurse must obtain written informed consent, ensuring the patient understands the procedure and potential risks. Bowel preparation is often ordered, such as a mild laxative or enema the evening before, because fecal matter and gas in the colon can obscure the kidneys on the X-ray films. Patients are typically instructed to remain NPO (nil per os) for several hours prior to the test to reduce the risk of aspiration if nausea occurs from the dye. Finally, the nurse instructs the client to empty their bladder immediately before the procedure starts so baseline images of the empty bladder can be obtained.

Intra-Procedure Care and the Role of the Nurse

During the IVP, the patient lies on the X-ray table while the radiologist or nurse practitioner establishes an intravenous line. Once the radiopaque contrast is pushed into the vein, the nurse must closely observe the patient for immediate adverse reactions. A common, expected sensation is a transient flushing feeling, a salty or metallic taste in the mouth, and mild nausea. However, hives, difficulty breathing, or severe dizziness are abnormal and require immediate reporting. The radiologist takes a series of X-rays at timed intervals—often every few minutes—to track how the dye fills the renal pelvis, ureters, and bladder.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Management

Once the imaging is finished, nursing care shifts to recovery and elimination support. The most important post-procedure goal is to encourage high fluid intake, either orally or via IV fluids, to help flush the contrast dye rapidly out of the kidneys and prevent renal injury. The nurse must monitor and record the patient's urine output for the next several hours, noting any signs of oliguria or dysuria. Vital signs are checked periodically to ensure hemodynamic stability. The nurse also reminds the client that mild discomfort or frequency when urinating might occur briefly as the remaining dye exits the system.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

Although generally safe, IVP carries risks ranging from mild allergic reactions to severe medical emergencies. Nurses must remain vigilant for delayed or immediate hypersensitivity responses, which can manifest as generalized urticaria, bronchospasm, or anaphylactic shock. If a patient develops respiratory distress or facial swelling, the nurse must immediately stop any ongoing contrast infusion, maintain airway patency, and summon the emergency response team while preparing to administer epinephrine and IV antihistamines. Another significant risk is contrast-induced acute kidney injury, which requires careful ongoing laboratory monitoring of kidney function markers in the days following the test.
