Laryngoscopy

Introduction and Definition of Laryngoscopy

Laryngoscopy is a clinical procedure that allows healthcare providers to inspect the interior of the larynx, including the vocal cords, glottis, and surrounding structures. By utilizing specialized instruments such as mirrors, rigid scopes, or flexible fiberoptic cameras, a practitioner can visualize areas of the upper airway that are otherwise hidden from direct external view.

In nursing practice, understanding this procedure is essential because it bridges diagnostic evaluation and emergency airway management. Whether performed in an outpatient clinic to investigate persistent hoarseness or urgently at a patient's bedside during respiratory arrest, laryngoscopy provides vital visual access. Nurses must recognize that visualization of the larynx is not only a diagnostic tool for pathology like nodules or tumors but also the fundamental first step in placing an endotracheal tube during general anesthesia or resuscitation.

Indications and Clinical Purposes

Patients undergo laryngoscopy for a wide variety of diagnostic and therapeutic reasons. Recognizing these indications helps nurses anticipate patient needs, explain the rationale for the test, and prepare the correct equipment.

  • Persistent Hoarseness: Investigating structural changes, polyps, nodules, or malignancy on the vocal cords.
  • Airway Management: Visualizing the glottis to insert an endotracheal tube for general anesthesia or mechanical ventilation.
  • Foreign Body Retrieval: Locating and removing inhaled objects obstructing the upper airway.
  • Biopsy Collection: Obtaining tissue samples from suspicious lesions or masses within the larynx.
  • Evaluating Dysphagia: Assessing functional movement of the vocal cords and surrounding structures when a patient struggles to swallow.

Types of Laryngoscopy: Indirect, Direct, and Fiberoptic

There are three primary methods of viewing the larynx, each chosen based on the clinical goal and the patient's condition.

Indirect laryngoscopy is a traditional, non-invasive method using a small, angled mirror held at the back of the patient's throat alongside a headlamp or light source. While quick and performed in a clinic setting, it offers a limited field of view and triggers a strong gag reflex.

Direct laryngoscopy uses a rigid, illuminated blade inserted directly through the mouth to push the tongue aside and bring the vocal cords into a straight line of sight. This method is common in operating rooms and emergency departments primarily for endotracheal intubation.

Flexible fiberoptic laryngoscopy utilizes a thin, flexible tube equipped with a camera and light source passed through the nasal cavity. This method is well-tolerated, can be performed while the patient is awake and seated, and allows for dynamic assessment of vocal cord movement during speech and breathing.

Equipment and Preparation for the Procedure

Proper preparation of equipment and the environment ensures safety and efficiency during any laryngoscopy. The nurse must gather and check all supplies before the procedure begins.

For a diagnostic fiberoptic scope, equipment includes the light source, camera stack, topical local anesthetic spray, nasal decongestant drops to shrink mucosal tissues, and antifog solution. For a direct rigid laryngoscopy, the nurse must prepare handles, blades of various sizes, suction tubing and Yankauer catheters, oxygen delivery equipment, and backup difficult airway tools.

Testing the light source of every laryngoscope blade is a non-negotiable nursing duty. A bulb that fails mid-procedure compromises airway visualization and endangers the patient.

Pre-Procedural Nursing Assessment and Patient Education

Thorough preparation involves assessing the patient's physical status and providing clear education to reduce anxiety. The nurse should review the medical record for allergies to local anesthetics or latex, check coagulation profiles if biopsies are planned, and note any history of difficult airways or neck mobility issues.

Patient education focuses on what to expect. Explain to the patient that they will feel a cold or bitter-tasting spray in the back of the throat or nose, that they may gag or feel pressure, but that they will still be able to breathe. Confirm that informed consent has been signed by the physician and ensure the patient remains NPO (nothing by mouth) for the prescribed duration to minimize aspiration risks if sedation is used.

Intra-Procedural Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

During the procedure, the nurse acts as the patient's advocate and monitor. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, including pulse oximetry, heart rate, and blood pressure, is mandatory.

Positioning the patient correctly depends on the method: a seated position leaning slightly forward for fiberoptic nasal scopes, or a sniffing position with the head extended and neck flexed for direct rigid laryngoscopy. The nurse must offer emotional reassurance, manage suction to keep secretions clear from the lens or field of view, and closely watch for signs of respiratory distress, severe laryngospasm, or oxygen desaturation.

Post-Procedural Care and Monitoring for Complications

After laryngoscopy, nursing care centers on maintaining airway safety and monitoring for delayed adverse effects. The most critical post-procedural rule is keeping the patient NPO until the gag reflex and swallowing ability completely return, which usually takes one to two hours following topical anesthesia.

Monitor the patient's respiratory status, voice quality, and ability to swallow their own saliva without coughing. Instruct the patient not to clear their throat aggressively or speak loudly until the numbing medication wears off to avoid vocal cord trauma. Provide ice chips or sips of water only after the nurse officially confirms the return of the swallow reflex.

Potential Complications and Emergency Management

Although generally safe, laryngoscopy carries inherent risks due to airway manipulation and local anesthesia. Nurses must be vigilant for complications and prepared to manage emergencies instantly.

  • Laryngospasm: Involuntary spasm of the vocal cords causing airway obstruction. Managed with 100% oxygen and positive pressure ventilation; severe cases may require muscle relaxants.
  • Aspiration: Inhalation of oral secretions or stomach contents into the lungs, leading to chemical pneumonitis or infection.
  • Bleeding and Trauma: Minor mucosal bleeding is common after biopsies, but severe trauma to the teeth, lips, or pharynx can occur during rigid instrumentation.
  • Allergic Reactions: Adverse reactions to topical anesthetics or sedative medications.
