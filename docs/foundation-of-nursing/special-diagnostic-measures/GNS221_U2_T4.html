Laryngoscopy

Introduction and Definition of Laryngoscopy

Laryngoscopy is a clinical procedure that allows direct or indirect visualization of the larynx, vocal cords, and surrounding pharyngeal structures. By inspecting this anatomical gateway, clinicians diagnose pathologies, assess airway patency, and facilitate advanced interventions like endotracheal intubation. The larynx sits deep within the neck, making direct visualization challenging without specific instrumentation that displaces the tongue and aligns the visual axes. In Nigerian tertiary hospitals, both diagnostic and procedural laryngoscopy are performed frequently in emergency departments, operating theatres, and ear, nose, and throat clinics. Mastery of this procedure's fundamentals helps nurses anticipate clinical needs and maintain patient safety during airway management.

Clinical Indications and Purposes

Clinicians order laryngoscopy for both diagnostic exploration and therapeutic intervention. Identifying the underlying cause of persistent hoarseness, chronic cough, foreign body aspiration, or unexplained neck pain often requires direct visualization of mucosal changes, lesions, or vocal cord paralysis. Therapeutically, the procedure is the gold standard for securing an emergency airway during general anesthesia, cardiac arrest, or severe respiratory failure. In busy Nigerian emergency rooms, recognizing these indications allows nursing staff to prepare airway trolleys swiftly before acute decompensation occurs.

Categorization: Indirect, Direct, and Fiberoptic Methods

- **Indirect Laryngoscopy**: A traditional, non-invasive technique using a small laryngeal mirror and a headlamp or light source. The patient opens their mouth, protrudes the tongue, and the clinician angles the mirror to reflect the laryngeal image. - **Direct Laryngoscopy**: An invasive technique using a rigid blade (such as Macintosh or Miller) attached to a handle with a light source. It requires proper head positioning—often the sniffing position—to bring the laryngeal inlet into direct line of sight. - **Fiberoptic Laryngoscopy**: Utilizes a flexible or rigid telescope equipped with fiberoptic bundles or a digital camera. It can be performed through the nose or mouth, offering superior visualization with minimal patient neck manipulation, making it ideal for patients with cervical spine precautions.

Equipment and Preparation

Successful visualization depends on meticulous preparation of both environment and instruments. The nurse must assemble a working laryngoscopy handle, appropriate blades, a light source, suction apparatus, and oxygen delivery systems. Checking bulb brightness and battery integrity before the procedure prevents sudden equipment failure during critical moments. For awake or fiberoptic procedures, topical local anesthetics and vasoconstrictors (such as co-phenylcaine or lidocaine spray) are prepared to reduce patient gagging and mucosal bleeding. Ensuring functional suction is immediately available remains a non-negotiable safety standard in every clinical setting.

Pre-Procedural Nursing Assessment and Patient Education

Before any elective laryngoscopy, the nurse performs a focused assessment of the patient's airway anatomy, noting mouth opening, thyromental distance, and Mallampati score. Allergies to local anesthetics or latex must be documented clearly. Fasting status (nil per os) is verified to minimize aspiration risks, especially if sedation is planned. Patient education reduces anxiety and encourages cooperation. The nurse explains sensations of pressure, the necessity of keeping the head still, and why speaking will be temporarily difficult or restricted during and after the procedure. In Nigerian clinical practice, explaining these steps in the patient's preferred local language fosters trust and compliance.

Intra-Procedural Responsibilities and Monitoring

During the procedure, the nurse maintains continuous observation of the patient's vital signs, oxygen saturation, and cardiac rhythm. Positioning support is vital; the nurse may assist in maintaining the correct head and neck alignment as requested by the proceduralist. Monitoring for signs of distress, excessive coughing, laryngospasm, or vagal stimulation (such as sudden bradycardia) requires constant vigilance. The nurse acts as an active assistant, handing instruments promptly and keeping the suction catheter ready for instant use.

Post-Procedural Care and Complication Monitoring

Immediate post-procedural care centers on airway protection and physiological stability. The patient must remain NPO (nil per os) until local pharyngeal anesthesia wears off completely, typically tested by checking for an active swallowing reflex. Allowing oral intake too early carries a high risk of aspiration. The nurse monitors for hoarseness, stridor, bleeding, and respiratory difficulty. Continuous pulse oximetry is maintained until the patient is fully recovered from any sedation. Documenting the procedure outcome and patient tolerance completes the immediate nursing record.

Potential Complications and Emergency Management

Although generally safe, laryngoscopy carries inherent risks. Minor complications include transient sore throat, mucosal abrasions, and dental trauma from improper blade leverage. Severe complications involve laryngospasm, bronchospasm, severe hemorrhage, and hypoxic injury. If laryngospasm occurs, immediate intervention includes applying 100% oxygen via positive pressure mask and calling for medical assistance. Emergency airway carts, including equipment for surgical cricothyroidotomy, must remain immediately accessible whenever invasive airway procedures are performed.
