Laryngoscopy

Introduction and Definition of Laryngoscopy

Laryngoscopy is a clinical procedure that allows direct or indirect visualization of the larynx, commonly referred to as the voice box. This diagnostic and therapeutic intervention enables healthcare providers to inspect the vocal cords, glottis, and surrounding subglottic or supraglottic structures. It forms a cornerstone of airway management, particularly during endotracheal intubation in emergency rooms, operating theatres, and intensive care units across Nigerian hospitals. Understanding this procedure requires familiarity with upper respiratory anatomy, specifically the alignment of the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes.

Indications and Clinical Purposes

Clinicians perform laryngoscopy for a broad range of diagnostic and therapeutic reasons. Diagnostic uses include investigating persistent hoarseness, chronic cough, foreign body aspiration, unexplained neck pain, or suspected malignancy. Therapeutic indications focus primarily on securing a compromised airway, removing foreign objects, or assisting in surgical biopsies of vocal cord lesions.

Types of Laryngoscopy: Indirect, Direct, and Fiberoptic

  • Indirect Laryngoscopy: A traditional bedside method using a small, angled mirror held at the back of the patient's throat alongside a headlamp or light source.
  • Direct Laryngoscopy: Uses a rigid, lighted blade (such as Macintosh or Miller blades) inserted directly through the mouth to displace anatomical structures and expose the glottis.
  • Fiberoptic Laryngoscopy: Employs a flexible or rigid endoscope attached to a camera source, allowing visualization through the nasal passages with minimal patient discomfort.

Equipment, Instrumentation, and Pre-Procedural Preparation

Successful laryngoscopy depends on meticulous preparation of the environment and equipment. For direct procedures, the nurse must assemble and test a functional laryngoscope handle and various blade sizes, ensuring the light bulb shines brightly. Oxygen delivery equipment, suction apparatus, and appropriate-sized endotracheal tubes must stand ready. Personal protective equipment, topical local anesthetics, and sedation agents are organized according to hospital policy to ensure patient safety and comfort.

Pre-Procedural Nursing Assessment and Patient Education

Before the procedure, nurses conduct a focused respiratory assessment, noting baseline oxygen saturation, heart rate, and any loose teeth that could become dislodged. Assessing for allergies to local anesthetics, latex, or sedatives prevents adverse drug reactions. Patient education involves explaining the steps of the procedure, obtaining informed consent, and enforcing fasting instructions to minimize the risk of aspiration during sedation.

Intra-Procedural Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

During the procedure, the nurse maintains a sterile or clean environment, hands instruments to the clinician promptly, and continuously monitors the patient's vital signs. Observing oxygen saturation, cardiac rhythm, and signs of respiratory distress remains paramount. The nurse also offers reassurance to conscious patients undergoing fiberoptic examinations, monitoring for excessive coughing or laryngospasm.

Post-Procedural Care and Monitoring for Complications

Following laryngoscopy, the nurse monitors the airway for swelling, bleeding, hoarseness, or difficulty swallowing. If a topical local anesthetic was sprayed in the pharynx, oral intake must remain restricted until the gag reflex completely returns, usually tested with a small sip of water. Patients require positioning in a semi-Fowler's posture to promote chest expansion and reduce upper airway edema.

Potential Complications and Emergency Management

Although generally safe, laryngoscopy carries risks such as dental trauma, mucosal lacerations, nosebleeds during nasal routes, and laryngospasm. Severe laryngeal edema can cause acute airway obstruction, manifesting as stridor and respiratory distress. Emergency management requires immediate administration of oxygen, readiness for re-intubation or surgical cricothyroidotomy, and emergency medication delivery.
