Laparoscopy

Introduction to Laparoscopy and Minimally Invasive Surgery

Laparoscopy, frequently referred to as keyhole surgery, represents a major shift away from traditional open surgical techniques. Instead of making a large abdominal incision, the surgeon creates several small punctures, typically measuring between 5 to 12 millimeters. Through these ports, a specialized telescope connected to a high-definition camera and long, slender instruments are introduced. To visualize the internal organs clearly, the peritoneal cavity must be artificially expanded. This is achieved by insufflating the abdomen with medical-grade carbon dioxide gas, creating a working space between the abdominal wall and the viscera.

The benefits of this approach over traditional laparotomy are well-documented. Patients experience significantly less post-operative pain because the abdominal wall muscles are separated rather than cut. Smaller incisions translate to reduced blood loss, a lower risk of wound site infection, and a dramatically shortened hospital stay. In busy surgical wards across Nigeria, this rapid turnover allows for optimal bed utilization without compromising patient safety. Cosmetically, patients are left with minimal scarring, which holds considerable psychological value.

Indications and Diagnostic Uses in Nigerian Healthcare

Laparoscopy serves both diagnostic and therapeutic functions in modern surgical practice. In many secondary and tertiary health facilities in Nigeria, it has become the gold standard for investigating acute and chronic abdominal pain of unclear etiology.

  • Gynecological evaluations: Investigating unexplained female infertility, diagnosing ectopic pregnancies, staging pelvic inflammatory disease, and performing ovarian cystectomies or tubal ligations.
  • Acute abdomen: Rapid identification of perforated peptic ulcers, acute appendicitis, and torsion of intra-abdominal organs when ultrasound findings are inconclusive.
  • General surgical interventions: Elective and emergency cholecystectomies for symptomatic gallstone disease, various hernia repairs, and staging of abdominal malignancies.
  • Biopsy acquisition: Direct visualization allows surgeons to take targeted tissue samples from suspicious liver lesions, peritoneal masses, or enlarged lymph nodes with high diagnostic accuracy.

Contraindications and Risk Assessment

While minimally invasive surgery offers extensive benefits, certain clinical conditions render the approach unsafe or technically impossible. A thorough pre-operative risk assessment must identify these absolute and relative barriers.

  • Absolute Contraindications: Severe uncorrected coagulopathy, where bleeding cannot be controlled, and inability to tolerate general anesthesia due to end-stage cardiopulmonary failure.
  • Relative Contraindications: Extensive prior abdominal surgeries with severe adhesions, massive generalized peritonitis, large abdominal wall hernias, and third-trimester pregnancy.
  • Cardiorespiratory Impact: The creation of pneumoperitoneum increases intra-abdominal pressure, raising the diaphragm and reducing functional residual capacity while elevating peak airway pressures. Patients with compromised cardiac ejection fractions or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease may experience severe hypercapnia and hemodynamic instability under these conditions.

Pre-Operative Nursing Assessment and Patient Preparation

Preparing a patient for a laparoscopic procedure involves comprehensive physiological and psychological readiness. Nurses begin with a baseline clinical assessment, noting vital signs, current medication histories, and any known drug allergies. Blood investigations, including a full blood count, serum electrolytes, urea, creatinine, and cross-matching, must be checked and placed in the chart.

Informed consent verification is a crucial nursing responsibility. Ensure the patient understands that while the procedure is planned laparoscopically, there is always a small possibility of conversion to an open laparotomy if unexpected complications or severe adhesions arise. Gastrointestinal preparation typically involves fasting from midnight for elective cases, and sometimes bowel clearance agents depending on the target organ. Ensure all metal jewelry, dentures, and nail polish are removed. Finally, administer pre-anesthetic medications exactly as prescribed and confirm that the surgical site has been correctly marked.

Intra-Operative Nursing Care and Equipment Management

Operating theater nursing during laparoscopy requires meticulous attention to specialized instrumentation and patient positioning. The scrub and circulating nurses work as a synchronized team to manage light sources, camera systems, video monitors, insufflators, and electrosurgical units.

  • Patient Positioning: Depending on the target organ, patients may be placed in the Trendelenburg position for pelvic procedures or the reverse Trendelenburg position for upper gastrointestinal surgeries. Secure limbs properly with padding to prevent pressure neuropathies and deep vein thrombosis.
  • Equipment Checklist: Insufflation tubing, Veress needles, trocars of varying sizes, and laparoscopic graspers must be checked for structural integrity and electrical insulation before use.
  • Intra-operative Monitoring: Continuous observation of insufflation pressure, total gas volume absorbed, patient core body temperature, and end-tidal carbon dioxide levels helps prevent gas embolisms and hypothermia during prolonged cases.

Post-Operative Nursing Care and Recovery Monitoring

Recovery monitoring begins immediately upon arrival in the post-anesthesia care unit. Vital signs are recorded every fifteen minutes until stable. Nurses observe the tiny wound dressings for any signs of oozing blood, serosanguinous drainage, or subcutaneous emphysema—which feels like a crackling sensation under the skin and indicates gas tracking outside the peritoneal cavity.

Pain management is a primary focus. While incisional pain is mild, patients frequently complain of referred shoulder pain caused by residual carbon dioxide gas irritating the phrenic nerve beneath the diaphragm. Early ambulation is strongly encouraged as soon as spinal or general anesthetic effects wear off; this simple intervention accelerates bowel motility and helps dissipate trapped gas. Monitor fluid balance strictly, assess for passage of flatus, and ensure the patient can tolerate oral sips before advancing their diet.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

Although laparoscopy is safe, complications can occur during or after the procedure. Nurses must remain vigilant for subtle indicators of internal trauma or system failure.

  • Vascular Injury: Accidental puncture of major vessels like the aorta or iliac vessels by the Veress needle or primary trocar causes catastrophic hemorrhage. Signs include sudden, unexplained hypotension and tachycardia.
  • Visceral Injury: Unintentional thermal or mechanical injury to the bowel, bladder, or ureters may present days later as localized peritonitis, fever, and persistent abdominal rigidity.
  • Pneumothorax or Gas Embolism: Rare but life-threatening events resulting from gas tracking through congenital diaphragmatic defects or entering an open vein directly.
  • Wound Infections and Port-Site Hernias: Localized erythema, purulent discharge, or a visible bulge at a trocar site weeks after surgery.

Patient Discharge Education and Home Care

Effective discharge education empowers patients to manage their recovery safely at home and identifies when to seek urgent medical attention. Instruct patients to keep their small port-site incisions clean and dry. Standard steri-strips should be left to fall off naturally. Showering is usually permitted after forty-eight hours, but soaking in a bathtub should be avoided until wounds are completely healed.

Provide clear dietary guidelines, advising small, frequent meals as the digestive tract adjusts. Warn patients that mild shoulder discomfort and abdominal bloating are normal and will resolve on their own within a few days. Emphasize red-flag symptoms that require immediate return to the clinic: severe worsening abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, high fever with chills, spreading redness or pus at any incision site, and inability to pass urine. Finally, schedule a follow-up appointment for suture removal and wound check.
