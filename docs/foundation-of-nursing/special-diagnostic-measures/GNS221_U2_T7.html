Proctoscopy

Introduction and Definition of Proctoscopy

A **proctoscopy** is a diagnostic endoscopic procedure that allows direct visualization of the lower rectum and anal canal. Using a rigid or semi-rigid lighted instrument known as a proctoscope, healthcare providers can inspect the mucosal lining for abnormalities such as hemorrhoids, fissures, polyps, or inflammation. Unlike a colonoscopy or sigmoidoscopy which visualize larger segments of the large intestine, proctoscopy focuses exclusively on the terminal 8 to 15 centimeters of the gastrointestinal tract. This makes it a rapid, cost-effective outpatient investigation frequently performed in surgical outpatients, general practice clinics, and specialized gastroenterology units across Nigeria.

Indications for Clinical Practice

Proctoscopy is ordered when a patient presents with symptoms localized to the anorectal region. Clinicians utilize this procedure to confirm diagnoses, assess severity, or guide minor interventions. - **Rectal Bleeding**: Investigating the source of bright red blood per rectum, often due to internal hemorrhoids. - **Anorectal Pain**: Evaluating persistent discomfort, throbbing pain, or acute sharp pain associated with bowel movements. - **Pruritus Ani**: Finding underlying dermatological changes, chronic inflammation, or small lesions causing severe anal itching. - **Palpable Masses**: Visualizing and potentially biopsying lumps, skin tags, or suspected neoplastic growths felt during a digital rectal examination. - **Foreign Bodies**: Assisting in the visualization and retrieval of objects inserted into the rectum.

Contraindications and Risk Assessment

Although a low-risk procedure, proctoscopy is deferred or modified when the potential for harm outweighs diagnostic benefit. Absolute contraindications include severe acute fulminant colitis, extensive anorectal trauma, or an uncooperative patient who cannot maintain the required position. Relative contraindications involve patients with severe coagulopathies or active, extremely painful perianal sepsis where instrumentation would exacerbate trauma or cause intolerable pain. A thorough pre-procedure assessment must check bleeding tendencies, current anticoagulant use, and the patient's psychological readiness to ensure safety.

Equipment and Anatomy of the Proctoscope

The standard proctoscope is a short, hollow, rigid or semi-rigid metal or plastic tube measuring roughly 7 to 10 centimeters in length. It features an obturator—a smooth, rounded plug that fits inside the tube to prevent mucosal trauma during initial insertion. Once the instrument is advanced into the rectum, the obturator is removed, allowing a built-in or fiberoptic light source to illuminate the rectal lumen. Nurses must check that the light source functions properly before the procedure begins and ensure lubricating jelly is readily available. Understanding the anatomy is vital: the instrument must follow the natural curve of the sacrum to prevent injury to the rectal wall.

Pre-Procedure Patient Preparation and Education

Effective patient preparation minimizes anxiety and ensures a clear view for the clinician. Unlike full colonoscopies, proctoscopy rarely requires extensive oral bowel preparation. Instead, patients often receive a simple rectal enema, such as a sodium phosphate or normal saline enema, approximately 30 to 60 minutes before the procedure to clear the lower bowel of feces. Nursing education focuses on explaining the steps, addressing fears of embarrassment, and reassuring the patient about privacy. The nurse instructs the client to empty their bladder immediately before the procedure and describes the pressure sensations they will experience.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities and Positioning

During the procedure, the nurse plays a critical role in maintaining patient comfort, dignity, and safety. The patient is typically positioned in either the **left lateral (Sims') position** with knees flexed toward the chest, or the **knee-chest position**, depending on patient mobility and physician preference. The nurse drapes the patient appropriately to expose only the anal region, reducing psychological distress. Continuous monitoring of the patient's vital signs, facial expressions, and verbal feedback is essential. The nurse also manages the light source, hands instruments to the examiner, and provides immediate emotional reassurance if the patient experiences cramping or distress.

Post-Procedure Care and Monitoring

Once the proctoscope is withdrawn, immediate post-procedure care focuses on comfort and hygiene. The nurse provides tissue or wipes to clean the perianal area and helps the patient slowly transition from the examination position to avoid orthostatic dizziness. Patients may experience a mild, temporary urge to defecate or pass gas due to air insufflation during the procedure; the nurse should reassure them that this resolves quickly. Vital signs are checked as needed, and outpatient discharge instructions are reviewed. The client is advised that minor spotting can occur, especially if biopsies were taken, but heavy bleeding or severe abdominal pain must be reported immediately.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although proctoscopy is generally safe, nurses must remain vigilant for potential complications. The most serious risk is **rectal perforation**, which presents with severe abdominal pain, abdominal rigidity, fever, and tachycardia. Minor complications include localized bleeding, particularly if polyps were removed or hemorrhoids irritated, and mild vasovagal episodes during insertion due to sphincter stretching. Nursing interventions include monitoring for delayed bleeding, assessing abdominal status, and immediately notifying the surgical team if peritoneal signs develop. Educating the patient on red-flag symptoms ensures prompt intervention if complications arise after discharge.
