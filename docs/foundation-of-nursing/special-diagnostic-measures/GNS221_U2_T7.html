Proctoscopy

Introduction and Definition of Proctoscopy

A proctoscopy is a diagnostic procedure that allows a healthcare provider to visually examine the rectum and the anal canal using a specialized instrument called a proctoscope. This instrument is a rigid, hollow tube equipped with a light source, typically ranging from about 7 to 15 centimeters in length. Unlike a full colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy, a proctoscopy focuses strictly on the lower terminal portion of the large intestine. Nurses encounter this procedure frequently in outpatient clinics, surgical suites, and gastroenterology departments. It provides a direct, magnified view of the mucosal lining, enabling clinicians to identify localized pathologies without subjecting the patient to extensive bowel preparation or sedation.

Indications for Clinical Practice

Clinicians order proctoscopy to investigate persistent lower gastrointestinal symptoms or to perform routine surveillance of known rectal conditions. Common reasons for the procedure include:

  • Rectal bleeding: Finding the source of bright red blood per rectum, such as internal or external hemorrhoids.
  • Anorectal pain: Evaluating localized discomfort, tenderness, or suspected fissures.
  • Masses or polyps: Visualizing, biopsying, or removing suspicious growths found during digital rectal exams.
  • Foreign bodies: Locating and safely retrieving objects inserted into the rectum.
  • Pruritus ani: Investigating severe, unexplained itching or chronic inflammation around the anal margin.

Equipment, Anatomy, and Setup

Performing a successful proctoscopy requires a coordinated set of instruments and a solid grasp of local pelvic anatomy. The proctoscope itself features a hollow metal or plastic cylinder, an obturator with a rounded tip to ease initial insertion without scraping the mucosa, and an attached light source.

Nurses must ensure all items are gathered, functional, and within sterile expiry dates before the doctor enters the room. Maintaining patient privacy by keeping the tray covered until the last possible moment helps reduce anxiety.

Pre-Procedure Patient Preparation

Adequate preparation ensures a clear view of the rectal mucosa and minimizes patient distress. Unlike a full colonoscopy, a proctoscopy usually requires only a simple bowel preparation, such as a single sodium phosphate or saline enema administered 30 to 60 minutes before the examination.

The nurse plays a vital teaching role by explaining each step in simple, reassuring language. Address common fears regarding pain and loss of dignity. Confirm that the patient has emptied their bladder just prior to the exam, which reduces pelvic pressure and makes positioning more comfortable. Verify informed consent, making sure the patient understands what sensations to expect, such as a strong urge to defecate when the scope is introduced.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities

During the actual procedure, the nurse acts as both a clinical assistant and a patient advocate. Positioning is critical for visualization and comfort. The preferred positions are the left lateral (Sims') position or the knee-chest position.

  1. Assist with positioning: Help the patient settle into Sims' position with knees flexed toward the chest, or the knee-chest position if they can tolerate it.
  2. Provide drape management: Drape the patient strategically to expose only the anal region, preserving their dignity throughout.
  3. Monitor vital signs and comfort: Watch the patient's facial expressions and monitor for signs of vasovagal symptoms, such as sudden sweating, dizziness, or lightheadedness.
  4. Manage specimens: Receive tissue biopsies or swabs from the clinician, label them immediately, and place them in the correct fixative containers.

Post-Procedure Care and Monitoring

Once the proctoscope is withdrawn, the immediate nursing priority shifts to patient recovery, hygiene, and comfort. Clean the perianal area gently with tissues or wipes to remove any remaining lubricant or discharge. Help the patient slowly change from their procedural position to a sitting or lying posture, as rapid movement can trigger orthostatic dizziness.

Inform the patient that they may feel a lingering sensation of fullness or a mild urge to pass gas due to air introduced during the examination. Provide clear instructions on warning signs to watch for at home, including severe abdominal cramping, heavy rectal bleeding, or high fever. Most patients can resume their normal diet and activities immediately unless biopsies or minor surgical removals were performed.
