Paracentesis Abdominis

Introduction and Definition of Paracentesis Abdominis

Paracentesis abdominis is a bedside medical procedure where a needle or catheter is safely introduced into the peritoneal cavity to remove accumulated fluid. This abnormal fluid collection is known as ascites. Ascites frequently develops as a complication of end-stage liver disease, portal hypertension, heart failure, or severe peritonitis. When fluid builds up excessively, it places immense pressure on the diaphragm and abdominal organs, causing severe discomfort and respiratory distress. Performing a paracentesis can either be diagnostic, to analyze the fluid for infection and cancer cells, or therapeutic, to relieve intense pressure and breathing difficulties.

Indications and Therapeutic Goals

Healthcare providers order a paracentesis for two primary reasons: diagnosis and treatment. Diagnostic taps are performed on patients with new-onset ascites or those suspected of having spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), a dangerous infection of the fluid. Therapeutic taps are reserved for patients with massive ascites causing severe pain, early satiety, or compromised breathing.

  • Diagnostic analysis: Checking fluid cell counts, protein levels, and culture results.
  • Symptom relief: Draining large volumes, sometimes up to several liters, to restore patient comfort.
  • Respiratory improvement: Lowering the diaphragm so the lungs can expand fully.

Contraindications and Risk Assessment

While paracentesis is a routine procedure, certain conditions make it unsafe or require extreme caution. A major absolute contraindication is an acute surgical abdomen requiring immediate exploratory surgery. Relative contraindications involve bleeding risks, such as severe coagulopathy or profound thrombocytopenia, where the patient's blood fails to clot properly. Nurses must carefully check laboratory results like the International Normalized Ratio (INR) and platelet count before the procedure begins. Severe bowel distention or extensive surgical scars in the lower abdomen also increase the risk of accidental organ puncture.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Thorough preparation reduces patient anxiety and prevents complications. The nurse begins by ensuring an informed consent form has been signed by the doctor and understood by the patient. Next, the nurse asks the patient to empty their bladder completely. An empty bladder drastically reduces the accidental risk of bladder perforation during needle insertion. Baseline vital signs and abdominal girth measurements are documented. Positioning the patient properly is also key; the semi-Fowler's position or sitting upright on the edge of the bed allows fluid to pool dependently in the lower abdomen.

Equipment Needed and Sterile Technique

A paracentesis is an invasive procedure requiring strict aseptic technique to prevent introducing bacteria into the sterile peritoneal cavity. The nurse gathers a standard paracentesis tray along with local anesthetic, syringes, needles, scalpels, drainage tubing, and collection containers. Personal protective equipment, sterile gowns, caps, masks, and sterile drapes must be utilized by the operator. The skin over the insertion site is thoroughly cleaned with an antiseptic solution like chlorhexidine, and the area is draped to maintain a large sterile field.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Assistance and Patient Monitoring

During the actual procedure, the nurse's role shifts to supporting the patient and monitoring their vital signs. The doctor typically inserts the needle or catheter in the lower quadrant, avoiding the inferior epigastric artery and the umbilicus. The nurse talks calmly to the patient, watches for signs of discomfort, and records blood pressure, pulse rate, and oxygen saturation. Rapid fluid removal can cause a sudden drop in blood pressure, known as hypotension or post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction. If the patient feels dizzy, lightheaded, or short of breath, the nurse must notify the doctor immediately so drainage can be slowed or stopped.

Post-Procedure Care, Specimen Handling, and Documentation

Once the required amount of fluid is removed, the catheter is withdrawn, and a sterile pressure dressing is applied to the puncture site. The nurse monitors the dressing frequently for any signs of persistent fluid leakage or bleeding. Collected fluid specimens are properly labeled, placed in biohazard bags, and sent immediately to the laboratory with matching requisition forms. The nurse also records the total volume of fluid drained, its appearance and color, the patient's post-procedure vital signs, and how well the patient tolerated the procedure.

Potential Complications and Emergency Nursing Interventions

Complications can arise during or after a paracentesis and require prompt recognition. The most common immediate complication is persistent fluid leakage from the puncture site. More serious risks include accidental laceration of the intestine or bladder, severe intra-abdominal hemorrhage, and circulatory shock from rapid fluid shifts. If a patient shows signs of severe abdominal pain, rigid abdomen, or falling blood pressure after the procedure, the nurse must treat this as an emergency, notify the healthcare provider instantly, and prepare for fluid resuscitation or emergency surgery.
