Paracentesis Abdominis

Introduction and Definition of Paracentesis Abdominis

Paracentesis abdominis is a routine clinical procedure performed to remove ascitic fluid from the peritoneal cavity for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. Ascites refers to the abnormal accumulation of serous fluid within the peritoneal cavity, frequently resulting from conditions such as portal hypertension, liver cirrhosis, peritonitis, or malignancy. In the context of nursing care in Nigerian hospitals, understanding this procedure is vital for managing patients presenting with severe abdominal distension, respiratory compromise due to diaphragmatic elevation, or spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. The procedure involves the insertion of a sterile needle or catheter through the abdominal wall into the peritoneal cavity under local anesthesia. As a nursing student, you must recognize that paracentesis can yield vital diagnostic fluid samples to identify infection, malignancy, or protein levels, while also serving as a rapid method to relieve discomfort in patients with tense, refractory ascites. Safety, strict aseptic technique, and comprehensive patient assessment form the cornerstone of effective nursing management before, during, and after the intervention.

Indications and Therapeutic Goals

The indications for paracentesis are broadly categorized into diagnostic and therapeutic goals. Diagnostic paracentesis is indicated for any patient presenting with new-onset ascites of unknown origin, suspected spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP), or ascites accompanied by fever, abdominal pain, or unexplained worsening of renal or hepatic function. Analysis of the peritoneal fluid helps determine the serum-ascites albumin gradient (SAAG), cell count, Gram stain, and culture. On the other hand, therapeutic paracentesis is indicated for patients with tense or massive ascites that causes severe abdominal pain, early satiety, or respiratory compromise due to pressure on the diaphragm. By removing large volumes of fluid, the therapeutic goal is to alleviate patient discomfort, improve pulmonary function, and restore mobility. Nurses must carefully evaluate the clinical objective of the procedure, as therapeutic large-volume paracentesis often requires concurrent intravenous administration of albumin to prevent post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction, maintaining hemodynamic stability throughout the intervention.

Contraindications and Risk Assessment

Before any paracentesis is performed, a rigorous risk assessment and evaluation of contraindications must be conducted by the multidisciplinary team, including the nurse. Absolute contraindications are rare but include an acute abdomen that requires immediate exploratory laparoscopy or surgery. Relative contraindications require extreme caution and include severe bleeding diathesis, uncorrected coagulopathy (such as a dangerously low platelet count or prolonged prothrombin time/INR), significant fibrinolysis, pregnancy, severe bowel distension, and prior abdominal wall surgery with suspected extensive intra-abdominal adhesions. Nurses must review the patient's most recent laboratory coagulation profile and platelet counts. Furthermore, the nurse should assess the abdomen for surgical scars, visible distended veins (caput medusae), or localized skin infections at the proposed puncture site, which could introduce pathogens into the peritoneal cavity. Conducting a thorough pre-procedure risk assessment ensures that potential complications like hemorrhage or bowel perforation are minimized.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Pre-procedure nursing responsibilities are vital to ensure patient safety, informed consent, and physical comfort. The nurse begins by verifying that informed consent has been properly obtained and documented by the physician. Next, the patient is educated about what to expect during the procedure, alleviating anxiety and ensuring cooperation. The nurse must instruct the patient to empty their urinary bladder immediately before the procedure to minimize the risk of accidental bladder perforation during needle insertion. Vital signs are recorded as a baseline. The patient is typically positioned in a semi-Fowler's position or sitting up slightly in bed, which allows fluid to pool dependently in the lower abdomen. The nurse exposes the abdominal area while maintaining patient dignity and inspects the site for any visible landmarks or contraindications. Finally, the nurse gathers and organizes all necessary equipment, ensuring that emergency suction and resuscitation tools are readily available nearby if the patient's condition unexpectedly destabilizes.

Equipment Needed and Sterile Technique

Execution of a paracentesis requires meticulous adherence to sterile technique to prevent iatrogenic peritonitis. The equipment needed includes a sterile paracentesis tray or kit containing antiseptic skin preparation solution (such as chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine), sterile drapes, local anesthetic (e.g., 1% lidocaine), needles (smaller gauge for local infiltration and a larger gauge needle or specialized angiocatheter for fluid withdrawal), syringes, specimen collection tubes, scalpel (if needed for a small skin nick), tubing, a collection bag or vacuum bottles, and sterile dressings. The nurse assists the physician by maintaining the sterile field, opening sterile supplies without contaminating them, and handing instruments as requested. The physician typically selects the insertion site in the lower quadrant (usually the left lower quadrant to avoid the cecum) or at the midline between the umbilicus and the pubis. Maintaining strict aseptic boundaries throughout setup and execution protects the vulnerable, immune-compromised patient from severe intra-abdominal infections.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Assistance and Patient Monitoring

During the procedure, the nurse's role shifts to continuous patient monitoring, supporting comfort, and assisting the medical officer. The nurse must closely observe the patient's facial expressions, level of consciousness, and verbal feedback for signs of pain, anxiety, or vasovagal symptoms such as dizziness, diaphoresis, nausea, or lightheadedness. Vital signs—including blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation—must be monitored and recorded at regular intervals, especially during large-volume paracentesis where rapid fluid shifts can precipitate hypotension. The nurse also monitors the flow of ascitic fluid, ensuring the catheter remains patent and securely positioned. If vacuum bottles or drainage bags fill up, the nurse assists in changing them aseptically. Should the patient experience severe sudden pain, shortness of breath, or sharp declines in blood pressure, the nurse must immediately notify the physician to pause or terminate the procedure.

Post-Procedure Care, Specimen Handling, and Documentation

Post-procedure care requires diligent nursing actions to ensure recovery and prevent complications. Once the needle or catheter is removed, the nurse applies a sterile pressure dressing over the puncture site and monitors it frequently for any persistent fluid leakage or bleeding. The patient should remain in a comfortable position, typically resting in bed for a specified period (e.g., 1 to 2 hours) to allow the puncture tract to seal. Baseline vital signs are reassessed and compared with pre-procedure values. If a large volume of ascitic fluid was removed, the nurse administers intravenous albumin or other prescribed volume expanders as ordered to prevent post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction. Specimen tubes must be properly labeled with the patient's details, requisition forms completed, and samples dispatched promptly to the laboratory. Finally, comprehensive documentation in the nursing care plan must include the total volume and appearance of fluid removed, patient tolerance, site condition, and post-procedure vital signs.

Potential Complications and Emergency Nursing Interventions

Although paracentesis is generally safe when performed correctly, nursing students must be vigilant regarding potential complications and emergency interventions. Major complications include persistent fluid leakage from the puncture site, subcutaneous hematoma, abdominal wall hemorrhage, accidental perforation of the intestine or urinary bladder, and peritonitis. Furthermore, rapid fluid withdrawal without adequate volume replacement can trigger circulatory collapse, renal failure, and hepatorenal syndrome. The nurse must perform ongoing assessments: checking the dressing for excessive serosanguinous drainage, palpating the abdomen for new tenderness or guarding indicative of bowel perforation, and monitoring urine output closely for signs of renal impairment. In the event of an emergency such as severe hemorrhage, profound hypotension, or signs of acute peritonitis (severe rebound tenderness, rigidity, and spiking fever), the nurse must immediately alert the physician, initiate emergency intravenous fluid resuscitation, secure emergency equipment, and prepare the patient for potential surgical or intensive care interventions.
