Paracentesis Abdominis

Introduction and Definition of Paracentesis Abdominis

Paracentesis abdominis is an invasive bedside or operative procedure involving the insertion of a needle or catheter into the peritoneal cavity to withdraw ascetic fluid. This fluid accumulation, known as ascites, frequently results from portal hypertension linked to chronic liver disease, malignancy, or heart failure. The procedure serves dual purposes: diagnostic evaluation of the fluid to determine the underlying etiology or rule out spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, and therapeutic relief of severe abdominal distension that compromises respiratory status and patient comfort. Understanding the peritoneal anatomy is critical for safe execution; clinicians typically target the lower quadrants, particularly the left lower quadrant, due to a thicker fluid layer and the avoidance of the cecum.

Indications and Therapeutic Goals

Clinical motivations for performing a paracentesis fall into two distinct categories: diagnostic and therapeutic. Diagnostic paracentesis is indicated for any patient presenting with new-onset ascites of unknown origin, or when hospitalized patients with existing ascites show clinical deterioration, fever, or worsening encephalopathy that suggests infection. Therapeutic paracentesis is reserved for individuals with large-volume ascites causing severe discomfort, early satiety, or respiratory compromise due to diaphragmatic elevation. The main therapeutic goal is immediate pressure relief and improvement of cardiopulmonary mechanics, rather than permanent disease correction.

Contraindications and Risk Assessment

Absolute contraindications for paracentesis are rare but include an acute abdomen requiring immediate exploratory laparotomy. Relative contraindications demand careful risk-benefit analysis and include severe coagulopathy, extensive abdominal wall cellulitis at the puncture site, significant bowel distension, and multiple previous abdominal surgeries with suspected adhesions. Severe thrombocytopenia or prolonged coagulation profiles should ideally be corrected with fresh frozen plasma or platelet transfusions prior to needle insertion, unless the procedure is an absolute emergency.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Thorough preparation prevents complications and alleviates patient anxiety. 1. **Consent**: Verify that a signed informed consent form is present in the patient chart. 2. **Bladder Emptying**: Instruct the patient to void immediately before the procedure to minimize the risk of accidental bladder perforation. 3. **Baseline Assessment**: Record vital signs, measure abdominal girth at the umbilicus, and obtain an accurate pre-procedure weight. 4. **Positioning**: Assist the patient into a semi-Fowler's position or supine with the head slightly elevated to allow fluid to pool dependently in the lower abdomen.

Equipment Needed and Sterile Technique

A successful paracentesis relies on strict aseptic technique and organized equipment staging. The nurse gathers a standard paracentesis tray, sterile gloves, surgical gowns, skin antiseptic solutions such as chlorhexidine, local anesthetic agents, needles of various sizes, a scalpel blade, a drainage catheter or needle-catheter assembly, three-way stopcocks, connecting tubing, vacuum bottles or collection bags, and specimen tubes. Maintaining a sterile field prevents introduction of bacteria into the peritoneal cavity, which could trigger secondary peritonitis.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Assistance and Patient Monitoring

During the procedure, the nurse acts as a circulating assistant and patient advocate. Responsibilities include reassuring the anxious patient, monitoring facial expressions and verbal feedback for signs of distress, and continuously tracking vital signs, particularly blood pressure and pulse. Rapid fluid shifts can precipitate hypovolemic shock or paracentesis-induced circulatory dysfunction. The nurse watches for signs of impending collapse, such as dizziness, diaphoresis, sudden tachycardia, or hypotension, and immediately alerts the physician if these indicators appear.

Post-Procedure Care, Specimen Handling, and Documentation

After catheter removal, apply a sterile pressure dressing over the puncture site and monitor it frequently for persistent oozing or frank fluid leakage. Position the patient comfortably, often lying on the opposite side to keep the site elevated and dry. Label and send collected fluid specimens to the laboratory promptly for cell count, differential, total protein, albumin, and culture. Finally, document the procedure details thoroughly: exact volume and appearance of drained fluid, patient tolerance, pre- and post-procedure vital signs, abdominal girth measurements, and any interventions performed.

Potential Complications and Emergency Nursing Interventions

Although generally safe, paracentesis carries risks that require vigilant monitoring. Persistent fluid leakage from the puncture site can lead to skin breakdown or secondary infection. Accidental laceration of an abdominal blood vessel may cause intra-abdominal bleeding or a hematoma. Bowel perforation, though rare, presents with severe abdominal pain, guarding, and rebound tenderness. Nurses must recognize these warning signs early, maintain intravenous access for fluid resuscitation, and ensure emergency surgical consultation is requested when catastrophic complications arise.
