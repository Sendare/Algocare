Liver Function Tests

Introduction and Core Purpose of LFTs

Liver function tests, commonly referred to as LFTs or a hepatic panel, are a group of blood tests that provide objective data regarding the status of the liver. Despite their traditional name, many of these assays do not actually measure how well the liver is performing its metabolic chores; instead, they detect cellular integrity, synthetic capacity, and biliary drainage. These panels are invaluable diagnostic tools utilized in routine wellness screenings, preoperative assessments, and the active monitoring of hepatotoxic pharmacological regimens. In busy clinical settings across Nigeria, from tertiary teaching hospitals to private laboratories, interpreting these biochemical markers allows clinicians to catch silent hepatic pathologies before clinical jaundice or ascites manifests.

Enzymes Indicating Hepatocellular Injury

When hepatocytes are damaged or inflamed, their cellular membranes become leaky, releasing intracellular enzymes into the bloodstream. Two primary enzymes dominate this category:

  • Alanine Aminotransferase (ALT): Highly concentrated within the cytoplasm of hepatocytes, making it a relatively specific marker for acute liver cell injury.
  • Aspartate Aminotransferase (AST): Found in high concentrations not only in the liver but also in cardiac muscle, skeletal muscle, kidneys, and red blood cells, rendering it less liver-specific than ALT.

In most viral or drug-induced hepatocellular injuries, ALT levels rise higher than AST. However, an AST-to-ALT ratio greater than 2:1 is strongly suggestive of alcoholic liver disease, a common pattern seen during clinical evaluations in Nigerian gastroenterology wards.

Markers of Cholestasis and Biliary Obstruction

Biliary stasis or obstruction prevents bile from flowing freely into the duodenum, causing specific canalicular and ductal enzymes to spill into circulation.

  • Alkaline Phosphatase (ALP): Found in the liver, bones, placenta, and intestines. Elevated levels strongly point toward biliary disease, though physiological bone growth in adolescents or pregnancy can also raise it.
  • Gamma-Glutamyl Transferase (GGT): An enzyme located in the cell membranes of the biliary tract. Because GGT is not elevated by bone disorders, it is used to confirm that an isolated high ALP level originates from the liver.

Assessing Synthetic Function: Bilirubin, Albumin, and Clotting Factors

Evaluating whether the liver is actively working involves measuring substances it produces or clears.

  • Albumin: The major plasma protein synthesized exclusively by hepatocytes. Chronic liver failure leads to low albumin, resulting in decreased oncotic pressure and peripheral edema or ascites.
  • Bilirubin: A breakdown product of heme metabolism. Unconjugated (indirect) bilirubin suggests hemolysis or Gilbert syndrome, whereas conjugated (direct) bilirubin points to biliary obstruction or hepatocellular dysfunction.
  • Prothrombin Time (PT) / INR: Measures coagulation factors produced by the liver. Because many of these clotting factors have very short half-lives, a rising INR is an acute indicator of failing synthetic capacity.

Clinical Indications for Ordering LFTs

Clinicians order liver panels across diverse clinical scenarios, ranging from routine physical examinations to emergency admissions. Common indications include investigating right upper quadrant abdominal pain, unexplained fatigue, persistent nausea, or symptoms of jaundice like scleral icterus and dark tea-colored urine. Furthermore, LFTs are mandatory baseline tests before initiating medications known for hepatotoxicity, such as antituberculosis drugs widely used in managing tuberculosis endemic in Nigeria. Regular surveillance is subsequently maintained to detect drug-induced liver injury early before irreversible necrosis occurs.

Pattern Recognition in Abnormal Results

Interpreting an LFT panel requires looking at enzyme patterns rather than viewing each value in isolation. A hepatocellular pattern is identified when elevations in ALT and AST are disproportionately higher than ALP elevations, typically pointing toward viral hepatitis, ischemic injury, or toxins. Conversely, a cholestatic pattern features a disproportionately high elevation of ALP and GGT compared to transaminases, indicating bile duct obstruction or intrahepatic cholestasis. Mixed patterns frequently appear in complex systemic illnesses, requiring correlation with abdominal ultrasound findings and patient history.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Nurses play a critical role in the pre-test, intra-test, and post-test phases of diagnostic blood draws. Proper preparation includes checking whether fasting is required, as heavy meals can transiently alter certain parameters. The nurse must verify patient identity, ensure correct vial selection, and apply venipuncture techniques that minimize hemolysis, since ruptured red blood cells falsely elevate AST and potassium levels. Post-procedure care involves applying pressure to the puncture site to prevent hematoma formation, especially in patients with compromised synthetic function and underlying coagulopathies.
