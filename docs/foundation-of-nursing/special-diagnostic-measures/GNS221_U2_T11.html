Liver Function Tests

Introduction to Liver Function Tests (LFTs)

Liver function tests, commonly known as LFTs, are a group of blood tests used to check how well the liver is working. They measure specific enzymes, proteins, and substances produced or processed by the liver. While called function tests, some of these markers actually show damage to liver cells rather than how well the organ performs its jobs. Nurses see LFT results often in medical and surgical wards. These tests help doctors spot liver disease, figure out the cause of jaundice, and track how well a patient is responding to treatments or medications.

Key Enzymes Indicating Hepatocellular Injury

When liver cells are inflamed or damaged, they leak specific enzymes into the bloodstream. The two main enzymes nurses look for are ALT and AST.

  • ALT (Alanine Aminotransferase): Found mostly inside liver cells. It is the most specific marker for liver cell injury.
  • AST (Aspartate Aminotransferase): Found in the liver, but also in the heart, skeletal muscles, kidneys, and brain. Because it is found in other tissues, it is less specific to the liver than ALT.

An extremely high spike in these enzymes usually points to acute damage, such as a severe viral hepatitis infection or an overdose of paracetamol.

Markers of Hepatic Synthesis and Excretion

Beyond cell damage, LFTs measure how well the liver builds important substances and clears waste products.

  • Albumin: The main protein made by the liver. Low levels suggest chronic liver disease because a damaged liver cannot synthesize enough protein over time.
  • Bilirubin: A yellow breakdown product of hemoglobin. The liver processes and excretes it into bile. When this process fails, bilirubin builds up in the blood, causing jaundice.
  • ALP (Alkaline Phosphatase): An enzyme found in the liver, bones, and bile ducts. It rises significantly when there is a blockage in the bile ducts, known as cholestasis.

Clinical Indications and Interpretation

Doctors order LFT panels for many reasons, including routine health checks, monitoring medications that affect the liver, and diagnosing unexplained fatigue, abdominal pain, or yellowing of the eyes. Interpreting these results requires looking at the pattern of elevation. For instance, an isolated rise in ALP points toward the biliary system, whereas soaring ALT and AST point directly to the liver parenchyma. Nurses must review these values alongside the patient's full clinical picture, symptoms, and medication history.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Nurses play a vital role in ensuring accurate LFT results and keeping patients safe during the diagnostic process. Before the blood draw, check if the patient needs to fast, as some panels require an empty stomach. Review the patient's medication chart, noting any drugs known to cause hepatotoxicity, such as certain antibiotics, statins, or high-dose paracetamol. After the blood draw, apply pressure to the puncture site and watch for bruising, especially in patients with advanced liver disease who may have coexisting coagulopathies due to poor clotting factor production.
