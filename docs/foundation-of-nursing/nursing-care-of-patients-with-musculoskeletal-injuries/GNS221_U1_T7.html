Care of Patients on Traction

Introduction and Purpose of Traction in Orthopedic Care

Traction involves the application of a pulling force to a specific part of the body to treat fractures, reduce muscle spasms, and correct deformities. In orthopedic wards across Nigeria, encountering patients with long bone fractures managed via traction is commonplace, especially where immediate surgical fixation is delayed or unavailable. The primary goals are realigning bone fragments, immobilizing the affected extremity, and resting the injured limb before definitive surgery or natural healing occurs.

  • Reduction: Restoring bone fragments to their normal anatomical alignment.
  • Immobilization: Preventing movement at the fracture site to promote undisturbed healing.
  • Spasm reduction: Relieving painful, involuntary muscle contractions surrounding the injury.

Types of Traction: Skin versus Skeletal Traction

Orthopedic traction is broadly divided into two main categories based on how the pulling force is applied to the body.

  1. Skin Traction: Force is applied directly to the skin via adhesive or non-adhesive tapes, foam boots, or slings. This method uses lighter weights, typically not exceeding 2 to 5 kilograms, to prevent skin breakdown, blistering, and shearing injuries. Common examples include Buck's extension used frequently for hip fractures.
  1. Skeletal Traction: Force is applied directly to the bone using surgically inserted metal pins, wires, or tongs (such as Steinmann pins or Kirschner wires). This allows much heavier weights—often 5 to 15 kilograms or more—to achieve effective reduction of severe fractures. Because pins pierce the skin and bone, this method carries a direct risk of infection.

Basic Principles of Effective Traction Maintenance

For traction to achieve its therapeutic goals, specific mechanical rules must be maintained rigorously around the clock. The fundamental rule is that weights must hang freely at all times and never touch the floor or rest on the bed frame. If weights touch the ground, the pulling force is immediately lost.

Ropes must sit securely within the grooves of the pulleys, unfrayed and unobstructed by bed linens or blankets. Counter-traction is equally essential; this is usually provided by the patient's own body weight, often facilitated by elevating the foot of the bed. Never alter, remove, or lift the weights without a direct medical order, as even brief interruptions can disrupt fracture alignment.

Nursing Assessment of the Patient in Traction

Comprehensive nursing assessment ensures that the traction is working safely without compromising neurovascular status or skin integrity. The nurse performs regular neurovascular checks—often remembered by the mnemonic of the 5 Ps: Pain, Pallor, Pulses, Paresthesia, and Paralysis—on the affected limb.

Frequent checks are vital because excessive pressure or tight bandages can compromise blood flow and nerve conduction, leading to compartment syndrome or permanent tissue damage. The skin surrounding straps, boots, or skeletal pins must be inspected for pressure ulcers, redness, or signs of irritation during every shift change.

Routine Pin Site Care for Skeletal Traction

Skeletal traction involves metal pins passing directly through the skin and into the bone, creating a direct pathway for microorganisms to enter and cause osteomyelitis or localized soft tissue infection. Routine pin site care is therefore a sterile, non-negotiable nursing duty.

  • Frequency: Performed according to hospital protocol, usually every shift or whenever dressings become soiled.
  • Solution: Typically cleaned using sterile normal saline or chlorhexidine swabs, working in a circular motion outward from the pin.
  • Observation: Inspect daily for purulent drainage, increasing redness, warmth, foul odor, or loose pins.

Prevention of Immobilization Complications

Prolonged bed rest and restricted movement place patients in traction at high risk for systemic complications. Nurses must actively implement preventative measures across multiple body systems.

  • Respiratory: Encourage deep breathing exercises and use of incentive spirometry every hour to prevent atelectasis and hypostatic pneumonia.
  • Circulatory: Apply anti-embolism stockings, encourage ankle pumps, and administer prophylactic anticoagulants to prevent deep vein thrombosis (DVT).
  • Integumentary: Reposition uncompromised areas carefully, use pressure-relieving mattresses, and keep skin clean and dry to ward off pressure injuries.
  • Gastrointestinal/Renal: Promote high fiber intake, adequate fluid hydration, and stool softeners to combat constipation and urinary stasis.

Patient Education and Psychosocial Support

Living immobile in traction can trigger feelings of helplessness, frustration, and anxiety, particularly given long hospital stays often seen in resource-constrained settings. Effective nursing care extends beyond physical maintenance to encompass emotional reassurance and clear communication.

explain the daily routine, reassure the patient regarding the temporary nature of the traction equipment, and involve family members in bedside care where appropriate. Encourage the patient to participate in upper body exercises and keep personal items within arm's reach to foster a sense of independence and control.
