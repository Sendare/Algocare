Care of Patients on Traction

Introduction and Purpose of Traction in Orthopedic Care

Traction is a fundamental therapeutic modality utilized in orthopedic nursing care. It involves the application of a pulling force to a specific part of the body to attain several crucial clinical objectives. The primary purpose of traction is to reduce fractures, meaning to align fractured bone ends properly so that proper healing and bone union can take place. Additionally, traction is highly effective in relieving muscle spasms that frequently accompany severe bone injuries and fractures. By stretching the affected muscle groups, the nurse helps mitigate severe pain and prevents further tissue trauma caused by bone fragments grinding against each other. Furthermore, traction plays an essential role in correcting or preventing skeletal deformities, correcting muscle contractures, and resting an inflamed or injured joint prior to surgical intervention. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where patients may present late with complicated fractures due to delayed access or reliance on traditional bone setters, understanding traction is vital for student nurses. The application of traction requires careful calculation of weights, continuous monitoring, and an acute understanding of biomechanics to ensure that the pulling force is therapeutically effective without causing harm to the patient's skin, nerves, or vascular structures. Nurses act as the primary monitors of the traction apparatus, ensuring that the therapeutic alignment is maintained around the clock.

Types of Traction: Skin versus Skeletal Traction

Traction is broadly categorized into two main types: skin traction and skeletal traction, each with distinct indications, applications, and nursing management requirements. Skin traction involves applying a pulling force directly to the patient's skin through the use of adhesive or non-adhesive tapes, foam boots, or slings. The weights are then attached to these materials. Common examples include Buck's extension traction, frequently used temporarily for hip fractures before surgery, and Russell's traction. Because skin traction relies on the skin's integrity, it is generally used with lower weight amounts—typically between 2.3 to 4.5 kilograms—to prevent skin breakdown and shearing injuries. Furthermore, it is contraindicated in patients with skin infections, abrasions, or severe vascular disease. In contrast, skeletal traction is applied directly to the patient's bone using metal pins, wires, or tongs, such as Kirschner wires or Steinman pins, which are surgically inserted through the bone. This method allows for significantly heavier weights, usually 7 to 11 kilograms or more, to effectively reduce complex fractures or severe spinal and cervical injuries over longer durations. Skeletal traction requires rigorous pin-site care and continuous monitoring because the hardware creates a direct pathway for bacteria into the bone tissue, posing a high risk for osteomyelitis. Student nurses must clearly distinguish between the two types to provide safe, targeted care.

Basic Principles of Effective Traction Maintenance

Maintaining effective traction requires strict adherence to basic mechanical and clinical principles to ensure safety and therapeutic success. The most critical principle is that traction must remain continuous and uninterrupted to achieve proper bone alignment and muscle relaxation. Weights must hang freely at all times and must never be removed, lifted, or allowed to rest on the bed, floor, or any equipment, as any interruption disrupts the pulling force and delays healing. Furthermore, the ropes associated with the traction apparatus must be unobstructed, centered within the grooves of the pulleys, and free from knots or fraying that could cause friction or sudden slippage. Another vital principle is maintaining the patient's proper body alignment in direct line with the pull of the traction. The nurse must ensure that the patient does not slip down in bed or turn improperly, which could alter the line of pull and cause counter-traction failure. Counter-traction is the opposing force—often generated by the patient's own body weight or specialized positioning—necessary to make the traction effective. If a patient slides down and their feet touch the foot of the bed, or if weights touch the floor, counter-traction is lost, and the traction becomes ineffective. Nurses must perform regular, systematic checks of the entire traction setup during every shift to guarantee optimal mechanical functioning.

Nursing Assessment of the Patient in Traction

Comprehensive nursing assessment of a patient in traction is essential for detecting early complications and monitoring the overall effectiveness of the therapy. The nursing assessment begins with a focused neurovascular evaluation of the affected limb, frequently remembered by the mnemonic '5 Ps': Pain, Pallor, Pulses, Paresthesia, and Paralysis. The nurse must assess peripheral pulses distal to the traction site, evaluate capillary refill time, check skin temperature, and ask the patient about any numbness, tingling, or burning sensations, which may indicate nerve compression or compromised blood flow due to tight bandages or excessive weight. Additionally, the nurse must assess the skin for integrity issues, particularly over bony prominences such as the heels, sacrum, elbows, and areas under skin traction straps. Pain assessment is crucial; while some discomfort from the fracture is expected, sudden, severe, or unmanaged pain—especially pain unrelieved by prescribed analgesics or pain that worsens with passive movement of the fingers or toes—can signal acute compartment syndrome, a surgical emergency. The nurse also inspects the mechanical integrity of the traction apparatus itself, checking pulley ropes, weight positions, and bed attachments. Regular documentation and prompt reporting of abnormal findings to the attending physician or orthopedic surgeon prevent irreversible tissue damage and promote rapid recovery.

Routine Pin Site Care for Skeletal Traction

Routine pin site care is a critical nursing responsibility for patients undergoing skeletal traction to prevent localized infections that can easily progress to osteomyelitis or systemic sepsis. Pins, wires, or tongs pierce the skin and enter the bone, creating a permanent portal of entry for microorganisms. Nursing care must follow strict aseptic technique protocols as prescribed by institutional policy. Generally, pin sites should be cleaned at regular intervals—such as once every shift or daily—using sterile saline or a prescribed antiseptic solution, depending on hospital guidelines. The nurse must use a separate sterile cotton-tipped applicator or gauze for each pin site to prevent cross-contamination between sites. Cleaning should start near the pin and move outward in a circular motion. The nurse inspects each pin site for classic signs of infection and inflammation, including purulent or foul-smelling drainage, skin erythema, localized warmth, swelling, and increasing patient tenderness. It is also normal to observe a small amount of serosanguineous drainage or crusting during the initial days post-insertion; however, any sudden increase in drainage or loose pins must be reported immediately. The nurse should never adjust or push protruding pin ends back into place and must ensure protective caps are placed over sharp pin tips to prevent patient injury or snagging on bed linens.

Prevention of Immobilization Complications

Patients in traction face prolonged immobility, which places them at high risk for multiple secondary complications affecting nearly every body system. Respiratory complications, such as atelectasis and hypostatic pneumonia, can occur due to restricted chest expansion and shallow breathing; the nurse must encourage deep breathing and coughing exercises every two hours, along with the use of an incentive spirometer. Cardiovascular complications, notably deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), arise from venous stasis caused by muscle inactivity. Prevention strategies include applying graduated compression stockings, administering prophylactic anticoagulants as ordered, and encouraging active range-of-motion exercises for unaffected limbs. Gastrointestinal complications like constipation are common due to decreased bowel motility from opioids and inactivity; nurses should promote high-fiber diets, adequate fluid intake, and stool softeners. Urinary stasis and urinary tract infections can develop due to incomplete bladder emptying in a supine position, making adequate hydration essential. Finally, pressure injuries (bedsores) over bony prominences are a constant threat due to sustained pressure and friction; regular repositioning within the limits of traction, use of pressure-relieving mattresses, and meticulous skin hygiene are mandatory. Early identification and proactive nursing interventions significantly reduce morbidity in immobile orthopedic patients.

Patient Education and Psychosocial Support

Patient education and psychosocial support are essential components of holistic orthopedic nursing care for individuals undergoing traction. Being confined to bed with restricted movement can lead to profound feelings of helplessness, anxiety, depression, and sensory deprivation. The nurse plays a pivotal role in establishing therapeutic communication, encouraging patients to express their fears regarding their recovery, potential permanent disability, and prolonged hospitalization. Explaining every nursing procedure before performing it helps reduce anxiety and fosters patient cooperation. Furthermore, patient and family education should begin early and cover the purpose of traction, the importance of maintaining alignment, and how to participate in their own care safely—such as using the overhead trapeze bar to lift the body slightly for bedpan placement or skin hygiene, provided the traction line of pull is not compromised. Nurses should also instruct patients and caregivers on how to recognize early warning signs of complications, such as numbness, coldness, or unusual pain in the limb, and when to notify the healthcare team immediately. In Nigerian nursing practice, involving family caregivers in education is especially important, as families often assist with daily feeding, hygiene, and emotional encouragement during hospital stays. Providing reassurance and maintaining a supportive environment greatly enhances the patient's coping mechanisms and overall rehabilitation outcomes.
