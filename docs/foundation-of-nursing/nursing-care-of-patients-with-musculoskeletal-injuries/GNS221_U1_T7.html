Care of Patients on Traction

Purpose and Types of Orthopedic Traction

Traction is the application of a pulling force to a part of the body to treat fractures, reduce muscle spasms, and correct skeletal deformities. By stretching the soft tissues and pulling bones back into alignment, traction helps rest injured joints and prepares limbs for surgical repair or final casting. There are two primary categories used in clinical practice:

  • Skin traction: Applies pulling force directly to the skin using adhesive tape, foam boots, or straps. This method uses lighter weights, usually under five kilograms, to prevent skin breakdown. It is frequently used short-term to control muscle spasms before hip fracture surgery, such as with Buck's traction.
  • Skeletal traction: Uses metal pins, wires, or tongs inserted surgically directly into the bone. Heavier weights, often five to fifteen kilograms, are applied over longer periods to overcome strong muscle pulls in complex fractures, such as those of the femur or cervical spine.

Fundamental Principles of Traction Maintenance

Keeping traction effective requires strict adherence to mechanical rules. If traction is compromised, healing slows and deformities can return. Nurses must routinely monitor the setup to ensure the system remains uninterrupted.

  1. Maintain continuous pull: Weights must never be removed, lifted, or allowed to rest on the floor or bed frame. Interrupting the pull disrupts bone alignment and triggers immediate muscle spasms.
  2. Ensure free-hanging weights: Weights must hang freely clear of the bed, footboard, or any equipment at all times.
  3. Check ropes and pulleys: Ropes must ride smoothly in the center of the pulley grooves without fraying or catching on knots against the frame.
  4. Maintain proper body alignment: The patient's body must stay in a straight line with the pull of the traction to prevent counterproductive angles.

Nursing Assessment of the Patient in Traction

Comprehensive daily assessment focuses heavily on the neurovascular status of the affected limb. Compromised blood flow or nerve damage can develop quickly due to swelling or improper equipment positioning.

  • Circulation check: Assess skin color, temperature, peripheral pulses, and capillary refill time in the toes or fingers of the affected extremity.
  • Sensation and movement: Ask the patient to wiggle their digits and report any numbness, tingling, or burning sensations.
  • Skin integrity: Inspect pressure points over bony prominences, especially the heels, sacrum, and areas where skin straps or boots apply pressure.
  • Pain evaluation: Distinguish between normal baseline ache from the fracture and sharp, ischemic pain caused by pressure or tight bandages.

Routine Pin Site Care for Skeletal Traction

Skeletal traction involves metal pins passing through the skin and soft tissue directly into the bone. This creates an open pathway for bacteria, making infection control a top priority throughout the treatment period.

  • Infection risk: Pin site care prevents osteomyelitis, a serious bone infection. Nurses must observe the area daily for redness, warmth, swelling, purulent drainage, and severe localized tenderness.
  • Cleaning technique: Use sterile technique according to hospital policy, typically utilizing sterile normal saline or chlorhexidine swabs. Clean in a circular motion moving outward from the pin site.
  • Crust removal: Gentle removal of dried exudate around the pin is necessary because trapped crusts can harbor bacteria beneath the surface.
  • Loose pins: Never adjust or push loose pins. Notify the attending orthopedic surgeon immediately if hardware appears unstable.

Prevention of Prolonged Immobilization Complications

Patients in traction face extended bed rest, which places them at high risk for widespread systemic complications. Nursing interventions must target multiple body systems simultaneously to keep the patient safe.

  • Respiratory care: Encourage deep breathing and coughing exercises every hour using an incentive spirometer to prevent atelectasis and hypostatic pneumonia.
  • Thromboembolism prevention: Apply sequential compression devices, administer ordered anticoagulants, and encourage active ankle pumps to lower the risk of deep vein thrombosis.
  • Skin protection: Turn the patient slightly or shift weight using specialized mattresses and repositioning sheets while keeping the traction alignment intact.
  • Bowel and bladder health: Provide high-fiber diets, adequate hydration, and stool softeners to prevent painful immobilization-induced constipation.

Patient Education and Psychosocial Support

Coping with the restrictions of traction can cause frustration, anxiety, and feelings of helplessness. Because patients are confined to a specific position for weeks, addressing their mental well-being is just as important as managing their physical injuries.

  • Clear communication: Explain every aspect of the traction equipment so the patient understands why movement is restricted and what sounds or sensations to expect.
  • Environment adaptation: Place personal items, call bells, and entertainment devices within easy reach to promote a sense of independence.
  • Emotional reassurance: Validate the patient's frustration with immobility and encourage visits or communication from friends and family.
  • Pain management education: Teach patients to communicate pain levels promptly so nurses can balance comfort needs with safe activity participation.
