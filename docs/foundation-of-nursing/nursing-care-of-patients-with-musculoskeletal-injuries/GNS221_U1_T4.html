Physiotherapy in Nursing Care

Introduction to Physiotherapy in Nursing

Physiotherapy in nursing focuses on restoring physical function, preventing disability, and maximizing independence for individuals recovering from illness, surgery, or trauma. While physical therapists design specialized rehabilitation regimens, nurses maintain continuous bedside contact, translating these movement goals into daily ward routines. Bedside care requires constant evaluation of how physical movement impacts vital signs, pain levels, and overall recovery. In many Nigerian healthcare settings where dedicated therapy staff may be unavailable on every shift, nurses shoulder the responsibility of initiating basic range-of-motion routines and early mobilization to prevent hospital-acquired complications like deep vein thrombosis and pressure injuries.

Goals and Objectives of Therapeutic Exercises

Therapeutic exercises are systematically planned physical movements designed to address specific functional deficits. The primary goals include improving joint flexibility, building muscular strength, enhancing cardiovascular endurance, and re-educating neuromuscular pathways. - **Passive Range of Motion (PROM)**: Performed entirely by the nurse or therapist for patients who cannot move a limb independently, maintaining joint flexibility without patient exertion. - **Active-Assistive Range of Motion**: The patient performs the movement with mechanical or manual assistance from the nurse to support weak muscle groups. - **Active Range of Motion**: The patient executes the movement independently using their own muscular strength, promoting endurance and tone. - **Resistive Exercises**: Movement performed against an opposing force to stimulate significant muscle hypertrophy and strength gains.

Types of Physiotherapy Modalities in Patient Care

Beyond physical movement, physiotherapy encompasses various physical modalities that aid pain management and tissue healing. Superficial thermal agents, such as hot packs, promote vasodilation and relax tight muscle spasms prior to stretching exercises. Conversely, cryotherapy utilizes cold application to induce local vasoconstriction, effectively reducing acute inflammation and numbing sharp pain following orthopedic trauma or soft tissue injury. Electrical stimulation units, including transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), interfere with pain signal transmission to the spinal cord, offering a non-pharmacological adjunct for chronic pain relief. Hydrotherapy leverages buoyancy and hydrostatic pressure to facilitate low-impact movement for patients suffering from severe arthritis or neurological deficits.

The Nurse's Role in Assisting with Mobility and Ambulation

Assisting patients out of bed requires careful mechanical assessment and adherence to safe ergonomic principles to protect both the patient and the nursing staff. Before attempting ambulation, the nurse must assess baseline orthostatic tolerance by having the patient sit at the edge of the bed for a few moments while monitoring for dizziness, pallor, or significant blood pressure drops. Gait belts should be utilized for unsteady individuals to provide a secure physical anchor. When walking with an assistive device such as a walker or crutches, the nurse walks slightly behind and to the weaker side of the patient, ready to provide structural support if balance fails.

Positioning and Preventing Musculoskeletal Complications

Immobility wreaks havoc on the musculoskeletal system, precipitating muscle atrophy, joint contractures, and bone demineralization within a remarkably short timeframe. Nurses prevent these debilitating shifts through meticulous repositioning schedules, turning bedbound individuals at least every two hours. Proper anatomical alignment is mandatory; trochanter rolls prevent external hip rotation, while footboards combat plantar flexion contractures commonly referred to as foot drop. Pillows placed strategically between bony prominences protect integrity, while splints or specialized boots maintain functional resting postures for compromised joints.

Collaborative Care: Working with Physical Therapists

Optimal rehabilitative outcomes depend on seamless interprofessional communication between nurses and physical therapists. While physical therapists perform formal diagnostic assessments of gait, balance, and strength, nurses observe how these functional capacities translate into 24-hour self-care activities such as feeding, bathing, and toileting. Establishing a unified care plan requires regular verbal handover and documented progress notes shared across the healthcare team. When a nurse notes unexpected fatigue, pain escalation, or neurological decline, prompt communication with the physical therapy department ensures the exercise regimen is safely modified rather than blindly executed.

Patient Education and Safe Discharge Planning

Preparing a patient for return to the home environment requires comprehensive education regarding safe movement, home hazard identification, and energy conservation techniques. Nurses must instruct both the patient and their family caregivers on proper body mechanics during transfers, correct utilization of assistive devices like canes or crutches, and the importance of adhering to prescribed home exercise regimens. Discharge planning must also address environmental safety by identifying potential fall risks such as loose rugs, poor lighting, or absent handrails in bathrooms. Providing clear, written instructions empowers patients to maintain their physical independence and reduces the likelihood of preventable hospital readmissions.
