Physiotherapy in Nursing Care

Introduction to Physiotherapy in Nursing Care

Physiotherapy and nursing work hand in hand to restore a patient's physical independence and prevent complications of bed rest. While physical therapists design specific rehabilitation programs, nurses spend the most time with the patient, integrating these movements into daily routines. Understanding basic physical therapy principles allows a nurse to reinforce exercises during morning care, transfers, and walks. Without this daily reinforcement, patients quickly lose muscle strength and joint flexibility, especially during extended hospital stays.

Goals and Objectives of Therapeutic Exercises

Therapeutic exercises are systematic body movements prescribed to recover or maintain physical function. The core goals include improving muscle tone, increasing joint range of motion, enhancing cardiovascular endurance, and preventing contractures.

  • Active exercises: Performed independently by the patient to build strength and coordination.
  • Active-assistive exercises: The patient moves the limb with help from the nurse or therapist due to weakness.
  • Passive exercises: The nurse or therapist moves the patient's joints through their range of motion because the patient cannot move at all.

Physiotherapy Modalities in Patient Care

Beyond physical exercises, various physical modalities are used to manage pain, reduce inflammation, and promote tissue healing. Nurses often assist in preparing patients for these treatments or monitoring their skin and comfort levels afterward.

Assisting with Mobility and Ambulation

Getting a patient out of bed is a critical nursing intervention that prevents deep vein thrombosis, pneumonia, and pressure injuries. Before attempting ambulation, the nurse must assess the patient's vital signs, balance, and level of dizziness.

Always use a gait belt for safety during transfers. Stand on the patient's weaker side if they need support, but allow them to use assistive devices like walkers or canes correctly. Ensure the device is fitted to the patient's height so their elbows bend at a comfortable angle of about twenty to thirty degrees.

Positioning and Preventing Musculoskeletal Complications

Proper body alignment and regular position changes are essential for immobile patients. Without intervention, joints settle into permanent flexion, known as contractures, and pressure ulcers develop over bony prominences.

Use pillows, trochanter rolls, and footboards to maintain neutral alignment. A footboard prevents foot drop by holding the ankles at a ninety-degree angle. Reposition patients at least every two hours, checking skin color and temperature over pressure points during every turn.

Collaborative Care with Physical Therapists

Patient recovery relies on clear communication between nurses and physical therapists. While physical therapists evaluate complex movement deficits and establish rehabilitation targets, nurses observe how well the patient carries out these skills throughout the rest of the day.

If a patient complains of severe pain during therapy or shows sudden fatigue, the nurse must communicate this feedback before the next session. Collaborative care ensures that safety precautions, such as weight-bearing limits, are consistently enforced by all members of the healthcare team.

Patient Education and Safe Discharge Planning

Preparation for home care begins on the day of admission. Nurses and physical therapists must educate both the patient and their family members on safe movement techniques, home hazard removal, and the correct use of assistive devices.

Teach family members how to assist with transfers without injuring their own backs. Review red-flag symptoms such as sudden shortness of breath, severe joint pain, or dizziness during activity. Ensure the home environment is free of loose rugs and cluttered pathways before the patient arrives home.
