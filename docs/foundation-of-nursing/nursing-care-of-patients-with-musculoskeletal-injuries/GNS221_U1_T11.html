Skull Traction

Introduction and Definition of Skull Traction

Skull traction is a specialized orthopedic method used to align, stabilize, and reduce fractures or dislocations of the cervical spine. By applying a continuous pulling force directly to the skull using metal pins or tongs, healthcare providers can safely restore the normal anatomical position of the vertebrae and relieve pressure on the spinal cord. This sustained pulling counters the heavy weight of the head and the spastic contractions of neck muscles that pull damaged bones out of place.

Unlike simple soft cervical collars, skull traction provides rigid, skeletal-level control. Weights are attached to a rope running through a pulley system, creating a mechanical advantage that keeps the spine straight and immobile. This setup is crucial during the acute phase of spinal trauma before surgical fusion or long-term bracing can be safely performed.

Indications and Clinical Purposes

Medical teams use skull traction primarily for patients who have suffered traumatic cervical spine fractures or dislocations. When a vertebra breaks or slips out of place, it can dangerously narrow the spinal canal and compress the spinal cord, threatening permanent paralysis. Traction pulls the bones apart gently, creating space inside the canal to relieve this dangerous pressure.

Beyond treating acute trauma, surgeons occasionally use traction before an operation to gradually stretch tight ligaments and make surgical realignment easier. It acts as a bridge therapy, buying precious time to stabilize other life-threatening injuries before the patient goes to the operating theater for definitive spinal stabilization.

Types of Skull Traction Devices and Equipment

Several types of devices are used to achieve skeletal traction, each featuring metal pins that insert directly into the outer layer of the skull bone (the cranium).

  • Gardner-Wells Tongs: The most common device, featuring spring-loaded pins that are tightened to a specific pressure level on opposite sides of the scalp.
  • Crutchfield Tongs: An older style of tongs secured into the skull with screws that require regular tightening to prevent loosening.
  • halo vest rings: A circular metal ring secured to the skull with four pins, which can then be attached to a plastic vest to allow safe out-of-bed mobility.

All these devices connect to ropes, pulleys, and hanging weight bags. The equipment setup must remain completely unobstructed so the weights hang freely in the air at all times.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Patient Education

Before the doctor places skull traction, thorough preparation helps reduce patient anxiety and prevents procedural complications. Nurses must explain the procedure in simple, reassuring terms, making sure the conscious patient understands that the metal pins will puncture the scalp, but local anesthesia will numb the area first.

Consent must be verified, baseline neurological assessments documented, and baseline vital signs recorded. The nurse should gather the traction kit, local anesthetic, cleansing solution, and the exact weight bags ordered by the physician. Shaving a small area of the scalp where the pins will enter is often done to keep the site clean and visible.

Ongoing Nursing Assessment and Traction Maintenance

Once skull traction is in place, maintaining the integrity of the system is a top priority for patient safety. Nurses must check the entire apparatus every shift to ensure the ropes run smoothly through the pulleys, the knots are secure, and the weights hang completely free above the floor. Never lift, remove, or alter the traction weights unless specifically ordered by the physician, as even a momentary loss of tension can cause the fractured vertebrae to slip and damage the spinal cord.

Simultaneously, neurological checks must be performed frequently. Assess the patient's motor function by asking them to wiggle their toes or squeeze your hands, and test sensation by gently touching their arms and legs. Report any new numbness, tingling, or weakness immediately.

Pin Site Care and Prevention of Infection

Because skull traction pins pierce the skin and enter the bone, they create a direct pathway for bacteria to cause local scalp infections or dangerous osteomyelitis. Strict aseptic technique is required during all pin site care. Nurses should inspect the sites every shift for signs of infection, such as redness, warmth, swelling, foul odor, or purulent drainage.

Cleansing protocols usually involve sterile normal saline or a prescribed antiseptic solution, gently removing any crusts around the metal pin. Never forcefully remove crusts, as they act as a natural protective barrier. Always check hospital policy on whether protective ointment or dry sterile dressings should be applied afterward.

Complications and Emergency Interventions

Skull traction carries serious potential complications that demand constant vigilance from the nursing team. Pin slippage or loosening can cause sudden loss of spinal alignment, while excessive weight can over-distract the vertebrae, tearing spinal ligaments and worsening nerve damage. Skin breakdown on the back of the head, shoulders, and heels is also common because the patient must remain flat on their back.

In the event of an acute respiratory emergency or cardiac arrest, the nurse must know how to handle the traction safely. Never remove the traction weights during resuscitation unless specifically authorized by protocol or a physician, because moving the neck without traction can sever the spinal cord. Always keep an emergency Allen wrench or pin tightening tool taped to the head of the bed for halo devices.
