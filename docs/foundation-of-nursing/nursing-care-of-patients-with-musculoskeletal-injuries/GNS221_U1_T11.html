Skull Traction

Introduction and Purpose of Skull Traction

Skull traction is an orthopedic intervention designed to realign and immobilize fractures or dislocations of the cervical spine. By applying a continuous longitudinal pulling force directly to the skull, this method reduces vertebral displacement, relieves pressure on the spinal cord, and restores spinal alignment. In Nigerian tertiary health institutions managing trauma cases from road traffic accidents, skull traction serves as a critical bridge between acute injury stabilization and definitive surgical fusion or conservative halo-vest immobilization.

Types of Skull Traction Devices

  • Gardner-Wells Tongs: Features spring-loaded pins that are inserted into the outer table of the skull. A calibrated indicator shows when the correct pressure is achieved.
  • Crutchfield Tongs: Requires small burr holes to be drilled into the skull outer table before insertion of the tongs. Less commonly used today due to the availability of simpler devices.
  • Halo Ring Systems: A circular metal ring secured to the skull using four pins, connected via vertical rods to a thoracic vest, allowing for early mobilization.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Preparation

Before the physician applies skull traction, the nurse must prepare both the equipment and the patient. Informed consent is secured, and baseline neurological assessments are thoroughly documented to establish a reference point for future comparisons. Baseline vital signs and pain scores are recorded. The patient and their family receive clear explanations regarding the procedure, local anesthesia use, and the absolute requirement to keep the head and neck in strict neutral alignment. Scalp hair near the planned insertion sites is clipped short to maintain a clean working field.

Nursing Assistance During Application

During the actual application of skull traction, the nurse supports the physician and monitors the patient closely.

  1. Positioning: Maintain the patient in a flat, supine position with the head aligned straight.
  2. Support: Support the patient's head manually in neutral alignment while the physician places the local anesthetic and secures the device.
  3. Observation: Watch for signs of vasovagal response, such as sudden bradycardia or hypotension, due to pain or anxiety during pin placement.
  4. Weight Attachment: Gently apply the prescribed weights to the traction rope once secured, ensuring the weights hang completely free without touching the floor or bed frame.

Ongoing Traction Maintenance and Alignment

Maintaining effective traction requires unyielding vigilance. The line of pull must remain straight, aligned with the long axis of the spinal column, preventing any rotation or lateral bending of the neck. Weights must remain untouched unless specifically ordered by the physician; under no circumstances should a nurse lift or remove the weights during routine nursing care, such as bed baths or linen changes. All pulleys, ropes, and knots must be inspected during every shift change to verify that ropes move smoothly in their tracks without fraying or catching.

Pin Site Care and Infection Control

Skull traction pins create a direct portal of entry for microorganisms from the skin into deeper tissues, making meticulous pin site care non-negotiable. Nurses perform pin site cleaning according to institutional protocol, typically using sterile normal saline or chlorhexidine solution to gently remove crusting. Any loose pins must be reported immediately; nurses should never attempt to tighten pin screws themselves. Signs of infection include purulent drainage, increasing local pain, warmth, erythema, and a foul odor.

Complications and Emergency Interventions

Complications of skull traction range from hardware failure to catastrophic neurological deterioration. Respiratory depression can occur if high cervical spine edema ascends or if alignment is compromised. Pin slippage or detachment requires immediate manual stabilization of the head and neck in neutral alignment while summoning the medical team. Skin breakdown on the occiput or back from immobility demands proactive pressure relief strategies that do not compromise spinal stability.
