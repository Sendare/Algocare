Passive Range of Motion Exercise

Introduction and Definition of Passive Range of Motion (PROM)

Passive Range of Motion (PROM) is a fundamental nursing and physiotherapy intervention defined as the movement of a joint through its complete available range by an external force, without any active exertion or muscular contraction by the patient. In the context of nursing education in Nigeria, understanding PROM is vital for managing immobilized patients across various healthcare settings, from busy tertiary teaching hospitals to primary health care facilities. When a patient is unable to move independently due to profound weakness, paralysis, sedation, or unconsciousness, the nurse steps in to provide the necessary mechanical assistance. The primary driving force behind PROM is the caregiver or a mechanical device, meaning the patient's own skeletal muscles remain completely relaxed throughout the procedure. This intervention serves as a cornerstone of nursing care aimed at preserving joint integrity and preventing the severe musculoskeletal complications associated with prolonged bed rest. By systematically moving each limb through flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and rotation, the nurse maintains the physiological elasticity of muscles, tendons, and ligaments. It is important for student nurses to distinguish PROM from active or active-assistive range of motion, as the absence of patient muscle contraction means PROM will not build muscle strength or endurance, but rather focuses strictly on joint mobility, circulation enhancement, and the prevention of disabling contractures that can permanently limit a patient's functional recovery.

Purposes and Clinical Indications for PROM Exercises

The clinical indications for Passive Range of Motion (PROM) exercises are centered around patients who are physically unable or clinically restricted from moving their own body parts. In Nigerian clinical practice, common scenarios include stroke patients with hemiplegia, individuals in intensive care units who are comatose or heavily sedated, trauma patients in traction, and clients with severe generalized debility or spinal cord injuries. The primary purpose of PROM is to maintain joint and soft tissue integrity, thereby minimizing the risk of severe joint contractures and shortening of periarticular connective tissues. Furthermore, PROM significantly aids in enhancing local blood circulation and venous return through the milking action of muscles and movement of joints, which subsequently decreases the risk of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pressure injuries. It also assists in maintaining the patient's joint awareness and tactile stimulation, helping to preserve the neurological pathways between the joints and the brain. By routinely performing these movements, nurses help decrease cartilage degeneration and synovial fluid stasis within the joint capsules, ensuring that when the patient eventually regains voluntary movement, the joints remain supple, flexible, and ready to support active rehabilitation efforts without structural limitations.

Contraindications and Precautions in Nigerian Clinical Practice

While Passive Range of Motion is an essential nursing intervention, student nurses practicing in Nigerian hospitals must be acutely aware of its contraindications and safety precautions to prevent patient harm. PROM is strictly contraindicated immediately following acute fractures, severe soft tissue trauma, ligamentous tears, or joint dislocations until stabilization and medical clearance are confirmed by the attending physician or surgeon. Forcing movement in these acute situations can exacerbate bleeding, disrupt healing bone fragments, and cause severe patient distress. Additionally, precautions must be taken in patients with severe osteoporosis, hemophilia, bony ankylosis, or localized infection and inflammation around the joint. In local clinical settings where resources may sometimes be constrained, nurses must carefully review the patient's case notes and collaborate with physiotherapists before initiating exercises on clients with unexplained pain or suspected deep vein thrombosis. If a nurse encounters sharp pain, resistance, muscle spasm, or bone-on-bone blockage during the procedure, the exercise must be stopped immediately. Recognizing these danger signs prevents iatrogenic injuries, protects fragile tissues, and ensures that nursing care remains safe, evidence-based, and tailored to the unique clinical condition of every patient.

General Principles and Guidelines for Safe Execution

Executing Passive Range of Motion safely requires adherence to established biomechanical and nursing principles. Before beginning, the nurse must ensure proper body mechanics for themselves by adjusting the bed height to a comfortable working level and positioning the patient in a comfortable, supine or dorsal position with adequate privacy maintained through screens or curtains. Proper support of the extremity is paramount; the nurse should cup the joints with their hands, providing proximal and distal support to prevent shearing forces and hyperextension. Movements should be executed smoothly, rhythmically, and slowly, repeating each motion typically five to ten times per session or as prescribed in the care plan. Each joint should be moved only within its available, pain-free physiological range, never forcing a joint past the point of natural tissue resistance. Communication with the patient, even if they are unconscious or obtunded, is an essential professional standard; the nurse should explain what is being done before touching the limb. Furthermore, the nurse must maintain correct posture, keep their back straight, and use their legs rather than their back muscles to lift and guide heavy limbs, thereby preventing work-related musculoskeletal injuries among nursing personnel in busy Nigerian healthcare wards.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Upper and Lower Extremity PROM

The step-by-step execution of Passive Range of Motion covers both upper and lower extremities, requiring systematic organization to ensure no joint is missed. For the upper extremities, the nurse begins at the shoulder by performing flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and internal/external rotation while supporting the elbow and wrist. Next, the elbow is flexed and extended, followed by pronation and supination of the forearm. Wrist movements include flexion, extension, radial deviation, and ulnar deviation, finishing with individual finger flexion, extension, abduction, and opposition of the thumb. For the lower extremities, the nurse starts at the hip and knee by simultaneously flexing and extending both joints, followed by hip abduction, adduction, and internal/external rotation, taking care to maintain patient modesty with a bed sheet. The ankle is then moved through dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion, and eversion. Finally, the nurse gently flexes and extends the toes. Throughout this systematic procedure, the nurse maintains a firm yet gentle grip, observes the patient's facial expressions and vital signs for any signs of discomfort, and ensures that each movement is performed methodically from head to toe or proximal to distal, depending on institutional nursing protocols.

Patient Assessment, Comfort, and Monitoring During the Exercise

Patient assessment, comfort, and continuous monitoring are critical components of administering safe Passive Range of Motion. Before initiating the session, the nurse must assess the baseline clinical status of the patient, including checking vital signs, evaluating skin integrity over bony prominences, and noting any pre-existing joint stiffness, swelling, or localized warmth. Comfort measures include ensuring the room environment is well-ventilated, positioning pillows to support unaffected body parts, and maintaining patient dignity by draping unworked areas. During the actual exercise, the nurse must maintain continuous verbal and non-verbal communication, actively observing the patient's face for signs of pain, distress, diaphoresis, or grimacing, especially when dealing with patients who have communication barriers or cognitive impairments. Monitoring physiological responses such as sudden changes in heart rate, respiratory rate, or blood pressure is also essential. If the patient shows any signs of distress or if the nurse feels abnormal resistance, snapping, or crepitus in the joint, the exercise must be paused immediately. Regular reassessment ensures that the nursing intervention remains therapeutic rather than harmful, promoting optimal rehabilitation outcomes in clinical practice.

Documentation, Reporting, and Complications Management

Accurate documentation and reporting are professional responsibilities that complete the process of administering Passive Range of Motion exercises. Immediately following the procedure, the nurse must record the intervention in the patient's nursing care plan and medical record, noting the specific joints exercised, the number of repetitions performed, and the patient's tolerance or reaction to the activity. Any unusual findings, such as joint crepitus, resistance, swelling, skin redness, or expressions of pain during the movements, must be clearly documented and reported promptly to the supervising nurse, physician, or physiotherapist. Managing complications effectively involves knowing how to respond if an adverse event occurs; for instance, if soft tissue strain or joint trauma is suspected, the nurse must immediately halt the exercise, apply appropriate comfort measures or ice as ordered, and notify the medical team. In Nigerian nursing practice, thorough documentation provides legal protection, ensures continuity of care across different shift handovers, and allows the multidisciplinary healthcare team to evaluate the ongoing effectiveness of the rehabilitation regimen.
