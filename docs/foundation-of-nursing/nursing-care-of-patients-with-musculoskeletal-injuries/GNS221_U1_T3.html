Passive Range of Motion Exercise

Introduction and Definition of Passive Range of Motion

Passive range of motion (PROM) refers to the movement of a joint through its full available arc achieved entirely by an external force. That external force may originate from a therapist, a mechanical device, or a caregiver, while the patient remains completely relaxed, expending no muscular energy of their own. Unlike active or active-assistive exercises, the target musculature contracts neither concentrically nor eccentrically during PROM. This intervention serves patients who cannot or should not move their limbs independently due to paralysis, severe weakness, coma, or strict medical bedrest orders. Preserving joint integrity remains the primary physiological target, as uninterrupted immobility quickly triggers contractures, adhesions, and progressive cartilage degradation within synovial joints.

Clinical Indications and Purposes of PROM

Clinicians order PROM across diverse care settings to preserve baseline musculoskeletal function when active movement is impossible. The primary goals include: - **Preserving Joint Mobility**: Prevents the shortening of ligaments, tendons, and joint capsules. - **Maintaining Circulation**: Enhances local blood return through the milking action of soft tissues on nearby vascular beds. - **Minimizing Adhesions**: Keeps synovial membranes lubricated and prevents cross-link formation in connective tissues. - **Evaluating Joint Integrity**: Allows the examiner to feel baseline resistance and mechanical limits. - **Preparing for Active Exercise**: Serves as a transitional step before the patient progresses to active-assisted movements.

Contraindications and Precautions

While PROM is therapeutic, moving an unstable joint causes catastrophic harm. Absolute contraindications include recent fractures with unhealed bone ends, acute joint dislocations, and immediately following tendon or ligament repair surgeries where stress disrupts suture lines. In busy Nigerian hospital wards, where patient loads are heavy and supervision ratios low, nurses must remain vigilant for conditions requiring strict caution. These include severe osteoporosis, bone metastasis, hematomas, and areas of hypersensitivity or severe inflammation. Forcing a joint beyond its natural pathological limit or pushing through sudden, sharp pain tears muscle fibers and damages joint capsules. If acute muscle spasm, severe pain, or bony blockages occur, stop the maneuver immediately and report the finding to the attending physician.

General Principles for Safe Execution

Execution technique determines whether PROM acts as a therapy or an injury mechanism. Always position the patient comfortably in good anatomical alignment, adjusting the bed height to protect your own lumbar spine from strain. Support the limb proximally and distally to the target joint; never leave a joint dangling unsupported during manipulation. Move the limb smoothly and slowly, avoiding jerky or ballistic motions that trigger protective muscle guarding. Take each joint only to the point of natural resistance, never forcing past firm end-feels. Repeat each specific movement gently, typically five to ten times per session, keeping the overall routine organized from head to toe or proximal to distal.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Upper and Lower Extremities

1. **Hand and Wrist**: Cradle the forearm, support the palm, and gently guide the wrist through flexion, extension, hyperextension, and circular rotation. Flex and extend individual fingers. 2. **Elbow and Shoulder**: Support the elbow and wrist, flexing and extending the arm. For the shoulder, support under the elbow and wrist, moving the arm into flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and internal/external rotation. 3. **Hip and Knee**: Cradle the heel and knee, lifting the leg into flexion and extension. Move the straight leg into abduction and adduction, and roll the entire limb for internal and external rotation. 4. **Ankle and Foot**: Support the lower calf and foot sole, guiding the ankle through dorsiflexion, plantarflexion, inversion, and eversion.

Patient Assessment, Comfort, and Monitoring

Continuous observation safeguards the client throughout the physical therapy session. Monitor facial expressions, skin color, and breathing patterns for non-verbal signs of discomfort or fatigue. In clients with spinal cord injuries or neurological deficits who cannot feel pain, monitor autonomic signs like sudden sweating, flushing, or spikes in blood pressure. Keep the environment private and maintain respectful draping to preserve patient dignity, a value deeply emphasized in Nigerian nursing ethics. Speak calmly throughout the process to reassure the client and maintain a therapeutic nurse-patient connection.

Documentation and Complications Management

Accurate charting protects both the patient and the healthcare team by establishing a clear record of functional maintenance. Document the specific joints exercised, the number of repetitions completed, the patient's tolerance, and any observed limitations in joint mobility. If resistance, pain, or muscle spasms occurred, note the exact angle or threshold where they appeared. Report unexpected swelling, joint instability, or persistent pain immediately to the primary provider. Consistent charting allows multidisciplinary rehabilitation teams to track progress and adjust care plans safely over time.
