Cervical Traction

Introduction to Cervical Traction and Purpose

Cervical traction is a therapeutic nursing and medical intervention utilized to apply a pulling force to the cervical spine. The primary purpose of this procedure is to reduce muscle spasms, alleviate pain, correct or prevent spinal deformities, and immobilize the cervical spine following trauma or surgery. By stretching the cervical vertebrae and widening the intervertebral spaces, traction helps to relieve pressure on compressed nerve roots and reduce herniated intervertebral discs. In the Nigerian clinical context, where road traffic accidents frequently result in acute spinal injuries, understanding cervical traction is vital for student nurses aiming to stabilize patients and prevent secondary neurological damage. The pulling force can be applied continuously or intermittently, depending on the specific clinical objectives outlined by the multidisciplinary healthcare team. Effective management of cervical traction requires a firm grasp of spinal anatomy, diligent monitoring of equipment, and vigilant observation of the patient's neurological and respiratory status throughout the duration of therapy.

Anatomy and Biomechanics of the Cervical Spine

The cervical spine consists of seven vertebrae (C1 to C7) that support the head, protect the spinal cord, and allow for a wide range of motion including flexion, extension, rotation, and lateral bending. The upper two vertebrae, the atlas (C1) and the axis (C2), possess unique anatomical structures that facilitate nodding and head rotation. Intervertebral discs sit between the vertebral bodies from C2-C3 down to C7-T1, acting as shock absorbers and allowing flexibility. Biomechanically, the cervical spine is vulnerable to injury due to its high mobility and the relatively heavy weight of the skull it supports. When applying cervical traction, the biomechanical goal is to safely alter the physiological curves and alignment of these vertebrae without causing excessive distraction, which could stretch or damage the spinal cord and surrounding ligaments. Understanding the normal alignment, facet joints, and nerve root exits helps nursing students comprehend why specific angles of traction pull are prescribed for different cervical spine pathologies.

Types of Cervical Traction: Skeletal vs. Skin

Cervical traction is broadly categorized into two main types: skin traction and skeletal traction. Skin traction involves applying a pulling force via external devices such as a head halter, foam strap, or pelvic belt attached to weights using ropes and pulleys. It is non-invasive, typically used for short-term relief of muscle spasms or mild nerve root impingement, and requires limited weight (usually less than 5 pounds or 2.3 kg) to prevent skin breakdown and irritation. In contrast, skeletal traction is an invasive method where a surgeon or trained physician inserts metal pins (such as Gardner-Wells tongs or a Halo ring) directly into the skull of the patient. This allows for significantly heavier weights to be applied safely over a long period to achieve and maintain vertebral alignment and reduction of severe cervical fractures or dislocations. Nursing care differs vastly between the two, with skin traction focusing heavily on skin integrity and halter placement, whereas skeletal traction requires meticulous sterile pin site care and continuous alignment checks.

Indications and Contraindications for Use

The application of cervical traction is governed by clear clinical indications and strict contraindications to ensure patient safety. Indications include cervical disc degeneration, cervical spondylosis, torticollis, minor neck sprains, and the stabilization of specific cervical fractures or dislocations pre- or post-surgery. Conversely, absolute and relative contraindications must be meticulously screened by the nurse prior to initiation. Contraindications include severe osteoporosis, rheumatoid arthritis involving the upper cervical spine, spinal cord tumors, bone metastasis, acute spinal cord injuries where traction could worsen instability, and infections of the skin or bone in the area of traction application. Unstable fractures that require immediate surgical decompression rather than traction pull are also excluded. Student nurses must review the patient's medical history, imaging reports, and physician orders carefully to identify any contraindications before assisting with or maintaining cervical traction therapy.

Equipment Setup and Application Procedure

Setting up and applying cervical traction requires precision, safety, and adherence to institutional protocols. For skin traction, the nurse ensures the head halter is properly fitted under the patient's chin and occiput, attaching it to a spreader bar, rope, and weight bag as ordered. For skeletal traction, the nurse assists the physician during the placement of tongs or a halo ring, ensuring emergency airway equipment is nearby. A fundamental rule of traction management is that all weights must hang freely at all times and must never rest on the floor or the bed frame, as this compromises the prescribed pulling force. The ropes must be aligned correctly in the grooves of the pulleys without any fraying or knots catching on the apparatus. The bed should remain in the prescribed position (often flat or with the head elevated slightly depending on counter-traction needs), and the patient's body alignment must be maintained straight to ensure the pull remains in the correct axis of the cervical spine.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing assessment for a patient in cervical traction involves continuous monitoring of neurological, respiratory, and cardiovascular status. The nurse must perform frequent neurological checks, assessing cranial nerves, sensory function, motor strength in all four extremities, and deep tendon reflexes to detect any signs of spinal cord compromise early. Respiratory status is equally critical; high cervical injuries or over-traction can affect the phrenic nerve and paralyze the diaphragm, leading to respiratory depression or failure. Therefore, respiratory rate, depth, breath sounds, and oxygen saturation must be monitored vigilantly. Pain levels should be evaluated regularly to distinguish between expected muscle soreness and pain signaling nerve impingement or device displacement. Furthermore, the nurse inspects the skin under straps or halters for pressure ulcers, redness, and breakdown, and monitors vital signs for baseline changes indicative of autonomic dysreflexia or shock.

Patient Care and Pin Site Management

Patient care during cervical traction requires meticulous attention to hygiene, comfort, and, in skeletal traction, specialized pin site management. For patients with skeletal traction (such as Gardner-Wells tongs), pin sites must be inspected every shift for signs of infection, including redness, swelling, heat, purulent drainage, and foul odors. Pin site care is performed using sterile technique according to hospital policy, typically utilizing sterile normal saline or chlorhexidine swabs to gently clean crusts from around the pin sites while avoiding excessive manipulation of the pins. If a pin becomes loose or pulls out, the nurse must immediately stabilize the head in neutral alignment, call for help, and notify the physician. General patient care also includes assisting with activities of daily living, providing pressure relief for non-trapped areas, preventing complications of immobility such as deep vein thrombosis and constipation, and ensuring emotional support since immobilization can cause anxiety and sensory deprivation.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Patients undergoing cervical traction are at risk for several potential complications that require prompt nursing interventions. Skin breakdown and pressure ulcers can develop over the occiput, chin, ears, or back due to immobility and friction from halters or bed linen; nurses must implement frequent repositioning of pressure points where permitted and use padding. Nerve palsy can occur if straps or traction forces press directly on superficial nerves, necessitating immediate adjustment. Respiratory complications, such as atelectasis and pneumonia, arise from restricted chest movement and hypoventilation, which are mitigated by encouraging deep breathing, incentive spirometry, and frequent turning if allowed. Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is another major risk due to prolonged immobility, requiring the use of compression stockings, sequential compression devices (SCDs), and prophylactic anticoagulants as ordered. Psychologically, patients may experience 'traction delirium' or intense frustration, which requires therapeutic communication and reorientation.

Patient Education and Discontinuation Phase

The patient education and discontinuation phase marks the transition from intensive cervical traction to rehabilitation and recovery. Before discontinuing traction, the nurse educates the patient and their family about the purpose of removal, expected sensations (such as temporary muscle stiffness), and signs of complications to report. When the physician orders the discontinuation of traction, the nurse assists by gradually releasing weights if a weaning protocol is prescribed, or carefully removing skin traction straps and supporting devices. In cases of skeletal traction, the physician removes the tongs or halo pins, and the nurse cleans and dresses the resulting skin sites using sterile technique. Post-discontinuation care focuses on cervical collar compliance (such as wearing a Philadelphia or soft collar as ordered), gentle range-of-motion exercises prescribed by physical therapy, posture training, and warning signs of recurrent nerve compression. Educating the patient on safe home care ensures lasting recovery and prevents re-injury.
