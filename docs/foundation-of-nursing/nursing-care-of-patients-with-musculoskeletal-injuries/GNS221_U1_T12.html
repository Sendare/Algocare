Cervical Traction

Introduction and Purpose of Cervical Traction

Cervical traction is a therapeutic technique that applies a pulling force to the cervical spine. The primary goal is to stretch the soft tissues, widen the intervertebral foramina, and reduce pressure on compressed nerve roots or spinal discs. It is commonly used in Nigerian orthopedic and neurosurgical wards to manage acute neck injuries, chronic cervical radiculopathy, and to align fractures before definitive surgical intervention.

Types of Cervical Traction

Cervical traction is categorized into two main types based on how the pulling force is secured to the patient:

  • Skin Traction: Uses a halter device (such as a head halter) wrapped around the chin and occiput. It is non-invasive, used for short-term relief, and limited by low weight limits to prevent skin breakdown.
  • Skeletal Traction: Uses metal pins or tongs (such as Gardner-Wells tongs) inserted directly into the skull. This method allows much heavier weights for precise alignment of unstable cervical spine fractures.

Indications and Contraindications

Selecting the right patient for cervical traction requires careful clinical evaluation.

Indications:

  • Cervical disc herniation
  • Muscle spasm and torticollis
  • Unstable cervical spine fractures requiring reduction

Contraindications:

  • Severe osteoporosis or bone-softening diseases
  • Rheumatoid arthritis involving the upper cervical spine
  • Spinal cord tumors or acute infections
  • Any spinal instability where pulling forces worsen neurological deficits

Equipment Setup and Weight Management

Proper mechanical setup ensures safe and effective treatment. Ropes must run freely through pulleys without catching on the bed frame or headboard. Weights must hang completely free above the floor at all times. Never lift, remove, or add traction weights without a direct medical order, as sudden weight changes can cause violent muscle spasms or spinal displacement.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Nurses must perform frequent neurovascular and physical assessments on patients undergoing cervical traction. Check the upper extremities regularly for motor function, grip strength, sensation, and capillary refill. Inspect the skin under head halters or around traction apparatus for redness, pressure sores, and irritation. Assess the patient's pain level and document any new numbness or tingling immediately.

Pin Site Care for Skeletal Traction

Skeletal traction pins create an open pathway into the bone, presenting a high risk for osteomyelitis. Perform pin site care strictly using aseptic technique according to hospital protocol. Inspect the sites daily for purulent drainage, redness, warmth, and loosening. Never remove or adjust the protective caps or nuts on traction pins yourself.

Complications and Emergency Nursing Actions

Complications range from minor skin irritation to life-threatening respiratory distress. If a patient experiences sudden respiratory difficulty, severe pain, or acute neurological decline, support the airway and call for medical help immediately. In emergency scenarios where weights must be removed to save a life, stabilize the head manually while releasing the tension.

Patient Education and Discontinuation Phase

Transitioning out of cervical traction requires patience and clear communication. Teach the patient not to attempt sitting up or moving their neck without authorization. Explain the importance of wearing prescribed neck collars during the step-down phase. Reassure the patient and family about recovery expectations, mobilization schedules, and physical therapy plans.
