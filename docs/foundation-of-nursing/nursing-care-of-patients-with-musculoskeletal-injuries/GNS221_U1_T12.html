Cervical Traction

Introduction and Purpose of Cervical Traction

Cervical traction is a therapeutic technique that applies a pulling force to the neck structures. Healthcare providers use this method to treat various spinal conditions, relieve chronic neck pain, and correct spinal misalignments. By gently stretching the cervical spine, traction helps open up the intervertebral foramina, which can relieve pressure on pinched nerves and stretched muscles.

The primary goals of cervical traction include:

  • Pain Relief: Reducing pressure on compressed nerve roots and inflamed facet joints.
  • Muscle Relaxation: Easing painful muscle spasms in the neck and upper shoulders.
  • Spinal Alignment: Correcting minor subluxations and stabilizing fractures.
  • Decompression: Increasing the space between vertebrae to allow bulging discs to recede.

Types of Cervical Traction: Skeletal versus Skin

There are two main categories of cervical traction used in clinical practice: skin traction and skeletal traction. The choice depends on how much pulling force is needed and how long the patient requires treatment.

  • Skin Traction: This non-invasive method uses a halter, foam strap, or chin strap attached to a weight system. It applies pulling force indirectly through the skin and soft tissues. Because the skin can break down or slip under heavy loads, skin traction is typically limited to lighter weights and short-term use.
  • Skeletal Traction: This invasive method involves surgical placement of metal devices, such as tongs or a halo ring, directly into the skull bones. These devices attach to ropes and weights, allowing for much heavier pulling forces and precise control. Skeletal traction is necessary for stabilizing severe cervical fractures and dislocations over weeks or months.

Indications and Contraindications

Knowing when to use cervical traction and when to avoid it is critical for patient safety. Improper application can cause permanent spinal cord injury.

Common indications include cervical radiculopathy, degenerative disc disease, muscle spasms, and stable cervical fractures. Conversely, healthcare teams must avoid traction in patients with conditions that make the spine unstable or fragile.

Absolute contraindications include severe osteoporosis, spinal cord tumors, recent neck surgery, and unstable spinal fractures where pulling could sever the spinal cord. A thorough diagnostic workup, including imaging studies, must happen before any traction device is put in place.

Equipment Setup and Weight Management

Setting up cervical traction safely requires meticulous attention to mechanical alignment and weight precision. For skeletal traction, the ropes must run freely through the pulleys, and the weights must hang completely unobstructed above the floor.

A common beginner mistake is allowing the traction weights to rest on the bed or touch the floor, which instantly defeats the therapeutic pull. The nurse must verify that the patient's body alignment matches the line of pull. For example, if skull tongs are used, the pull should be in a straight line with the long axis of the spine. Any friction against the headboard or bed frame alters the prescribed weight load and compromises patient safety.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Patients undergoing cervical traction require frequent, systematic assessments to catch complications early. The nurse monitors neurological status, skin integrity, and pain levels around the clock.

  • Neurovascular Checks: Assess hand grip strength, finger sensation, capillary refill, and movement in all extremities every few hours. Numbness or tingling can signal increasing spinal cord pressure.
  • Skin Integrity: Check areas under skin traction straps, braces, or halo vests for redness, pressure sores, and skin breakdown.
  • Pain Evaluation: Distinguish between normal muscle ache from stretching and sharp, shooting nerve pain that indicates worsening compression.

Pin Site Care and Infection Prevention

Patients with skeletal traction who have skull pins or halo screws are at high risk for localized bone infection, known as osteomyelitis. Meticulous pin site care is a core nursing responsibility to keep microorganisms out of the sterile cranial bone.

Nurses perform pin site care using sterile technique, typically cleaning around each pin with normal saline or a prescribed antiseptic solution to remove crusting. It is normal to see a small amount of clear serous drainage or mild crusting during the first few days after placement. However, purulent yellow discharge, foul odors, severe pain, or loosening pins indicate infection and must be reported to the physician immediately.

Complications and Emergency Interventions

Although cervical traction helps heal spinal injuries, it carries significant risks. Nurses must be prepared to act quickly if an emergency arises.

  • Respiratory Distress: High cervical injuries or overly aggressive traction can impair the diaphragm and breathing muscles. Watch for shortness of breath or use of accessory muscles.
  • Vagus Nerve Irritation: Sudden pulling can stimulate the vagus nerve, causing severe bradycardia and fainting.
  • Pin Slippage: If a skull pin slips or detaches, the patient's neck loses support. The nurse must manually stabilize the head in neutral alignment and call for emergency medical help.
