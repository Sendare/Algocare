Cervical Traction

Introduction and Purpose of Cervical Traction

Cervical traction applies a longitudinal pulling force to the cervical spine to achieve specific therapeutic goals. By stretching the soft tissues and separating the vertebrae, this mechanical intervention reduces muscle spasms, relieves pressure on compressed nerve roots, and helps align fractured or dislocated cervical segments. In acute trauma settings, such as motor vehicle accidents managed in Nigerian teaching hospitals, traction provides temporary stabilization before definitive surgical intervention. The overarching objective is to restore or maintain spinal alignment while minimizing neurological compromise.

Types of Cervical Traction: Skeletal versus Skin

Cervical traction is broadly categorized into two main delivery methods based on how the pulling force is anchored to the patient. - **Skeletal traction**: Utilizes surgically inserted devices, such as Gardner-Wells tongs or a halo ring with pins anchored directly into the skull. This method permits heavier weights—often up to several kilograms per vertebral level—and provides rigid, precise alignment necessary for severe fractures and dislocations. - **Skin traction**: Employs non-invasive external devices like a Glissor head halter attached to a pulley system and weight bag. It is restricted to much lighter weights to prevent skin breakdown and is typically used for muscle spasms or minor nerve impingement.

Indications and Contraindications

Selecting patients for cervical traction requires careful clinical evaluation. - **Indications**: Cervical spine fractures, vertebral dislocations, severe chronic neck pain secondary to nerve root compression, and intractable muscle spasms. - **Contraindications**: Severe osteoporosis, osteomyelitis of the spine, rheumatoid arthritis involving the upper cervical spine, and ligamentous instability where pulling forces could worsen spinal cord transection. An oversight in screening for bone-weakening conditions can result in catastrophic structural failure during weight application.

Equipment Setup and Mechanical Principles

Safe traction relies on strict adherence to mechanical physics. Weights must hang freely at all times, suspended clear of the floor, bed frame, and any linen draping. If a weight rests on a chair or the mattress, the traction pull is immediately neutralized, rendering the setup ineffective. Ropes must run smoothly through unobstructed pulleys, and the knots must remain secure. The patient's body weight often serves as counter-traction; therefore, the bed should typically remain flat or in a slight Trendelenburg position as prescribed, ensuring the patient does not slide down toward the weight source.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Continuous observation protects the patient from secondary neurological and physical injury. Perform frequent neurovascular checks on the upper extremities, evaluating grip strength, finger sensation, capillary refill, and pulse quality. Document baseline findings meticulously so subtle declines are caught early. Inspect the skin under halter straps or around traction apparatuses for pressure injury, redness, or breakdown. Assess the patient's level of comfort, noting whether pain is typical musculoskeletal soreness or indicative of dangerous cord impingement.

Pin Site Care and Skeletal Maintenance

For patients fitted with skeletal traction via skull tongs or halo pins, pin site maintenance is a sterile imperative to prevent osteomyelitis and localized soft tissue infection. Facilities typically follow specific institutional protocols, often utilizing sterile saline or chlorhexidine solutions to cleanse crusting around the insertion sites. Inspect the pins regularly for loosening, purulent drainage, or surrounding erythema. Never adjust, tighten, or manipulate traction pins independently; any mechanical adjustment of skeletal hardware falls strictly within the medical purview of the attending physician or orthopedic surgeon.

Recognizing and Managing Complications

Prolonged immobilization and mechanical pulling introduce several clinical risks. Respiratory depression can occur due to impaired chest wall movement, especially if high cervical injuries affect phrenic nerve function. Skin breakdown threatens immobilized areas, particularly the occipital region of the head and the scapulae. Severe, unyielding pain often signals excessive weight, improper alignment, or neurological distress and requires immediate physician notification. Constipation and deep vein thrombosis are frequent systemic hazards secondary to enforced bed rest.

Patient Education and Discontinuation Phase

As the healing phase progresses, preparation for discontinuing traction begins. Patients and their families are educated on the rationale behind movement restrictions and the importance of reporting new numbness or weakness. When the physician orders traction removal, the process is executed gradually or transitioned to a cervical collar (such as a Philadelphia or Miami J collar) to support the neck muscles. Nurses monitor the patient closely for rebound muscle spasms, dizziness, or structural instability once the external weights are lifted.
