Stockinette

Introduction and Definition of Stockinette

A stockinette is a seamless, knitted tubular fabric primarily used in orthopedic nursing as a foundational layer beneath plaster of Paris or synthetic casts. Unlike woven bandages, the knitted construction gives it inherent stretch and elasticity, allowing it to conform smoothly to the contours of human limbs without forming restrictive ridges or creases. In Nigerian clinical settings, where orthopedic wards often manage high volumes of trauma and fracture cases, understanding this basic material is essential for every student nurse preparing for plaster room duties.

Purposes and Clinical Indications in Orthopedic Care

The application of a stockinette serves multiple protective and functional purposes in orthopedic management:

  • Skin protection: Acts as a physical barrier between sensitive skin and abrasive casting materials.
  • Moisture absorption: Traps minor perspiration to reduce skin maceration beneath impermeable casts.
  • Friction reduction: Minimizes shear forces between the hard cast interior and bony prominences.
  • Wound dressing securement: Holds non-adhesive primary dressings gently in place over superficial abrasions before casting.

Types and Materials of Stockinette Used in Clinical Practice

Stockinettes are manufactured from various fibers, each suited for different clinical scenarios. Selecting the correct material ensures patient comfort and prevents allergic reactions or excessive moisture retention.

Preparation of the Patient and Equipment Selection

Before applying a stockinette, thorough preparation prevents procedural delays and ensures a safe, comfortable fit. The nurse must verify the physician's order, inspect the affected skin for open wounds, rashes, or severe edema, and ensure the limb is clean and dry.

Equipment gathering requires selecting the appropriate diameter of the tubular fabric. A stockinette that is too narrow will dangerously constrict the limb, while one that is too wide will bunch up and create painful pressure ridges under the cast. Rolls should be unrolled to measure approximately 15 to 20 centimeters longer than the intended cast span at both proximal and distal ends, allowing sufficient material to fold back over the cast edges later.

Step-by-Step Application Technique

  1. Inspect and Cleanse: Check the skin integrity and ensure the limb is clean and thoroughly dry.
  2. Select Size: Choose a tubular stockinette width that accommodates the limb comfortably without excessive stretching or looseness.
  3. Measure Length: Cut a piece of stockinette that exceeds the proximal and distal boundaries of the anticipated cast by about 10 to 15 centimeters on each end.
  4. Gently Slide: Gather the tubular fabric like a ring and smoothly roll or glide it over the extremity, avoiding aggressive pulling that could shear fragile skin.
  5. Smooth Wrinkles: Pull the material taut in opposite directions to eliminate all creases, wrinkles, and folds.
  6. Position Safely: Ensure bony prominences are evenly covered before padding and casting materials are applied.

Post-Application Care and Monitoring for Complications

Once the cast is applied over the stockinette, ongoing vigilance is required to detect early signs of neurovascular compromise or skin irritation. The protruding cuffs of the stockinette provide a useful window to observe skin color and temperature at the extremities. Nurses must monitor for the classic signs of compartment syndrome—pain out of proportion to the injury, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, and paralysis. Any dampness trapped within the stockinette must be reported immediately, as persistent moisture softens padding and fosters fungal or bacterial growth against compromised skin.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Patient education forms a cornerstone of safe orthopedic recovery. Individuals discharged with a cast over a stockinette must understand home care protocols to prevent severe complications. Patients should be instructed never to insert foreign objects such as coat hangers, knitting needles, or sticks down the stockinette to scratch an itch, as this easily breaks fragile skin and introduces infection. Furthermore, they must report foul odors, burning sensations, or sudden tightness immediately. Nurses maintain the legal and professional responsibility of documenting the procedure accurately, noting skin status, stockinette size, and patient tolerance.
