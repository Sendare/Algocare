Gallows Traction

Introduction and Definition of Gallows Traction

Gallows traction, also known historically as Bryant's traction, is a specialized skin traction technique primarily utilized in orthopaedics for infants and young children. By definition, it involves applying traction weights to both lower extremities while the child's hips are flexed at a ninety-degree angle to the body, and the knees are extended. The unique feature of gallows traction is that the child's weight acts as the counter-traction, resulting in the buttocks being lifted slightly off the bed. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice and board examinations in Nigeria, understanding this setup is vital because it is specifically designed for paediatric patients who weigh less than thirty pounds or are generally under two years of age. Heavier or older children cannot safely use this method because their body weight is too great to provide proper counter-traction, risking circulatory compromise or ineffective reduction of the femoral fracture. Gallows traction is most commonly indicated for the treatment of femoral shaft fractures in toddlers, aiding in the alignment of bone fragments before definitive healing or casting occurs. Nurses must be intimately familiar with this intervention to ensure safe execution, maintain correct alignment, and prevent potential secondary injuries during the course of hospitalisation.

Indications for Gallows Traction in Paediatric Patients

The primary indication for gallows traction in paediatric patients is the management and stabilization of femoral shaft fractures. In toddlers and infants, femoral fractures can be challenging to immobilize using traditional adult methods due to the rapid healing rates and constant movement characteristic of this developmental stage. Gallows traction helps maintain the alignment of the fractured femur by utilizing gravity and counter-traction generated by the child's own body weight. Besides femoral shaft fractures, clinicians may occasionally consider skin traction options for pre-operative reduction of congenital developmental dysplasia of the hip, although harness methods are more standard nowadays. Nursing students must recognize that because infants have highly malleable bones and are prone to muscle spasms, maintaining constant, uninterrupted traction is essential for proper bone healing and preventing overriding bone ends. Furthermore, because this traction lifts the infant's buttocks off the mattress, it provides a practical method for maintaining hygiene while keeping the fracture immobilized. Identifying the correct indications ensures that inappropriate candidates, such as older or heavier children, are not subjected to this form of traction, thereby avoiding dangerous complications like neurovascular compromise or inadequate fracture reduction.

Anatomical and Physiological Principles of Paediatric Traction

Understanding the anatomical and physiological principles underlying paediatric traction is critical for safe nursing care. In gallows traction, the force is applied directly to the skin via adhesive or non-adhesive traction strips (skin traction), rather than surgically placed pins into the bone (skeletal traction). The physiological goal is to overcome the strong pull of the thigh muscles, particularly the quadriceps and hamstrings, which tend to cause overriding of the fractured bone ends. By suspending the child's lower extremities vertically and lifting the buttocks just clear of the bed, the child's upper torso acts as the counter-weight. Nurses must appreciate that an infant's skin is extremely delicate, thin, and prone to rapid breakdown, meaning that skin traction cannot withstand excessive weight. The weights used must be just enough to keep the buttocks hovering off the mattress—usually only a few pounds—because too much weight can completely compromise blood flow to the lower extremities. Additionally, paediatric circulation is sensitive; any improper alignment or excessive pressure can lead to severe ischemic injuries like compartment syndrome. Therefore, knowing how the pull of gravity, counter-traction, and skin integrity interact forms the scientific foundation for daily nursing assessments and interventions.

Equipment Setup and Application Procedure

The setup and application of gallows traction require precision, teamwork, and adherence to strict safety protocols. First, the nurse gathers the necessary equipment, including an overhead traction frame securely attached to the paediatric cot, ropes, pulleys, foam padding, skin traction strips (such as moleskin or foam traction wraps), elastic bandages, and prescribed lightweight hanging weights. The child's legs must be carefully washed and dried, and skin integrity should be thoroughly assessed before applying any adhesive or wrapping material. Tincture of benzoin may be applied to protect the skin and enhance adhesion, provided the infant's skin is not broken or hypersensitive. The traction strips are applied longitudinally along the lateral and medial aspects of both lower extremities, avoiding direct pressure over bony prominences like the malleoli. An assistant helps hold the infant's legs in a ninety-degree flexion position while the nurse wraps the limbs gently with elastic bandages in a spiral, distal-to-proximal manner to ensure even pressure without restricting circulation. Once wrapped, the ropes are attached to the traction spreader bars, threaded through the overhead pulleys, and connected to the prescribed weights. Nurses must ensure that the weights hang freely, never touching the floor or the bed frame, and that the child's buttocks remain elevated just enough to slide a sheet underneath, confirming proper counter-traction.

Nursing Assessment and Neurovascular Monitoring

Nursing assessment and neurovascular monitoring are arguably the most crucial responsibilities when caring for a child in gallows traction. Because infants cannot verbalize localized pain, numbness, or tingling effectively, the nurse must rely on objective clinical indicators. Routine neurovascular checks—commonly referred to as the '5 Ps' (Pain, Pallor, Pulses, Paresthesia, and Paralysis)—must be performed and documented at least every two to four hours. The nurse evaluates the color and temperature of the toes, comparing them to ensure adequate arterial perfusion. Capillary refill time should be brisk, typically less than two seconds. Peripheral pulses, such as the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial, must be palpated or assessed with a Doppler device if difficult to feel. Additionally, the nurse must monitor for signs of excessive or unmanaged pain, which in infants often manifests as continuous, inconsolable crying, irritability, or restlessness despite comforting measures. Edema or swelling in the feet and toes indicates venous congestion, which necessitates immediate intervention. Any sudden changes, such as cold, pale toes or absent pulses, must be reported to the attending physician immediately, as they point toward impending compartment syndrome or vascular occlusion that can result in permanent limb damage.

Daily Care, Hygiene, and Skin Integrity Management

Maintaining daily care, hygiene, and skin integrity is exceptionally challenging when managing an infant in gallows traction due to the constant presence of diapers, limited mobility, and the elevated position of the buttocks. Because the child's buttocks hover just above the mattress, standard diapering is impossible; instead, nurses and parents must use folded diapers or incontinence pads tucked beneath the buttocks and changed frequently to prevent urine and stool from contaminating the skin or traction dressings. Moisture can rapidly cause skin maceration and breakdown, particularly under adhesive wraps. Therefore, the skin around the traction strips must be inspected every shift for redness, irritation, or blistering. Bed linen changes must be executed with extreme care using a rolling technique or assistance, ensuring that the traction alignment is never compromised and weights are never temporarily lifted or displaced without a doctor's order (or unless specified in institutional protocols for brief hygienic care). Gentle cleansing and thorough drying of the skin surrounding the traction straps, along with meticulous perineal care, are paramount. If skin breakdown occurs, it can force the discontinuation of skin traction, delaying the healing of the femoral fracture and increasing the risk of infection.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Working with gallows traction exposes paediatric patients to several potential complications that require vigilant nursing surveillance and prompt intervention. The most serious vascular complication is compartment syndrome, caused by increased pressure within the fascial compartments of the leg, which can lead to ischemia, nerve damage, and Volkmann's ischemic contracture if left untreated. Signs include severe pain that does not respond to analgesics, pallor, pulselessness, paralysis, and excruciating pain upon passive stretch of the toes. Another frequent complication is skin breakdown and pressure ulcers, particularly over the sacrum, heels, or areas where traction wraps create undue friction. Additionally, mechanical complications can occur, such as slipped traction bandages, frayed ropes, displaced weights resting on the floor, or the child sliding down the bed so that their buttocks rest flat on the mattress, which completely negates the counter-traction effect. Respiratory complications can also arise due to immobility and restricted chest expansion; nurses must encourage deep breathing, monitor breath sounds, and change positions slightly if permitted. Nursing interventions include readjusting slipping bandages (in collaboration with the surgical team), ensuring weights hang freely, administering prescribed analgesics, and performing regular position shifts within the limits of the traction apparatus.

Patient and Family Education and Psychosocial Support

Patient and family education and psychosocial support are fundamental components of comprehensive nursing care when a child is placed in gallows traction. Parents often experience intense anxiety, guilt, and fear upon seeing their infant hoisted up in an overhead traction frame with weights hanging nearby. Nurses must provide empathetic, clear, and continuous explanations regarding the purpose of the traction, how it promotes bone healing, and why safety measures like not tampering with the weights are non-negotiable. Parents should be taught how to perform gentle touch, talking, and sensory stimulation, as immobilization can severely hinder an infant's normal developmental milestones and exploratory play. Because the infant cannot be held in the usual manner, nurses must teach parents alternative ways to bond, such as holding the baby while sitting by the bed, stroking their face, and offering toys that hang within reach. Furthermore, nursing students should educate family members on recognizing warning signs that must be reported immediately, such as cold toes, bluish discoloration, sudden swelling, or continuous inconsolable crying. Providing robust psychosocial support helps alleviate parental stress, fosters a collaborative care environment, and ensures that the family feels confident and supported throughout the hospital stay.

Weaning and Removal of Traction

The weaning and removal of gallows traction mark a significant milestone in the recovery of the paediatric patient, usually occurring once sufficient callus formation and fracture stability have been radiologically and clinically confirmed by the orthopaedic surgeon. Weaning may involve gradually decreasing the traction weight over a short period or directly discontinuing the traction when the bone is strong enough to withstand minor stresses without displacement. The removal procedure requires a coordinated multidisciplinary approach. The nurse gathers supplies, carefully untapes or removes the skin traction wraps, and cleans any adhesive residue from the infant's delicate skin using gentle, non-irritating solutions. Following traction removal, the child's skin must be reassessed for pressure marks, rashes, or minor abrasions, and appropriately treated. Because the legs have been immobilized in a flexed, suspended position for weeks, stiffness, muscle atrophy, and joint immobility are common. Nurses must assist with gentle, passive range-of-motion exercises as ordered and monitor the child's initial attempts at movement or spica casting if a follow-up cast is applied. Educating parents on what to expect post-removal—such as temporary stiffness or apprehension when moving the legs—ensures a smooth transition from hospital care back to home life.
