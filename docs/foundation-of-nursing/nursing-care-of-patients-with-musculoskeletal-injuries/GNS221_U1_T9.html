Skeletal Traction

Introduction to Skeletal Traction and Its Indications

Skeletal traction is an orthopedic intervention used to align fractured bones and correct deformities by applying a direct pulling force to the skeletal system. Unlike skin traction, which applies force through soft tissues, skeletal traction utilizes metallic pins, wires, or tongs surgically inserted directly into the bone. This method allows for the use of heavier weights over extended periods without causing significant skin breakdown or soft tissue compromise. Common clinical indications include: - **Femoral shaft fractures**: Particularly in adult patients where significant muscle spasm pulls bone fragments out of alignment. - **Severe acetabular fractures**: Requiring continuous distraction of the femoral head from the pelvic socket. - **Cervical spine injuries**: Utilizing devices like Gardner-Wells tongs to maintain spinal immobilization and realign vertebrae. - **Long-standing joint contractures**: Providing steady, progressive tension to stretch tight periarticular structures prior to surgical correction.

Equipment Setup and Mechanics of Traction

The mechanics of skeletal traction rely on Newton's laws of motion to maintain a balanced, continuous pull. An effective setup requires a coordinated arrangement of pins, ropes, pulleys, weights, and a counter-traction force. - **Pins and Wires**: Steinmann pins or Kirschner wires are drilled transversely through the distal femur, proximal tibia, or calcaneus. - **Bohler-Stumpf or Thomas Splints**: Often used alongside suspension slings to support the limb and prevent dependent edema. - **Ropes and Pulleys**: Must be freely movable, with ropes resting securely in the center grooves of each pulley without fraying or catching against the bed frame. - **Counter-traction**: Usually achieved by elevating the foot of the bed, allowing the patient's body weight to pull in the opposite direction, preventing the entire bed from sliding toward the traction weights.

Nursing Assessment and Continuous Monitoring

Continuous monitoring is essential to detect early complications associated with prolonged immobility and mechanical force. The neurovascular status of the affected limb must be assessed at least every two to four hours during the acute phase. - **Circulation**: Evaluate capillary refill, skin color, temperature of the digits, and peripheral pulses distal to the fracture. - **Sensation**: Check for numbness, tingling, or paresthesia, which indicate nerve compression or ischemia. - **Motion**: Test the patient's ability to wiggle toes or fingers. - **Traction Integrity**: Ensure weights hang freely above the floor at all times, ropes remain unobstructed in pulleys, and knots are secure. Never lift or remove weights during care activities unless a specific medical order permits temporary release.

Pin Site Care and Infection Prevention

Because skeletal traction pins pierce the skin and enter the sterile bone environment, the pin sites serve as direct pathways for microorganisms, creating a constant risk for osteomyelitis. Standardized protocols for pin site care minimize this hazard. - **Aseptic Technique**: Perform all pin site care using strict sterile technique, wearing sterile gloves and utilizing sterile instruments. - **Cleaning Solutions**: Commonly utilize sterile normal saline or chlorhexidine solutions according to hospital policy, gently removing crusts that form around the pin entry point. - **Frequency**: Conduct care per institutional guidelines, typically once daily or every shift, and whenever drainage is observed. - **Observation**: Monitor for signs of localized infection, including purulent drainage, increasing local erythema, warmth, foul odor, and persistent tenderness.

Patient Mobility and Complication Prevention

Prolonged bed rest and restricted movement create significant physiological challenges for patients in skeletal traction. Nursing interventions must target the prevention of secondary systemic complications. - **Thromboembolism**: Encourage active ankle pumps and leg exercises within the limits of traction, administer prescribed anticoagulants, and utilize sequential compression devices if ordered. - **Skin Breakdown**: Use pressure-relieving mattresses, encourage independent repositioning using an overhead trapeze bar when permissible, and inspect pressure points regularly. - **Respiratory Stagnation**: Promote deep breathing exercises and incentive spirometry every hour while awake to prevent atelectasis and hypostatic pneumonia. - **Elimination**: Provide adequate hydration, high-fiber diets, and stool softeners to combat immobility-induced constipation.

Maintenance of Traction Weights and Alignment

The therapeutic value of skeletal traction depends entirely on maintaining unbroken, correct mechanical forces. Nurses carry the legal and clinical responsibility of preserving this alignment. - **Weight Positioning**: Weights must always hang freely in the air, suspended well above the floor and clear of the bed frame, side rails, or chairs. - **Rope Integrity**: Knots must never rest against pulleys, and ropes must remain seated in their tracks. - **Alignment Maintenance**: The patient's body must be kept in straight alignment with the line of pull. If the patient slides downward, repositioning requires coordinated assistance to avoid disturbing the fracture site. - **Interruption Rule**: Never remove, lift, or alter traction weights without a direct, explicit medical prescription, as even momentary release can cause bone fragments to displace and damage surrounding tissue.

Discontinuation of Traction and Transition to Rehabilitation

Skeletal traction is typically a temporary bridging therapy until definitive fracture management—such as open reduction internal fixation (ORIF), casting, or functional bracing—can be performed. - **Removal Procedure**: The orthopedic surgeon or trained practitioner removes the traction pins using local anesthesia. The pin sites are then cleaned and dressed with sterile protective coverings. - **Post-Traction Assessment**: Re-evaluate the extremity for skin integrity, range of motion, and neurovascular status immediately following removal. - **Rehabilitation Support**: Assist the patient with early mobilization, physical therapy referrals, and gradual weight-bearing as prescribed by the surgical team. Provide education regarding home care, pin site observation if any scabs remain, and adherence to activity restrictions.
