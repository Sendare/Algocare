Skeletal Traction

Introduction to Skeletal Traction and Its Indications

Skeletal traction is a specialized method used by orthopedic teams to realign fractured bones and reduce muscle spasms. Unlike skin traction which applies pulling force to the soft tissues, skeletal traction uses metal pins, wires, or tongs surgically inserted directly through the bone. This direct attachment allows healthcare providers to apply much heavier weights for a longer duration without causing skin breakdown or soft tissue damage.

Common indications include severe femur fractures, displaced acetabular fractures, and cervical spine injuries where stabilization is urgently required before definitive surgical repair. It acts as a temporary bridge to surgery or as a primary treatment when a patient is too unstable for immediate open reduction and internal fixation.

Equipment Setup and Mechanics of Traction

A successful skeletal traction setup requires precise mechanical alignment to achieve its therapeutic goals. The system consists of metal pins (such as Steinmann pins or Kirschner wires), a traction bow, ropes, pulleys, and hanging weights.

  • Weights: Must hang freely at all times and never touch the floor or rest on the bed frame.
  • Ropes: Must run smoothly through the center of each pulley without fraying or catching.
  • Counter-traction: Usually provided by the patient's own body weight by elevating the foot or head of the bed, preventing the patient from sliding down toward the pulley.

If a weight rests on the floor, the pulling force is instantly lost, which can cause the fractured bone ends to shift and trigger severe muscle spasms.

Nursing Assessment and Continuous Monitoring

Continuous nursing surveillance is vital to catch complications early before permanent damage occurs. The nurse must perform comprehensive neurovascular checks of the affected limb at regular intervals.

  1. Circulation: Check peripheral pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature, and color.
  2. Motion: Ask the patient to wiggle their toes or fingers distal to the fracture.
  3. Sensation: Assess for numbness, tingling, or the inability to feel touch.

Pain assessment is another critical duty. While some background discomfort is expected with a fracture, sharp, unmanaged pain or sudden deep muscle spasms often indicate slipping weights, improper alignment, or developing compartment syndrome.

Pin Site Care and Infection Prevention

Because skeletal traction pins pass directly through the skin into the bone, they create a direct pathway for bacteria to enter the body, putting the patient at high risk for osteomyelitis. Pin site care must be performed meticulously using strict aseptic technique according to hospital protocol.

Normal findings around a healing pin site include mild serosanguinous drainage during the first 48 to 72 hours. However, purulent discharge, increasing redness, warmth, foul odor, and severe local tenderness are hallmark warning signs of infection. Nurses should avoid removing protective crusts unless specifically ordered, as these crusts help seal the wound during early healing.

Patient Mobility, Positioning, and Complication Prevention

Immobility is a major challenge for patients undergoing skeletal traction. Confined to bed rest in a fixed position, patients face a high risk of pressure injuries, deep vein thrombosis, respiratory atelectasis, and constipation.

Nursing interventions must target these risks proactively:

  • Encourage deep breathing exercises and use of an incentive spirometer every hour to keep the lungs clear.
  • Perform passive and active range-of-motion exercises on all unaffected joints.
  • Apply sequential compression devices (SCDs) or administer anticoagulant medications as ordered to prevent blood clots.
  • Use specialized support surfaces and assist with gentle, limited shifting while maintaining the strict alignment line.

Discontinuation and Transition to Rehabilitation

Skeletal traction is typically a temporary measure. Once soft tissue swelling subsides, callus formation begins, or the patient is cleared for internal fixation or a cast, the traction is discontinued by the medical team.

During removal, the nurse supports the limb to prevent sudden jarring movements that could cause severe pain or refracture the healing bone. Following removal, the focus shifts entirely to rehabilitation. Physical and occupational therapists step in to help the patient rebuild muscle strength, regain joint flexibility, and gradually resume weight-bearing activities safely.
