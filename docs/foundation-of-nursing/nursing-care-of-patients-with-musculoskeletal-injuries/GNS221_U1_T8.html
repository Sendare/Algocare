Skin Traction

Introduction to Skin Traction

Skin traction is a temporary orthopedic method used to align fractured bones, reduce muscle spasms, and immobilize a limb before surgery or definitive casting. Unlike skeletal traction, which relies on pins or wires surgically inserted directly into the bone, skin traction applies pulling force externally. Adhesive or non-adhesive traction tapes, foam boots, or slings are secured to the patient's skin, and weights are attached via a rope and pulley system. Because the pulling force is transmitted through the skin and soft tissues, the amount of weight that can safely be applied is strictly limited—usually between 2.3 to 4.5 kilograms. Exceeding this limit causes skin breakdown, blistering, and tearing rather than providing additional skeletal correction. Understanding this limitation helps nurses monitor the prescribed weight configuration and recognize that skin traction is a stabilization measure, not a permanent treatment.

Purposes and Indications

Orthopedic teams order skin traction primarily to manage painful muscle spasms surrounding a fresh fracture. When a long bone breaks, the surrounding muscles naturally contract violently, pulling the bone ends past each other and causing intense pain and further soft tissue damage. A steady, gentle pulling force fatigues these muscles, allowing them to relax and restoring the limb closer to its normal anatomical alignment.

Common indications include:

  • Hip fractures: Used temporarily in elderly patients awaiting internal fixation surgery.
  • Femur fractures: Helps immobilize the thigh bone in children or adults before definitive care.
  • Lower back pain: Sometimes used short-term to relieve lumbar muscle spasms, though less common today.
  • Joint contractures: Assists in gradually stretching tight tendons and ligaments prior to surgical correction.

Types and Equipment

Several specialized devices deliver skin traction depending on which part of the body needs alignment.

  • Buck's extension: The most common type, frequently used for lower limb fractures. A foam boot or adhesive strips secure the lower leg, and a single weight hangs freely off the end of the bed.
  • Russell traction: Similar to Buck's, but uses a sling under the knee and an overhead pulley to provide a dual pulling direction, often used for knee and hip injuries.
  • Bryant's traction: A specialized form where both legs are suspended vertically at a 90-degree angle from the bed, primarily used for congenital hip dislocations in infants.

All systems require specific hardware, including ropes, pulleys, spreaders bars, and designated weight bags. Nurses must ensure the equipment matches the doctor's specific order.

General Principles of Traction Maintenance

For skin traction to work correctly, the mechanical setup must remain completely uninterrupted. The core rule is that weights must hang freely at all times. If a weight bag rests on the floor, or if the ropes catch on the bed frame, mattress, or bed linens, the pulling force is instantly lost.

Nurses must routinely inspect the entire apparatus during every shift change. Ropes must sit squarely within the grooves of the pulleys without fraying or knotting. The patient's body alignment should be maintained straight and in line with the pull of the weights. Never lift or remove the weights when repositioning the patient unless specifically ordered by the physician, as doing so immediately triggers painful muscle spasms and disrupts bone alignment.

Nursing Assessment and Neurovascular Checks

A patient in skin traction faces risks of impaired circulation and nerve compression in the affected limb. Nurses perform systematic neurovascular checks—often remembered by the mnemonic of the '5 Ps'—every two to four hours.

  • Pain: Assess for sudden, severe, or unmanaged pain that does not respond to prescribed analgesics, which may indicate compartment syndrome.
  • Pulse: Check distal pulses (like dorsalis pedis or radial) to ensure adequate blood flow.
  • Pallor: Inspect skin color; pale or cyanotic skin signals poor arterial supply.
  • Paresthesia: Ask about numbness, tingling, or burning sensations indicating nerve pressure.
  • Paralysis: Test the patient's ability to wiggle their toes or fingers distal to the traction.

Any sudden deficit requires immediate notification of the physician.

Skin Integrity and Prevention of Complications

Because skin traction applies direct pressure via adhesive tapes, foam wraps, or boots, the underlying skin is highly vulnerable to breakdown and pressure ulcers. The nurse must inspect the skin underneath straps and devices daily, ensuring the skin is clean, dry, and free of blisters. In many facilities, foam boots are briefly removed according to protocol—with another staff member supporting the limb's alignment—to check vulnerable pressure points like the heel, ankle bones, and calf.

Complications extend beyond the skin. Prolonged immobility increases the risk of deep vein thrombosis, pneumonia, and constipation. Nurses encourage incentive spirometry, leg exercises for the unaffected limbs, adequate hydration, and prophylactic anticoagulation as ordered.

Patient Education and Psychosocial Support

Being confined to bed with restricted movement is mentally and emotionally taxing for patients. They often feel helpless, frustrated, and anxious about their recovery timeline. Nurses play a crucial role by explaining every aspect of the traction setup, reassuring the patient that temporary discomfort is normal, and teaching them how to assist with permissible movements, such as using the overhead trapeze bar to lift their hips slightly for bedpan use.

Education should also involve family members so they understand why they must never tamper with the weights or position of the bed. Offering diversional activities, ensuring easy access to personal items, and maintaining open communication greatly alleviate anxiety and foster a more positive healing environment.
