Skin Traction

Introduction and Purpose of Skin Traction

Skin traction is a temporary orthopedic intervention used to apply a pulling force to a fractured extremity through the soft tissues using adhesive or non-adhesive tapes, foam boots, or slings. Unlike skeletal traction, which relies on pins or wires inserted directly into the bone, skin traction is non-invasive and places less demand on sterile theatre setups. In Nigerian clinical practice, it is frequently utilized in district hospitals and secondary health facilities as an interim measure to stabilize long bone fractures—most notably femoral shaft fractures—while patients await definitive surgical fixation or when traction equipment for skeletal application is unavailable. The primary goals of applying skin traction include reducing muscle spasms around the fracture site, immobilizing the limb to prevent further soft tissue injury, and correcting or preventing skeletal deformities. Because the maximum safe pulling weight for skin traction is generally limited to about 4.5 kilograms (10 pounds) to prevent skin breakdown, its ability to achieve full bone reduction is restricted compared to skeletal methods. Understanding this weight limitation prevents excessive loading that could shear the epidermis away from the underlying fascia.

Common Types and Equipment Setup

Several specific devices are utilized to deliver skin traction depending on the affected anatomical site. Proper setup ensures the pulling force remains aligned with the long axis of the bone. - **Russell's Traction**: Often used for femoral fractures, utilizing a system of pulleys and a knee sling to support the thigh while pulling longitudinally. - **Buck's Extension**: A straightforward, straight-line traction applied to the lower extremity, commonly used for hip fractures or knee immobilization prior to surgery. - **Bryant's Traction**: Vertical suspension traction utilized primarily for congenital hip dislocations or femoral fractures in infants and young children under three years old. - **Head Halter**: A cervical traction device utilizing a padded chin and occipital wrap, connected to weights to relieve muscle spasms in the cervical spine.

Core Principles of Traction Maintenance

Effective traction therapy depends on maintaining uninterrupted mechanical alignment. Even minor shifts in equipment can compromise fracture reduction and increase patient discomfort. | Mechanical Principle | Clinical Action | Purpose | |---|---|---| | **Continuous Pull** | Weights must hang freely at all times | Ensures constant therapeutic tension | | **Line of Pull** | Ropes must align with the long axis of the bone | Prevents angular deformity at the fracture site | | **Counter-traction** | Elevate the foot of the bed or use body weight | Prevents the patient from sliding down the bed | | **Friction Reduction** | Keep weights off the floor and bed linens clear | Preserves the exact calculation of pulling force |

Neurovascular Assessment and Circulation Checks

The application of bandages, foam boots, or strapping around a limb creates a risk of localized compression. Regular neurovascular assessments protect the extremity from ischemic injury and permanent nerve damage. The nursing evaluation focuses on five key parameters, often remembered as the '5 Ps': Pain, Pallor, Pulses, Paresthesia, and Paralysis. Assess peripheral pulses distal to the traction site every two to four hours, comparing the affected limb with the unaffected side. Skin temperature should feel warm, and capillary refill time ought to be brisk—less than three seconds. Report any report of severe, unremitting pain or a burning sensation beneath the traction tapes immediately, as these symptoms frequently indicate impending compartment syndrome or localized pressure ischemia.

Skin Integrity and Complication Prevention

Prolonged immobility combined with the friction of adhesive tapes or foam straps places patients at high risk for skin breakdown. Inspect the underlying skin regularly by gently loosening or peeling back non-adhesive components according to protocol, while ensuring the fracture remains supported. Keep the skin clean and completely dry, as moisture trapped beneath straps softens the epidermis and accelerates maceration. Encourage frequent shifting of unconstrained body parts, implement pressure-relieving mattress overlays, and provide adequate nutritional support rich in protein and vitamin C to promote tissue resilience. Watch closely for allergic reactions or contact dermatitis triggered by rubber-based adhesives, swapping to hypoallergenic alternatives when irritation develops.
