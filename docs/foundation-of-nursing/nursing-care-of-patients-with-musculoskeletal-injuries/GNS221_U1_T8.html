Skin Traction

Introduction to Skin Traction

Skin traction is a temporary orthopaedic intervention used to apply a pulling force to a fractured or diseased limb through the skin and soft tissues. Unlike skeletal traction, which utilizes pins or wires surgically inserted directly into the bone, skin traction relies on adhesive or non-adhesive tapes, foam boots, or slings attached externally to the affected extremity. Attached weights hanging freely off the bed apply a continuous, gentle traction force. In Nigerian clinical settings, where resources might sometimes necessitate careful improvisations or reliance on standard traction kits like Buck's extension, understanding the mechanics of skin traction is fundamental for student nurses. The primary goal is to manage muscle spasms, immobilize a body part, and maintain bone alignment temporarily before definitive surgical fixation or as part of conservative management. Because the force is transmitted through the skin, the amount of weight that can be safely applied is strictly limited—usually not exceeding 5 kilograms (10 to 15 pounds)—to prevent skin breakdown, blistering, and severe tissue ischemia. Nursing students must recognize that skin traction is never meant to achieve complete bone reduction in major fractures, but rather to stabilize the extremity, relieve painful muscle spasms that accompany bone displacement, and maintain correct anatomical length. Vigilant monitoring and proper setup are essential to ensure the weights do not touch the floor and that the patient's body weight acts as a natural counter-traction by positioning the bed correctly.

Purposes and Indications in Musculoskeletal Care

In musculoskeletal nursing care, skin traction serves several critical therapeutic and preparatory purposes. The most common indication is the relief of painful, involuntary muscle spasms that frequently occur following a fracture, such as a fracture of the proximal femur or hip. When a bone breaks, surrounding muscles reflexively contract to splint the area, causing bone ends to override each other, which intensifies pain and causes soft tissue damage. The gentle, continuous pull of skin traction fatigues these spasming muscles, allowing them to relax and helping to restore or maintain normal limb length and alignment. Additionally, skin traction is frequently utilized as a temporary immobilization measure before definitive surgical management, such as an open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) for hip fractures, thereby reducing preoperative discomfort and preventing further deformity. It may also be used in cases of mild contractures, low back pain caused by muscle guarding, or specific pediatric orthopedic conditions where gentle stretching and alignment are required. For nursing students in clinical practice, recognizing these indications helps in understanding why a patient is ordered traction and what therapeutic responses to evaluate during shifts. Effective traction application translates directly to reduced patient distress, minimized risk of secondary neurovascular injury from jagged bone ends, and a stabilized limb awaiting specialized surgical intervention.

Types of Skin Traction and Equipment

Various types of skin traction and specialized equipment are utilized depending on the clinical presentation and anatomical site involved. The most widely known form is Buck’s extension traction, frequently used for lower limb injuries such as hip fractures, acetabular fractures, or pre-operative knee immobilization. Buck's traction typically involves foam traction boots secured around the lower leg, connected to a spreader bar, rope, and a set of hanging weights that travel freely over a pulley system attached to the bed frame. Another variation is Russell traction, which incorporates a sling under the knee and an overhead pulley to allow some knee flexion while maintaining horizontal pulling force. Overhead or vertical traction, such as Bryant's traction, is occasionally used in pediatric practice for congenital hip dislocations or femur fractures in infants under a specific weight threshold, where the child's buttocks are slightly lifted off the mattress by the traction weights. Nursing students must be thoroughly familiar with all components of the equipment: ropes must be unfrayed and running freely in the center of the pulley grooves, weights must hang completely free without touching the floor or the bed frame, and spreader bars must not press against the patient's foot or heel. Any obstruction to the pulley or weights immediately neutralizes the traction force, rendering the treatment ineffective and compromising patient comfort and safety.

General Principles of Traction Maintenance

Maintaining effective skin traction requires strict adherence to biomechanical and clinical principles. The fundamental rule of traction maintenance is that the line of pull must remain uninterrupted and aligned with the long axis of the bone. Nurses must ensure that ropes run smoothly through the center of pulley grooves and that knots are tied securely and do not slip or catch on the pulleys. Counter-traction is another critical principle: the patient's body weight acts as the natural counter-pull, meaning the bed should typically be maintained in a flat position or in reverse Trendelenburg if ordered, and the bed frames must not be altered without considering its effect on traction tension. Under no circumstances should nursing staff remove or lift the weights to reposition the patient, as this instantly removes the traction force, triggers severe muscle spasms, and disrupts bone alignment. If weights must be temporarily supported during specific nursing interventions—such as sliding a bedpan underneath—another healthcare provider must manually maintain the traction pull to avoid sudden jarring movements. Furthermore, routine checks must confirm that the foam boot or adhesive tape is neither too tight to restrict circulation nor too loose to slip off the skin. Regular collaboration with the multidisciplinary team, including physiotherapists and orthopaedic surgeons, ensures that equipment settings remain optimal throughout the course of conservative or pre-operative care.

Nursing Assessment and Neurovascular Checks

Comprehensive nursing assessment of a patient in skin traction centers heavily on frequent neurovascular evaluations of the affected extremity. Because skin traction applies external pressure via wraps or boots, and because underlying trauma causes swelling, patients are at high risk for compromised blood flow and nerve function. The nurse must perform and document neurovascular checks—commonly remembered by the '5 Ps' (Pain, Pallor, Pulses, Paresthesia, and Paralysis)—at least every two to four hours, or more frequently during the initial postoperative or post-application phase. Checking distal pulses (such as the dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial pulses for lower limb traction) ensures adequate arterial perfusion, while assessing capillary refill time and skin temperature provides immediate insight into peripheral circulation. Paresthesia (numbness or tingling) and motor function (ability to wiggle toes or fingers) help detect early nerve compression or impending compartment syndrome, a serious medical emergency. Pain assessment is equally critical; while baseline discomfort is expected from the injury, sudden, deep, throbbing pain that is unrelieved by prescribed analgesics or pain that worsens with passive movement of the digits is a major red flag indicating excessive pressure or neurovascular compromise. Student nurses must immediately report abnormal neurovascular findings to the physician, as prompt intervention can prevent permanent tissue necrosis, nerve damage, or limb loss.

Skin Integrity and Prevention of Complications

Maintaining skin integrity is one of the most demanding nursing responsibilities when caring for a patient in skin traction. Because traction straps, adhesive tapes, or foam boots exert constant contact and pressure on the skin, and because the patient's mobility is severely restricted, the risk for pressure ulcers and skin tears is remarkably high. Student nurses must inspect the skin beneath and around traction devices regularly by carefully lifting or loosening straps per institutional protocol, ensuring that skin is clean, dry, and free of blistering, redness, or shearing forces. Bony prominences, such as the heel, malleoli, and Achilles tendon, are particularly vulnerable and require protective padding, repositioning aids, or specialized mattresses. Good skin hygiene involves gentle washing, thorough drying without vigorous rubbing, and avoiding the application of harsh chemical irritants under adhesive tapes. If tape-based skin traction is used, nurses must monitor for allergic contact dermatitis, skin stripping during tape removal, and friction burns caused by slippage of the traction wrap. Frequent turning schedules must be maintained within the allowable limits of the traction apparatus—often utilizing slight lateral shifting or bed mobilization techniques that do not compromise the line of pull—to relieve pressure across the sacrum, hips, and shoulders, thereby safeguarding the patient's overall integumentary health.

Patient Education and Psychosocial Support

Patient education and psychosocial support are vital components of holistic nursing care for individuals undergoing skin traction. Immobilization can be extremely frustrating, frightening, and physically uncomfortable, often leading to feelings of helplessness, anxiety, or depression. Nurses must establish a therapeutic rapport, actively listening to patient concerns and explaining the purpose of every equipment adjustment, such as why weights must never be touched or why bed movement is restricted. Involving family members in the education process, particularly in resource-constrained environments where family support is integral to patient care, helps build a collaborative care network. Patients should be taught to recognize and promptly report warning signs like numbness, tingling, sudden pain changes, or slipping traction boots. Furthermore, nurses must address the complications of prolonged immobility—such as constipation, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and respiratory stasis—by encouraging deep breathing exercises, active range of motion for unaffected limbs, adequate hydration, and high-fiber diets. Providing diversional activities, ensuring easy access to call bells and personal items, and offering reassurance help reduce psychological distress. By combining meticulous physical maintenance with empathetic psychosocial care, nursing students can significantly improve the patient's coping mechanisms and overall recovery experience while in skin traction.
