Plaster of Paris (POP)

Introduction and Definition of Plaster of Paris

Plaster of Paris, commonly abbreviated as **POP**, is a rigid dressing used extensively in orthopaedic nursing to immobilize fractured bones and correct skeletal deformities. Chemically, it is calcined gypsum, manufactured by heating calcium sulfate dihydrate to drive off water of crystallization, creating calcium sulfate hemihydrate. When water is reintroduced, an exothermic reaction occurs, causing the powder to recrystallize and harden into a solid cast. In Nigerian tertiary hospitals, POP remains a popular choice for fracture management due to its affordability and durability compared to synthetic fiberglass alternatives. However, the exothermic setting reaction requires careful monitoring, as excessive heat can cause thermal injury to vulnerable skin underneath if proper padding techniques are ignored.

Purposes and Indications for POP Application

Rigid immobilization serves multiple therapeutic and prophylactic functions in trauma and orthopaedic care. The primary objective is to maintain anatomical alignment of reduced fractures while soft tissue healing takes place. Beyond fracture management, casts are utilized to: - **Immobilize injured joints**: Prevents movement to reduce acute pain and prevent further soft tissue damage. - **Correct congenital deformities**: Gradually reposition structures in conditions such as clubfoot through serial casting. - **Support weak limbs**: Provide structural stability following tendon repairs or severe nerve injuries. - **Promote wound healing**: Protect underlying skin grafts or osteomyelitis sites while allowing controlled access.

A successful casting procedure relies on gathering and organizing all necessary supplies prior to touching the patient. Step-by-Step Procedure for POP Application

1. **Assess and prepare the limb**: Clean and dry the skin, checking for any preexisting open wounds or abrasions that require sterile dressings. 2. **Apply stockinette**: Roll the tubular stockinette over the limb, ensuring it extends several centimeters beyond both ends of the planned cast. 3. **Wrap cast padding**: Apply soft padding in overlapping spiral turns, overlapping each turn by half the width. Extra layers are placed over bony prominences. 4. **Immerse the POP bandage**: Submerge the roll vertically in tepid water until bubbling stops, indicating saturation. Gently squeeze excess water from the ends without wringing or twisting. 5. **Apply the plaster**: Roll the wet bandage smoothly around the limb, maintaining even tension. Do not pull tightly, as a drying cast shrinks slightly. 6. **Smooth and mold**: Rub the layers together with the palms of the hands to laminate them into a unified, strong structure. Mold carefully around anatomical contours. 7. **Clean and finish**: Trim the stockinette and fold it back over the cast edges to create a soft border before the plaster fully hardens.

Post-Application Nursing Care and Monitoring

Immediate nursing vigilance after cast application prevents catastrophic ischemic complications. Because a drying cast is inflexible, any internal swelling can rapidly compromise blood flow. - **Positioning**: Elevate the casted limb above the level of the heart using pillows to promote venous return and minimize edema. - **Drying**: Expose the cast to circulating air. Support wet casts on flat palms or pillows rather than hard surfaces to avoid flat spots that press on the skin. - **Neurovascular checks**: Assess color, warmth, capillary refill, peripheral pulses, sensory function, and motor movement every 30 minutes for two hours, then hourly. - **Cast inspection**: Monitor for strike-through, where blood or drainage seeps through the plaster, and outline the stain with a marker to track expansion.

Complications Associated with Plaster of Paris

Rigid immobilization carries inherent risks that demand prompt recognition by nursing staff. 1. **Compartment Syndrome**: Increased pressure within an enclosed fascial space compromises circulation and nerve function. Signs include pain on passive stretch, paresthesia, pallor, pulselessness, and paralysis. 2. **Pressure Sores**: Caused by rough cast edges, foreign objects inserted inside the cast, or inward indentations from improper molding. Patients report a burning sensation or localized hot spot. 3. **Skin Breakdown and Infection**: Moisture trapped under the cast macerates the skin, creating an ideal environment for bacterial growth and foul odors. 4. **Joint Stiffness and Muscle Atrophy**: Prolonged immobility leads to loss of muscle mass and restricted range of motion, requiring extensive rehabilitation post-removal.

Patient Education and Home Care Guidelines

Empowering patients and their caregivers ensures safe recovery outside the clinical setting. Clear instructions prevent common self-care errors that compromise fracture healing. - **Keep the cast dry**: Protect the plaster from water, as moisture softens the material and ruins structural integrity. Use plastic bags sealed with rubber bands during bathing. - **Never insert objects**: Instruct patients that rulers, pencils, or knitting needles must never be pushed down the cast to scratch an itch, as this easily breaks the skin and causes severe infection. - **Report danger signs**: Teach patients to immediately report numbness, tingling, bluish nail beds, foul odors, or unexplained wetness. - **Maintain joint mobility**: Encourage active movement of uncasted joints, such as wiggle fingers or toes regularly to stimulate circulation.

Removal of Plaster of Paris

Cast removal occurs once radiological evidence confirms adequate bone healing. Specialized equipment is required to dismantle the rigid structure safely without injuring the underlying skin. The removal process utilizes an electric cast saw with an oscillating blade. Unlike a spinning circular saw, an oscillating blade vibrates back and forth at high speed, cutting through the rigid, brittle plaster while remaining harmless to soft, resilient skin. The nurse reassures the patient, explaining that the loud buzzing noise is normal and that the blade only vibrates against the skin without cutting. After the plaster is split using spreader instruments, the underlying padding and stockinette are cut away with bandage scissors. The limb is then gently washed and moisturized, as the skin will appear dry, scaly, and covered in accumulated dead epidermal cells.
