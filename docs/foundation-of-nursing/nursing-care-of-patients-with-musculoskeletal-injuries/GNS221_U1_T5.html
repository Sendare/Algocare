Plaster of Paris (POP)

Introduction and Definition of Plaster of Paris

Plaster of Paris, commonly abbreviated as POP, is a quick-setting gypsum plaster consisting of a fine white powder (calcium sulfate hemihydrate) that hardens when moistened and allowed to dry. When water is added, the dry powder undergoes an exothermic chemical reaction, reverting back to calcium sulfate dihydrate and forming a rigid, solid mold. Nurses frequently encounter POP in orthopedic wards and emergency rooms because it remains a reliable, cost-effective method for immobilizing fractured bones and supporting injured joints during healing.

The setting process is exothermic, meaning it releases heat as it hardens. Nursing students must understand this chemical property because excessive heat can cause thermal burns to the patient's skin if improper water temperature or poor technique is used. Unlike modern synthetic fiberglass casts, POP is heavier, takes longer to dry completely, and breaks down if it gets wet. However, its superior moldability makes it the material of choice for complex fracture reductions where exact anatomical contouring is vital.

Purposes and Indications for POP Application

  • Fracture Immobilization: Holds broken bone ends in proper alignment to promote steady healing and prevent displacement.
  • Joint Support: Rests severely sprained, strained, or inflamed joints to reduce painful movement and swelling.
  • Deformity Correction: Gradually corrects congenital or acquired skeletal deformities, particularly in pediatric orthopedic care.
  • Soft Tissue Protection: Shields open wounds, skin grafts, or surgical repairs on extremities from accidental trauma during recovery.

Equipment and Materials Needed

Before starting a POP application procedure, the nurse must gather and organize all necessary supplies to ensure a smooth, efficient process. Incomplete preparation can lead to delays while the plaster is already setting.

  • POP Bandages: Rolls of various widths appropriate for the specific limb size.
  • Basin of Water: Luke-warm water; hot water accelerates setting dangerously, while cold water delays it.
  • Stockinette: Tubular cotton material placed directly against the skin to protect it from rough plaster.
  • Cast Padding (Webril): Synthetic or cotton padding wrapped over the stockinette to cushion bony prominences.
  • Protective Gear: Gloves and plastic aprons, as wet plaster is messy and hard to remove from clothing.
  • Scissors and Shear Equipment: For trimming stockinette and padding edges neatly.

Step-by-Step Procedure for POP Application

  1. Preparation and Positioning: Explain the procedure to the patient, expose the affected limb, and position the patient comfortably. Ensure the joint is held in the correct functional alignment throughout.
  2. Skin Protection: Slip the tubular stockinette over the limb, ensuring it extends several inches past the planned cast edges. Wrap the cast padding smoothly, overlapping by fifty percent, with extra padding over bony prominences like the heel or elbow.
  3. Soaking the Bandage: Submerge the POP bandage vertically into the lukewarm water until bubbling stops completely. Gently squeeze both ends of the roll to remove excess water without wringing or twisting it.
  4. Application: Roll the wet plaster smoothly around the limb, overlapping each turn by half the width of the bandage. Never pull tight, as a tight wrap causes dangerous constriction as the plaster sets.
  5. Finishing Edges: Fold the stockinette and padding outward over the cast edges to create a smooth, soft cushion against the patient's skin. Mold the cast gently using the palms of your hands, never your fingertips, to avoid creating indentations that press into the skin.
  6. Drying: Leave the cast uncovered in a well-ventilated area until it is fully dry and hard.

Post-Application Nursing Care and Monitoring

Once the cast is applied, vigilant nursing care begins. The newly applied cast is soft, heavy, and vulnerable to damage. Nurses must support wet casts on pillows rather than hard surfaces to prevent flat spots from forming while the plaster cures.

Neurovascular assessments are the highest priority in the immediate post-application phase. The nurse must check the extremity using the 5 Ps: Pain, Pallar, Pulses, Paresthesia, and Paralysis. Assess capillary refill on the nail beds of the fingers or toes. If refill is sluggish or absent, or if the patient complains of a tight, burning pain that is not relieved by prescribed analgesics, report it immediately because compartment syndrome may be developing.

Complications Associated with Plaster of Paris

Plaster of Paris therapy carries several well-documented risks that nurses must monitor for and prevent:

  • Compartment Syndrome: Increased pressure inside an enclosed muscle fascia compartment, cutting off blood supply. It causes excruciating pain, loss of pulse, and paralysis.
  • Pressure Sores: Caused by rough cast edges, wrinkles in padding, or finger indentations pressing persistently on bony areas.
  • Skin Breakdown and Infection: Moisture trapped under a damaged or cracked cast creates a breeding ground for bacteria, leading to foul odors and skin maceration.
  • Joint Stiffness and Muscle Atrophy: Prolonged immobilization inevitably leads to muscle wasting and reduced joint range of motion after cast removal.
  • Thermal Burns: Excessive heat generated during the setting phase if incorrect water temperature or thick padding is used.

Patient Education and Home Care Guidelines

Empowering patients with clear home care instructions prevents accidental cast ruin and dangerous complications. The nurse must teach the patient to keep the cast completely dry because water causes POP to soften and crumble. If bathing, the limb must be protected inside heavy plastic bags sealed securely at the top.

Patients must never insert foreign objects like pencils, coat hangers, or knitting needles down the cast to scratch an itch; this easily breaks the skin and causes deep, hard-to-treat infections. Instead, they can use a hair dryer on a cool setting to blow air down the cast. Finally, patients need a clear list of warning signs to report immediately, including foul odors, cracking of the cast, severe pain, or persistent numbness.

Removal of Plaster of Paris

Removing a Plaster of Paris cast is performed when healing is confirmed by follow-up X-rays or when the treatment plan changes. Nurses play a vital role in preparing the patient, as the removal process can be intimidating due to the noise and vibration of the cast saw.

The nurse should explain that the electric cast saw uses an oscillating blade rather than a spinning circular saw. The blade vibrates back and forth rather than spinning rapidly, which means it cuts through the hard, rigid plaster by vibrating against it, but it will not cut soft human skin if it makes contact. Protective eye goggles are given to the patient to guard against flying plaster dust. Once the plaster is split open on both sides using the saw, cast spreaders are used to open the shell gently, and bandage scissors cut away the underlying padding and stockinette. After removal, the skin often appears dry, scaly, and pale with accumulated dead skin cells; the nurse should instruct the patient to wash the limb gently with warm water and soap without scrubbing aggressively.
