Active Range of Motion Exercise

Introduction to Active Range of Motion Exercises

Active Range of Motion (**AROM**) refers to movement of a joint through its complete plane of motion entirely by the voluntary contraction of the muscles acting across that joint. Unlike passive exercises where an external force moves the limb, AROM requires conscious neuromuscular control, active muscle work, and adequate skeletal integrity from the individual. This type of therapeutic movement is foundational in physical rehabilitation and routine nursing care, serving as a bridge between complete bedrest and independent ambulation. When a patient executes these movements, they engage the central and peripheral nervous systems, reinforcing neural pathways responsible for voluntary motor function.

Purposes and Clinical Benefits of AROM

Regular execution of AROM yields numerous physiological and psychological advantages for bedridden or recovering patients. Clinicians prescribe these movements to maintain joint flexibility, prevent joint contractures, and preserve the functional capacity of muscle fibers. - **Circulatory enhancement**: Muscle contraction acts as a natural pump, improving venous return and reducing deep vein thrombosis risk. - **Bone density preservation**: Weight-bearing and active muscle pull stimulate osteoblast activity, slowing disuse osteoporosis. - **Cardiorespiratory conditioning**: Light physical exertion elevates heart rate and improves ventilation. - **Psychological uplift**: Regaining independent movement boosts patient confidence and morale during long hospital admissions.

Indications and Contraindications

Proper clinical judgment ensures exercises help rather than harm. AROM is indicated for patients who retain the ability and cognitive awareness to voluntarily contract muscles across joints, such as individuals recovering from mild strokes, generalized weakness, or orthopedic surgeries where active movement is permitted. Conversely, strict contraindications exist. AROM must be withheld if movement disrupts acute healing fractures, causes severe unmanaged pain, or risks tearing freshly repaired tendons and ligaments. In Nigerian clinical settings, nurses must carefully verify the orthopedic or surgical team's specific activity orders before initiating any movement regimen in the ward.

Pre-Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before guiding a patient through AROM, a thorough baseline assessment prevents adverse incidents. The nurse evaluates the patient's level of consciousness, baseline vital signs, pain score, and existing joint limitations. Communication is paramount; explaining the procedure in a language the patient understands—such as English, Pidgin, or local Nigerian languages like Yoruba, Igbo, or Hausa—gains cooperation and reduces anxiety. Preparing the physical environment involves adjusting the hospital bed to a comfortable working height, ensuring adequate lighting, and providing privacy with bedside screens.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Execution

1. **Hand Hygiene and Privacy**: Wash hands and close curtains to maintain infection control and patient dignity. 2. **Positioning**: Place the patient in a supine or comfortable sitting position with proper body alignment. 3. **Proximal Support**: Support the joint proximal to the area being exercised with one hand to stabilize the body part. 4. **Systematic Movement**: Guide the patient to move each joint smoothly through flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and rotation as anatomically appropriate. 5. **Repetition**: Repeat each movement gently five to ten times, respecting individual patient fatigue thresholds. 6. **Observation**: Watch the patient's facial expressions and verbal feedback continuously for signs of distress.

Safety Precautions and Complications

Safety remains the central priority during active exercises. The nurse must stop the session immediately if the patient reports sharp pain, dizziness, shortness of breath, or chest discomfort. Forcing a joint past its natural physiological resistance can tear muscle fibers or damage joint capsules. In busy Nigerian hospital wards with limited personnel, nurses must ensure beds have raised side rails and call bells are within reach before leaving a recovering patient post-exercise. Never compromise patient stability by rushing through exercise routines.

Documentation and Evaluating Patient Response

Accurate nursing documentation completes the care cycle. Following the exercise session, the nurse records the specific joints exercised, the number of repetitions completed, the patient's overall tolerance, and any adverse symptoms like pain or fatigue. Evaluating patient response involves comparing post-exercise vital signs and mobility levels against baseline assessments. Clear notes in the patient's chart ensure continuity of care among nursing shifts and provide objective data for the multidisciplinary healthcare team.
