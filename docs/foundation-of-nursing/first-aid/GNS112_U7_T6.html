Bites and Stings

Introduction and Clinical Significance of Bites and Stings

Bites and stings represent a diverse category of acute trauma that frequently presents in emergency departments and primary health clinics across Nigeria. From the venomous snakes encountered in rural agrarian communities to aggressive arthropods in urban settings, these injuries demand prompt recognition and systematic nursing intervention. The clinical severity ranges from mild, localized discomfort to rapidly fatal systemic toxicity or overwhelming allergic reactions. Understanding the underlying mechanisms of exposure allows nursing professionals to prioritize care effectively and mitigate the risk of long-term tissue damage or mortality.

Pathophysiology of Venoms, Toxins, and Toxins

Venoms and animal toxins are complex biochemical cocktails containing enzymes, peptides, and non-protein constituents that disrupt normal human physiology. Depending on the species involved, the pathological process generally targets one of three major systems: - **Cytotoxins**: Cause localized tissue destruction, necrosis, and hemolysis by breaking down cellular membranes. - **Neurotoxins**: Intersect with neuromuscular junctions or the central nervous system, leading to paralysis, respiratory failure, and cranial nerve palsies. - **Hemotoxins**: Disrupt the coagulation cascade, causing consumptive coagulopathy, spontaneous bleeding, or widespread thrombosis.

Assessment and Triage of Affected Patients

A systematic triage approach is essential for patients presenting with suspected bites or stings. The primary survey must immediately evaluate airway patency, breathing efficacy, and circulatory stability, particularly when an IgE-mediated reaction or potent neurotoxin is suspected. Following stabilization, a focused secondary assessment examines the local injury site. Nurses should document the exact time of the incident, the suspected animal species, the progression of swelling or pain, and any systemic symptoms such as nausea, dizziness, or paresthesia. In snakebite cases, marking the leading edge of edema with a pen alongside the time provides an objective metric for tracking local venom spread.

Management of Insect Stings and Localized Reactions

Most insect stings from bees, wasps, hornets, and ants cause localized pain, redness, and edema that can be managed effectively without complex interventions. For honeybee stings, the stinger should be gently scraped off the skin using a flat edge rather than squeezed with tweezers, as squeezing can inject remaining venom from the attached sac. Applying a cold compress reduces local pain and slows systemic absorption by causing vasoconstriction. Elevation of the affected extremity minimizes dependent edema, while oral antihistamines and mild analgesics provide symptomatic relief for itching and discomfort.

Emergency Care for Snakebites and Mammal Bites

Snakebites and mammalian bites carry high risks of severe tissue damage, systemic poisoning, and infectious complications. For snakebites, historical practices such as incision, suction, application of tight tourniquets, and ice application are strictly contraindicated because they worsen local tissue necrosis and ischemia. The cornerstone of specific medical treatment is the timely administration of antivenom when indicated by systemic or rapidly progressive local signs. For mammalian bites—such as those from dogs, cats, or humans—aggressive wound irrigation with copious sterile saline or clean water is paramount to reduce bacterial load. Rabies prophylaxis and tetanus update status must be evaluated immediately for any animal bite.

Recognizing and Treating Anaphylactic Shock

Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction that can occur rapidly after an insect sting or animal bite. Clinical presentation involves multi-system involvement, including cutaneous flushing, urticaria, angioedema, respiratory distress via bronchospasm or laryngeal edema, and cardiovascular collapse manifested by hypotension and tachycardia. The absolute first-line pharmacological treatment is **epinephrine**, administered intramuscularly into the anterolateral thigh. Nurses must ensure rapid access to emergency airway equipment, initiate intravenous fluid resuscitation, and position the patient flat with lower extremities elevated unless respiratory distress is severe.

Complications, Secondary Infections, and Wound Care

Wounds resulting from bites and stings are inherently prone to complications due to inoculated pathogens and compromised local tissue perfusion. Mammal bites frequently introduce polymicrobial flora, including *Pasteurella* species, leading to rapidly developing cellulitis, abscess formation, or osteomyelitis. Necrotizing fasciitis and compartment syndrome are severe surgical emergencies that can arise from cytotoxic snakebites or infected puncture wounds. Nursing care involves regular wound inspections, sterile dressing changes, monitoring distal neurovascular status, and administering prescribed antibiotics and tetanus toxoid boosters.

Patient Education and Prevention Strategies

Preventative education is a vital nursing responsibility, particularly in endemic regions where outdoor work and rural living expose individuals to hazardous fauna. Patients and communities should be advised to wear protective footwear, use insect repellents containing DEEP or appropriate alternatives, and avoid disturbing tall grass, rock piles, or hollow logs where snakes and insects shelter. Individuals with documented venom allergies must be educated on carrying an autoinjector epinephrine device, wearing medical alert identification, and recognizing early signs of systemic reactions so they can seek immediate medical attention.
