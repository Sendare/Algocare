Haemorrhage

Definition and Overview of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is defined as the escape of blood from the vascular system, occurring when the integrity of a blood vessel wall is compromised by trauma, disease, or surgical incision. Total blood volume in an adult is approximately 70 to 75 millilitres per kilogram of body weight, meaning an average adult holds about 5 litres of blood. The body possesses intrinsic mechanisms to halt bleeding spontaneously through hemostasis, which involves vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and the coagulation cascade. When blood loss overwhelms these compensatory mechanisms, tissue perfusion drops, depriving vital organs of oxygen and nutrients. Clinical severity depends on the volume of blood lost, the rate at which the bleeding occurs, and the age and baseline health status of the individual. In Nigerian clinical practice, acute traumatic blood loss from road traffic accidents represents a major presentation in emergency departments, requiring rapid evaluation to prevent catastrophic circulatory collapse.

Classification of Haemorrhage by Vessel Type and Location

  • Arterial haemorrhage: Characterized by bright red blood that spurts rhythmically in sync with the heartbeat, reflecting high pressure within the arterial system. This type leads to rapid volume depletion.
  • Venous haemorrhage: Features dark red blood that flows in a steady, continuous stream under lower pressure, making it generally easier to control than arterial bleeding.
  • Capillary haemorrhage: Involves a slow, oozing flow of blood from minor superficial vessels, commonly seen in simple abrasions and minor cuts.
  • External haemorrhage: Occurs when blood escapes visibly through a break in the skin or via natural body orifices.
  • Internal haemorrhage: Takes place within closed body cavities or tissues, such as the cranial vault, thorax, abdomen, or retroperitoneal space, where blood loss is hidden and difficult to quantify immediately.

Pathophysiology and Stages of Hypovolemic Shock

Hypovolemic shock ensues when a critical reduction in intravascular volume causes inadequate tissue perfusion and cellular hypoxia. The physiological response unfolds across distinct stages as the body fights to preserve perfusion to vital organs like the brain and heart.

  1. Compensated shock: Blood loss reaches up to 15 percent of total volume. Baroreceptors detect falling blood pressure, triggering sympathetic nervous system activation. Vasoconstriction, tachycardia, and catecholamine release maintain normal blood pressure, though the skin may feel cool and pale.
  2. Decompensated shock: Blood loss ranges from 15 to 40 percent. Compensatory mechanisms begin to fail. Blood pressure drops significantly, tachycardia worsens, tachypnea develops, and peripheral pulses weaken. Urine output drops sharply as renal blood flow is diverted.
  3. Irreversible shock: Blood loss exceeds 40 percent. Prolonged cellular hypoxia leads to widespread cellular death, microvascular thrombosis, and multi-organ dysfunction syndrome. At this stage, even aggressive fluid resuscitation and medical intervention often fail to save the patient.

Clinical Manifestations and Signs of Blood Loss

Recognizing the clinical signs of hemorrhage requires understanding how the body reacts to diminishing blood volume. Initial responses include tachycardia, as the heart beats faster to maintain cardiac output despite lower volume. Peripheral vasoconstriction redirects blood inward, causing pallor in the skin, nail beds, and mucous membranes, alongside cool, clammy skin. As blood loss progresses past 20 percent, systolic blood pressure falls, leading to hypotension. Patients frequently experience tachypnea (rapid, shallow breathing) as the respiratory system attempts to compensate for metabolic acidosis caused by poor tissue oxygenation. Central nervous system changes range from mild anxiety and restlessness in early stages to confusion, lethargy, and eventual unconsciousness as cerebral perfusion declines. Capillary refill time lengthens beyond two seconds, offering a quick bedside indicator of peripheral circulatory shutdown.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring of Bleeding Patients

Systematic nursing assessment is vital for detecting subtle signs of clinical deterioration before irreversible shock sets in. Establish baseline vital signs immediately and repeat them frequently—every 15 minutes for unstable patients. Monitor trends rather than single isolated readings; a rising heart rate coupled with a narrowing pulse pressure (the gap between systolic and diastolic pressure) often signals ongoing hidden bleeding long before overt hypotension appears. Assess neurological status using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale, as declining alertness points directly to reduced cerebral perfusion. Inspect surgical dressings, wound sites, and drainage tubes regularly, measuring and recording all fluid output accurately. Maintain strict intake and output charts, keeping a close eye on hourly urine output via an indwelling catheter to evaluate renal perfusion. Always communicate sudden hemodynamic shifts immediately to the attending medical team.

Emergency Management and First Aid for Haemorrhage

Immediate action during an acute hemorrhage focuses on stopping the bleeding and restoring vital organ perfusion. For external bleeding, apply direct, firm pressure over the wound using a clean pad or sterile dressing. Elevate the bleeding extremity above the level of the heart if no fracture is suspected, as gravity helps slow the blood flow. If direct pressure fails to control severe arterial limb bleeding, apply a tourniquet proximal to the wound, noting the exact time of application. For internal hemorrhage, keep the patient flat, warm, and still to minimize metabolic demand, avoiding any oral intake in anticipation of possible emergency surgery. Secure large-bore intravenous access immediately (typically 16-gauge or 18-gauge cannulae) to facilitate rapid fluid resuscitation with crystalloids such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate, followed by cross-matched blood products as soon as available.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

When first-aid and fluid resuscitation prove insufficient, medical and surgical interventions become mandatory to achieve definitive hemostasis. Surgical exploration involves opening the affected body cavity or wound to directly ligate bleeding vessels using sutures, clips, or electrocautery. Endoscopic procedures are frequently utilized for gastrointestinal bleeding to band varices or inject sclerosing agents. Interventional radiology offers minimally invasive options like transcatheter arterial embolization, where coils or particles are guided via catheter to block bleeding vessels in pelvic or visceral trauma. Pharmacological agents also play a key supportive role; tranexamic acid (TXA) is administered early to inhibit fibrinolysis and stabilize blood clots, while blood component therapy—including packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets—corrects coagulopathy and restores oxygen-carrying capacity.

Potential Complications and Post-Haemorrhage Care

Survivors of severe haemorrhage require vigilant post-resuscitation care to mitigate delayed systemic complications. Acute kidney injury (AKI) frequently develops due to prolonged renal hypoperfusion during the shock phase, demanding careful monitoring of serum creatinine and urine output. Massive blood transfusions carry inherent risks, including transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), electrolyte imbalances such as hypocalcemia caused by citrate preservatives, and transfusion reactions. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) can emerge from severe trauma and shock, consuming clotting factors and paradoxically causing widespread bleeding and microvascular thrombosis. Post-haemorrhage nursing care centers on supporting tissue oxygenation, maintaining normothermia, managing nutritional support as gut function returns, and monitoring for signs of surgical site infection or sepsis.
