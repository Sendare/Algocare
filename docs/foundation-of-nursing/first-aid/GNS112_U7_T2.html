Haemorrhage

Definition and Overview of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is defined as the escape of blood from the vascular system, occurring when blood vessels are damaged by trauma, surgery, disease processes, or coagulation disorders. As a nursing student in Nigeria, understanding haemorrhage is vital because it remains a leading cause of maternal mortality, surgical complications, and trauma-related deaths across clinical settings. Blood volume in a normal adult is approximately 5 litres, and a rapid loss of a significant portion of this volume can lead to circulatory collapse, inadequate tissue perfusion, and ultimately death if prompt interventions are not instituted. Haemorrhage can compromise the delivery of oxygen and essential nutrients to vital organs like the brain, heart, and kidneys, initiating a cascade of compensatory mechanisms. Nurses must be equipped to recognize the early signs of bleeding, understand the underlying causes, and act swiftly to restore homeostasis. Whether dealing with postpartum haemorrhage in a busy obstetric ward in Lagos or managing a traumatic bleed in an emergency department, recognizing haemorrhage promptly is the cornerstone of effective nursing care and patient survival.

Classification of Haemorrhage by Vessel Type and Location

Haemorrhage is classified based on the type of blood vessel involved and its anatomical location. Vessel classification includes arterial, venous, and capillary bleeding. Arterial bleeding is characterized by bright red blood that spurts rhythmically with the heartbeat, reflecting high pressure, and is notoriously difficult to control. Venous bleeding involves dark red blood that flows in a steady, continuous stream under lower pressure. Capillary bleeding oozes slowly from minor cuts and is usually self-limiting. Based on anatomical location, haemorrhage is categorized as external or internal. External haemorrhage is visible, with blood escaping directly through a break in the skin or body orifices. Internal haemorrhage occurs within body cavities or tissues, such as the cranial, thoracic, or abdominal cavities, and is often insidious and difficult to detect without specialized diagnostic tools. Common examples include intracranial bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeds, and concealed postpartum haemorrhage. Recognizing these classifications helps Nigerian nurses anticipate the severity of blood loss, determine the urgency of intervention, and apply appropriate pressure techniques or prepare for emergency surgical exploration depending on the source.

Pathophysiology and Stages of Hypovolemic Shock

When a patient experiences significant blood loss, the body initiates complex physiological compensatory mechanisms to maintain perfusion to vital organs, ultimately leading to hypovolemic shock if uncorrected. Hypovolemic shock progresses through distinct stages. In the compensated stage, blood loss is up to 15% (roughly 750 mL in an adult). The sympathetic nervous system is activated, releasing catecholamines like adrenaline and noradrenaline, causing tachycardia, vasoconstriction of non-essential vascular beds, and cool, pale skin. Blood pressure often remains within normal limits. In the decompensated (or progressive) stage, blood loss reaches 15% to 40% (750–2000 mL). Compensatory mechanisms begin to fail; cardiac output drops, systolic blood pressure falls, tachypnea worsens, and the patient becomes confused, anxious, or lethargic due to cerebral hypoperfusion. The final stage is irreversible shock, characterized by severe tissue hypoxia, cellular necrosis, multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and refractory hypotension leading to cardiac arrest. Understanding these stages allows nursing students to identify early warnings before irreversible damage occurs, guiding rapid fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion therapy.

Clinical Manifestations and Signs of Blood Loss

Clinical manifestations of blood loss vary depending on the speed and volume of haemorrhage, as well as the patient's age and baseline health status. Early signs of acute blood loss include mild tachycardia, anxiety, restlessness, and slight pallor as the body attempts to compensate. As blood volume continues to drop, clinical signs become more pronounced. Tachycardia intensifies, and hypotension develops as compensatory mechanisms fail. The patient's skin becomes cold, clammy, and diaphoretic due to peripheral vasoconstriction. Tachypnea (rapid breathing) occurs as the body tries to compensate for metabolic acidosis resulting from poor tissue oxygenation. Urine output drops significantly (oliguria, less than 30 mL/hour), which is a sensitive indicator of poor renal perfusion. Neurologically, patients may progress from confusion and lethargy to stupor and unconsciousness as cerebral perfusion diminishes. In internal bleeding, additional localized signs may appear, such as abdominal rigidity, distension, haematemesis, melaena, or swelling at a fracture site. Nurses must monitor these signs continuously because subtle changes in vital signs can signal a rapid deterioration in a patient's condition.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring of Bleeding Patients

Nursing assessment and monitoring of a bleeding patient requires a systematic, rapid, and continuous approach. The primary survey follows the ABCDE protocol (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure), ensuring immediate life threats are managed first. For circulation, nurses must assess heart rate, blood pressure, capillary refill time, peripheral pulse quality, and skin temperature. Continuous vital sign monitoring is mandatory, as downward trends in blood pressure combined with upward trends in heart rate are classic indicators of ongoing blood loss. Nurses should establish large-bore intravenous (IV) lines (such as 16-gauge or 18-gauge cannulas) immediately to facilitate rapid fluid resuscitation and blood sampling for baseline investigations, including full blood count (haemoglobin and haematocrit levels), clotting profile, and cross-matching. Insertion of an indwelling urinary catheter is essential for accurate, hourly measurement of urine output, providing objective data on kidney perfusion. Furthermore, nurses must monitor neurological status using tools like the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and perform frequent abdominal and surgical site checks for signs of swelling, tenderness, or re-bleeding. Effective nursing documentation and communication using structured handover tools ensure rapid escalation to medical teams.

Emergency Management and First Aid for Haemorrhage

Emergency management and first aid for external haemorrhage focus on prompt control of bleeding to prevent shock and death. The immediate priority is applying direct pressure to the bleeding site using a clean dressing, sterile pad, or cloth. If direct pressure fails or is insufficient, pressure bandages can be applied securely. Elevation of the bleeding extremity above the level of the heart can assist in reducing blood flow to the area when applicable. If direct pressure and elevation fail to control severe, life-threatening extremity bleeding, the application of a tourniquet proximal to the wound is a life-saving intervention. Nurses and healthcare providers must note the exact time of tourniquet application and communicate this clearly to incoming emergency or surgical teams. For epistaxis (nosebleed), first aid involves sitting the patient upright, leaning forward slightly, and pinching the soft cartilaginous part of the nose for at least 10 to 15 minutes while encouraging mouth breathing. Rescuers must adhere to standard precautions, including wearing gloves and protective eyewear, to prevent blood-borne pathogen transmission. Once bleeding is controlled, stabilizing the patient, administering high-flow oxygen, and preparing for rapid transport or advanced medical intervention are critical next steps.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

Medical and surgical interventions for haemorrhage aim to stop the source of bleeding and restore adequate oxygen-carrying capacity and volume. Medical management includes aggressive fluid resuscitation using crystalloids (such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate) and the administration of blood and blood products, including packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitate, guided by massive transfusion protocols (MTP) in severe cases. Pharmacological agents such as tranexamic acid (TXA) are frequently administered to inhibit fibrinolysis and promote clot stabilization, particularly in trauma and postpartum haemorrhage. Coagulopathies are corrected using specific clotting factors or vitamin K. When conservative measures fail, surgical interventions become necessary. These include exploratory laparotomy or thoracotomy to locate and ligate bleeding vessels, repair of injured organs, application of hemostatic agents, and packing of cavities (e.g., pelvic packing). In obstetric emergencies, procedures like uterine balloon tamponade, B-Lynch sutures, uterine artery ligation, or hysterectomy may be performed to save the mother's life. Interventional radiology techniques, such as transarterial embolization, are also increasingly utilized to occlude bleeding vessels minimally invasively. Nigerian nurses play an essential role in preparing patients for theatre, assisting with blood administration, and monitoring post-intervention recovery.

Potential Complications and Post-Haemorrhage Care

Potential complications following haemorrhage require vigilant post-haemorrhage care by nursing personnel. One of the most severe complications is acute kidney injury (AKI) resulting from prolonged renal hypoperfusion during shock, which can necessitate dialysis. Another critical risk is multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), where secondary tissue hypoxia damages the lungs, liver, and heart. Coagulation disorders, such as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), can develop due to the consumption of clotting factors during massive bleeding and resuscitation. Transfusion-related complications, including transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), and hemolytic transfusion reactions, must be vigilantly watched for during and after blood administration. Post-haemorrhage anemia is common and requires nutritional support with iron, folate, and high-protein diets to aid erythropoiesis. Psychological complications, such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or anxiety related to the traumatic event, should not be overlooked. Post-haemorrhage nursing care involves monitoring vital signs, tracking fluid balance meticulously, inspecting surgical or trauma wounds for infection, encouraging early ambulation when stable, and providing patient and family education regarding recovery, nutrition, and follow-up care.
