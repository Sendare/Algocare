Haemorrhage

Definition and Types of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is the escape of blood from the vascular system due to damaged blood vessels. It can be classified by the type of vessel injured or by where the blood goes in the body.

  • Arterial haemorrhage: Blood is bright red and spurts out in jets matching the heartbeat because it is under high pressure. This type is very dangerous and can cause rapid blood loss.
  • Venous haemorrhage: Blood is dark red and flows in a steady, even stream because veins are under lower pressure.
  • Capillary haemorrhage: Blood oozes slowly from minor cuts and usually stops on its own due to normal clotting.
  • Internal haemorrhage: Bleeding occurs inside the body cavities or tissues, such as the abdomen or chest, where it cannot be seen directly.
  • External haemorrhage: Bleeding is visible on the outside of the body through a break in the skin or a natural opening.

Stages and Pathophysiology of Hypovolemic Shock

When a patient loses a significant amount of blood, the circulating blood volume drops sharply, leading to hypovolemic shock. The body attempts to compensate for this loss to keep vital organs like the brain and heart functioning.

In the early stage, compensatory mechanisms kick in. The sympathetic nervous system releases adrenaline, causing blood vessels to constrict and the heart to beat faster. Blood is redirected away from the skin, kidneys, and gut to protect the brain and heart.

As bleeding continues and exceeds roughly 30 to 40 percent of total blood volume, these compensatory mechanisms fail. Blood pressure drops dangerously low, organs become starved of oxygen, and cells begin to die. Without urgent intervention, this progressive state leads directly to irreversible organ failure and death.

Clinical Manifestations and Signs of Blood Loss

Recognizing the signs of blood loss allows nurses to act before a patient deteriorates into severe shock. As compensation begins, the heart rate increases (tachycardia) and skin becomes pale, cool, and clammy because blood is pulled away from the skin surface.

As blood loss worsens, systolic blood pressure starts to fall, and the patient may feel dizzy, weak, or confused due to reduced oxygen delivery to the brain. Urine output drops significantly because the kidneys receive less blood flow.

A common clinical trap is relying solely on blood pressure readings: young adults can lose a dangerous amount of blood while maintaining a normal blood pressure due to strong compensatory vasoconstriction.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Continuous assessment is vital for any patient at risk of haemorrhage. Nurses must frequently measure vital signs, paying special attention to trends rather than isolated numbers. A rising heart rate paired with a falling blood pressure is a classic warning sign of ongoing bleeding.

Neurological status must be checked regularly using tools like the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale to detect early signs of cerebral hypoxia. Nurses also monitor capillary refill time, skin color and temperature, and strict fluid balance charts. Measuring hourly urine output via an indwelling catheter is one of the most reliable indicators of adequate kidney perfusion and overall fluid status.

Emergency Management and First Aid

Immediate emergency management focuses on stopping the bleeding and restoring tissue perfusion. For external bleeding, apply direct, firm pressure over the wound using a clean pad or bandage. If direct pressure fails on a limb, applying a tourniquet proximal to the wound can save a life.

Position the patient flat on their back to improve blood flow to the brain, unless head or breathing injuries prevent this. Elevating the legs slightly can help return blood from the lower extremities to the central circulation. Keep the patient warm to prevent hypothermia, which interferes with blood clotting, and never attempt to remove embedded objects from a wound.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

Once in a healthcare facility, medical teams focus on aggressive resuscitation and definitive bleeding control. Large-bore intravenous lines are inserted quickly to infuse warmed crystalloid fluids and blood products.

  • Blood transfusion: Replaces lost red blood cells, platelets, and clotting factors to restore oxygen-carrying capacity and stop coagulopathy.
  • Surgical exploration: Performed in an operating theatre to ligate bleeding vessels, repair damaged organs, or pack cavities.
  • Endoscopic therapy: Uses scopes to find and treat gastrointestinal bleeding through cauterization or clipping.
  • Interventional radiology: Uses catheters inside blood vessels to block bleeding arteries with tiny coils or particles.

Complications and Post-Haemorrhage Care

Survivors of severe haemorrhage face several potential complications that require vigilant nursing care. Acute kidney injury can occur due to prolonged low blood flow during the shock state. Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome may develop if hypoxia persists across multiple body systems.

Nursing care during recovery includes monitoring haemoglobin and haematocrit levels, watching for signs of infection at surgical sites or from transfusion reactions, and slowly reintroducing nutrition. Nurses also provide psychological support, as patients and families often experience anxiety or fear following a life-threatening bleeding event.
