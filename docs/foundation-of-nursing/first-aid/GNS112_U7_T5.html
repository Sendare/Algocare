Poisoning

Introduction to Poisoning and Toxicological Principles

Poisoning occurs when any substance—solid, liquid, or gas—interferes with normal body chemistry, causing structural damage or functional disturbance. In toxicology, the fundamental rule is that the dose makes the poison. Almost any substance, including water and oxygen, can become toxic if the quantity consumed overwhelms the body's metabolic pathways.

Toxicokinetics describes what the body does to a poison through absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME). Toxicodynamics describes what the poison does to the body at the cellular and receptor levels. A nurse must understand that a toxin's speed of action depends heavily on its route of entry and chemical formulation. Lipophilic poisons, for instance, readily cross cell membranes and the blood-brain barrier, leading to rapid central nervous system depression.

Classification and Routes of Exposure

Toxins are classified by their source, chemical nature, or intended use, including household cleaning agents, industrial chemicals, agricultural pesticides, pharmaceuticals, and venomous animal bites. Understanding how these substances enter the body dictates how quickly symptoms appear and which emergency measures will work.

  • Ingestion: Swallowing household items, contaminated food, or medication overdoses. This is the most common route in accidental pediatric poisonings.
  • Inhalation: Breathing in toxic gases, carbon monoxide, or chemical fumes, which rapidly cross the alveolar-capillary membrane into the bloodstream.
  • Absorption: Direct contact with skin or mucous membranes, common with agricultural organophosphate pesticides.
  • Injection: Intravenous drug abuse, traditional incisions, or stings and bites from snakes and scorpions.

Etiology and Local Risk Factors in Nigeria

Poisoning presentations in Nigerian healthcare facilities reflect unique socioeconomic, domestic, and occupational realities. Accidental pediatric ingestions frequently involve kerosene stored in old beverage bottles, caustic soda used for local soap making, and traditional herbal remedies administered in unmeasured doses.

Agricultural exposures are prevalent in rural farming communities due to improper storage and handling of organophosphate and carbamate pesticides. Intentional self-poisoning (suicide attempts) often involves herbicides like paraquat or rodenticides containing zinc phosphide. Additionally, snakebites remain a major public health emergency in rural savannah belts, particularly affecting farmers and herdsmen during the rainy season.

Pathophysiology and Systemic Effects

Once a toxin enters circulation, it targets specific organs depending on its chemical properties. Corrosive substances like acids and strong alkalis cause immediate coagulation or liquefactive necrosis upon direct contact with the upper gastrointestinal tract, risking perforation.

Systemic poisons disrupt vital cellular machinery. For example, carbon monoxide binds avidly to hemoglobin, forming carboxyhemoglobin and shifting the oxygen-hemoglobin dissociation curve to the left, which starves tissues of oxygen even when pulse oximetry readings appear deceptively normal. Cyanide blocks cellular respiration by inhibiting cytochrome c oxidase within the mitochondria, forcing cells into anaerobic metabolism and causing profound lactic acidosis.

Emergency Management and Decontamination

The immediate priority in any poisoning case is the ABCDE approach: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure. Never attempt decontamination before ensuring the patient is stable and breathing adequately.

For dermal exposures, immediately remove contaminated clothing and flush the skin with copious amounts of water. If a toxic chemical splashes into the eyes, continuous irrigation with normal saline or clean water must begin immediately for at least 15 to 20 minutes.

Gastrointestinal decontamination has become more selective. Induced vomiting using syrup of ipecac is obsolete and dangerous. Activated charcoal may be given if the patient ingested a binding poison within one hour and has a protected airway. Gastric lavage is rarely performed due to high risks of esophageal perforation and aspiration.

Pharmacological Antidotes and Supportive Care

While supportive care remains the cornerstone of toxicology—managing vitals, electrolytes, and organ function—specific antidotes can rapidly reverse the mechanism of certain poisons. Antidotes work through competitive receptor antagonism, chemical neutralization, or by bypassing blocked metabolic pathways.

  • Naloxone: Competitively blocks opioid receptors to reverse severe respiratory depression.
  • Atropine: Blocks muscarinic receptors to counteract organophosphate-induced cholinergic excess.
  • Acetylcysteine (NAC): Replenishes glutathione reserves to prevent liver necrosis after paracetamol overdose.
  • Antivenom: Binds venom components following venomous snakebites common in agricultural regions.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Monitoring

A comprehensive nursing care plan for a poisoned patient centers on continuous physiological monitoring, safety, and psychological support. Nurses must closely track neurological status using the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), monitor cardiac rhythms via telemetry, and record strict hourly fluid intake and urine output to protect renal function.

For patients recovering from intentional self-poisoning, safety is paramount. Implement suicide precautions, remove hazardous personal items, maintain direct observation, and involve psychiatric support teams before discharge. Clear documentation of all interventions, witness accounts, and recovered container labels is essential for legal and clinical continuity.

Prevention, Education, and Public Health

Nursing responsibility extends far beyond acute hospital treatment into community education and injury prevention. Nurses play a frontline role in teaching families about safe household storage. Toxic agents like kerosene, bleach, and insecticides must never be transferred into empty food or drink bottles, and should be locked away high out of children's reach.

Public health campaigns should also educate farmers on using personal protective equipment when handling agrochemicals and promote safe disposal methods for expired medications. Collaborating with local community leaders helps dispel dangerous traditional first-aid myths, such as forcing caustic substances or dirty concoctions down a poisoned person's throat.
