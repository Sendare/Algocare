Poisoning

Introduction to Poisoning and Toxicological Principles

Poisoning occurs when any substance—solid, liquid, or gas—interferes with normal body chemistry, causing structural damage or functional disturbance. In toxicology, the fundamental principle is that dose makes the poison, meaning virtually any chemical substance can act as a toxin if the quantity absorbed exceeds the body's threshold for safe metabolism and elimination. Understanding toxicokinetics (how the body processes a toxic substance through absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion) allows clinical staff to anticipate how long a patient will remain at risk and when peak systemic concentrations will occur.

Classification and Common Routes of Poisoning

Toxins enter the human body via several distinct physiological pathways, each dictating a different rate of systemic onset and tissue damage.

  • Ingestion: The most frequent route, involving swallowing household chemicals, pharmaceuticals, or contaminated food items.
  • Inhalation: Rapidly transfers gases, vapors, or fine aerosols across the alveolar-capillary membrane directly into the systemic circulation.
  • Absorption: Direct dermal or mucous membrane penetration by lipophilic chemicals, agricultural pesticides, or organic solvents.
  • Injection: Direct introduction into tissue or blood via animal bites, insect stings, or contaminated needles, leading to immediate bioavailability.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Clinical Context

In Nigerian healthcare facilities, poisoning presentations carry distinct epidemiological patterns shaped by socioeconomic factors, domestic practices, and occupational hazards. Accidental ingestions among toddlers frequently involve unsupervised household items stored in recycled beverage bottles, such as kerosene, caustic soda, or local insecticides. Intentional self-harm admissions often utilize readily available agricultural pesticides like organophosphates or aluminium phosphide, driven by economic stress or psychiatric distress. Herbal preparations and traditional remedies purchased from unregulated vendors also account for a significant share of acute renal and hepatic toxicity cases due to heavy metal contamination or unverified botanical dosages.

Clinical Assessment and Toxidromes

A toxidrome is a constellation of clinical signs and symptoms that strongly suggests a specific class of poison. Recognizing these patterns enables rapid diagnosis even before laboratory panels are finalized.

  • Cholinergic: Diarrhea, urination, miosis, bronchospasm, emesis, lacrimation, and salivation (DUMELS), commonly seen with organophosphates.
  • Anticholinergic: Blind as a bat (mydriasis), mad as a hatter (delirium), red as a beet (flushed skin), hot as a hare (hyperthermia), and dry as a bone (dry skin).
  • Opioid: CNS depression, respiratory depression, and pinpoint pupils (miosis).
  • Sympathomimetic: Hypertension, tachycardia, mydriasis, agitation, and diaphoresis, typical of amphetamines or cocaine.

Emergency Management and Decontamination

Initial care for any poisoned patient follows the standard ABCDE resuscitation framework: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure. Once hemodynamic stability is secured, decontamination procedures aim to minimize further absorption. Gastrointestinal decontamination has evolved significantly; routine gastric lavage is rarely performed due to high complication risks like aspiration pneumonia or esophageal perforation. Activated charcoal remains useful if administered within one hour of ingesting certain toxins, as it adsorbs chemicals in the gut lumen. Surface decontamination requires copious water irrigation for dermal exposures, taking care to protect healthcare personnel from secondary contamination.

Pharmacological Antidotes and Supportive Care

While only a small fraction of poisons have specific pharmacological antidotes, those that do rely on targeted mechanisms: blocking receptor sites, chemically binding the toxin, or bypassing blocked metabolic pathways. For instance, naloxone competitively reverses opioid receptor blockade, while atropine blocks muscarinic receptors to counteract excessive acetylcholine in organophosphate poisoning. Supportive care remains the cornerstone of toxicology; maintaining adequate tissue perfusion, managing electrolyte derangements, controlling seizures with benzodiazepines, and supporting respiratory function save far more lives than antidotes alone.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Monitoring

Clinical nursing care for the poisoned patient requires vigilant, continuous monitoring of neurological status, cardiac rhythm, and respiratory efficiency. Nurses must accurately record fluid balance, as aggressive intravenous hydration or forced diuresis may be ordered for specific renally excreted poisons. For patients admitted after intentional self-harm, constant observation and environmental safety checks are non-negotiable to prevent self-inflicted harm while awaiting psychiatric evaluation. Documentation must include precise timelines of interventions, exact descriptions of any expelled substances or vomitus, and serial vital sign trends.

Prevention, Education, and Public Health Strategies

Mitigating the burden of poisoning requires robust community outreach and public health education. Nursing professionals play a pivotal role in teaching families about safe chemical storage—emphasizing that toxic agents should never be transferred into empty food or drink containers. Public campaigns must also raise awareness regarding the dangers of unverified herbal concoctions and promote proper disposal of expired household pharmaceuticals. Strengthening poison information networks and advocating for strict regulatory control over hazardous agricultural chemicals further reduce accidental and intentional poisonings nationwide.
