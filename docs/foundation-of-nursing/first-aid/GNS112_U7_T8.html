Priorities of Emergency Management

Introduction to Emergency Nursing and Triage

Emergency nursing requires rapid decision-making under high-pressure conditions. When multiple casualties arrive at a Nigerian health facility simultaneously, waiting in a traditional queue can cost lives. Triage solves this by sorting patients based on clinical urgency rather than arrival time. The goal is doing the greatest good for the greatest number using available resources.

Most emergency departments use a structured five-tier system:

  • Resuscitation: Immediate life-saving intervention needed for cardiac arrest or severe trauma.
  • Emergent: High risk, confused, or severe pain requiring treatment within 10-15 minutes.
  • Urgent: Stable but complex problems needing evaluation within 30-60 minutes.
  • Semi-urgent: Simple conditions like minor lacerations that can wait up to 2 hours.
  • Non-urgent: Chronic or minor ailments suitable for general clinic outpatient care.

The Primary Survey and Resuscitation (ABCDE Approach)

The primary survey provides a systematic framework to identify and manage immediate threats to life. It follows the ABCDE mnemonic: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure. Every step must be addressed in strict alphabetical order. If an airway obstruction is found, the clinician stops and clears it before moving on to check breathing. Resuscitation measures occur concurrently as each life-threatening condition is discovered.

In emergency settings, assuming a stable patient without checking can lead to sudden deterioration. The nurse acts as an active observer, constantly reassessing vital parameters. Nigerian emergency units often face resource constraints, making this rapid mental checklist vital for directing scarce oxygen and intravenous fluids to the sickest individuals first.

Airway Management and Cervical Spine Protection

Airway patency takes absolute precedence in trauma care. Obstruction can result from the tongue falling back in an unconscious patient, foreign bodies, facial fractures, or laryngeal edema. Standard initial maneuvers include the chin-lift and jaw-thrust techniques, with the jaw-thrust preferred when cervical spine injury is suspected because it avoids neck extension.

Basic mechanical aids like oro-pharyngeal or naso-pharyngeal airways help maintain an open passage until advanced airway placement occurs. Whenever trauma involves high-impact mechanisms like road traffic crashes or falls, assume a cervical spine injury is present. Apply manual in-line stabilization immediately during any airway manipulation to prevent irreversible spinal cord damage.

Breathing and Ventilation Assessment

Once the airway is secured, evaluate breathing and ventilation. Look for chest symmetry, use of accessory muscles, and respiratory rate. Auscultate bilateral lung fields to confirm equal air entry and check for absent breath sounds that might indicate a pneumothorax.

Life-threatening conditions discovered during this phase include tension pneumothorax, flail chest, and massive hemothorax. A tension pneumothorax causes progressive air accumulation in the pleural space, shifting the mediastinum and collapsing the vena cava. This leads to rapid cardiovascular collapse requiring immediate needle decompression. Oxygen should be administered aggressively to all trauma and resuscitation patients to maintain adequate tissue oxygenation.

Circulation, Hemorrhage Control, and Shock Management

Circulatory assessment focuses on evaluating tissue perfusion and identifying sources of bleeding. Assess pulse rate, rhythm, skin color, temperature, and capillary refill. Rapid, thready pulses and cool, clammy skin often indicate early shock.

Control external hemorrhage immediately using direct firm pressure and elevation. If bleeding persists on a limb despite pressure, apply a tourniquet proximal to the wound. Internal hemorrhage in the chest, abdomen, or pelvis is harder to detect but equally lethal; monitor for signs of internal blood loss such as abdominal distension or pelvic instability. Establish large-bore intravenous access promptly to initiate fluid resuscitation or blood replacement.

Disability and Neurological Evaluation

The disability assessment is a brief neurological evaluation. The AVPU scale offers a quick framework:

  • A: Alert
  • V: responds to Vocal stimuli
  • P: responds to Painful stimuli
  • U: Unresponsive

For more detailed findings, use the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), which assesses eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. A falling GCS score indicates worsening intracranial pressure, hypoxia, or progressive shock. Check pupillary light reflexes simultaneously; unequal or sluggishly reacting pupils can signal serious neurological injury or brain herniation.

Exposure, Environmental Control, and the Secondary Survey

The final step of the primary survey is exposure. Remove the patient's clothing completely to inspect the entire body for hidden injuries, rashes, or deformities. Because trauma patients lose body heat rapidly due to shock and cold intravenous fluids, preventing hypothermia is crucial. Cover the patient with warm blankets immediately after examination.

Once the primary survey is complete and life threats are managed, begin the secondary survey. This involves a head-to-toe physical examination, taking a detailed history (AMPLE: Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, Events preceding injury), and performing more detailed diagnostic tests.

Documentation, Communication, and Handover in Emergencies

Clear communication and accurate documentation maintain patient safety during emergency care transitions. When handing over a patient to the intensive care unit or surgical team, use a standardized tool like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). This ensures vital details are not omitted.

Documentation must be factual, chronological, and concise. Record the exact time interventions were performed, response to medication, and serial vital signs. In busy Nigerian emergency wards, written records protect both the patient and the healthcare professional by establishing a clear timeline of clinical decisions.
