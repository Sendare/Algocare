Priorities of Emergency Management

Introduction to Emergency Nursing and Triage

Emergency nursing requires rapid decision-making under high pressure. When multiple patients arrive simultaneously, nurses must sort them based on the severity of their illness or injury. This process is called triage. The goal of triage is to ensure that critically ill patients who need immediate, life-saving interventions are treated first.

Most emergency departments use a structured system, such as a five-level triage scale. Patients are quickly assessed using vital signs, presenting complaints, and general appearance. Those with unstable airways or uncontrolled bleeding are placed at the highest priority level, while individuals with minor injuries wait safely until resources permit care. Clinical prioritization is the backbone of safe emergency care.

The Primary Survey and Resuscitation (ABCDE Approach)

The Primary Survey is a systematic approach used to identify and treat immediate threats to life. It follows the ABCDE framework, moving sequentially from the most critical system to the next. Every emergency nurse must memorize this sequence because missing a step can lead to rapid patient deterioration.

  • Airway: Check for patency and clear obstructions.
  • Breathing: Assess rate, effort, and oxygenation.
  • Circulation: Evaluate pulses, bleeding, and perfusion.
  • Disability: Perform a quick neurological check.
  • Exposure: Undress the patient to find hidden injuries while preventing hypothermia.

Airway Management and Cervical Spine Protection

An open airway is the absolute first priority in emergency care because a person can survive only a few minutes without oxygen. Nurses must assess for signs of airway obstruction, such as choking, gurgling sounds, or the presence of foreign bodies. If the patient is unconscious, the tongue can fall back and block the pharynx.

In trauma cases, a nurse must always suspect a neck injury. Therefore, cervical spine protection must be maintained during any airway maneuver. The jaw-thrust maneuver is preferred over the head-tilt/chin-lift technique when a cervical injury is suspected, as it avoids bending the neck.

Breathing and Ventilation Assessment

Once the airway is secure, the nurse must immediately evaluate breathing and ventilation. Watching the chest rise and fall is not enough. The nurse listens for breath sounds, checks the respiratory rate, and observes for symmetrical chest expansion.

Critical conditions like a tension pneumothorax can develop rapidly, causing air to trap in the chest cavity, compress the heart, and lead to cardiac arrest. Nurses look for absent breath sounds on one side, tracheal deviation, and severe respiratory distress. Quick recognition and assistance with needle decompression or chest tube insertion can save a patient's life.

Circulation, Hemorrhage Control, and Shock Management

Circulation assessment involves checking peripheral pulses, skin color, temperature, and capillary refill time. Uncontrolled bleeding can lead to hypovolemic shock within minutes. Direct, firm pressure should be applied immediately to external bleeding sites. If direct pressure fails, a tourniquet may be required for severe extremity hemorrhage.

In the emergency setting, nurses must quickly establish large-bore intravenous lines to replace lost fluids and blood products. Signs of shock include cool, clammy skin, rapid and weak pulses, and a dropping blood pressure. Early recognition stops the progression from simple blood loss to multi-organ failure.

Disability and Neurological Evaluation

The disability assessment is a quick neurological check performed during the primary survey. Nurses often use the AVPU mnemonic to grade responsiveness: Alert, Verbal, Pain, or Unresponsive. For a more detailed assessment, the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is used to evaluate eye opening, verbal response, and motor response.

In addition to level of consciousness, the nurse checks the pupils for size, shape, and reaction to light. Unequal pupils or a sudden drop in the GCS score indicate worsening brain injury, intracranial bleeding, or severe hypoxia. Prompt reporting of these changes is vital for neurosurgical intervention.

Exposure, Environmental Control, and Secondary Survey

The final step of the primary survey is exposure. The nurse must cut away clothing to inspect the entire body for hidden injuries, wounds, or rashes that might otherwise be missed. However, leaving a patient exposed can rapidly lead to hypothermia, which worsens bleeding and clotting disorders. Nurses must use warm blankets and thermal management devices.

Once life-threatening conditions are managed, the nurse begins the secondary survey. This involves a head-to-toe physical examination, taking a detailed medical history using the SAMPLE mnemonic (Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events leading to injury), and obtaining full baseline vital signs.

Documentation, Communication, and Handover in Emergencies

Clear communication is just as vital as clinical interventions in a crisis. Emergency nurses must provide structured handovers when transferring care to the intensive care unit or operating theater. Tools like ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) help organize critical information so nothing is left out.

Documentation in emergencies must be concise, factual, and time-stamped. Because events unfold rapidly, recording interventions as they occur prevents memory lapses and maintains a legal, chronological record of patient care. Effective teamwork relies on honest, direct communication among all members of the resuscitation team.
