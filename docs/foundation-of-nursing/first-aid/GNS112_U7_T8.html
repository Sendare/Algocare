Priorities of Emergency Management

Introduction to Emergency Nursing and Triage

Emergency nursing is a fast-paced, high-stakes specialty requiring rapid critical thinking and decisive action. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where emergency departments often face heavy patient loads and resource limitations, efficient triage is the cornerstone of safe care. Triage involves sorting patients based on the severity of their illness or injury to ensure that those with life-threatening conditions receive immediate medical attention. The Emergency Triage and Treatment (ETAT) protocol and the South African Triage Scale (SATS) are examples of systems utilized to categorize patients into priority levels, ranging from immediate resuscitation to routine care. As a nursing student, understanding triage means recognizing that priorities must be constantly re-evaluated. You must master the ability to quickly scan a patient, identify immediate threats to life, and initiate care using a systematic approach. Triage is not a one-time event; it is a continuous dynamic process that happens from the moment a patient arrives at the health facility until they are safely admitted, transferred, or discharged. Effective triage optimizes patient outcomes, prevents overcrowding, and ensures the best possible utilization of available human and material resources in emergency departments across Nigeria.

The Primary Survey and Resuscitation (ABCDE Approach)

The foundational framework for managing any critically ill or injured patient is the Primary Survey, systematically executed through the ABCDE approach: Airway with cervical spine immobilization, Breathing and ventilation, Circulation and hemorrhage control, Disability (neurological status), and Exposure/Environment control. This structured methodology allows nurses and other members of the healthcare team to identify and immediately treat life-threatening conditions before moving on to less urgent problems. In emergency nursing, you must address whatever failure you find in the current letter before moving to the next. For instance, if a patient has an obstructed airway (A), you do not assess breathing (B) or circulation (C) until the airway is completely cleared and secured. Resuscitation measures happen concurrently with the primary survey; as soon as a life-threatening issue is identified, interventions are initiated immediately. This approach prevents cognitive overload and ensures that critical priorities are never missed, even in chaotic resuscitation rooms. Mastering the ABCDE approach is essential for every nursing student preparing to work in accident and emergency units, as it forms the universal language of trauma and acute medical resuscitation.

Airway Management and Cervical Spine Protection

Airway management is the absolute first priority in emergency care because a blocked airway causes hypoxia, brain damage, and death within minutes. When assessing the airway, nurses must look for signs of obstruction such as stridor, gurgling, foreign bodies, or facial trauma. In patients with suspected trauma—common from road traffic crashes on Nigerian roads—cervical spine protection must be applied simultaneously to prevent permanent spinal cord injury. Manual in-line stabilization (MILS) should be maintained while opening the airway using safe maneuvers like the jaw-thrust rather than the head-tilt/chin-lift if cervical spine injury is suspected. Basic airway adjuncts such as oropharyngeal (Guedel) airways or nasopharyngeal airways can be utilized to maintain patency in unconscious patients. If these measures fail, or if the patient is unable to protect their own airway, advanced airway interventions like endotracheal intubation or surgical cricothyroidotomy may be required. As a nursing student, you must be proficient in preparing suction equipment, oxygen delivery systems, and assisting senior clinicians during emergency intubation while ensuring the cervical collar remains correctly fitted and secure.

Breathing and Ventilation Assessment

Once the airway is secured, the next priority in the primary survey is evaluating breathing and ventilation (B). Ensuring a patent airway does not guarantee that adequate gas exchange is occurring. The nurse must quickly inspect, auscultate, and palpate the chest. Look for chest symmetry, symmetrical rise and fall, use of accessory muscles, and respiratory rate and depth. Auscultate breath fields bilaterally to confirm equal air entry and check for absent or diminished sounds which may indicate conditions like a pneumothorax or hemothorax. Palpate the chest wall for tenderness, subcutaneous emphysema, or tracheal deviation, which is a hallmark sign of a tension pneumothorax requiring urgent needle decompression. Administer high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask to all trauma and critically ill patients until oxygen saturation can be verified using pulse oximetry. If a patient is in respiratory arrest or severe respiratory failure, bag-valve-mask (BVM) ventilation must be initiated immediately. As a nursing student, recognizing abnormal breathing patterns and acting swiftly can save a patient from progressing to cardiac arrest.

Circulation, Hemorrhage Control, and Shock Management

Circulation and hemorrhage control (C) represent the third vital step in the primary survey. Uncontrolled hemorrhage is one of the leading preventable causes of death in emergency and trauma settings. Nurses must immediately locate and stop external bleeding by applying direct firm pressure, pressure dressings, or tourniquets when extremity bleeding is life-threatening. Simultaneously, evaluate the patient's circulatory status by checking central and peripheral pulses for rate, rhythm, and quality, assessing skin color, temperature, and moisture, and measuring capillary refill time. Poor perfusion combined with tachycardia, altered mental status, and low blood pressure strongly indicates shock, commonly hemorrhagic shock in trauma patients or septic/cardiogenic shock in medical emergencies. To restore circulating volume, quickly establish large-bore intravenous (IV) lines (16-gauge or 18-gauge) and initiate warmed isotonic crystalloid infusions or blood products as prescribed. Monitoring urine output via an indwelling urinary catheter is an essential indicator of end-organ perfusion during shock management. As a nurse, prompt recognition of circulatory failure and aggressive fluid resuscitation save lives.

Disability and Neurological Evaluation

The 'D' in the primary survey stands for Disability, which involves a rapid neurological evaluation to assess the patient's level of consciousness and brain function. A quick and widely used tool for this is the AVPU scale: Alert, Verbal, Pain, Unresponsive. If a more detailed score is required, the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is utilized to evaluate eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. A dropping GCS score indicates worsening neurological status, increasing intracranial pressure, or systemic hypoxia and shock. Additionally, nurses must check the pupils for size, equality, and reactivity to light, as fixed or unequal pupils can signal severe brain injury, herniation, or neurological disease. Blood glucose testing must also be performed immediately during the disability assessment, as hypoglycemia can mimic stroke, head injury, or altered mental status and is easily corrected with intravenous dextrose. As an emergency nurse, documenting baseline neurological function and detecting subtle changes rapidly can guide life-saving neurosurgical or medical interventions.

Exposure, Environmental Control, and the Secondary Survey

The final step of the primary survey is Exposure and Environmental Control (E). To thoroughly examine a trauma patient for hidden injuries, lacerations, burns, or deformities, the patient's clothing must be fully removed, often by cutting it away. However, exposing the patient places them at high risk for hypothermia, which can worsen coagulopathy and acidosis in trauma patients—forming the deadly 'triad of death.' Therefore, nurses must simultaneously practice environmental control by using warm blankets, warming IV fluids, and increasing room temperature. Once life-threatening conditions identified in the primary survey are managed and the patient is stabilized, the healthcare team transitions to the Secondary Survey. This is a comprehensive head-to-toe physical examination, including taking a detailed medical history using the SAMPLE mnemonic (Signs/symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events leading to the illness or injury). The secondary survey ensures no subtle fractures, soft-tissue injuries, or hidden medical conditions are missed before definitive care or transfer.

Documentation, Communication, and Handover in Emergencies

Clear documentation, effective communication, and structured handover are vital components of emergency management that ensure continuity of care and patient safety. In the chaotic environment of an emergency department, verbal communication among multidisciplinary team members—doctors, nurses, laboratory scientists, and pharmacists—must be concise, accurate, and closed-loop (repeating back instructions). Written documentation must be prompt, factual, and timestamped, capturing every assessment finding, intervention, medication administered, and the patient's response to treatment. When transferring a patient to the intensive care unit, operating theater, or another healthcare facility, nurses must use a standardized handover tool such as ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). ISBAR provides a structured format that prevents vital information from being omitted during the transition of care. As a nursing student, mastering professional communication and meticulous record-keeping safeguards legal protection, promotes teamwork, and directly improves clinical outcomes for patients presenting with emergency health conditions.
