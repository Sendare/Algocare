Principles of Emergency Management

Introduction to Emergency Nursing and Triage

Emergency nursing requires rapid decision-making under high-pressure conditions. The primary gatekeeper of any emergency department is triage, the systematic sorting of patients based on the severity of their illness or injury rather than their order of arrival. Nurses must quickly identify individuals who are deteriorating and require immediate life-saving interventions.

  • Emergency Severity Index (ESI): A common five-level triage tool categorizing patients from level 1 (most urgent, like cardiac arrest) to level 5 (least urgent, like a simple rash).
  • Immediate vs Delayed: Patients with compromised airways, severe bleeding, or altered mental status bypass waiting rooms instantly.
  • Continuous Reassessment: Triage is not a one-time event; a stable patient in the queue can deteriorate and must be monitored regularly.

The Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The primary survey is a rapid, systematic approach designed to identify and immediately treat life-threatening conditions. Every emergency nurse relies on the standard ABCDE mnemonic to structure this initial evaluation.

  • A - Airway: Check if the airway is open and clear. Listen for obstruction, gurgling, or stridor.
  • B - Breathing: Assess chest rise, breath sounds, and respiratory rate. Administer oxygen if needed.
  • C - Circulation: Check pulses, capillary refill, skin color, and control any major external bleeding.
  • D - Disability: Perform a quick neurological check using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale.
  • E - Exposure: Remove clothing to inspect for hidden injuries while protecting the patient from hypothermia.

Initial Stabilization and Airway Management

Once immediate threats are found during the primary survey, stabilization begins without delay. Airway management is the most critical step in this phase. An obstructed or unprotected airway leads to hypoxia and brain death within minutes.

Basic maneuvers like the head-tilt chin-lift or jaw-thrust open the upper airway. Nasopharyngeal and oropharyngeal airways keep the passage clear in unconscious individuals. When a patient cannot maintain their own airway or fails to oxygenate, advanced interventions such as endotracheal intubation or surgical cricothyroidotomy become necessary. Alongside the airway, establishing large-bore intravenous access ensures that fluids and emergency drugs can reach the central circulation rapidly.

Shock Recognition and Fluid Resuscitation

Shock is a life-threatening condition where tissue perfusion is severely inadequate, starving cells of oxygen and vital nutrients. Recognizing the early warning signs saves lives before irreversible organ damage sets in.

  • Hypovolemic Shock: Caused by severe blood or fluid loss, common in trauma or severe dehydration from gastroenteritis.
  • Signs: Cold, clammy skin, tachycardia, low blood pressure, and delayed capillary refill.
  • Fluid Resuscitation: Rapid administration of isotonic crystalloids like normal saline or Ringer's lactate through large-bore IV lines restores intravascular volume.

A common clinical mistake is waiting for blood pressure to drop before treating shock; blood pressure often remains normal initially due to intense compensatory vasoconstriction.

The Secondary Survey and Comprehensive Assessment

The secondary survey begins only after the patient is stable and life-threatening ABCDE issues have been addressed. It involves a head-to-toe physical examination and the collection of a detailed medical history.

Nurses use the SAMPLE history mnemonic to gather vital background information efficiently:

  • S: Signs and symptoms currently present.
  • A: Allergies to medications or foods.
  • M: Medications currently taken at home.
  • P: Past medical or surgical history.
  • L: Last oral intake (crucial if surgery is needed).
  • E: Events leading up to the emergency.

This comprehensive assessment helps uncover hidden injuries, fractures, or medical conditions that were missed during the fast primary survey.

Emergency Pharmacotherapy and Administration

Administering medications in an emergency setting demands absolute precision and speed. Nurses must calculate dosages correctly under immense stress and deliver drugs via routes that guarantee rapid absorption.

  • Intravenous (IV) Route: The gold standard in emergencies for immediate systemic drug distribution.
  • Intraosseous (IO) Route: Used when IV access is impossible, allowing direct infusion into bone marrow.
  • Common Emergency Drugs: Epinephrine for cardiac arrest and severe allergic reactions, atropine for symptomatic bradycardia, and naloxone for opioid overdoses.

Double-checking calculations with a colleague and verifying allergies prevents fatal medication errors in chaotic resuscitation rooms.

Legal, Ethical, and Documentation Standards

Emergency nursing operates in a high-stress legal environment. Nurses must balance urgent clinical duties with strict adherence to professional ethics and legal obligations.

  • Consent: In life-threatening emergencies where patients are unconscious, implied consent allows healthcare providers to render necessary treatment without explicit permission.
  • Documentation: Accurate, time-stamped records are mandatory. If an intervention is not documented, legally, it was not done.
  • Patient Autonomy: Competent adults retain the right to refuse treatment, requiring nurses to explain risks without abandoning the patient.

Maintaining confidentiality and upholding professional standards protect both the patient's rights and the nurse's license.
