Principles of Emergency Management

Introduction to Emergency Nursing and Triage

Emergency nursing requires rapid decision-making under high-pressure conditions. The core objective is swift identification of life-threatening conditions through structured sorting protocols. Triage acts as the frontline sorting mechanism, matching patient acuity to available resources, especially crucial in resource-constrained Nigerian tertiary health centers where casualty numbers frequently overwhelm physical space. - **Emergency Severity Index (ESI)**: A five-tier algorithm categorizing patients by acuity and predicted resource needs, ranging from level 1 (resuscitation) to level 5 (non-urgent). - **Mass Casualty Incident (MCI) Triage**: Uses the **START** (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) protocol, which categorizes victims using respiration, perfusion, and mental status within sixty seconds per patient. - **Golden Hour**: The critical window immediately following traumatic injury where prompt surgical or medical intervention drastically improves survival rates.

The Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach in Resuscitation

The primary survey provides a systematic framework to identify and immediately manage life-threatening injuries. This approach follows a strict chronological order, addressing the most immediately fatal conditions first. If an abnormality is found at any step, intervention occurs immediately before moving to the next letter. 1. **A - Airway with Cervical Spine Protection**: Ensure a patent airway while preventing excessive movement of the cervical spine in suspected trauma. 2. **B - Breathing and Ventilation**: Assess chest rise, breath sounds, and respiratory rate, administering high-flow oxygen as indicated. 3. **C - Circulation and Hemorrhage Control**: Evaluate peripheral pulses, capillary refill, and skin color while actively stopping external bleeding. 4. **D - Disability**: Perform a rapid neurological check utilizing the **AVPU** scale or **Glasgow Coma Scale** alongside pupillary light response. 5. **E - Exposure and Environmental Control**: Fully expose the patient to locate hidden injuries while preventing hypothermia with warm blankets.

Initial Stabilization and Airway Management

Maintaining airway patency remains the single most critical intervention in emergency care. Obstruction can lead to irreversible brain hypoxia within minutes. Basic airway maneuvers include head-tilt/chin-lift (in non-trauma cases) or jaw-thrust (when cervical spine injury is suspected). Oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways assist in keeping the tongue from occluding the pharynx. Advanced management involves endotracheal intubation or surgical cricothyroidotomy when upper airway trauma, severe burns, or profound unconsciousness prevents adequate ventilation. Suctioning equipment must always remain functional and ready at the bedside to clear secretions, blood, or emesis.

Shock Recognition and Fluid Resuscitation

Shock is a state of cellular and tissue hypoxia resulting from reduced oxygen delivery, inadequate consumption, or abnormal utilization. Recognizing early signs—such as subtle tachycardia, delayed capillary refill, and anxiety—prevents progression to irreversible organ failure. | Type of Shock | Primary Underlying Cause | Common Clinical Presentation | |---|---|---|> | Hypovolemic | Loss of blood or body fluids | Flat neck veins, cool skin, hypotension | | Cardiogenic | Pump failure from myocardial infarction | Elevated JVP, pulmonary crackles, weak pulse | | Septic | Severe systemic infection and vasodilation | Fever, warm flushed skin initially, bounding pulses | | Anaphylactic | Severe allergic reaction | Bronchospasm, urticaria, angioedema | Fluid resuscitation restores intravascular volume, typically utilizing isotonic crystalloids like normal saline or Ringer's lactate. In hemorrhagic shock, aggressive over-resuscitation can dislodge fragile clots; balanced blood product replacement is preferred where available.

The Secondary Survey and Comprehensive Patient Assessment

Once the primary survey is completed and life threats are stabilized, the secondary survey begins. This entails a head-to-toe physical examination and acquisition of a detailed medical history using the **SAMPLE** mnemonic: - **S**: Signs and symptoms - **A**: Allergies - **M**: Medications currently taken - **P**: Past medical history - **L**: Last oral intake - **E**: Events leading up to the illness or injury Diagnostic investigations such as baseline blood tests, radiography, and focused bedside ultrasonography are executed during this phase. Continuous monitoring of vital signs ensures that any deterioration is caught immediately.

Emergency Pharmacotherapy and Drug Administration

Emergency medication administration requires absolute precision, speed, and knowledge of pharmacokinetics. Intravenous access is the preferred route; when peripheral veins collapse during shock, intraosseous (IO) access serves as a rapid, life-saving alternative for fluid and drug delivery. High-alert emergency medications—such as adrenaline in cardiac arrest or anaphylaxis, atropine in bradycardia, and anticonvulsants in status epilepticus—demands strict dosage double-checking. Rapid calculation errors in emergency settings can be fatal, making standardized weight-based formulas and resuscitation charts indispensable tools in every emergency bay.

Multidisciplinary Communication and Crisis Resource Management

Emergency departments function in chaotic, high-stress environments where clear communication prevents fatal errors. Crisis Resource Management (CRM) principles emphasize leadership, role clarity, closed-loop communication, and situational awareness. During a resuscitation, a designated team leader assigns distinct roles—such as airway manager, medication administrator, and chest compressor—ensuring no task is duplicated or missed. Closed-loop communication requires the sender to give an order, the receiver to repeat the order back confirming understanding, and the sender to acknowledge completion. This simple verbal loop prevents misinterpretations common during high-adrenaline resuscitations.

Legal, Ethical, and Documentation Standards in Emergency Care

Emergency nursing operates under intense legal and ethical scrutiny. The principle of beneficence mandates that nurses act in the patient's best interest, while emergency doctrine often implies consent when an unconscious patient faces imminent loss of life or limb. Documentation must be contemporaneous, factual, and accurate; in emergency litigation, if an intervention is not documented, legally it was never performed. In Nigerian practice, forensic evidence preservation is vital when managing victims of gunshot wounds, assaults, or road traffic accidents. Police report requirements must never delay life-saving emergency medical interventions, adhering to the national law that prioritizes stabilization over administrative or legal formalities.
