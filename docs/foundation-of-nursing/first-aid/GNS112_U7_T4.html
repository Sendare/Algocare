Fractures

Introduction, Classification, and Causes of Fractures

A fracture is a break or disruption in the structural continuity of a bone. When the physical force applied to a bone is greater than the bone's own strength, it cracks or breaks completely.

  • Closed fracture: The bone is broken, but the overlying skin remains intact with no open wound.
  • Open fracture: The broken bone pierces through the skin, creating an open wound and a high risk for severe infection.
  • Complete fracture: The break goes all the way across the bone, dividing it into two or more distinct pieces.
  • Incomplete fracture: The bone is cracked or buckled, but not completely separated into parts.
  • Displaced fracture: The bone pieces are out of alignment and no longer sit in their normal positions.
  • Nondisplaced fracture: The bone cracks, but the pieces remain lined up properly.

Trauma is the leading cause of fractures, usually resulting from motor vehicle accidents, falls, or direct blows during sports. However, pathological fractures occur when a disease weakens the bone structure, making it break under normal daily stress without any major trauma.

Pathophysiology and Bone Healing Stages

When a bone breaks, the body immediately begins a complex healing cascade. Understanding this process helps nurses monitor recovery and identify abnormal delays.

  1. Hematoma formation: Blood vessels bleed into the fracture site, forming a clot within the first 24 to 72 hours.
  2. Fibrocartilaginous callus formation: Granulation tissue replaces the clot, and fibroblasts lay down a soft bridge of cartilage and collagen.
  3. Bony callus formation: Osteoblasts deposit immature bone, turning the soft bridge into a hard, calcified bridge over three to four weeks.
  4. Remodeling: Osteoclasts absorb excess bone while osteoblasts lay down solid, compact bone to restore the original shape and strength.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

Assessing a fractured extremity requires a systematic approach to detect classic signs of injury and monitor for vascular or nerve compromise.

  • Pain and tenderness: Caused by muscle spasms, direct tissue trauma, and movement of bone fragments.
  • Deformity: Caused by the force of the injury and the pull of surrounding muscles on the broken bone.
  • Loss of function: The patient cannot properly use or bear weight on the affected limb due to instability and pain.
  • Swelling and bruising: Result from local bleeding and inflammation in the soft tissues around the break.
  • Crepitus: A grating or crunching sound heard or felt when the broken bone ends rub together.

Nurses perform frequent neurovascular assessments using the five Ps: Pain, Pallor, Pulses, Paresthesia, and Paralysis. A decline in pulses or the onset of severe numbness signals impaired blood flow or nerve damage that requires immediate medical intervention.

Emergency Management and Immediate Care

Initial care at the scene of an injury aims to prevent further damage, reduce pain, and preserve blood flow to the affected limb.

  1. Ensure safety: Check the scene for dangers and stabilize the patient's airway, breathing, and circulation.
  2. Control bleeding: Apply direct pressure to any open wounds using clean dressings to prevent excessive blood loss.
  3. Immobilize the joint: Splint the bone in the position it was found, including the joints immediately above and below the break.
  4. Apply cold therapy: Use ice packs wrapped in a cloth to reduce swelling and numb the local pain.
  5. Elevate: If possible and without causing extra pain, raise the injured limb above the level of the heart to minimize edema.

A common mistake is trying to straighten a deformed limb. Nurses and first responders should never manipulate or push a protruding bone back under the skin, as this can sever nearby blood vessels and nerves.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

Treatment depends on the location and severity of the fracture, aiming to restore proper bone alignment and stability so healing can occur.

  • Closed reduction: A non-surgical manual realignment of the broken bone fragments back into place using traction and manipulation under anesthesia.
  • Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF): A surgical procedure to open the fracture site and align the bone, securing it with metal plates, screws, pins, or rods.
  • External fixation: A surgical frame placed outside the limb with pins inserted through the skin directly into the bone, often used for complex open fractures with severe soft tissue damage.
  • Traction: The application of a pulling force to stretch and align a fractured body part, using weights and pulleys to reduce muscle spasms.

Potential Complications

Fractures can lead to serious local and systemic complications if not managed carefully. Nurses must remain vigilant for early warning signs.

  • Compartment syndrome: Increased pressure within a confined muscle compartment cuts off blood flow, causing severe, unrelenting pain that is not relieved by opioid pain medications.
  • Fat embolism syndrome: Fat globules from the bone marrow are released into the bloodstream, traveling to the lungs and brain, causing respiratory distress and confusion.
  • Osteomyelitis: A severe bone infection that can occur after open fractures or surgical fixation, requiring long-term antibiotic therapy.
  • Delayed union or nonunion: A failure of the bone ends to heal together within the expected time frame.

Compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency. If pressure is not relieved quickly through a procedure called a fasciotomy, permanent muscle and nerve damage will occur within hours.

Nursing Care Plan and Rehabilitation

Comprehensive nursing care focuses on promoting healing, preventing complications, and helping the patient regain independence.

  • Pain management: Administer prescribed analgesics and apply non-pharmacological comfort measures.
  • Skin and pin care: Inspect skin around casts or external fixator pins daily for signs of redness, drainage, or foul odors.
  • Mobility support: Assist with safe transfers and encourage the use of assistive devices like crutches or walkers as ordered by the physical therapist.
  • Nutritional support: Encourage a balanced diet rich in calcium, vitamin D, and protein to support bone regeneration.

Rehabilitation begins early in the recovery process. Physical therapy helps restore joint range of motion and muscle strength, ensuring the patient safely returns to their pre-injury level of activity.
