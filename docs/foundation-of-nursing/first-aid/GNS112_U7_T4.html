Fractures

Introduction and Definition of Fractures

A **fracture** represents a complete or partial disruption in the structural continuity of bone tissue. When the physical force applied to a bone exceeds its ultimate tensile or compressive strength, the bone fails, resulting in a break. While bone possesses remarkable resilience and elasticity, intense mechanical trauma, repetitive stress, or pathological weakening can compromise its integrity. In Nigerian clinical practice, bone fractures are frequently encountered in emergency departments and orthopedic units, largely stemming from road traffic crashes involving commercial motorcycles and vehicular transport, alongside traditional bone-setting complications that frequently present late with malunion or osteomyelitis.

Classification and Types of Fractures

Orthopedic classification systems categorize fractures based on their anatomical configuration, the relationship of the bone fragments to the external environment, and the stability of the injury. Recognizing these patterns guides both emergency stabilization and definitive surgical planning. - **Transverse fracture**: The break runs straight across the bone shaft at a right angle. - **Oblique fracture**: The fracture line crosses the bone at an slanted or diagonal angle. - **Spiral fracture**: Encircling the bone shaft, this pattern typically results from a twisting or rotational force. - **Comminuted fracture**: The bone shatters into three or more distinct fragments. - **Greenstick fracture**: An incomplete fracture where one side of the bone breaks while the other bends, commonly seen in pediatric patients due to softer bones. - **Open (compound) fracture**: The broken bone pierces through the skin or a wound extends down to the fracture site, carrying a high risk of deep tissue infection.

Pathophysiology and the Bone Healing Process

Bone healing is a sophisticated biological regeneration process rather than mere scar formation. It proceeds through four overlapping phases: 1. **Hematoma formation**: Immediately following the fracture, blood vessels rupture, bleeding into the injury site and forming a localized clot within hours. 2. **Fibrocartilaginous callus formation**: Granulation tissue invades the hematoma. Fibroblasts and osteoblasts migrate to the area, creating an unstable soft callus that bridges the broken ends. 3. **Bony callus formation**: Osteoblasts deposit spongy bone, gradually converting the soft callus into a harder, more rigid bony bridge over several weeks. 4. **Remodeling**: Osteoclasts resorb excess bony debris while osteoblasts lay down compact bone along lines of stress, eventually restoring the original bone contour and strength.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

A comprehensive assessment of a suspected fracture requires systematic evaluation of both local and systemic signs. Common clinical indicators include localized pain that worsens with movement, loss of normal function, visible deformity, localized swelling, bruising, and muscle spasm. Crepitus—a grating sensation caused by bone ends rubbing together—may be felt or heard, though examiners should avoid deliberately eliciting it to prevent further soft tissue damage. Crucially, the nurse must perform rigorous neurovascular assessments distal to the fracture site. This involves evaluating the **five Ps**: **pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, and paralysis**. Any compromise in capillary refill, temperature, or sensation signals impending vascular or nerve entrapment requiring urgent medical intervention.

Emergency Management and Immediate Care

Immediate pre-hospital and emergency room care focuses on preventing further injury, controlling hemorrhage, and stabilizing the patient. Before moving a suspected fracture, the injured extremity must be immobilized in the position it was found. Splinting joints both proximal and distal to the fracture site minimizes movement of the broken bone ends. If the fracture is open, the protruding bone must never be forcibly pushed back into the wound; instead, the area should be covered with a sterile dressing to reduce infection risk. Tetanus prophylaxis and broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics are standard early interventions in Nigerian emergency units when managing open fractures contaminated by soil or road debris.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

Definitive management aims to achieve reduction (realignment of bone fragments) and retention (immobilization until healing occurs). Reduction can be closed through manual manipulation or open through surgical exposure. Retention methods vary widely: - **Closed reduction and cast immobilization**: External plaster or fiberglass casts maintain alignment for stable fractures. - **Traction**: Application of a pulling force helps reduce muscle spasms and align bone, utilizing skin traction or skeletal pins. - **Internal fixation (ORIF)**: Surgeons use plates, screws, nails, or intramedullary rods to secure bone fragments internally. - **External fixation**: Pins inserted through the skin into the bone connect to an external metal frame, frequently utilized for complex open fractures with severe soft tissue damage.

Potential Complications of Fractures

Fractures carry risks of both early and late complications that can threaten limb survival or overall health. **Compartment syndrome** occurs when increased pressure within a confined muscle compartment restricts blood flow, leading to ischemic necrosis if not relieved promptly via fasciotomy. **Fat embolism syndrome** arises when fat globules from the yellow bone marrow enter the bloodstream, traveling to the lungs and brain, characteristically presenting with petechiae, respiratory distress, and altered mental status. Other notable complications include deep vein thrombosis, delayed union, nonunion (failure to heal), malunion (healing in a deformed position), and osteomyelitis, a severe bone infection particularly prevalent following contaminated open fractures or traditional bone-setting procedures.

Nursing Care Plan and Rehabilitation

A structured nursing care plan for a patient with a fracture focuses on pain management, infection prevention, maintenance of neurovascular status, and promotion of mobility. Nurses administer prescribed analgesics, monitor surgical or pin sites for purulent drainage or erythema, and educate patients on cast care—emphasizing that casts must remain dry and foreign objects should never be inserted beneath them to scratch itching skin. Rehabilitation begins early. Isometric exercises prevent muscle atrophy while immobilized, and progressive range-of-motion routines restore joint flexibility. Nutritional support rich in protein, calcium, and vitamin D is vital to supply the metabolic building blocks required for optimal bone matrix synthesis.
