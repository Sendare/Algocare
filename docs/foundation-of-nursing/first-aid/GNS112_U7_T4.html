Fractures

Introduction and Definition of Fractures

A fracture is defined as a break or disruption in the structural continuity of a bone. In the context of nursing care in Nigeria, understanding fractures is crucial as student nurses will encounter patients with traumatic injuries from road traffic accidents, falls, and workplace hazards. When a bone breaks, the surrounding soft tissues, blood vessels, nerves, and periosteum are frequently damaged as well. Nurses must recognize that a fracture is not merely an isolated skeletal injury, but a complex systemic event affecting the patient's mobility, comfort, and physiological stability. The integrity of the skeletal system is essential for posture, protection of vital organs, and hematopoiesis. Therefore, any compromise to this system demands prompt clinical evaluation and holistic intervention. Recognizing the anatomy of bone, including its cortical and trabecular components, helps nurses comprehend why certain bones heal differently and how specific forces generate particular fracture patterns. As future healthcare professionals, nursing students must master these foundational definitions to effectively communicate with multidisciplinary teams, interpret radiological reports, and deliver safe, evidence-based care to orthopedic patients across various clinical settings in Nigeria.

Pathophysiology and the Bone Healing Process

The bone healing process is a remarkable physiological journey divided into distinct yet overlapping phases: hematoma formation, fibrocartilaginous callus formation, bony callus formation, and bone remodeling. Immediately after a fracture, bleeding from the broken bone ends and damaged periosteum forms a hematoma within the fracture site, usually within the first 24 to 72 hours. This inflammatory phase triggers the migration of fibroblasts, osteoblasts, and chondroblasts. Next, during the fibrocartilaginous callus phase, capillaries grow into the hematoma, and phagocytic cells clear away cellular debris. Fibroblasts and chondroblasts then secrete collagen and cartilage, creating a soft callus that bridges the broken bone ends. In the third phase, osteoblasts begin to deposit mineralized woven bone, transforming the soft callus into a hard, bony callus over a period of 3 to 4 weeks. Finally, the bone remodeling phase occurs over months to years. During this period, osteoclasts resorb excess bony debris and osteoblasts lay down new compact bone along lines of mechanical stress, eventually restoring the bone to its original shape and strength. Nursing students must understand this sequence to accurately gauge patient recovery timelines, monitor for delayed union or non-union, and educate patients on why adherence to activity restrictions and nutrition is vital during bone repair.

Classification and Types of Fractures

Fractures are classified based on several criteria including their extent, the direction of the fracture line, the relationship to the external environment, and the stability of the fracture. A closed (simple) fracture leaves the skin intact, whereas an open (compound) fracture involves a break in the skin through which the bone protrudes, significantly increasing the risk of osteomyelitis and deep tissue infection. Regarding the pattern of the break, a transverse fracture occurs straight across the bone shaft, an oblique fracture happens at an angle, and a spiral fracture twists around the bone, often raising suspicion of non-accidental trauma or severe rotational forces. Other types include comminuted fractures, where the bone fragments into multiple pieces, greenstick fractures, which are incomplete breaks commonly seen in children due to their softer, more pliable bones, and impacted fractures, where bone ends are driven into each other. Understanding these classifications helps nurses anticipate specific treatment modalities, such as open reduction internal fixation for unstable comminuted fractures versus simple casting for stable greenstick fractures. For nursing practice in Nigeria, recognizing open fractures promptly and initiating sterile wound care is a life- and limb-saving competency.

Etiology and Common Causes

The etiology of fractures relates to the underlying mechanisms and causes that exceed the mechanical strength of bone tissue. The most common cause is direct or indirect trauma, such as motor vehicle accidents, pedestrian knockdowns, falls from heights, and occupational injuries involving heavy machinery or construction. In Nigeria, road traffic accidents involving commercial motorcycles (okadas) and buses are a major epidemiological source of severe open and comminuted fractures. Pathological fractures represent another critical category, occurring in bones weakened by underlying disease processes such as osteoporosis, bone tumors, osteogenesis imperfecta, or bone infections (osteomyelitis). In these cases, normal daily activities or minimal stress can cause a bone to break because its structural integrity is already compromised. Additionally, stress or fatigue fractures occur due to repeated, prolonged micro-trauma, frequently seen in athletes, military recruits, or individuals starting unaccustomed strenuous physical labor. Recognizing these etiologies allows nurses to implement effective preventative education, such as fall precautions for elderly patients and safety measures for industrial workers, while tailoring the acute nursing assessment based on the mechanism of injury.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

Clinical manifestations of fractures include a constellation of localized and systemic signs that nurses must systematically assess. Cardinal symptoms include acute pain and tenderness at the fracture site caused by muscle spasms, tissue trauma, and direct nerve irritation. Patients typically present with obvious deformity, unnatural alignment, or shortening of the affected limb due to muscle pull. Swelling and ecchymosis (bruising) develop rapidly from localized hemorrhage and inflammation. Crepitus, a grating sensation or sound produced by bone fragments rubbing together, may be felt or heard, though nurses should avoid purposely eliciting this sign as it causes unnecessary pain and further tissue damage. Loss of function or inability to bear weight is nearly universal. Furthermore, nurses must perform comprehensive neurovascular assessments—checking pulses, capillary refill, skin color, temperature, motor function, and sensation distal to the injury site—to detect early signs of ischemia or nerve compression. Pain that is disproportionate to the injury and unresponsive to prescribed analgesics is a red flag for acute compartment syndrome, a surgical emergency that requires immediate reporting.

Emergency Management and Immediate Care

Emergency management of fractures focuses on preserving life, preventing further injury, reducing complications, and relieving pain. At the scene of an accident, the priority follows the ABCDE trauma protocol (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure). Once systemic stability is assured, attention turns to the injured extremity. The golden rule of emergency splinting is to 'splint them where they lie' without attempting to forcibly straighten deformed fractures, unless neurovascular compromise is imminent and distal pulses are absent. Splinting immobilizes the fracture fragments, decreases pain, reduces the risk of further damage to adjacent blood vessels and nerves, and minimizes bleeding. For open fractures, the nurse or first responder should cover the wound with a clean or sterile dressing to prevent gross contamination, but must never attempt to push protruding bone ends back into the tissue. Ice packs can be applied cautiously to reduce localized swelling and hemorrhage. Tetanus prophylaxis and broad-spectrum antibiotics are typically administered upon arrival at the hospital, especially for open fractures. Effective immediate care sets the stage for successful orthopedic interventions and lowers long-term morbidity.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

Medical and surgical interventions for fractures aim to achieve reduction (alignment of bone fragments), immobilization (holding fragments in place until healing occurs), and restoration of function. Reduction can be closed, where manual manipulation is applied under sedation or anesthesia, or open, which involves a surgical incision to directly visualize and align the bone fragments. Following reduction, immobilization is achieved through external or internal fixation. External immobilization includes casts, splints, and traction (skin or skeletal), which maintain alignment using external devices. Cast care is a major nursing responsibility; nurses must monitor for cast tightness, keep the cast clean and dry, and perform frequent neurovascular checks. Surgical interventions frequently utilize Open Reduction and Internal Fixation (ORIF), where orthopedic surgeons use hardware such as metal plates, screws, intramedullary nails, and wires to secure the bone internally. Internal fixation permits earlier mobilization and rehabilitation, which is vital for preventing complications of immobility. Nursing care post-surgery involves pain management, meticulous wound care, monitoring drain output, and collaborating with physiotherapists to safely initiate early range-of-motion exercises.

Potential Complications of Fractures

Fractures can lead to serious local and systemic complications that require vigilant monitoring by nursing staff. Early complications include acute compartment syndrome, characterized by increased pressure within a confined muscle compartment that compromises blood flow and nerve function, leading to ischemic necrosis if not decompressed via fasciotomy. Fat embolism syndrome is another life-threatening systemic complication, commonly associated with long bone fractures (such as the femur), where fat globules enter the bloodstream and lodge in pulmonary and cerebral capillaries, presenting with petechiae, respiratory distress, and altered mental status. Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) can arise from venous stasis due to immobility. Hemorrhage and hypovolemic shock are also risks, particularly with pelvic or open femoral fractures. Late complications include delayed union (healing taking longer than usual), non-union (failure of the bone to heal), malunion (healing in an abnormal position), avascular necrosis (bone death due to interrupted blood supply), and osteomyelitis (bone infection, especially prevalent in open fractures or post-surgery). Recognizing these complications early allows for prompt medical intervention and significantly improves patient outcomes.

Nursing Care Plan and Rehabilitation

A comprehensive nursing care plan for a patient with a fracture addresses pain management, tissue perfusion, mobility, nutrition, and psychological support. Nurses must administer prescribed analgesics (opioids and non-opioids) regularly and use non-pharmacological comfort measures such as repositioning and ice therapy. To maintain tissue perfusion and prevent complications of immobility, nurses facilitate frequent position changes, encourage deep breathing and coughing exercises, apply anti-embolism stockings, and administer prophylactic anticoagulants as ordered. Nutritional support is vital; a diet high in protein, calcium, vitamin C, and vitamin D promotes collagen synthesis and bone mineralization. The nursing care plan also includes education on assistive devices (crutches, walkers, or wheelchairs) and safe transfer techniques. Rehabilitation begins early in the recovery process, involving physiotherapists to restore joint range of motion, muscle strength, and functional independence. Nurses play an integral role in coordinating this multidisciplinary care, assessing the patient's readiness for discharge, and ensuring that home environments in Nigeria are modified to support safe ambulation and prevent subsequent falls.
