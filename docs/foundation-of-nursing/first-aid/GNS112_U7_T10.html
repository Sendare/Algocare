Transportation of Casualty for Further Management

Introduction and Core Principles of Casualty Transportation

Casualty transportation is the systematic movement of an injured or ill person from the scene of an incident to a healthcare facility capable of providing definitive care. The primary objective is to move the patient safely without causing further injury. Before lifting anyone, the nurse or responder must evaluate the environment for active hazards such as fire, electric wires, or hostile crowds. Speed is secondary to safety; a rushed evacuation that compromises the airway or damages the spine does more harm than good. Every movement must be deliberate, coordinated, and communicated clearly among all members of the rescue team using simple commands.

Initial Assessment and Stabilization Prior to Transport

No patient should be moved until a rapid clinical assessment has been completed and life-threatening conditions are addressed. In nursing and emergency care, the standard ABCDE framework governs this phase:

  • Airway: Ensure the airway is clear of obstructions, blood, or vomit, using suction or manual maneuvers if necessary.
  • Breathing: Check chest rise and respiratory rate, administering supplemental oxygen if oxygen is low.
  • Circulation: Control severe external bleeding and check central pulses.
  • Disability: Perform a quick neurological check assessing responsiveness using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive).
  • Exposure: Expose key injury sites to assess damage while preventing hypothermia by covering the patient with a blanket.

Stabilizing fractures, securing loose tubes, and applying a cervical collar must happen before the wheels start rolling.

Patient Positioning During Transport

How a patient is positioned during transit depends entirely on their specific medical condition or injury pattern. Incorrect positioning can obstruct an airway, increase intracranial pressure, or worsen shock.

Regularly reassessing the patient ensures that the chosen position continues to serve their physiological needs throughout the journey.

Emergency Transport Equipment and Devices

A variety of specialized tools are available to facilitate safe casualty movement, each designed for specific environments and patient needs.

  1. Rigid Spine Board: Used primarily for patients with suspected spinal trauma; allows secure strapping and immobilization.
  2. Scoop Stretcher: Splits into two halves longitudinally, letting rescuers slide it under a patient without rolling them, minimizing movement.
  3. Basket Stretcher (Stokes Litter): Ideal for rough terrain, search and rescue, or vertical hoisting because it completely cradles the body.
  4. Wheelchair and Transfer Chair: Used for stable patients who can sit upright and only require movement across flat hospital corridors.

Every piece of equipment must be inspected for structural integrity and clean working order before deployment in an emergency.

Manual Lifting and Body Mechanics

When mechanical stretchers are unavailable or manual assistance is required to adjust a patient, proper body mechanics are vital to protect the health team from debilitating musculoskeletal injuries. Rescuers must keep their feet shoulder-width apart to create a stable base of support. Bending should always occur at the hips and knees, never at the waist, utilizing the immense strength of the quadriceps and gluteal muscles rather than the delicate lumbar spine. Keep the patient close to your body's center of gravity to reduce strain. Twisting while lifting is a major hazard; if a change of direction is needed, pivot with your feet instead of turning your torso.

Monitoring and Care During Transit

Transportation is a high-risk period because the patient is away from stationary hospital resources, and motion can mask or worsen clinical changes. The nurse must continuously monitor vital signs, including blood pressure, pulse rate, oxygen saturation, and respiratory effort. Intravenous lines, urinary catheters, and oxygen tubing must be secured so they do not dislodge when the vehicle goes over bumps or turns corners. The nurse should observe the patient's face for signs of pain, anxiety, or declining consciousness. Portable suction and emergency medications must remain within immediate arm's reach throughout the trip.

Handover Communication and Documentation

The transfer of care concludes with a structured handover to the receiving clinical team. In emergency nursing, the SBAR technique (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) provides a concise, professional format to communicate critical details without confusion. The nurse must verbally transmit the patient's identity, chief complaint, vital signs trend, interventions performed during transport, and any known allergies. Following the verbal handover, a complete written or electronic record of the transport care must be placed in the patient's chart. Clear documentation protects the patient's legal rights and ensures continuity of care as they transition to the next unit.
