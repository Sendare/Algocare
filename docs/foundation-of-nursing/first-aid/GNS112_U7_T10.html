Transportation of Casualty for Further Management

Principles and Preparation for Casualty Transport

Moving an injured or ill person from an accident scene or peripheral health post to a higher level of care requires careful coordination to prevent further injury. Before any movement begins, the primary survey must be complete, securing the airway, breathing, and circulation. Hasty evacuation without stabilization frequently leads to preventable mortality, particularly in resource-constrained settings where transport times to tertiary facilities are prolonged. Rescuers must prioritize safety, efficient handling, and maintaining spinal alignment if trauma is suspected. The chosen transport method must match the severity of the condition and the terrain to be navigated.

Modes of Transportation and Patient Positioning

Selecting how a patient travels depends on distance, urgency, and medical stability. Road ambulances remain the standard for ground transit, while air ambulances are reserved for long distances or remote terrains. Patient positioning dictates physiological safety during the journey: - **Recovery position**: Ideal for unconscious, breathing patients to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. - **Semi-Fowler or upright position**: Preferred for individuals with severe respiratory distress or heart failure to ease lung expansion. - **Flat supine with legs elevated**: Used for patients experiencing hypovolemic shock to enhance venous return to the heart. - **Spine immobilization board**: Mandatory for anyone with a suspected cervical or spinal column injury.

Emergency Transport Equipment and Devices

Specialized gear protects the casualty from secondary trauma and facilitates smooth handling by rescue personnel. Standard devices include rigid spine boards, scoop stretchers, basket stretchers for rough terrain, and flexible Reeves stretchers for narrow hallways. Improvised stretchers made from poles and blankets should only be used as an absolute last resort when professional equipment is entirely unavailable. Cervical collars and head immobilizers must accompany any spinal board application to maintain neutral alignment of the neck throughout movement.

Manual Lifting, Transfer, and Loading Techniques

Physical transfer of a casualty tests the teamwork and ergonomics of the nursing and rescue staff. Poor lifting mechanics lead directly to back injuries among healthcare workers and accidental dropping of patients. - **Communication**: One designated team leader must give clear commands for lifting and lowering. - **Body mechanics**: Rescuers must keep their backs straight, bend at the knees, and keep the patient close to their center of gravity. - **Lifting methods**: The log-roll technique is standard for turning a trauma victim onto a spine board, ensuring the head, torso, and pelvis move as a single rigid unit without twisting the spine.

Monitoring and Interventions During Transit

Movement introduces physical stressors like vibration, engine noise, and gravitational forces that can destabilize a fragile patient. Continuous observation is non-negotiable. Nurses must monitor vital signs, check intravenous line patency, and ensure oxygen delivery systems do not run empty mid-journey. Bumpy Nigerian federal highways can dislodge endotracheal tubes or displace fracture splints; thus, securing all lines and tubes before departure is vital. If a patient's condition deteriorates while in motion, the team must pull over to a safe spot if a critical intervention like reintubation or cardiopulmonary resuscitation is required.

Handover Communication and Documentation

The transport process concludes only when safe, structured transfer of care occurs at the receiving facility. Using a standardized framework like **SBAR** (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) ensures critical clinical facts are not omitted amid the noise of arrival. The handover must detail the baseline condition, interventions performed en route, and any medication doses administered. Clear documentation protects professional accountability and provides the receiving medical team with an accurate timeline of the patient's clinical trajectory.
