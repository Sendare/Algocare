Transportation of Casualty for Further Management

Principles of Casualty Transportation

Transporting a casualty requires strict adherence to core principles to prevent further injury. The primary rule is that resuscitation and stabilization must always precede movement. Never rush a transfer at the expense of airway clearance or hemorrhage control. Every movement should be smooth, deliberate, and coordinated by a designated team leader. Communication within the transport team prevents sudden jolts that could worsen spinal or skeletal trauma. Understanding the mechanism of injury dictates how a patient is handled from the incident scene to the definitive care facility.

Initial Assessment and Stabilization Prior to Transport

Before any journey begins, nurses must systematically evaluate the patient using the ABCDE framework.

  • Airway: Ensure the airway is clear and secure, using adjuncts if breathing is compromised.
  • Breathing: Check respiratory rate, depth, and oxygen saturation, providing supplemental oxygen when needed.
  • Circulation: Control active bleeding, establish reliable intravenous access, and assess capillary refill.
  • Disability: Evaluate neurological status using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale.
  • Exposure: Expose the body to find hidden injuries while preventing hypothermia through warm blankets.

Failing to correct hypoxia or shock before moving a patient over rough Nigerian roads can lead to rapid deterioration.

Modes of Transportation and Patient Positioning

The choice of transport mode depends on distance, terrain, and clinical urgency. Road ambulances remain the mainstay for pre-hospital transfers, while air ambulances are reserved for long distances or remote locations. Patient positioning is equally critical for safety.

Emergency Transport Equipment and Devices

Specialized equipment safeguards the patient against further injury during handling and movement.

  • Spine boards and cervical collars: Used together to immobilize the entire vertebral column when spinal damage is suspected.
  • Scoop stretcher: Allows safe lifting and placement of patients with minimal movement by splitting apart vertically.
  • Vacuum mattress: Molds to the contours of the patient's body when air is sucked out, providing rigid and comfortable immobilization.
  • Ambu bag (Bag-Valve-Mask): Essential manual ventilation device used when spontaneous breathing fails.

Always inspect mechanical integrity and cleanliness of these devices before deployment in the field.

Manual Lifting and Transfer Techniques

When mechanical stretchers are unavailable, healthcare workers rely on coordinated manual techniques. Proper body mechanics protect nurses from debilitating back injuries. Always bend at the knees, keep the load close to your center of gravity, and avoid twisting your spine while lifting. Common transfer methods include the log roll, the draw-sheet method, and multi-person carries. The log roll requires at least three people to maintain neutral spinal alignment while turning a patient onto a side or a spine board.

Monitoring and Care During Transport

The inside of a moving ambulance is a dynamic and challenging environment. Continuous observation of vital signs prevents sudden clinical crises from going unnoticed. Nurses must regularly check consciousness levels, respiratory effort, pulse rate, and oxygen saturation. Intravenous lines and chest tubes require vigilant checks to ensure they do not dislodge due to vehicle vibrations or rough road bumps. Reassuring an anxious patient also helps stabilize their heart rate and overall physiological response.

Handover Communication and Documentation

Safe patient transfer concludes with a structured clinical handover at the receiving facility. Using standardized tools like ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) ensures vital details are never omitted. Nurses must verbally communicate the mechanism of injury, baseline vitals, treatments administered en route, and current stability. Written documentation detailing times of interventions and medication administration must accompany the patient. Clear handover prevents gaps in continuity of care.
