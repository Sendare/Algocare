Transportation of Casualty for Further Management

Introduction and Principles of Casualty Transportation

Casualty transportation is a critical phase in emergency care, involving the safe movement of an injured or ill patient from an accident scene or lower-level healthcare facility to a definitive care center. The primary goal is to prevent further injury, maintain vital organ perfusion, and expedite specialized medical intervention. Basic principles dictate that transportation must never compromise the patient's airway, breathing, or circulation (ABCs). Before any movement is initiated, rescuers must ensure the scene is safe for both the healthcare provider and the casualty. Furthermore, meticulous planning is required to choose the appropriate transport mode and ensure that necessary life-support equipment accompanies the patient. In the context of Nigerian healthcare settings, where inter-facility transfer distances can be extensive and road networks variable, adhering to these fundamental principles significantly reduces morbidity and mortality. Healthcare providers must maintain effective communication throughout the process, ensuring that the receiving facility is adequately prepared to receive the incoming casualty. Ultimately, a well-managed transport bridges the gap between pre-hospital emergency response and advanced hospital care, serving as a vital link in the chain of survival for trauma and medical emergencies alike.

Initial Assessment and Stabilization Prior to Transport

Before any casualty is loaded onto a transport vehicle, a thorough and systematic initial assessment and stabilization must be performed. Healthcare providers utilize the standard ABCDE approach—Airway with cervical spine protection, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure/Environment. Immediate life-threatening conditions, such as tension pneumothorax, severe external hemorrhage, or airway obstruction, must be identified and managed on-site rather than delaying care until arrival at the hospital. Stabilization involves securing a patent airway, administering supplemental oxygen, establishing reliable intravenous or intraosseous access for fluid resuscitation, and controlling bleeding with direct pressure or tourniquets if necessary. Additionally, immobilizing the spine in suspected trauma cases is non-negotiable to prevent secondary spinal cord damage. In resource-limited settings common across Nigeria, improvising with available clean bandages and ensuring rapid stabilization can mean the difference between life and death. Transporting an unstable patient without basic resuscitation efforts drastically increases the risk of cardiac arrest en route. Therefore, the Golden Hour rule emphasizes that stabilization and transport must occur efficiently, ensuring the patient is physiological optimized as much as possible before the wheels start rolling.

Modes of Transportation and Patient Positioning

Selecting the appropriate mode of transportation and maintaining correct patient positioning are vital components of safe transfer. Modes of transport range from basic wheel stretchers and basic life support (BLS) ambulances to advanced life support (ALS) mobile intensive care units and air ambulances, depending on the patient's acuity and geographical distance. The choice must match the clinical needs of the casualty. Patient positioning directly influences physiological stability; for instance, an unconscious breathing patient should be placed in the recovery position (lateral decubitus) to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. Conversely, a patient with suspected spinal injuries requires strict flat supine alignment on a rigid board with manual in-line stabilization or a cervical collar. Patients experiencing acute respiratory distress or congestive heart failure generally tolerate a semi-Fowler's or upright sitting position better, as it eases diaphragmatic excursion. Nurses must continually assess how the chosen position affects the patient's vital signs and comfort, adjusting immediately if signs of respiratory compromise or hemodynamic instability appear during transit across varying road terrains.

Emergency Transport Equipment and Devices

Emergency transport relies heavily on specialized equipment and devices designed to maintain life support and secure patients during movement. Standard equipment includes scoop stretchers, spine boards, rigid cervical collars, head immobilizers, and vacuum mattress splints. For airway and breathing support, portable suction units, bag-valve-mask (BVM) resuscitators, oxygen cylinders with flow meters, and pulse oximeters are indispensable. Circulatory support tools encompass automated external defibrillators (AEDs), sphygmomanometers, and infusion pumps. Nursing students must be proficient in checking, preparing, and operating these devices prior to deployment, ensuring that batteries are charged, oxygen tanks are full, and consumables are adequately stocked. In many Nigerian emergency medical service contexts, equipment failure during transit can be catastrophic due to distance from backup resources. Therefore, pre-transport equipment checklists are mandatory. Furthermore, securing equipment properly inside the transport vehicle is essential; unsecured heavy items can become lethal projectiles during sudden stops, sharp turns, or vehicular accidents.

Manual Lifting and Transfer Techniques

Manual lifting and transfer techniques are fundamental skills that protect both the casualty from further injury and the healthcare provider from debilitating musculoskeletal disorders. Proper body mechanics must be strictly observed: bending at the knees rather than the waist, keeping the load close to the body's center of gravity, and avoiding simultaneous twisting and lifting. Team coordination is vital; one rescuer must act as the designated team leader to give clear, synchronized commands such as 'ready, lift' for moves like the log-roll, bridge lift, or direct ground lift. Transferring a patient from a bed to a stretcher, or vice versa, requires utilizing friction-reducing sliding sheets, draw sheets, or transfer boards to minimize shear forces on the patient's skin and reduce physical strain on the staff. In Nigerian hospitals and pre-hospital scenarios where mechanical lift aids may occasionally be absent, reliance on well-coordinated human resources is high. Regular training drills on manual handling prevent fatigue-related drops and awkward transfers that could jeopardize spinal alignment or fracture stability in vulnerable trauma patients.

Monitoring and Care During Transport

Monitoring and care during transport require continuous vigilance because a stable patient can rapidly deteriorate due to motion, vehicle vibrations, and the stress of travel. Nurses and emergency personnel must continuously monitor vital signs—including heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and level of consciousness—documenting trends at regular intervals. Intravenous lines, urinary catheters, and chest tubes must be checked frequently to ensure they remain patent, unkinked, and properly secured. Pain management, psychological reassurance, and environmental comfort (such as temperature control) are also vital aspects of en-route care, as anxiety and pain can exacerbate physiological instability. In transit, healthcare providers must anticipate complications; for instance, having suction ready for a patient prone to vomiting or ensuring manual ventilation capabilities are within arm's reach if a mechanical ventilator alarms. Prompt recognition of subtle changes, such as a falling oxygen saturation or a rising heart rate, allows for timely interventions before a life-threatening crisis develops inside the moving vehicle.

Handover Communication and Documentation

The final critical link in casualty transportation is the formal handover communication and comprehensive documentation. Upon arrival at the receiving facility, the transporting nurse must deliver a concise, structured verbal report to the receiving medical team. Standardized communication frameworks, such as the ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) tool, ensure that vital clinical details—such as baseline vital signs, interventions performed, medications administered, and the patient's current response—are transmitted without omission. Following the verbal handover, a complete written transfer record must be handed over and filed, detailing the timeline of events, pre-hospital care given, and any untoward incidents during transit. Thorough documentation protects legal liability, ensures continuity of care, and provides the receiving team with accurate historical data necessary for ongoing treatment plans. In busy Nigerian emergency departments, a structured handover prevents critical pieces of patient history from being lost in the transition, safeguarding the patient from medical errors.
