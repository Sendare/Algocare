Care of Hospital Equipment

Nursing Responsibility and Equipment Classification

Hospital equipment represents a significant financial investment and, more importantly, a direct link to patient safety. As a nurse, you are the primary custodian of these tools at the bedside. Accountability begins the moment an item is issued to the ward and extends through its daily use, cleaning, and eventual return or disposal.

Equipment is generally grouped into categories to guide how it is handled:

  • Diagnostic equipment: Tools like sphygmomanometers, stethoscope, and thermometers used to assess patient status.
  • Therapeutic equipment: Devices such as infusion pumps, oxygen cylinders, and nebulizers used actively in treatment.
  • Consumables and linen: Single-use items or fabrics requiring frequent laundering and replacement.
  • Surgical instruments: Specialized metal tools used during procedures, requiring rigorous processing.

General Principles for Handling and Using Equipment

Proper handling prevents premature wear and ensures devices function accurately when lives depend on them. Always read the manufacturer's operational manual before deploying unfamiliar technology. Never yank an electrical cord from the wall outlet; grip the molded plug firmly to disconnect the flow.

Mobile equipment, such as drip stands or mobile X-ray units, must be moved with locked brakes released fully to avoid wheel damage and tipping hazards. Keep electronic devices away from excessive moisture and direct heat sources. When handling battery-operated tools, ensure they are returned to charging docking stations promptly after use so they remain ready for emergency deployment during power fluctuations—a common reality in many Nigerian healthcare facilities.

Decontamination: Cleaning, Disinfection, and Sterilization

Infection control hinges on breaking the chain of transmission through proper instrument processing. This occurs in progressive steps, starting with thorough cleaning.

Process LevelTargetTypical Agent or Method
CleaningRemoval of organic matter and visible dirtWater, detergent, mechanical friction
DisinfectionElimination of most pathogenic microorganismsChemical disinfectants like hypochlorite
SterilizationDestruction of all microbial life including sporesAutoclaving, ethylene oxide, dry heat

Never skip the cleaning phase. Organic debris like blood or mucus acts as a protective shield for microorganisms, rendering subsequent chemical disinfection or autoclaving ineffective.

Maintenance, Storage, and Inventory Control

Equipment left neglected in utility rooms deteriorates rapidly. Routine checks involve inspecting rubber tubing for dry rot, testing electrical cables for exposed wires, and verifying calibration dates on monitoring apparatus.

Storage areas must be clean, dry, and well-ventilated, keeping sensitive items elevated off the floor. Inventory control relies on regular stock-taking audits. A missing item should be reported immediately through proper administrative channels rather than hidden, as unaccounted losses compromise patient care capacity and disrupt hospital logistics.

Safety Measures and Prevention of Cross-Infection

Medical devices can easily act as fomites, transferring hospital-acquired pathogens from one vulnerable patient to another if protocols fail. Stethoscopes, pulse oximeter probes, and blood pressure cuffs must be wiped with appropriate disinfectant wipes between every single patient interaction.

Sharps containers must never be overfilled; dispose of needles and lancets immediately at the point of use without recapping. When handling equipment contaminated with body fluids, wear appropriate personal protective equipment to safeguard both yourself and your colleagues from bloodborne pathogens.

Management of Defective or Damaged Equipment

Using faulty equipment endangers patients and staff alike. If a device sparks, makes unusual noises, displays error codes, or fails to perform its function, take it out of service instantly.

Do not leave a broken machine sitting in a corner where an unsuspecting colleague might pick it up. Affix a clear, highly visible 'OUT OF ORDER' or 'DEFECTIVE' warning tag to the item, document the exact nature of the fault in the ward equipment logbook, and send it to the biomedical engineering or maintenance department without delay.
