Care of Hospital Equipment

Nursing Responsibility in Equipment Care

Hospital equipment represents a significant financial investment for healthcare facilities and a direct link to patient survival. Nurses act as the primary custodians of these tools because they operate them at the bedside daily. Taking responsibility means more than just turning a machine on and off; it involves pre-use checks, proper handling, immediate reporting of faults, and ensuring the device is clean before the next patient arrives. When a nurse treats equipment with care, they protect both the hospital budget and the vulnerable human beings relying on those devices for accurate diagnosis and therapy.

Classification of Hospital Equipment

Medical devices in a clinical setting are categorized based on their function, usage frequency, and material composition. Understanding these categories helps nurses prioritize cleaning methods and storage requirements.

  • Diagnostic equipment: Tools used to identify diseases or monitor body functions, such as sphygmomanometers, thermometers, and ECG machines.
  • Therapeutic equipment: Devices utilized to treat conditions and aid recovery, including infusion pumps, suction machines, and nebulizers.
  • Disposable equipment: Single-use items designed to be discarded immediately after one procedure to prevent cross-contamination, like syringes and disposable catheter kits.
  • Capital equipment: High-value, durable machinery meant for long-term use across many patients, such as hospital beds and patient monitors.

General Principles for Handling and Using Equipment

Every piece of medical machinery comes with specific operational limits. Ignoring these guidelines shortens the lifespan of the device and creates immediate hazards. Always read the manufacturer instructions before operating unfamiliar technology. Never yank an electrical cord from the wall socket; grip the plug firmly to disconnect it. Avoid pulling tubing or cables across high-traffic walkways where they can be crushed by equipment wheels or cause staff to trip. Keep liquids away from electronic control panels unless the device is explicitly rated as waterproof.

Decontamination: Cleaning, Disinfection, and Sterilization

Infection control relies heavily on a three-step hierarchy of decontamination. Skipping steps renders the final process ineffective.

ProcessTarget LevelTypical Application
CleaningRemoves visible dirt, dust, and organic matterWashing bed frames and trays with soap and water
DisinfectionDestroys most pathogenic microorganismsWiping diagnostic probes with chemical solutions
SterilizationEliminates all forms of microbial life, including sporesAutoclaving surgical instruments and metal forceps

Cleaning must always precede disinfection and sterilization because dried blood or protein shields microbes from chemical agents.

Maintenance, Storage, and Inventory Control

Equipment longevity depends on routine preventive maintenance and organized storage. Clinical areas should never treat supply cupboards as dumping grounds. Store sensitive electronic equipment in dry, dust-free environments away from direct sunlight and extreme temperatures. Batteries in mobile devices, such as defibrillators and transport monitors, need regular charging cycles to prevent battery memory failure. Routine inventory audits help nursing teams track missing accessories, prevent hoarding in specific rooms, and ensure that emergency tools are always accounted for.

Management of Defective or Damaged Equipment

Discovering a broken or malfunctioning device during a patient procedure is a serious stressor. The immediate priority is patient safety: disconnect the faulty item and replace it with a working unit at once. Never attempt makeshift repairs using adhesive tape or improvised wiring on clinical gear. Tag the defective item clearly with a prominent 'OUT OF SERVICE' or 'faulty' warning label so no other colleague accidentally uses it. Finally, log the fault in the maintenance book and notify the biomedical engineering department promptly.
