Special Bed Making

Introduction and Principles of Special Bed Making

Special bed making is an essential nursing skill designed to meet the specific therapeutic, diagnostic, or comfort needs of a patient. Unlike a standard unoccupied or occupied bed, a special bed is prepared keeping a particular clinical condition or hospital procedure in mind.

  • Therapeutic Adaptation: Modifies the bed structure or linen arrangement to ease physical symptoms, such as respiratory distress or surgical recovery.
  • Infection Prevention: Minimizes microbial dispersion during the handling of used linens.
  • Skin Integrity: Reduces friction and shear forces that contribute to pressure injury development.
  • Patient Dignity: Ensures the patient feels secure, clean, and respected within their immediate hospital environment.

Good body mechanics protect the nurse from musculoskeletal injury, while smooth, wrinkle-free sheets protect the patient from skin breakdown.

General Equipment and Infection Control in Bed Making

Gathering the correct equipment before beginning saves time and reduces unnecessary movement around the ward. Standard items include a mattress protector, bottom sheet, draw sheet, top sheet, blanket, spread, pillowcases, and a laundry hamper or moisture-proof bag.

Infection control remains paramount during bed making. Microorganisms settle on bed linen from skin shedding, respiratory droplets, and wound drainage. Nurses must never shake dirty linen in the air, as this disperses dust and bacteria throughout the room. Linens should be rolled tightly inward toward the center to trap contaminants. Clean linen must be held away from the nurse's uniform to prevent cross-contamination, and soiled linen goes directly into the designated container rather than being placed on chairs or the floor.

Admission Bed

An admission bed, also known as an anæsthetic, open, or reception bed, is prepared to receive a newly admitted patient from the emergency department, clinic, or direct home referral. The primary goal is to provide a welcoming, warm, and easily accessible environment for a patient who may be fatigued or anxious.

The top covers—including the sheet, blanket, and spread—are folded back in a specific manner, such as a triangular fold, fan-folded to the side opposite the door, or rolled to the foot of the bed. This arrangement allows the patient to transfer easily from a stretcher or wheelchair without struggling with tucked-in blankets. Additional items like an admission pack, kidney basin, and water pitcher are often placed nearby.

Operation Bed

An operation bed—frequently referred to as a post-operative or recovery bed—is designed to receive a patient returning from the operating theater. The bed must facilitate a safe, frictionless transfer from a stretcher while protecting the mattress from potential drainage, blood, or vomit.

  • Protective Cover: A waterproof sheet or rubber draw sheet is placed over the middle third of the mattress.
  • Thermal Support: Hot water bottles or warming blankets may be used to counteract hypothermia from anesthesia, provided safety checks are strictly observed to prevent burns.
  • Access and Safety: Side rails are raised, and the top covers are fan-folded to the side or foot of the bed.
  • Emergency Readiness: An emesis basin, suction equipment, and oxygen tubing are kept close at hand.

Cardiac Bed

A cardiac bed is arranged specifically for patients experiencing severe heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, or severe respiratory distress. These patients often suffer from orthopnea—an inability to breathe comfortably while lying flat.

The bed is prepared in a high-Fowler's position, supported by a cardiac table or a cluster of firm pillows arranged behind the patient's back and arms. A footrest or pillow is placed at the bottom of the bed to prevent the patient from sliding downward due to gravity. This upright posture expands the thoracic cavity, decreases venous return to the overworked heart, and relieves pressure on the diaphragm.

Patient Safety, Comfort, and Ergonomics

Bed making involves physical labor that can strain a nurse's back if proper ergonomics are ignored. Raising the bed to a comfortable working waist height prevents bending and twisting of the spine. Nurses should keep their feet wide apart for a stable base of support and use leg muscles rather than back muscles when pulling or tucking heavy mattresses.

For the patient, safety features include ensuring that wheels are firmly locked before any transfer takes place. Side rails should be utilized according to hospital policy and the patient's cognitive status. Comfort is optimized by ensuring no harsh seams, crumbs, or creases remain under vulnerable pressure areas like the sacrum, heels, and scapulae.
