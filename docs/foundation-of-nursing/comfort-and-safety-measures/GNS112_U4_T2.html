Simple Bed Making

Introduction to Bed Making in Nursing Practice

Bed making is a fundamental nursing skill taught early in nursing education across Nigeria and globally, serving as a cornerstone of patient comfort, hygiene, and clinical safety. A neatly made bed not only enhances the aesthetic appeal of a hospital ward but also significantly impacts the patient's physical and psychological well-being. Prolonged bed rest exposes patients to various risks, including skin breakdown, pressure ulcers, and musculoskeletal discomfort, which can be mitigated by a smooth, wrinkle-free bed foundation. Furthermore, patient education often begins at the bedside, and a clean environment promotes a sense of healing, dignity, and trust in the healthcare team. As student nurses preparing for professional practice, mastering bed making ensures that you can systematically promote rest, prevent hospital-acquired complications, and maintain a therapeutic environment. Bed making can be performed as an unoccupied procedure when the client is ambulatory or out of bed, or as an occupied procedure when the patient is confined to bed due to illness, surgery, or physical limitations. Understanding the rationale behind each step empowers nurses to prioritize patient safety and comfort above mere routine execution, bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical bedside nursing excellence.

General Principles and Infection Control

Infection control is paramount during any bed-making procedure in Nigerian healthcare facilities to protect both patients and healthcare workers from nosocomial infections. Microorganisms can easily be transferred from soiled linens to the nurse's uniform, hands, or airborne environment if proper precautions are not meticulously followed. Standard precautions dictate that nurses must perform hand hygiene before and after the procedure and wear clean gloves, especially when handling visibly soiled or contaminated linens. A critical rule of infection control is never to shake soiled linen in the air, as this action disperses dust, lint, and pathogenic microorganisms across the room where they can be inhaled or settle on clean surfaces. Additionally, soiled linens should be held away from the nurse's uniform to prevent cross-contamination and immediately placed into an appropriate laundry hamper or moisture-resistant bag rather than placed on chairs or the floor. Ergonomic principles must also be integrated with infection control; adjusting the bed to a comfortable working height prevents back strain, while maintaining good body mechanics protects the nurse from injury. Proper hand hygiene after removing gloves concludes the infection control cycle, ensuring a safe therapeutic environment for everyone in the ward.

Essential Equipment and Linen Preparation

Before beginning any bed-making procedure, gathering and preparing all essential equipment and linen in an organized sequence is vital for efficiency and patient safety. The typical linen set includes a bottom sheet (fitted or flat), a draw sheet or slide sheet if indicated, a top sheet, a blanket or spread, and a pillowcase. Additional items may include a waterproof pad (mackintosh) to protect the mattress from bodily fluids, clean gloves, and a designated laundry bag or hamper. Linens should be collected in the exact reverse order of use—meaning the items needed last for the bed are placed at the bottom of the pile, and items needed first are placed at the top. This simple organizational technique prevents the nurse from walking back and forth across the room and avoids placing clean linen on contaminated surfaces like chairs or floors. Linen must be handled with clean hands, and excess linen should never be brought back to the general storage supply once it enters a patient's room, as it is considered contaminated by room air and must be laundered. Preparing the environment by checking for privacy curtains, eliminating drafts, and ensuring adequate lighting sets a professional tone before the actual physical bed-making process begins.

Unoccupied Bed

An unoccupied bed is made when the patient is out of bed, either walking, sitting in a chair, or undergoing diagnostic procedures, providing an ideal opportunity for a thorough and efficient linen change. The procedure begins by performing hand hygiene, gathering equipment, and adjusting the bed to a comfortable working height with the wheels locked. The nurse strips the old linens one by one, folding or rolling them inward to contain any pathogens or debris, and places them directly into the laundry bag. If a mattress pad or waterproof mackintosh is used, it should be wiped and checked, followed by placing the bottom sheet evenly across the mattress. Mitered corners are traditionally formed at the head and foot of the bed to secure the flat sheet tightly and prevent wrinkling under the patient. If a draw sheet and waterproof pad are used, they are positioned across the middle third of the bed to facilitate future patient repositioning and protect the mattress. The top sheet, blanket, and spread are then applied, tucking them neatly at the foot of the bed while leaving the sides hanging or making a neat toe pleat to prevent foot drop and pressure on the patient's toes. Finally, the clean pillowcase is slipped onto the pillow without touching the nurse's uniform, and the bed is returned to its lowest position for patient safety.

Occupied Bed

Making an occupied bed requires specialized skills, patience, and effective communication, as the patient remains in bed throughout the entire linen change. This procedure is indicated for clients who are critically ill, heavily sedated, paralyzed, or on strict bed rest restrictions. Before beginning, the nurse must explain the procedure to the patient, ensure privacy with screens or curtains, and assess the patient's pain level and tolerance. To ensure safety, the bed is raised to working height with side rails up on the opposite side of the bed where the nurse is not working, preventing accidental falls. The nurse works from one side of the bed at a time: the patient is gently turned onto their side facing away from the nurse, and the soiled bottom linens on the exposed side are loosened, rolled close to the patient's back, and tucked neatly underneath them. Clean bottom linens are then applied to the vacant half of the mattress, mitered, and the excess clean linen is similarly rolled against the patient's back. The nurse then assists or guides the patient to roll gently over the ridge of rolled linen onto the newly made side of the bed. Moving to the opposite side, the nurse removes the remaining soiled linens, pulls the clean linens taut, tucks them securely, and finishes by changing the top sheet and pillowcase while ensuring the patient remains covered and warm throughout.

Post-Procedure Care and Documentation

Post-procedure care and documentation conclude the bed-making process, ensuring continuity of care and maintaining high professional standards in nursing practice. Immediately after making either an occupied or unoccupied bed, the nurse must ensure the patient is in a safe, comfortable position with proper body alignment. The bed should be returned to its lowest horizontal position, and the wheels must be locked to prevent accidental movement when the patient attempts to get in or out of bed. The nurse must also ensure that essential items such as the call bell, water pitcher, bedside table, and personal belongings are within safe, easy reach of the patient. Following this, the nurse disposes of used equipment and PPE appropriately, performs hand hygiene to prevent cross-contamination, and cleans any reusable items according to hospital policy. Documentation is the final critical step: the nurse records the procedure in the patient's nursing notes, noting the date, time, type of bed making performed (occupied or unoccupied), skin condition observed during linen changes, and the patient's tolerance of the procedure. Reporting any unusual findings, such as skin redness, pressure sores, or incontinence, to the senior nurse or physician ensures prompt clinical intervention and comprehensive patient care.
