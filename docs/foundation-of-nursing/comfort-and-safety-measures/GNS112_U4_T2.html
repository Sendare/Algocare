Simple Bed Making

Introduction to Bed Making in Nursing Practice

Bed making is a foundational nursing skill designed to provide a clean, safe, and comfortable environment for patients. Beyond mere aesthetics, a well-made bed prevents skin breakdown, reduces friction, and promotes restful sleep which is vital for recovery. In Nigerian clinical settings, where family members often participate in patient care, nurses must instruct relatives on proper linen handling to maintain ward hygiene. Mastering this skill ensures that the basic physical needs of hospitalized individuals are consistently met while maintaining institutional standards of cleanliness.

General Principles and Infection Control

Infection prevention guides every movement during bed making. Pathogens on used linens can easily become airborne if shaken, contaminating the surrounding ward environment and posing risks to vulnerable patients.

  • Hand hygiene: Perform before touching clean linen and after handling soiled items.
  • Body mechanics: Adjust the bed to a comfortable working height to prevent nurse back strain.
  • Linen handling: Hold soiled linen away from the uniform and never shake sheets.
  • Cross-contamination prevention: Never place removed linen on the floor; use a designated laundry hamper instead.

Essential Equipment and Linen Preparation

Gathering supplies before starting prevents unnecessary trips and reduces physical disruption in the patient area. The standard linen bundle must be stacked in the exact reverse order of use—placing the items needed first at the very top of the pile.

  • Bottom sheet: Flat or fitted, placed first on the mattress.
  • Drawsheet: Optional moisture-barrier or cotton sheet placed across the middle for repositioning.
  • Top sheet: Placed over the patient, with the hem aligned to the top edge.
  • Blanket and spread: Added for warmth based on environmental temperature.
  • Pillowcases: Placed over pillows away from the uniform before transport.

Making an Unoccupied Bed

An unoccupied bed is prepared when the client is ambulatory, in the shower, or discharged. Efficiency is the primary goal here. Nurses often complete one entire side of the bed—securing the bottom sheet, drawsheet, and top covers with hospital corners—before moving to the opposite side. This method minimizes walking around the frame repeatedly. Achieving crisp mitered corners not only looks professional but keeps the bottom linen taut and wrinkle-free throughout the day.

Making an Occupied Bed

Managing an occupied bed requires continuous communication and dexterity, as the patient remains lying down throughout the process. The core technique involves turning the patient safely to one lateral side, rolling the soiled linen tightly against their back, and laying clean linen over the exposed half of the mattress. Once smoothed, the client rolls gently over the ridge of clean linen to the finished side, allowing the nurse to pull through and secure the remaining dirty materials. Privacy screens and reassuring dialogue are mandatory to reduce client anxiety during this vulnerable maneuver.

Post-Procedure Care and Documentation

The procedure concludes only after ensuring patient safety and environmental order. Lower the bed to its lowest position and lock the wheels to prevent falls, especially for elderly clients or those recovering from anesthesia. Call bells must be placed within easy reach. Finally, wash hands and document any relevant skin observations, such as redness over bony prominences, in the nursing notes.
