Concept of Health and Illness

Defining Health and Illness

Health is not simply the absence of disease. The World Health Organization defines health as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being. Illness, on the other hand, is a highly personal state where the person feels unhealthy, regardless of whether a doctor can find a physical disease. Disease is a medical term meaning an alteration in body functions that reduces the capacity or shortens the life span. A client can have a disease like hypertension without feeling ill, just as someone can feel deeply exhausted and unwell without any diagnosable medical condition.

The Illness-Wellness Continuum

The Illness-Wellness Continuum visualizes health as a sliding scale rather than a fixed box. At one end is premature death, and at the opposite end is high-level wellness. Moving toward the left means worsening health and increasing symptoms. Moving toward the right represents awareness, education, and personal growth that enhances well-being. This model proves that treatment of disease is only the first step; true health involves helping clients achieve their maximum potential beyond just being symptom-free.

Models of Health and Wellness

Nursing and medicine use different models to explain why people stay healthy or fall sick.

  • Clinical Model: Health means the absence of signs and symptoms of disease. Treatment focuses on fixing the broken body part.
  • Role Performance Model: Health is measured by a person's ability to fulfill societal roles like being a worker, parent, or student.
  • Adaptive Model: Health is creative adaptation to daily physical and social environmental changes and stressors.
  • Eudaimonistic Model: Health is actualization of human potential, incorporating total well-being of body, mind, and spirit.

Dimensions of Human Health

Human health is multidimensional. These areas interact constantly, meaning a problem in one dimension quickly affects the others.

  • Physical: Genetic makeup, body shape, age, and functional capacity.
  • Emotional: Ability to manage stress and express feelings appropriately.
  • Intellectual: Capacity to learn, think clearly, and make sound decisions.
  • Social: Ability to build healthy relationships and interact well with others.
  • Spiritual: Belief in a higher power, purpose, or meaning in life.
  • Environmental: Quality of the home, work, and community surroundings.

Stages of Illness and Illness Behavior

When people fall ill, they typically move through predictable behavioral stages:

  1. Experiencing Symptoms: The person notices something is wrong, such as pain or fatigue.
  2. Assuming the Sick Role: The person accepts that they are sick and seeks validation from family or friends, often taking time off work.
  3. Medical Care Contact: The person consults a healthcare provider for confirmation of illness and treatment advice.
  4. Dependent Client Role: The person undergoes treatment, relying on nurses and doctors for care.
  5. Recovery and Rehabilitation: The person gives up the sick role and returns to normal duties and health.

Internal and External Factors Influencing Health

Various internal and external variables shape how people view health and respond to illness. Internal factors include biological traits like genetics and sex, psychological stress levels, and cognitive beliefs about body function. External factors include the physical environment, socioeconomic status, family support systems, and cultural background. Cultural beliefs, for instance, dictate whether a person views a fever as a natural imbalance or an evil spell, directly influencing whether they visit a hospital or a traditional healer first.

The Impact of Illness on Patients and Families

Illness disrupts normal life routines. For the individual patient, sickness brings anxiety, loss of independence, financial strain, and altered body image. Families also experience significant stress. Roles must be shifted; if the breadwinner falls sick, financial stability shakes. If a mother is hospitalized, childcare responsibilities fall on others. Nurses must assess the whole family system because supporting the family improves the patient's chances of quick recovery and smooth rehabilitation.
