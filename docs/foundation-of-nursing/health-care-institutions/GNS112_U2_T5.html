Reception, Admission, Referral and Discharge Procedures

Introduction to Ward Reception and Orientation

The way a patient is received on the hospital ward sets the tone for their entire admission experience. First impressions matter immensely, especially for anxious individuals entering an unfamiliar environment. When a patient arrives from the emergency department or outpatient clinic, the receiving nurse must greet them warmly by name, introduce themselves, and immediately establish a reassuring presence. Ward orientation follows shortly after settling the patient into their bed space.

  • Physical environment: Show the patient how to operate the call bell, adjust the bed height, use the bedside locker, and locate the washroom.
  • Ward routine: Inform them about scheduled meal times, doctor visiting hours, and when vital signs are typically checked.
  • Safety measures: Explain fall prevention strategies, such as keeping side rails up when appropriate and non-slip footwear use.

An effective orientation significantly reduces patient anxiety, builds immediate trust, and promotes early cooperation with the care plan.

Types and Procedures of Hospital Admission

Hospital admission is the official process of accepting a patient for care and accommodation in a healthcare facility. Admissions generally fall into two main categories: emergency admissions and routine or elective admissions. Emergency admissions happen without prior planning, usually via the accident and emergency unit, due to acute illness or sudden trauma. Routine admissions are planned well in advance for scheduled surgeries, specialized investigations, or planned medical management.

The admission procedure requires systematic coordination. First, verify the patient's identity and match the admission order with the correct clinical specialty. Next, conduct a baseline physical assessment and check vital signs. Secure any valuables by either listing them on an inventory form and sending them home with family, or placing them in the hospital safe according to institutional policy. Finally, ensure the bed is comfortable and safe, complete all initial nursing documentation, and inform the attending medical team that the patient has arrived on the ward.

Documentation and Record Keeping During Admission

Accurate documentation during admission forms the legal and clinical foundation of the patient's hospital chart. The admission record captures essential baseline data, including demographic details, next-of-kin contacts, health insurance or payment information, and a detailed medical history.

Nurses use admission assessment tools to document physical findings, baseline vital signs, neurological status, skin integrity, and any known drug allergies. A common pitfall is leaving gaps in the initial assessment form or delaying documentation until the end of the shift, which can lead to forgotten clinical details. Every entry must be timely, legible, factual, and signed with the nurse's name and designation. These records protect the patient by ensuring continuity of care among different shift workers and protect the hospital legally by demonstrating adherence to professional standards.

Patient Referral Principles and Procedures

Patient referral is the formal process of sending a patient from one level of care to another, such as from a primary health clinic to a tertiary teaching hospital, or from a general ward to a specialized clinic. Referrals occur when a patient's medical needs exceed the resources, equipment, or staff expertise of the current facility.

A successful referral requires clear communication and meticulous paperwork. The transferring clinician must write a comprehensive referral letter summarizing the patient's history, diagnostic test results, treatment given so far, and the specific reason for transfer. Before moving the patient, the receiving facility must be contacted to confirm bed availability and acceptance. The nurse ensures that copies of all relevant charts, laboratory investigations, and nursing notes accompany the patient, along with any necessary transport equipment like oxygen cylinders or intravenous infusions.

Inter-Facility and Intra-Facility Transfer Guidelines

Patient transfers involve moving an individual either between different departments within the same hospital (intra-facility) or from one hospital to an entirely separate institution (inter-facility). Common intra-facility transfers include moving a stable patient from the intensive care unit to a general surgical ward, or transferring someone from the medical ward to the radiology department for specialized scans.

Safety is paramount during any transfer. The transferring nurse must provide a verbal handover to the receiving nurse, highlighting current clinical stability, active treatments, intravenous lines, and special precautions. The patient's vital signs must be stable enough to withstand the move. Appropriate escort personnel—such as a nurse or doctor—must accompany unstable patients during transit, carrying emergency equipment like a bag-valve-mask resuscitator and portable suction if needed.

Discharge Planning and Preparation

Discharge planning is a continuous, multi-disciplinary process that begins on the very day of admission, not just on the day the patient leaves. The goal is to ensure a smooth, safe transition from hospital-based care back to the home or a rehabilitation facility. Nurses work closely with doctors, physiotherapists, pharmacists, and medical social workers to assess what ongoing support the patient will need.

Key elements of discharge preparation include evaluating the home environment for safety hazards, arranging for required medical equipment like wheelchairs or wound care supplies, and scheduling follow-up clinic appointments. Patient and family education is vital during this phase. The nurse must explain the medication schedule, dietary restrictions, warning signs of complications, and activity limitations in clear, simple language that the family easily understands.

Types of Discharge and Nursing Responsibilities

Hospital discharge marks the official termination of the admission episode. There are several distinct types of discharge, each carrying specific nursing duties:

  • Routine Discharge: The patient has recovered and is released home with a medical clearance note and a supply of discharge medications.
  • Discharge Against Medical Advice (DAMA): A competent patient chooses to leave the hospital before the doctor recommends it. The nurse must inform the doctor, explain the risks of leaving early, and have the patient sign a DAMA form.
  • Discharge by Transfer: The patient is moved to another facility for continued care or rehabilitation.
  • Discharge by Death: The nurse provides respectful last offices, documents the time of death, ensures the doctor certifies it, and guides the grieving family.

Nursing responsibilities at discharge include returning any stored valuables, checking that all discharge medications and prescription forms are understood, providing the referral letter, and documenting the final mode of departure and time of leaving.
