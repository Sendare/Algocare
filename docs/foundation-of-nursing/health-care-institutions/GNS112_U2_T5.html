Reception, Admission, Referral and Discharge Procedures

Reception and Orientation to the Ward

The initial encounter between a patient and the healthcare team sets the emotional tone for their entire hospital stay. When a patient arrives at the ward, the receiving nurse must greet them by name, project a welcoming attitude, and immediately alleviate the anxieties common in unfamiliar hospital environments.

  • Physical Orientation: Show the patient their bed space, bathroom facilities, nurse call system, and emergency exits.
  • Personnel Introduction: Introduce the ward manager, primary nurse, attending physicians, and roommates if in a shared bay.
  • Routine Explanation: Clarify meal times, visiting hours, doctor's round schedules, and hospital policies regarding personal valuables.
  • Safety Briefing: Demonstrate how to adjust the bed, operate side rails, and use the bedside light.

Pathways and Protocols of Hospital Admission

Hospital admission is the official acceptance of a patient into a healthcare facility for diagnosis, observation, and treatment. Admissions generally fall into two distinct pathways:

  1. Routine Admission: Planned encounters where the patient is admitted from home following an outpatient consultation or elective surgical booking. Documentation and bed preparation occur ahead of time.
  2. Emergency Admission: Unplanned arrivals through the accident and emergency department due to acute trauma, sudden illness, or life-threatening crises. Stabilization takes precedence over administrative paperwork.

Upon arrival, the nurse must ensure bed readiness, verify identity bands, conduct baseline vital signs, and notify the attending physician.

Admission Documentation and Records Management

Accurate documentation during admission establishes the legal and clinical baseline for patient care. The nurse compiles several key documents including the admission register, inpatient clinical chart, nursing assessment form, and medication administration record.

Every entry must be legible, accurate, and timestamped. In Nigerian clinical practice, failing to record baseline observations or mislabeling the inpatient chart can lead to severe legal liabilities and compromised patient safety. Valuables such as jewelry, money, and phones must be documented on a property form, countersigned by the patient or relative, and either locked in the hospital safe or sent home with family according to institutional policy.

Principles and Procedures of Patient Referral

Referral is the formal process of directing a patient from one level of care to another, typically moving from a primary health center to a secondary or tertiary teaching hospital for specialized investigation and management.

An effective referral requires a comprehensive referral letter detailing the patient's history, findings, treatment given so far, and the specific reason for transfer. The transferring nurse must ensure the patient is hemodynamically stable for transit, accompanied by appropriate medical or nursing staff if critical, and equipped with necessary intravenous lines, oxygen, and emergency drugs. Communication with the receiving facility must occur prior to departure to guarantee bed availability and acceptance.

Transfer Guidelines: Intra-Facility and Inter-Facility

Patient movement within or between facilities demands rigorous coordination to prevent adverse events.

  • Intra-Facility Transfer: Movement between departments inside the same hospital, such as transferring a stable patient from the Intensive Care Unit to a general surgical ward. The nurse must forward the complete case file, provide a verbal handover to the receiving nurse, and verify that life-support equipment is maintained during transit.
  • Inter-Facility Transfer: Movement between separate hospitals. This requires specialized transport vehicles equipped with oxygen, suction, and monitoring devices, along with trained escorts capable of managing sudden deterioration.

Documentation of the exact time of transfer, mode of transit, and clinical status upon departure is mandatory.

Discharge Planning and Preparation

Discharge planning is a continuous, multidisciplinary process that begins at the moment of admission. It ensures the patient transitions safely from the hospital back to their home or a community care facility.

The nurse assesses the patient's home environment, family support systems, physical capabilities, and financial readiness for ongoing medication. Education is the cornerstone of this phase. Patients and caregivers must receive clear instructions regarding wound care, dietary modifications, physical activity limits, and warning signs that necessitate immediate re-admission. Coordination with community health extension workers or home-based care teams ensures seamless continuity of care in Nigerian settings where follow-up clinics may be distant.

Types of Discharge and Nursing Responsibilities

Discharge procedures vary depending on the circumstances under which a patient leaves the facility. The main categories include routine discharge upon clinical recovery, discharge against medical advice (DAMA), transfer to another facility, and death.

When a patient insists on leaving Against Medical Advice (DAMA), the nurse must inform the attending physician, explain the inherent medical risks of premature departure, and have the patient or legal guardian sign a DAMA release form. For a routine discharge, the nurse ensures all prescribed medications are dispensed, follow-up appointments are booked, personal belongings are returned, and the bed space is thoroughly decontaminated for the next admission.
