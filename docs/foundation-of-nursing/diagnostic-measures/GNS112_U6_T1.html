Introduction to General Examination of the Patient (Head-to-Toe)

Introduction and Purpose of General Examination

A general physical examination is a structured head-to-toe evaluation used to gather baseline data about a patient's health status. It allows the nurse to identify physical signs of illness, assess functional ability, and detect subtle changes before they become critical emergencies. Rather than focusing on a single complaint, this comprehensive review looks at the whole person, integrating physical findings with psychological and environmental observations. The information collected guides subsequent nursing care plans and medical diagnoses.

Preparation of the Environment and Patient

  • Environment: Ensure the room is quiet, well-lit, and comfortably warm. Draw curtains or close doors to protect privacy.
  • Patient Comfort: Ask the patient to empty their bladder before the examination. Explain every step clearly to reduce anxiety and gain cooperation.
  • Positioning: Adjust the bed to a comfortable working height for the nurse and position the patient appropriately for the body system being examined.

Essential Equipment and Infection Control Practices

A successful physical examination requires proper tools, including a stethoscope, sphygmomanometer, penlight, thermometer, and tongue depressor. Before touching the patient, hand hygiene must be performed using soap and water or alcohol-based rub. Clean all reusable equipment with disinfectant wipes between patients to prevent cross-contamination. Standard precautions must be maintained throughout the procedure, including wearing gloves if contact with body fluids or non-intact skin is anticipated.

General Survey and Initial Observations

The general survey begins the moment the nurse first sees the patient. It is a non-touch-based assessment of overall appearance, mental status, posture, and hygiene.

  • Apparent state of health: Does the patient look acutely ill, frail, or well?
  • Level of consciousness: Is the patient alert, oriented, drowsy, or unresponsive?
  • Signs of distress: Notice labored breathing, facial grimacing, or sweating.
  • Personal hygiene and dress: Are clothes appropriate for the weather? Is grooming neglected?

Measurement of Vital Signs

Vital signs provide objective data regarding the body's baseline physiological functions. These measurements include temperature, pulse rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation. Pain is often referred to as the fifth vital sign and should be assessed concurrently. Any abnormal value requires immediate re-evaluation and reporting to the primary clinician. Ensure equipment is calibrated and appropriate cuff sizes are used for blood pressure accuracy.

Systematic Head-to-Toe Physical Assessment Technique

A systematic physical assessment follows four core techniques in a specific order: inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. The only exception is the abdomen, where auscultation is performed before palpation and percussion to avoid altering bowel sounds. The examination moves logically from the head and face down to the neck, chest, abdomen, extremities, and skin. Maintaining a consistent routine ensures no body part or system is accidentally overlooked during the clinical encounter.

Documentation and Reporting of Findings

Accurate documentation creates a legal and clinical record of the patient's condition. Record findings promptly using clear, objective, and descriptive language rather than vague terms like 'normal' or 'fine.' Report critical or abnormal findings immediately to the attending physician or senior nurse. Ensure entries are signed, dated, and timed according to facility policy. Proper charting promotes continuity of care across different shifts and healthcare team members.
