Collection and Observation of Specimens

Introduction to Specimen Collection and Observation in Nursing

Specimen collection is a core nursing responsibility that directly impacts patient diagnosis and treatment plans. A specimen is a sample of tissue or body fluid taken from a patient to be examined in the laboratory. Nurses must collect these samples correctly so laboratory results reflect the patient's true condition. Any error during collection, such as contamination or mislabeling, can lead to wrong diagnoses and improper medical care. Observation begins the moment the specimen leaves the body. Nurses look at color, odor, consistency, and volume, recording these details in the patient chart. Clinical observation often provides immediate clues about a patient's health status before laboratory tests even return.

Standard Precautions and Infection Control During Collection

Collecting body fluids exposes healthcare workers to infectious agents. Standard precautions must be applied to every patient interaction, regardless of their known infection status. Hand hygiene before and after the procedure remains the single most effective way to stop cross-contamination.

  • Personal Protective Equipment: Wear clean gloves whenever handling body fluids, non-sterile items, or mucous membranes. Use masks and eye protection if splashing is possible.
  • Sharps Safety: Never recap used needles after drawing blood. Immediately dispose of sharp items in puncture-resistant containers located close to the point of use.
  • Spill Management: Clean up any spilled body fluids immediately using the approved hospital disinfectant solution and wearing heavy-duty gloves.

Collection and Observation of Urine Specimens

Urine collection varies depending on what the doctor is testing for. A random specimen is easy to get for routine urinalysis, but a clean-catch midstream specimen is needed when checking for urinary tract infections because it avoids bacteria living around the urethral opening. For a clean-catch, the patient cleans the genital area first, starts urinating into the toilet, and then catches the middle portion of the urine stream in a sterile cup.

For a 24-hour urine collection, the process requires saving all urine produced over a full day. The timer starts by having the patient empty their bladder and discarding that first void. Every drop of urine after that is saved in a special jug kept cold or with preservatives until the exact same time the next day.

Collection and Observation of Stool Specimens

Stool specimens are collected to check for gastrointestinal bleeding, parasites, or bacterial infections. When collecting stool, the nurse instructs the patient to pass the stool into a clean bedpan or a special collection hat placed over the toilet bowl. Urine must not mix with the stool sample because it can contaminate the results, especially when testing for parasites or microscopic blood.

Observation of stool includes noting its color, consistency, odor, and presence of abnormal elements like mucus, blood, or worms. For instance, black tarry stools often suggest bleeding in the upper gastrointestinal tract, while bright red blood usually points to bleeding in the lower colon or rectum.

Collection and Observation of Sputum Specimens

Sputum specimens are thick secretions coughed up from the lungs and lower respiratory tract, distinct from saliva produced in the mouth. They are typically collected to identify the specific microorganisms causing respiratory infections like tuberculosis or pneumonia. The best time to collect a sputum specimen is early in the morning before the patient eats or drinks, because secretions pool overnight.

The nurse asks the patient to take a few deep breaths and cough forcefully from deep within the chest to bring up an adequate sample. Observation focuses on color, consistency, and amount. Rusty or blood-streaked sputum can indicate pneumonia, while thick green or yellow sputum often points to a bacterial infection.

Collection and Observation of Vomitus

Vomitus, or emesis, is stomach contents expelled through the mouth. While not always sent to the lab for testing, careful observation and sometimes collection are required during acute illness. Nurses observe volume, color, consistency, and odor.

  • Clear or watery: Often indicates stomach acid or water.
  • Bile-stained (green or yellow): Suggests the presence of bile, common after prolonged vomiting when the stomach is empty.
  • Coffee-ground appearance: Indicates bleeding in the stomach where blood has been partially digested by gastric acid.
  • Fecal odor: Can indicate a bowel obstruction further down the digestive tract.

Documentation, Labelling, and Transport of Specimens

A perfectly collected specimen is useless if it is mislabeled or delayed in transit. Every specimen container must be labeled immediately at the bedside in the presence of the patient. The label must include the patient's full name, hospital identification number, date, time of collection, and the nurse's initials.

After labeling, place the specimen into a biohazard transport bag with the lab request form in the outer pouch. Transport the specimen to the laboratory promptly. Delays can cause bacteria to multiply or cellular components to degrade, giving false results to the medical team.
