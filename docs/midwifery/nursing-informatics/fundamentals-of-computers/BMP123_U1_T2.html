Hardware Components

Introduction to Computer Hardware

Computer hardware refers to the physical, tangible parts of a computer system that you can actually touch and see. In a modern healthcare setting, this includes everything from the desktop computer at the antenatal clinic desk to the specialized electronic fetal monitor by the bedside. Hardware relies on software—the intangible sets of instructions or programs—to function and perform useful tasks. Without hardware, software has nowhere to run; without software, hardware is just a collection of inert plastic and metal. Understanding these physical components helps midwives and healthcare workers properly utilize, care for, and troubleshoot the digital tools used for patient record-keeping and clinical management.

The Central Processing Unit (CPU)

Often called the brain of the computer, the Central Processing Unit (CPU) is responsible for executing instructions and processing data from software programs. It performs millions of calculations every second to make sure applications run smoothly. Inside the CPU, a control unit directs the flow of information, while the arithmetic logic unit (ALU) handles math calculations and logical decisions. When a midwife enters patient vital signs into an electronic health record, the CPU processes those numbers instantly to display graphs or alerts. CPU speed is typically measured in gigahertz (GHz), where a higher number generally indicates faster processing power.

Input Devices

Input devices allow users to feed data, commands, and signals into a computer system. They act as the primary bridge between human operators and machine processing. Common examples used daily in clinical and administrative environments include:

  • Keyboard: Used for typing text, patient names, and numeric data into records.
  • Mouse: Allows point-and-click navigation across the computer screen.
  • Barcode Scanner: Quickly reads patient wristband barcodes or medication labels to prevent errors.
  • Microphone: Captures voice input for digital dictation or telemedicine consultations.

Output Devices

Output devices take processed digital data from the computer and convert it into a human-readable form, such as text, images, sound, or physical printouts. Without them, users would have no way to see the results of the computer's work. Key output devices include:

  • Monitor: Displays visual windows, graphs, and patient records on a screen.
  • Printer: Produces hard copies of lab results, immunization cards, or admission forms.
  • Speakers: Emit audio alerts, alarm sounds, or voice prompts from software applications.

Storage Devices

Storage devices retain digital data either temporarily or permanently so it is not lost when the computer powers down.

  • Random Access Memory (RAM) provides fast, temporary workspace for data currently in use. When the computer turns off, RAM contents are completely erased.
  • Hard Disk Drives (HDD) and Solid State Drives (SSD) provide long-term storage for the operating system, applications, and saved patient files. SSDs use microchips and have no moving parts, making them much faster and more durable than older mechanical HDDs.
  • Flash drives and external hard drives offer portable storage for moving files between different computers in the facility.

Motherboard and Internal Components

The motherboard serves as the main circuit board of the computer. It acts as the central highway that connects every single component together, allowing the CPU, memory, storage drives, and peripheral ports to communicate. Power is supplied to the motherboard and internal parts by the Power Supply Unit (PSU), which converts standard wall outlet electricity into safe, lower voltages. Keeping these internal components cool is critical; cooling fans and heat sinks prevent the CPU and other sensitive chips from overheating during heavy workloads in warm environments.

Hardware Maintenance and Troubleshooting

Proper maintenance extends the lifespan of computer hardware and prevents sudden equipment failures in busy clinical environments. Dust accumulation can block airflow and cause overheating, making regular cleaning with compressed air essential. Using surge protectors shields sensitive electronics from sudden voltage spikes and unstable power grids common in many regions. When hardware problems occur—such as a computer failing to turn on—basic troubleshooting steps include checking power cable connections, ensuring wall sockets are active, and restarting peripheral devices before calling a technician.
