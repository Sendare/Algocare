Electronic Health Records (EHR) and Patient Data Security

Introduction to Electronic Health Records

An Electronic Health Record (EHR) is a digital version of a patient's paper chart. In modern Nigerian healthcare settings, moving from dusty paper folders to computer-based systems helps manage maternal and child health data efficiently. An EHR contains a comprehensive history of a patient's medical encounters, including antenatal visits, laboratory results, immunizations, and delivery notes. This shift allows different healthcare providers within a facility to access up-to-date client information instantly, reducing the delays often caused by tracking down missing physical files.

Benefits of Digital Documentation in Midwifery

Digital records transform how midwives monitor pregnant women and newborns. Unlike paper notes that can suffer from poor handwriting or physical damage, computer entries remain legible and standardized.

  • Legibility: Eliminates misinterpretation of handwritten drug dosages or clinical notes.
  • Speed: Retrieves past obstetric history within seconds during an emergency.
  • Decision Support: Provides automatic alerts for allergies, overdue vaccines, or abnormal vital signs.
  • Auditing: Tracks who accessed or modified a client file, improving accountability.

Core Components of Maternal and Neonatal EHR Systems

A well-designed maternal-neonatal EHR must capture specific clinical milestones unique to reproductive health. It typically includes an antenatal booking module to record baseline obstetric history, parity, and estimated due dates. Another crucial section is the partograph interface, which digitally plots cervical dilation and fetal heart rates during labor. Neonatal modules track immediate newborn resuscitation, APGAR scores, birth weight, and routine immunizations. Integrating these modules ensures seamless continuity of care from the first antenatal visit through postpartum and pediatric follow-up.

Patient Data Security and Confidentiality

Midwives handle sensitive personal health information every day. Patient data security involves protecting this information from unauthorized viewing, theft, or accidental loss. Confidentiality is both an ethical duty and a legal requirement. Clients must trust that their HIV status, obstetric complications, or personal histories will not be gossiped about or exposed to unauthorized staff. Breaching this trust damages the patient-provider relationship and discourages women from seeking timely facility-based care.

Common Cybersecurity Threats in Healthcare

Digital health systems face various threats that can compromise patient records. Ransomware attacks can lock hospital computers until cybercriminals receive payment, halting patient admissions and record retrieval. Phishing emails trick healthcare workers into giving up their login passwords. Additionally, physical threats remain high; leaving a computer workstation unlocked and unattended in a busy labor ward allows anyone to view or tamper with sensitive client files.

Legal and Ethical Frameworks in Nigeria

Health data management in Nigeria is guided by national legal standards. The Nigeria Data Protection Act (NDPA) governs how personal data, including health records, must be collected, processed, and stored. Healthcare institutions are legally mandated to protect client information and report data breaches. Furthermore, professional bodies like the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria enforce strict ethical codes regarding professional secrecy. Midwives who carelessly expose patient information face disciplinary hearings and potential legal penalties.

Best Practices for System Access and Password Hygiene

Securing electronic health records starts with daily personal habits at the computer terminal. Midwives must practice good password hygiene to prevent unauthorized access. Passwords should combine letters, numbers, and symbols, and must never be shared with colleagues or written on sticky notes attached to computer monitors. Staff should always log out of the EHR software when stepping away from the desk, even for a short break in the delivery suite.
