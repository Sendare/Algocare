Preterm Labor and Premature Rupture of Membranes (PROM / PPROM)

Understanding Preterm Labor

Preterm labor refers to regular uterine contractions that result in the progressive dilation and effacement of the cervix before the completion of 37 weeks of gestation. In Nigerian clinical practice, establishing an accurate gestational age is crucial because interventions change dramatically once the fetus reaches viability, typically pegged around 28 weeks in many resource-limited settings, though tertiary centers push toward 24 to 26 weeks with neonatal intensive care units.

The physiological transition from a quiet myometrium to active labor involves complex hormonal signals, including a drop in progesterone activity, a rise in estrogen effects, and the release of prostaglandins. When this cascade triggers too early, the neonate faces serious risks of organ immaturity, particularly involving the respiratory system. Midwives must recognize that preterm contractions are often painless or perceived merely as lower abdominal tightening or menstrual-like cramps, making patient education vital in antenatal clinics.

Premature Rupture of Membranes (PROM)

Premature Rupture of Membranes, commonly known as PROM, occurs when the amniotic sac breaks before the onset of true labor contractions, regardless of the gestational age. When this happens at term—at or after 37 weeks—the main priority is preventing ascending intrauterine infection and encouraging labor to start promptly to avoid prolonged rupture of membranes.

Midwives should listen carefully to a woman's history; a sudden gush of clear or pale fluid from the vagina is the classic symptom. Avoid routine digital vaginal examinations if labor is not imminent, as every internal examination introduces bacteria from the lower genital tract into the sterile intrauterine environment, significantly raising the risk of chorioamnionitis.

Preterm Premature Rupture of Membranes (PPROM)

When the amniotic sac ruptures before 37 weeks of gestation and before labor has begun, the condition is termed PPROM (Preterm Premature Rupture of Membranes). This clinical scenario requires a careful balancing act between the risks of intrauterine infection and the severe complications of extreme prematurity, such as respiratory distress syndrome and intraventricular hemorrhage.

Management usually involves hospital admission, avoidance of unnecessary vaginal examinations, administration of prophylactic antibiotics to prolong latency, and the use of corticosteroids to speed up fetal lung maturation. Midwives must monitor maternal vital signs closely, watching for early warning signs of infection like maternal tachycardia or uterine tenderness.

Causes and Risk Factors

The exact triggers for preterm labor and membrane rupture are often multifactorial, involving a mix of maternal, fetal, and environmental influences. Understanding these pathways helps midwives identify vulnerable women early during antenatal booking visits.

  • Genital Tract Infections: Bacterial vaginosis, urinary tract infections, and sexually transmitted infections release inflammatory cytokines that weaken fetal membranes and stimulate uterine contractions.
  • Obstetric History: A previous history of preterm birth or late miscarriage is one of the strongest predictors for recurrence in subsequent pregnancies.
  • Uterine Overdistension: Multifetal gestations, such as twins or triplets, and polyhydramnios stretch the myometrium beyond its normal capacity.
  • Placental Abnormalities: Conditions like placental abruption or previa interfere with normal placental function and blood flow.
  • Maternal Lifestyle and Health: Severe maternal malnutrition, hard physical labor, young or advanced maternal age, and chronic medical illnesses contribute significantly.

Clinical Presentation and Accurate Diagnosis

Diagnosing preterm labor and membrane rupture requires a combination of careful history taking, physical inspection, and specific bedside tests. A woman complaining of rhythmic backaches, pelvic pressure, or menstrual-like cramps warrants immediate assessment.

  • Speculum Examination: Used to inspect the cervix, assess dilation, and look for pooling of amniotic fluid in the posterior vaginal fornix.
  • Nitrazine Test: Checks the pH of vaginal secretions; amniotic fluid is alkaline (pH 7.0–7.5), turning Nitrazine paper blue, whereas normal vaginal secretions are acidic.
  • Fern Test: A sample of vaginal fluid is placed on a glass slide, allowed to dry, and examined under a microscope for a characteristic crystallization pattern resembling fern leaves.
  • Ultrasound Assessment: Measures cervical length and estimates amniotic fluid volume, helping confirm PPROM when fluid pockets are severely reduced.

Immediate Midwifery Assessment and Management

When a woman arrives showing signs of preterm labor or membrane rupture, the midwife must act promptly and methodically. Immediate priorities focus on stabilizing the mother, assessing fetal well-being, and initiating appropriate collaborative care.

Start by assessing maternal vital signs, noting any fever that might suggest chorioamnionitis. Perform an abdominal palpation to check fetal presentation and lie, followed by fetal heart rate auscultation or continuous electronic monitoring if available. Gather a detailed history regarding the time fluid began leaking or contractions started.

Collaborate with the medical team to administer prescribed medications such as tocolytics to temporarily suppress uterine contractions, corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity, and broad-spectrum antibiotics in cases of PPROM. Emotional support and clear communication are essential, as the mother and her family will likely feel frightened and anxious about the baby's early arrival.

Potential Complications for Mother and Neonate

Preterm labor, PROM, and PPROM carry significant risks for both the mother and the developing neonate. Recognizing these potential complications helps midwives maintain high vigilance during intrapartum and postnatal care.

For the neonate, the greatest threat stems from organ immaturity. Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) occurs due to a lack of surfactant. Other neonatal risks include intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis, neonatal sepsis, and long-term developmental delays.

For the mother, the primary hazards include chorioamnionitis (intrauterine infection), postpartum hemorrhage due to uterine atony from overdistension or infection, and the psychological trauma associated with delivering a premature or sick infant. Prompt recognition and team-based management remain the best tools to minimize these adverse outcomes.
