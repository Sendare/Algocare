Disordered Uterine Action

Introduction to Disordered Uterine Action

Normal labor relies on rhythmic, coordinated contractions that effectively dilate the cervix and push the fetus downward. When these contractions deviate from the normal pattern, the condition is known as disordered uterine action. Midwives practicing in Nigeria frequently encounter these anomalies, which can turn a straightforward birth into a prolonged or obstructed labor if not recognized early. Disordered action broadly falls into two categories: power failure, where contractions are too weak to progress labor, and abnormal coordination, where contractions work against each other or become excessively strong and rapid.

Hypotonic Uterine Dysfunction

In hypotonic uterine dysfunction, contractions start out normally during early labor but later become weak, infrequent, and completely ineffective at diluting the cervix. The uterus feels soft even during a contraction, and intra-uterine pressure remains low. This condition typically occurs in the active phase of the first stage of labor and is often linked to maternal exhaustion, overdistension from polyhydramnios or multiple gestation, or excessive use of pain medications. Midwives must differentiate this from cephalopelvic disproportion, as hypotonic inertia usually responds well to artificial rupture of membranes or carefully monitored oxytocin augmentation.

Hypertonic and Incoordinate Uterine States

Unlike hypotonic states, hypertonic uterine dysfunction features painful, frequent contractions that lack coordinated effectiveness. The resting tone of the myometrium is significantly elevated, meaning the muscle does not fully relax between contractions. This constant tension reduces blood flow to the placenta, frequently leading to early fetal distress. Women experiencing this state endure intense discomfort without making steady cervical progress. It is most common in anxious primigravidas during the latent phase of labor, often referred to as false labor or prodromal labor gone awry.

Uncoordinated Contractions and Uterine Polarity

Normal labor depends on uterine polarity, where the upper segment of the uterus dominates in strength and contracts rhythmically to push the baby down, while the lower segment remains relatively passive. In uncoordinated states, this harmony is lost. Pacemakers in the lower uterine segment may override the natural fundal pacemaker, creating reversed polarity. Alternatively, different parts of the uterus contract independently, causing spasm and trapping the fetus. This results in prolonged labor, maternal distress, and a high risk of uterine rupture if oxytocin is mistakenly administered.

Precipitate Labor and Rapid Delivery

At the opposite end of the spectrum from prolonged labor is precipitate labor, defined as a total labor and delivery lasting less than three hours. Contractions are intensely strong, frequent, and almost continuous from the start. While it sounds ideal to many pregnant women, rapid expulsion carries severe risks. The mother faces lacerations of the cervix, vagina, and perineum, along with an increased risk of postpartum hemorrhage due to uterine atony from exhaustion. The newborn faces risks of intracranial trauma from rapid pressure changes and potential hypoxia due to reduced placental refilling time.

Midwifery Assessment and Monitoring

Accurate assessment is the cornerstone of managing any uterine disorder. Midwives must use abdominal palpation to assess contraction frequency, duration, and resting tone, alongside vaginal examinations to track cervical dilation and effacement over time. The partograph serves as an essential tool in Nigerian maternity units to spot deviations from the normal alert and action lines. Continuous or intermittent fetal heart rate monitoring must accompany this, ensuring that the baby tolerates whatever contractile pattern is present.

Clinical Management and Interventions

Management depends strictly on identifying the underlying cause of the disordered action. For hypotonic dysfunction without obstruction, hydration, rest, amniotomy, and cautious oxytocin infusion are standard. For hypertonic or uncoordinated states, the approach focuses on rest, sedation, hydration, and removing any oxytocin immediately if it was running. Midwives must ensure continuous emotional support, proper positioning, and timely referral to a physician when dysfunctional labor fails to respond to conservative measures.
