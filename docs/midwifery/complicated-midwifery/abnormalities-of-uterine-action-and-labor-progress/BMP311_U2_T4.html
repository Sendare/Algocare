Uterine Rupture

Understanding Uterine Rupture and Predisposing Factors

Uterine rupture is a catastrophic obstetric emergency defined as a full-thickness separation of the uterine wall and the overlying serosa, occurring most frequently during advanced labor. When the muscular wall tears open, the intrauterine contents—including the fetus and placenta—may expel directly into the peritoneal cavity, posing an immediate, severe threat to maternal and fetal survival.

In Nigerian obstetric practice, this crisis arises from distinct background causes. Prior caesarean section scar dehiscence is a major contributor, especially where women attempt a vaginal birth after caesarean section without close monitoring. Another frequent driver is obstructed labor, where an unyielding pelvis or malpresentation forces the myometrium to contract against a physical block until the lower uterine segment thins out and gives way. Inappropriate use of uterotonic drugs like oxytocin or misoprostol can hyper-stimulate the uterus, triggering a rupture even in unscarred organs. Grand multiparity, direct abdominal trauma, and reckless intrauterine manipulations during breech deliveries or internal podalic version also feature prominently in local case reviews.

Complete Versus Incomplete: Types of Uterine Rupture

  • Complete Uterine Rupture: All layers of the uterine wall—endometrium, myometrium, and serosa—tear completely open. The amniotic cavity communicates directly with the peritoneal cavity. Fetal distress or demise occurs rapidly, and maternal hemorrhage is often massive.
  • Incomplete Uterine Rupture: The muscular wall tears, but the overlying peritoneum (serosa) remains intact. The fetus and placenta generally stay contained within the uterus or broad ligament. Bleeding is more concealed, making diagnosis trickier until routine postpartum exploration or ultrasound reveals the defect.

Recognizing the Clinical Features and Warning Signs

Spotting the early warning signs of an impending rupture saves lives. Before the actual tear, the patient may show signs of Bandl's ring, where a pathological retraction ring forms visibly across the abdomen, accompanied by excruciating lower uterine tenderness.

When the rupture actually happens, the clinical picture changes dramatically:

  1. Sudden sharp pain: The patient often feels a tearing sensation in her abdomen, sometimes describing a snap or pop.
  2. Cessation of contractions: Established labor contractions suddenly stop as the uterine muscle loses its structural integrity.
  3. Fetal distress to demise: Fetal heart sounds disappear instantly or drop to profound bradycardia as placental perfusion cuts off.
  4. Shock and collapse: Maternal pulse races, blood pressure plummets, and signs of internal hemorrhage mount quickly.
  5. Easy fetal palpation: The baby's parts can be felt unusually clearly through the abdominal wall, separate from the contracted uterine corpus.

Immediate Emergency Management on the Ward

Time is everything when managing a suspected or confirmed uterine rupture. The moment the diagnosis is suspected, call for senior medical help immediately while initiating critical resuscitation protocols.

Secure two large-bore intravenous cannulas (16G or 18G) without delay and commence rapid fluid resuscitation with normal saline or Ringer's lactate. Draw blood urgently for a full blood count, typing, and crossmatching of at least four units of whole blood. Pass an indwelling urinary catheter to monitor hourly urine output, which serves as a vital indicator of renal perfusion and shock recovery. Keep the patient nil per os in direct preparation for emergency laparotomy. Administer high-flow oxygen via a face mask to optimize oxygen delivery to any remaining placental tissue, and avoid giving further oxytocics.

Life-Saving Surgical Interventions and Repairs

Surgical management in the theatre aims to control hemorrhage, save the mother's life, and address the integrity of the uterus. Once the abdomen is opened via a midline incision, the team rapidly delivers the baby and placenta.

The choice between repairing the uterus (uterine conservation) and removing it (hysterectomy) depends on the patient's condition and parity:

  • Uterine Repair (Rupture Repair / Hysterorraphy): Performed if the tear is clean, easily accessible, and the patient is young with few or no children, or desires future fertility. The ragged edges are excised, and the myometrium is closed in layers.
  • Obstetric Hysterectomy: Indicated if the tear is jagged, extends down into the broad ligament or cervix, involves uncontrollable bleeding, or if the woman has completed her family. Total or subtotal hysterectomy is performed to save the patient's life from intractable hemorrhage.

Nursing Care and Prevention Strategies in Nigerian Maternity Units

Preventing uterine rupture requires vigilant intrapartum care across every level of the Nigerian health system. Midwives must plot every labor meticulously on the partograph, respecting action lines to catch prolonged or obstructed labor before the myometrium fails.

Careful auditing of oxytocic administration is non-negotiable. Never administer oxytocin or misoprostol without a clear indication, intact supervision, and ruling out cephalopelvic disproportion. For women with a history of a previous caesarean section, counsel them antenatally on the absolute necessity of delivering in a well-equipped facility rather than at home or in unmonitored faith clinics. Post-operatively, ensure meticulous wound care, monitor vital signs regularly, and offer compassionate psychological support to help mothers cope with the trauma of losing a baby or experiencing a life-threatening surgical ordeal.
