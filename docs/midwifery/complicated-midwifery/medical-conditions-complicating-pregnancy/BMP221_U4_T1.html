Anemia in Pregnancy

Physiological Changes and Hemodilution in Pregnancy

During pregnancy, a woman's body undergoes massive cardiovascular adjustments to support the developing foetus. The most striking change is a dramatic expansion in blood volume. Plasma volume increases by about 40 to 50 percent, while red blood cell mass increases by only 20 to 30 percent.

This uneven expansion creates a condition known as physiological anemia or hemodilution. Because the fluid portion of the blood increases much faster than the cells, laboratory tests show a lower concentration of hemoglobin and red blood cells. It is not a true deficiency of iron, but rather a state of being watered down. Recognizing this normal shift prevents midwives from panicking over mild drops in hemoglobin during routine antenatal booking visits.

Etiology and Risk Factors in Gravid Women

While hemodilution accounts for mild drops in hemoglobin, true pathological anemia stems from specific underlying causes. In Nigerian clinical practice, iron deficiency remains the leading culprit, driven by the high demands of the growing foetus and placenta coupled with poor dietary intake.

Other notable causes include:

  • Folate deficiency: Results from inadequate green leafy vegetable intake or increased cell turnover.
  • Malaria: Parasites destroy red blood cells rapidly, making routine prevention vital in endemic zones.
  • Hookworm infestation: Causes chronic, silent gastrointestinal blood loss.
  • Hemoglobinopathies: Conditions like sickle cell disease (HbSS or HbSC) complicate pregnancy management significantly.

Risk factors multiplying these threats include closely spaced pregnancies, teenage pregnancies, multiple gestations, and low socioeconomic status limiting access to nutritious food.

Clinical Presentation and Complications

Anemia often develops insidiously, allowing pregnant women to adapt to mild drops in hemoglobin. As the condition worsens, classic signs emerge. Midwives must watch out for pallor of the conjunctivae, nail beds, and palms, alongside complaints of generalized fatigue, shortness of breath on exertion, palpitations, and dizziness.

Left unmanaged, severe anemia threatens both mother and baby.

A tired, poorly oxygenated myocardium struggles to cope with the extra load of labor, making postpartum hemorrhage life-threatening even with normal blood loss.

Screening, Laboratory Diagnosis, and Thresholds

Early detection is the cornerstone of effective management. Every woman must have her hemoglobin or packed cell volume checked at her first booking visit, repeated at 28 weeks, and again at 36 weeks.

According to World Health Organization standards adopted in many settings:

  • First trimester: Hemoglobin below 11.0 g/dL indicates anemia.
  • Second trimester: Hemoglobin below 10.5 g/dL indicates anemia due to peak hemodilution.
  • Third trimester: Hemoglobin below 11.0 g/dL indicates anemia.

A reading below 7.0 g/dL signals severe anemia, requiring urgent medical intervention. Midwives should also review red blood cell indices when available to differentiate microcytic iron deficiency from other blood disorders.

Proactive Prevention Strategies

Preventing nutritional anemia is far more effective than treating it once established. Antenatal care clinics must prioritize routine supplementation and education.

  • Routine Iron and Folic Acid Supplementation: Administering daily tablets containing elemental iron and folic acid to all pregnant women attending clinics.
  • Intermittent Preventive Treatment (IPTp): Using sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine at specified intervals to clear malaria parasites.
  • Insecticide-Treated Nets (ITNs): Promoting consistent use to prevent mosquito bites.
  • Deworming: Providing a single dose of an anthelmintic medication in the second trimester where hookworm is prevalent.
  • Nutritional Counseling: Encouraging local diets rich in animal protein, dark green leafy vegetables, and vitamin C to boost iron absorption.

Medical and Pharmacological Treatment Protocols

When routine prevention fails and mild-to-moderate anemia develops, active therapeutic measures begin. Oral iron therapy remains the first-line treatment. Midwives must counsel women on expected side effects, such as dark stools and mild constipation, to ensure compliance.

To enhance absorption, oral iron is best taken between meals or with a source of vitamin C, while substances like tea, coffee, and milk should be avoided at the same time.

If oral iron fails due to gastrointestinal intolerance or malabsorption, or if the pregnancy is too far advanced in the third trimester with significant anemia, parenteral iron (intravenous infusion) is prescribed. Intravenous iron bypasses the gut, rapidly replenishing iron stores with fewer digestive complaints.

Midwifery Management of Severe Anemia and Emergencies

Severe anemia (hemoglobin below 7.0 g/dL) is an obstetric emergency, particularly when diagnosed in the late third trimester or close to expected delivery. The compromised myocardium cannot handle the stress of labor or postpartum blood loss.

Midwifery actions include:

  1. Immediate Hospital Admission: Transfer the patient to a facility with a functional blood bank and medical doctor support.
  2. Crossmatching and Blood Transfusion: Facilitate urgent blood grouping and transfusion of packed cells as ordered.
  3. Cardiac Monitoring: Watch closely for signs of heart failure, such as tachycardia, basal crepitations, and increasing breathlessness.
  4. Infection Screening: Check for and treat concurrent malaria or urinary tract infections.
  5. Active Management of the Third Stage: Prepare uterotonics meticulously during labor to minimize blood loss, since even standard postpartum bleeding can prove fatal here.
