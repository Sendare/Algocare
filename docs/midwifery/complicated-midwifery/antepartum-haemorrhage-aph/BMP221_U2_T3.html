Abruptio Placentae

Understanding Abruptio Placentae and Pathophysiology

Abruptio placentae, also known as placental abruption, is the premature separation of a normally implanted placenta from the uterine wall before the delivery of the fetus. This dangerous condition usually occurs after the 20th week of pregnancy. The primary event is bleeding into the decidua basalis, which forms a retroplacental blood clot. As this hematoma grows, it further separates the placental tissue, compresses the underlying muscle, and disrupts the vital oxygen and nutrient exchange between the mother and the baby.

The exact cause is often multi-factorial, but several strong risk factors are well-documented in Nigerian maternity practice:

  • Hypertensive disorders: Chronic hypertension or pre-eclampsia puts immense pressure on delicate spiral arterioles.
  • Abdominal trauma: Motor vehicle accidents, domestic violence, or accidental falls directly jar the uterus.
  • Sudden uterine decompression: Rapid loss of amniotic fluid, such as during the delivery of the first twin or severe polyhydramnios.
  • Previous abruption: A history of placental abruption significantly increases recurrence risk in subsequent pregnancies.
  • Substance use: Prolonged vasoconstriction linked to tobacco or illicit drug use weakens vascular integrity.

Classification and Types of Placental Abruption

Midwives classify abruptions based on how the blood escapes and the extent of placental detachment. Understanding these types helps predict clinical severity and hidden blood loss.

Type of AbruptionCharacteristicsClinical Picture
RevealedBlood tracks down between the membranes and the uterus, escaping through the vagina.External bleeding is visible; matches the degree of maternal shock.
ConcealedBlood is trapped behind the placenta; margins remain sealed.No external bleeding; severe pain and disproportionate uterine enlargement.
MixedInitial concealed hemorrhage that eventually breaks through and becomes visible vaginally.Severe pain with both internal pooling and external bleeding observed.

Clinically, abruptions are also graded from Grade 0 (asymptomatic, discovered after birth) to Grade 3 (severe, often resulting in fetal death and maternal coagulation failure). A common misconception is that visible blood volume always reflects total blood loss; in concealed abruption, a woman can be in profound hypovolemic shock with very little blood seen on the maternity bed.

Clinical Presentation and Maternal-Fetal Signs

The classic presentation of severe abruption is unmistakable once witnessed in the labour ward. The midwife must act quickly upon recognizing the triad of symptoms: sudden, sharp abdominal pain, dark vaginal bleeding, and uterine tenderness.

  • Uterine tenderness and tone: The uterus feels remarkably firm, board-like, and fails to relax between contractions (hypertonus).
  • Pain: Unlike normal labor contractions that come and go, abruption pain is constant, severe, and localized over the placental site.
  • Fetal heart rate abnormalities: Fetal distress, bradycardia, or absent heart sounds are common due to acute hypoxia.
  • Maternal shock signs: Pallor, sweating, rapid thready pulse, and a dropping blood pressure that often looks worse than the amount of blood on the linen.

Always evaluate the relationship between the amount of external bleeding and the degree of maternal shock. If a woman is pale and dizzy with minimal vaginal blood, suspect concealed abruption immediately.

Immediate Emergency Care and Resuscitation

Placental abruption is a true obstetric emergency requiring a multidisciplinary team response involving midwives, obstetricians, and anaesthetists. Time lost equals tissue death and potential maternal mortality.

  1. Call for help: Activate the emergency obstetric protocol immediately.
  2. Establish large-bore IV access: Insert two large-bore cannula lines (16G or 18G) to prepare for aggressive fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion.
  3. Draw emergency blood work: Send samples urgently for full blood count, clotting profile, grouping, and cross-matching.
  4. Administer high-flow oxygen: Provide face-mask oxygen to maximize fetal oxygenation.
  5. Continuous vital sign monitoring: Track maternal blood pressure, pulse, and oxygen saturation every 15 minutes.
  6. Expedite delivery: If the fetus is alive and mature, immediate caesarean section is standard. If fetal death has occurred, vaginal delivery may be considered if clotting parameters are stable and labor is progressing rapidly.

Severe Complications Including Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

The cascading effects of a major abruption extend far beyond simple blood loss. One of the most feared systemic complications is Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), a life-threatening condition where the body's blood-clotting mechanism becomes globally overactive.

When the placenta separates, large amounts of tissue thromboplastin are released from the decidua into the maternal circulation. This triggers widespread micro-clotting throughout the blood vessels, consuming platelets and vital clotting factors (fibrinogen). Consequently, the blood loses its ability to clot, leading to profuse, uncontrolled bleeding from venipuncture sites, surgical incisions, gums, and the uterus.

Other severe complications include Couvelaire uterus (uteroplacental apoplexy), where blood infiltrates the entire myometrium, turning the uterine surface bruised and purple, impairing post-delivery muscle contraction. Renal failure can also develop secondary to prolonged hypotension and shock, causing acute tubular necrosis.

Midwifery Monitoring and Collaborative Management

Continuous, meticulous midwifery care is the thin line between survival and tragedy for a mother experiencing placental abruption. Monitoring requires clinical vigilance and precise documentation.

  • Fluid balance chart: Insert an indwelling urinary catheter (such as a Foley catheter) to measure strict hourly urine output. Urine output below 30 mL/hour signals failing renal perfusion and developing shock.
  • Coagulation tracking: Monitor for petechiae, bleeding gums, or oozing from IV sites as early warning signs of coagulopathy.
  • Postpartum surveillance: Watch the uterus closely after delivery for uterine atony, especially in cases of Couvelaire uterus where the muscle is bruised and sluggish. Administer uterotonics as prescribed and perform bimanual compression if necessary.
  • Psychological support: Provide calm, reassuring communication to the frightened mother and her family members throughout the emergency procedures.
