Incomplete Abortion/Miscarriage

Understanding Incomplete Abortion

An incomplete abortion occurs when some, but not all, products of conception are expelled from the uterine cavity during a miscarriage. Typically happening before the 22nd week of pregnancy, this clinical state leaves behind placental or fetal tissue, preventing the myometrium from contracting effectively. Because the uterus cannot clamp down fully, open blood vessels bleed continuously, leading to the hallmark sign of persistent vaginal bleeding. In Nigerian clinical practice, midwives frequently encounter women presenting with this condition following either spontaneous pregnancy loss or unsafe, induced attempts. Recognizing this state promptly prevents heavy blood loss and severe infection. The retained tissue acts as a foreign body, keeping the internal cervical os open, which differentiates it from a missed abortion or a threatened abortion where the os remains closed.

Pathophysiology and Common Causes

The biological trigger for an incomplete abortion often begins with chromosomal abnormalities in the developing embryo, making normal growth impossible. Once embryonic death occurs, the body initiates a rejection process, dilating the cervix and contracting the myometrium. However, the process stalls halfway, leaving fragments attached to the decidua.

Beyond natural chromosomal errors, other factors contribute significantly:

  • Infections: Untreated reproductive tract infections weaken membranes and trigger early expulsion.
  • Endocrine disorders: Poorly controlled maternal conditions like severe diabetes or thyroid disease disrupt placental attachment.
  • Trauma or unsafe methods: Physical trauma, herbal concoctions, or clandestine instrumentation commonly force incomplete expulsion in resource-limited settings.

Midwives must understand these underlying triggers to provide holistic care and address root causes during recovery.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment

Women with an incomplete abortion typically arrive at the health facility complaining of lower abdominal cramp-like pains coupled with vaginal bleeding that ranges from moderate to severe. During the initial nursing assessment, ask about the approximate gestational age, the amount of blood passed, and whether any identifiable tissue masses were expelled at home.

Physical examination reveals:

  • Vital signs: Tachycardia and hypotension if blood loss has been heavy.
  • Abdominal palpation: Mild lower abdominal tenderness without true generalized rigidity.
  • Speculum examination: Blood in the vaginal vault and active bleeding through a dilated cervical os, sometimes with tissue visible at the opening.
  • Bimanual examination: A uterus that feels smaller than expected for the dates, but larger than non-pregnant, accompanied by cervical motion tenderness.

Careful assessment helps determine the urgency of intervention and guides fluid resuscitation.

Differential Diagnosis in Emergency Care

When a woman presents with early pregnancy bleeding and abdominal pain, midwives must quickly differentiate an incomplete abortion from other life-threatening conditions. A ruptured ectopic pregnancy presents with severe, sharp unilateral pain, signs of internal hemorrhage, and a uterus that may feel normal in size with a closed os. Molar pregnancy (hydatidiform mole) shows excessive uterine size for gestational age, passage of grape-like vesicles, and severely high blood pressure or hyperemesis. Threatened abortion features vaginal bleeding, but the cervical os remains tightly closed and the pregnancy remains viable inside. Additionally, pelvic inflammatory disease or a bleeding cervical polyp can mimic symptoms. Quick use of urine pregnancy tests, pelvic ultrasound, and careful history taking ensure the woman receives the correct life-saving management.

Emergency Stabilization and Nursing Care

Immediate stabilization takes precedence in managing any patient with acute bleeding from an incomplete abortion. Establish large-bore intravenous access without delay and commence rapid infusion of normal saline or Ringer's lactate to restore circulating blood volume if signs of shock appear. Draw blood for baseline investigations, including hemoglobin level, blood grouping, and cross-matching, in case an urgent blood transfusion becomes necessary.

Monitor vital signs every fifteen minutes until the patient is stable. Administer prescribed uterotonics, such as oxytocin or misoprostol, to encourage uterine contraction and minimize further blood loss while awaiting definitive evacuation. Provide continuous reassurance, as women experiencing pregnancy loss often suffer intense anxiety, fear, and grief. Keep accurate fluid balance charts and document all interventions meticulously.

Medical and Surgical Evacuation Methods

Once the patient is stabilized, the remaining products of conception must be cleared from the uterine cavity to stop bleeding and prevent infection. Two primary approaches exist: medical management and surgical evacuation.

  • Medical Management: Utilizes medications such as misoprostol to stimulate uterine contractions and expel the remaining tissue naturally. It avoids surgical risks but may cause prolonged cramping and bleeding.
  • Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA): A safe, highly effective surgical method performed under local anesthesia using a specialized handheld syringe to gently empty the uterus.
  • Suction Curettage (D&C): Performed in an operating theater using metal curettes and electric suction, often reserved for larger gestational ages or when MVA equipment is unavailable.

The choice depends on gestational age, extent of retained tissue, facility resources, and client preference.

Post-Abortion Counseling and Contraception

Recovery from an incomplete abortion extends far beyond physical evacuation; emotional healing and reproductive planning are equally vital components of midwifery care. Post-abortion counseling creates a safe space for the woman to express grief, guilt, or anxiety without fear of judgment. Midwives must address misconceptions about the cause of the miscarriage and provide compassionate reassurance.

Furthermore, fertility returns very quickly following an abortion—ovulation can resume within two weeks. Midwives should discuss contraceptive options before the patient is discharged home. Most methods, including oral contraceptive pills, injectables, implants, and intrauterine devices, can be initiated immediately if the woman desires. Tailoring the choice to her personal preferences ensures better adherence and empowers her reproductive health choices.

Complications and Preventive Midwifery Strategies

Unresolved or poorly managed incomplete abortions carry significant risks that can threaten a woman's long-term health and future fertility. Hemorrhage remains the most immediate acute danger, potentially leading to severe shock and organ failure. Septic abortion arises when bacteria ascend from the lower genital tract into the infected retained tissue, manifesting with high fever, foul-smelling discharge, and lower abdominal rigidity. Long-term complications include Asherman's syndrome (uterine synechiae), where aggressive curettage causes intrauterine adhesions that lead to secondary infertility or recurrent miscarriages.

Midwives play a crucial preventive role by providing timely post-abortion care, promoting safe abortion care where legal, ensuring sterile techniques during instrumentation, and educating communities on prompt healthcare-seeking behavior for abnormal bleeding.
