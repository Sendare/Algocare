Threatened Abortion/Miscarriage

Defining Threatened Abortion and Understanding the Underlying Pathology

A threatened abortion refers to any bleeding from the female genital tract before the 22nd week of pregnancy, while the cervical os remains completely closed and the products of conception have not been expelled. It is a warning sign that the pregnancy is in jeopardy, but it does not mean a miscarriage is inevitable. Many women who experience this condition go on to carry their pregnancies to full term without further incident.

The underlying pathology usually begins with partial detachment of the chorionic membrane from the decidua basalis. This minor separation causes bleeding into the intervillous space, which then tracks down through the cervical canal and appears on the perineum as vaginal spotting or light bleeding. Unlike inevitable or incomplete miscarriages, the uterine contractions are either absent or very mild, and the total volume of blood loss is typically small.

Etiology and Risk Factors in Early Pregnancy Bleeding

Most cases of first-trimester bleeding stem from chromosomal abnormalities in the developing embryo, which naturally disrupt normal growth. Other triggers can be maternal, environmental, or anatomical. Recognizing these triggers helps midwives provide targeted advice during antenatal booking clinics.

  • Chromosomal anomalies: Trisomies and monosomies account for over half of early pregnancy losses.
  • Endocrine deficiencies: Inadequate progesterone production by the corpus luteum can fail to maintain the thick endometrial lining.
  • Uterine anomalies: Submucous fibroids, uterine septa, or a bicornuate uterus can prevent proper implantation.
  • Infections: Severe systemic illnesses or ascending genital tract infections create hostile uterine environments.
  • Maternal lifestyle: Heavy alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, and illicit drug use sharply increase baseline risk.

Clinical Features and Presentation in the Midwifery Patient

When a pregnant woman walks into a primary health centre or tertiary hospital complaining of bleeding, midwives must perform a swift, calm assessment.

  • Vaginal bleeding: Usually painless or accompanied by mild, dull, intermittent lower backache or menstrual-like cramping.
  • Gestational age: Symptoms present before 22 completed weeks of gestation (often between 6 and 12 weeks).
  • Systemic signs: The patient is usually hemodynamically stable unless bleeding has persisted heavily over many days.
  • Abdominal examination: The uterus corresponds in size to the estimated period of amenorrhea, and palpation yields minimal or no tenderness.

Diagnostic Assessment and Ultrasonographic Evaluation

Clinical history and physical examination alone cannot confirm whether a threatened abortion will progress or resolve. Diagnostic tools are essential to visualize the intrauterine contents and measure hormone levels.

Pelvic ultrasonography—preferably transvaginal—is the gold standard. It checks for the presence of an intrauterine gestational sac, yolk sac, embryonic pole, and most importantly, cardiac activity. Seeing a live embryo with a regular heartbeat dramatically improves the prognosis.

Laboratory tests complement imaging. Serum human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) quantitative titers can be monitored 48 hours apart; a rising trend usually indicates a continuing pregnancy. Hemoglobin and hematocrit tests assess maternal blood status, while blood grouping and Rh factor screening are vital in case anti-D immunoglobulin is required for Rh-negative mothers.

Comprehensive Management and Supportive Care Protocols

Management of a threatened abortion focuses primarily on expectant observation and emotional reassurance. There are very few medications proven to reverse a failing pregnancy, so supportive care is the cornerstone of midwifery practice.

Bed rest was historically prescribed for weeks, though modern evidence shows strict bed rest does not prevent miscarriage. Instead, advising sensible lifestyle modification—avoiding strenuous physical labor, heavy lifting, and coitus until bleeding stops—is standard practice.

Progesterone supplementation (either oral, vaginal, or intramuscular) is sometimes prescribed by clinicians if a hormonal deficiency is suspected, particularly in women with a history of recurrent miscarriages. Midwives must ensure prescribed medications are administered correctly, monitor for side effects, and keep accurate charts of the patient's bleeding pattern and pain levels.

Psychological Support and Patient Counseling for Expectant Mothers

The emotional toll of bleeding in early pregnancy is profound. Many Nigerian women experience deep anxiety, fear of losing the pregnancy, or cultural stigma surrounding pregnancy complications. Midwives occupy a unique frontline position to offer empathetic psychological support.

Counseling sessions should create a safe space for the mother to express her fears without judgment. Midwives must explain the diagnosis in simple, clear language, dispelling myths that blame the woman for the bleeding. Reassurance should be honest; while many pregnancies survive, families should also be gently prepared for any outcome so they feel supported regardless of the result. Involving partners in these counseling sessions fosters family solidarity and strengthens home-based care.

Complications, Differential Diagnoses, and When to Refer

While many threatened abortions resolve favorably, midwives must remain vigilant for signs that the condition is worsening or that an alternative diagnosis is present.

  • Differential diagnoses: Ectopic pregnancy, molariform pregnancy (hydatidiform mole), cervical polyps, and implantation bleeding must be ruled out.
  • Progression: A threatened abortion can progress to an inevitable, incomplete, or missed abortion if bleeding increases or the cervix dilates.
  • Maternal complications: Severe hemorrhage, infection (septic abortion), and psychological trauma.
  • Indication for referral: Any patient presenting with severe lower abdominal pain, heavy bleeding soaking multiple pads per hour, signs of hypovolemic shock, or ultrasound findings of an ectopic pregnancy must be transferred immediately to a higher-level obstetric facility.
