Missed Abortion/Miscarriage

Understanding Missed Abortion/Miscarriage

A missed abortion, sometimes referred to as a silent miscarriage, occurs when an intrauterine pregnancy ceases to develop, but the conceptus is retained within the uterus for weeks without being expelled. Unlike a threatened or inevitable miscarriage, the typical clinical warning signs of acute vaginal bleeding and strong crampy abdominal pain are frequently absent. The placental hormones drop, but the cervix remains closed, and the products of conception do not pass.

In many Nigerian clinical settings, patients often discover this condition unexpectedly during routine antenatal ultrasound scans rather than due to alarming symptoms. Some women might notice a sudden disappearance of early pregnancy symptoms, such as breast tenderness or nausea, but these subtle changes are easily overlooked or attributed to normal hormonal fluctuations. Recognizing this silent loss is vital for preventing prolonged physical retention, which carries serious clinical risks.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The biological trigger for a missed miscarriage usually originates from chromosomal abnormalities within the embryo itself. When genetic codes fail to orchestrate normal cellular division, embryonic development halts. Following this cessation, the corpus luteum stops producing progesterone, causing the decidua to break down and detach the gestational sac.

While chromosomal errors account for the majority of first-trimester losses, maternal systemic factors also contribute significantly in tropical environments. Uncontrolled chronic infections, severe endocrine imbalances, and severe nutritional deficiencies can impair placental implantation and function. Midwives should understand that most missed miscarriages are natural selections against non-viable genetic pairings rather than the result of physical trauma or maternal fault.

Clinical Features and Patient Presentation

Patients presenting with a missed abortion often report subtle or absent symptoms. Common clinical indicators include:

  • Amenorrhea: Cessation of menses matching early gestational age, though the pregnancy is no longer progressing.
  • Brownish Vaginal Discharge: Light spotting or dark, old blood instead of bright red bleeding.
  • Regression of Symptoms: Sudden disappearance of nausea, vomiting, and breast fullness.
  • Uterine Size Discrepancy: The fundal height measures smaller than expected for the calculated dates based on the last menstrual period.

Diagnostic Evaluation in the Nigerian Healthcare Context

Accurate diagnosis relies heavily on a combination of clinical history, physical examination, and objective investigations. In many Nigerian primary and secondary health facilities, access to immediate ultrasonography varies widely.

  • Pelvic Ultrasonography: The gold standard. Transvaginal or transabdominal ultrasound reveals an empty gestational sac, a sac with an embryo lacking cardiac activity, or irregular gestational structures.
  • Serum Beta-hCG: Serial quantitative human chorionic gonadoprotein measurements show plateauing or falling levels rather than the expected doubling in a viable pregnancy.
  • Hemoglobin Estimation: Essential to baseline the patient's blood status, especially if low-grade chronic bleeding is present or if surgical evacuation is planned in settings with high anemia prevalence.

Management Strategies and Treatment Protocols

Management options for a missed abortion include expectant, medical, and surgical approaches. The choice depends on gestational age, resource availability, and patient preference.

  • Expectant Management: Allowing the body to pass the tissue naturally. This avoids medical or surgical intervention but requires patience and close follow-up.
  • Medical Management: Using medications such as misoprostol to stimulate uterine contractions and empty the cavity. This is particularly useful in resource-constrained settings where surgical theaters are limited.
  • Surgical Management: Uterine evacuation via Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) or Dilatation and Curettage (D&C). MVA is widely preferred in modern midwifery practice for first-trimester losses due to its safety profile and lower complication rates compared to sharp curettage.

Potential Complications and Emergency Response

Although many miscarriages proceed without major incident, retained tissue can lead to severe clinical complications. Midwives must remain vigilant for signs of hemorrhage, incomplete abortion, and septic abortion.

If infection develops due to ascending bacteria, the patient may present with foul-smelling vaginal discharge, pelvic pain, and fever. This demands urgent broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy alongside prompt uterine evacuation. Severe bleeding requires large-bore intravenous access, fluid resuscitation, and oxytocic agents. Recognizing early signs of shock prevents maternal mortality in emergency situations.

Psychological Support and Midwifery Care

The emotional toll of a missed miscarriage is frequently underestimated by families and communities who may dismiss early pregnancy loss as insignificant. Patients often experience profound grief, guilt, and anxiety about future fertility. Midwives play a central role in offering compassionate, non-judgmental counseling.

Providing a safe space for the mother and her partner to express their sorrow is as important as managing the physical body. Midwives should offer clear explanations of the medical process, dispel common cultural myths regarding the cause of the loss, and ensure appropriate follow-up care is scheduled before discharge.
