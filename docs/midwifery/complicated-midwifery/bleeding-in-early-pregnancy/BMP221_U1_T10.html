Cervical Incompetence

Understanding Cervical Incompetence in Early Pregnancy

Cervical incompetence, also known as cervical insufficiency, describes the inability of the uterine cervix to retain a pregnancy in the absence of uterine contractions. Typically, the cervix remains firm, closed, and long until the end of pregnancy. In a woman with an incompetent cervix, premature painless dilation and shortening occur, usually during the second trimester between 18 and 22 weeks. This leads to the expulsion of membranes and fetus without the classical warning signs of true labor, such as painful uterine tightening or bloody show.

Students in Nigerian clinical practice often confuse this condition with threatened miscarriage. While a threatened miscarriage usually starts with cramping or bleeding followed by cervical changes, cervical incompetence presents in reverse. The structural weakness gives way silently first, and bleeding or rupture of membranes happens afterward as a direct result of the prolapsing sac. Recognizing this distinction is vital for accurate emergency and ongoing management in district hospitals and tertiary facilities alike.

Underlying Pathophysiology

The mechanical strength of the cervix relies heavily on its unique tissue composition. Unlike the uterine body which is mostly muscular, the cervix is composed of approximately 70 to 80 percent extracellular matrix, primarily dense collagen fibers (mostly Type I and III), with smooth muscle making up the remaining fraction.

When cervical incompetence occurs, an alteration in this biochemistry takes place prematurely. Factors contributing to this structural failure include:

  • Congenital factors: Structural defects such as Mullerian duct anomalies or genetic variations in collagen synthesis.
  • Traumatic injury: Previous surgical procedures on the cervix, including deep cervical lacerations during a difficult delivery, dilation and curettage (D&C), or cone biopsies.
  • Biochemical degradation: Premature local activation of collagenases and inflammatory enzymes that break down the fibrous matrix much earlier than normal term labor.

Identifying Clinical Risk Factors and Patient History

A detailed obstetric history remains one of the most powerful tools a midwife has for identifying women at risk of cervical incompetence. Because the first pregnancy loss due to this condition often acts as the diagnostic clue, midwives must listen carefully to past reproductive timelines.

Key historical red flags include:

  • A history of recurrent second-trimester pregnancy losses that occurred rapidly and without pain.
  • Previous history of mid-trimester premature rupture of membranes followed by rapid expulsion.
  • History of fast, painless cervical dilation in past deliveries.
  • Prior gynecological surgeries involving the cervix, such as loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) or cone biopsy.

Identifying these risk factors during the booking visit allows the healthcare team to plan serial ultrasound monitoring before symptoms ever begin.

Diagnostic Approaches and Ultrasound Assessment

Diagnosing cervical incompetence often combines a careful review of past history with objective transvaginal ultrasound measurements. Transvaginal sonography is the gold standard for measuring cervical length because abdominal ultrasound can be distorted by a full bladder or maternal habitus.

During routine or targeted scans between 16 and 24 weeks, the midwife and sonographer look for specific signs:

  • Shortening length: A cervical length measuring less than 25 mm before 24 weeks is widely considered shortened.
  • Funneling: The internal os opens up while the external os remains closed, creating a V-shape or U-shape fluid-filled pocket of membranes bulging into the canal.
  • Dynamic changes: The cervix changes length or appearance during manual fundal pressure or coughing during the scan.

Surgical and Medical Management Strategies

Management options aim to provide mechanical support or alter physiological factors contributing to cervical weakness. The primary surgical intervention is cervical cerclage, where a strong suture is placed around the cervix to mechanically reinforce it until term.

Cerclage is typically removed around 36 to 37 weeks of gestation to allow for vaginal delivery, or earlier if spontaneous labor or preterm premature rupture of membranes occurs.

Midwife's Role in Ongoing Antenatal Care and Emotional Support

Caring for a woman with a cerclage or known cervical incompetence requires immense empathy and vigilance. Midwives provide continuous monitoring, checking regularly for signs of infection, uterine contractions, or vaginal spotting. Patients are often advised to avoid strenuous physical activity and sexual intercourse, instructions that can cause significant anxiety.

The emotional toll of carrying a pregnancy after experiencing previous mid-trimester losses is heavy. Midwives must create a safe space for mothers to express their fears, offer reassurance during antenatal visits, and provide clear education on warning signs like pelvic pressure, lower backache, or fluid leakage that require immediate hospital reporting.
