Septic Abortion/Miscarriage

Defining Septic Abortion and Its Pathophysiology

A septic abortion is any miscarriage or abortion complicated by intrauterine infection. In the Nigerian context, it remains a major cause of maternal morbidity and mortality, frequently arising from unsafe, clandestine terminations performed outside orthodox health facilities under unsterile conditions.

The disease process begins when pathogenic bacteria ascend from the lower genital tract into the uterine cavity. Retained products of conception serve as an ideal culture medium for microbial proliferation. Invasion of the decidua and myometrium leads to localized endometritis, which can rapidly track through the lymphatic channels and pelvic blood vessels, resulting in parametritis, pelvic peritonitis, and potentially life-threatening septicemia and endotoxic shock.

Common Etiological Agents and Risk Factors in Nigerian Practice

The microbial flora responsible for septic abortion are typically polymicrobial, originating from the patient's own lower genital tract or introduced via unsterile instruments during unsafe procedures.

  • Escherichia coli: A primary gram-negative bacillus originating from the bowel, frequently isolated in severe pelvic infections.
  • Staphylococcus aureus: A common pathogen associated with toxic shock syndrome and wound site infections.
  • Streptococcus species: Particularly group A and B streptococci, known for rapid tissue invasion and severe systemic toxicity.
  • Anaerobes: Organisms such as *Bacteroides* and *Clostridium* species thrive in devitalized, low-oxygen uterine tissues, sometimes leading to gas gangrene.

Key risk factors in local practice include low socioeconomic status, lack of access to safe contraceptive services, youthfulness, and patronage of unskilled traditional practitioners or patent medicine vendors for pregnancy termination.

Recognizing Clinical Features and Stages of Infection

Clinical presentation varies depending on how far the infection has spread from the uterine cavity. Midwives must maintain a high index of suspicion when evaluating any woman presenting with early pregnancy bleeding and systemic signs of illness.

  • Stage 1 (Localized): Infection is confined to the uterine cavity. Symptoms include lower abdominal pain, uterine tenderness, foul-smelling vaginal discharge, and low-grade fever.
  • Stage 2 (Spread): Infection extends beyond the uterus into the parametrium, tubes, or pelvic peritoneum. Patients exhibit severe pelvic pain, high spiking fevers, rebound tenderness, and a boggy, extremely tender uterus.
  • Stage 3 (Generalized): Infection enters the bloodstream, causing septicemia, septic shock, acute renal failure, and disseminated intravascular coagulation.

Emergency Stabilization and Initial Investigations

Managing a septic abortion requires immediate, structured resuscitation before any surgical evacuation is attempted. Delaying resuscitation to take the patient straight to the theatre can prove fatal.

  1. Assess Airway, Breathing, Circulation: Secure intravenous access using large-bore cannulae and commence rapid fluid resuscitation with normal saline or Ringer's lactate.
  2. Take Blood Samples: Send blood for full blood count, blood grouping and cross-matching, electrolytes, urea, and creatinine, alongside blood cultures.
  3. Administer Broad-Spectrum Antibiotics: Start intravenous antibiotics immediately after blood cultures are drawn to cover both aerobic and anaerobic organisms.
  4. Monitor Vital Signs: Keep a strict fluid balance chart, inserting an indwelling urinary catheter to measure hourly urine output closely.

Medical and Surgical Management Protocols

Once the patient is hemodynamically stable and has received at least a few doses of appropriate antibiotics, attention turns to evacuating the infected uterine contents.

Antibiotic therapy alone is rarely sufficient if necrotic tissue remains inside the uterus. A combination of broad-spectrum antibiotics (such as a cephalosporin, metronidazole, and an aminoglycoside) must be maintained throughout treatment.

Surgical evacuation of the uterus is performed gently using suction curettage or blunt evacuation forceps, taking extreme care because the infected, soft myometrium is highly susceptible to perforation. In severe cases with extensive uterine necrosis, a hysterectomy may be lifesaving.

Recognizing and Treating Severe Complications

Failure to manage septic abortion aggressively can lead to profound systemic complications. Midwives must remain vigilant for early warning signs.

  • Endotoxic Shock: Characterized by hypotension, tachycardia, oliguria, and altered mental state caused by gram-negative bacterial toxins.
  • Acute Renal Failure: Results from prolonged hypotension and tubular necrosis; monitored closely via hourly urine output.
  • Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC): A severe clotting disorder triggered by infection and tissue factor release, leading to widespread bleeding.
  • Pelvic Abscess: A localized collection of pus in the pouch of Douglas or adnexa requiring drainage if medical treatment fails.

Post-Abortion Care, Contraception, and Prevention

Recovery from septic abortion extends beyond acute medical intervention. Comprehensive post-abortion care (PAC) addresses physical healing, emotional support, and future reproductive health.

Counseling must be empathetic and non-judgmental, acknowledging the physical and psychological trauma the patient has experienced. Before discharge, midwives should discuss and provide preferred contraceptive methods to prevent repeat unwanted pregnancies and unsafe procedures. Community engagement and education on safe sex, prompt treatment of early pregnancy complications, and expanding access to safe, legal abortion services where applicable are vital preventive pillars.
