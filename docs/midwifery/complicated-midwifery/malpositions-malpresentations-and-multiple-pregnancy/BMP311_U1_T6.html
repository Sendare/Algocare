Transverse Lie

Understanding Transverse Lie and Fetal Axis

A transverse lie occurs when the long axis of the fetus lies horizontally or perpendicularly across the maternal long axis. Instead of the head or buttocks pointing toward the pelvic inlet, the fetal shoulder or side of the trunk usually sits over the cervix, forming a shoulder presentation. When checking the maternal abdomen by palpation, neither the head nor the breech can be felt in the lower uterine segment, and the fundus feels unusually empty. Midwives must recognize this abnormal lie early because a full-term fetus cannot deliver vaginally in a transverse position. The condition creates a dangerous clinical dilemma if allowed to go into labour without intervention, as the uterus will eventually rupture if obstructed.

Underlying Causes and Risk Factors

Several anatomical and physiological factors prevent the fetus from settling longitudinally into the pelvis. Multiparity is a frequent contributor because lax abdominal and uterine muscles fail to keep the fetus upright. Other common causes include placenta praevia, pelvic tumours like myomas, uterine anomalies such as a septate uterus, polyhydramnios which allows excessive fetal mobility, and fetal abnormalities. In many cases, no clear cause is found, particularly before thirty-four weeks of gestation when a transverse lie can still be a normal variant as the baby moves freely.

Antenatal Diagnosis and Clinical Assessment

Routine antenatal care allows midwives to detect abnormal lies through systematic abdominal examination using Leopold's manoeuvres. When inspection reveals a surprisingly broad or short maternal abdomen, suspicion should rise. Palpation confirms the absence of a presenting part in the lower pole, while auscultation often places the fetal heart rate near the umbilicus. Obstetric ultrasound is the definitive diagnostic tool, confirming the exact lie, placental location, and ruling out congenital abnormalities. If a transverse lie persists beyond thirty-six weeks, planned hospital admission and preparation for safe delivery options become necessary.

Associated Risks: Cord Prolapse and Obstructed Labour

A transverse lie carries catastrophic risks if mismanaged or left undiagnosed until active labour begins. Because the shoulder does not fill the pelvic brim tightly, the amniotic membranes are prone to early rupture. When membranes rupture with a sideways fetus, the umbilical cord often washes down past the presenting part, causing an acute cord prolapse that cuts off fetal oxygen. Furthermore, as uterine contractions continue, the shoulder is forced down into the pelvis while the upper body crowds in behind it, resulting in a neglected obstructed labour. This leads rapidly to uterine exhaustion, pathological retraction rings, and imminent uterine rupture.

Emergency Protocols for Acute Presentation

Discovering a transverse lie for the first time when a woman arrives in active labour is an obstetric emergency. Midwives must avoid performing aggressive vaginal examinations if bleeding is present or membranes have ruptured, as this can worsen cord prolapse. Immediate actions include calling for medical help, placing the patient in a knee-chest position or exaggerated Sims position to relieve pressure off the pelvis, and preparing for an emergency caesarean section. Intravenous access must be secured promptly, and blood grouped and cross-matched in anticipation of potential haemorrhage.

Midwifery Management and Intrapartum Care

Intrapartum care for a diagnosed transverse lie focuses on prevention of complications and timely surgical delivery. If identified during the antenatal period, elective caesarean section is scheduled around thirty-nine weeks before spontaneous labour starts. Midwives provide psychological support, explaining the surgical procedure and addressing maternal anxieties. During the immediate postpartum period, vigilant monitoring for postpartum haemorrhage is essential because the overdistended uterus from causes like polyhydramnios or multiple gestation contracts poorly, increasing the risk of uterine atony.
