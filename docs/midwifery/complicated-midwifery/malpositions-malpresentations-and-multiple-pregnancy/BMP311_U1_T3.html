Brow Presentation

Understanding Brow Presentation

Brow presentation is a rare malpresentation where the fetal head occupies a position midway between complete flexion (vertex) and complete extension (face). The presenting part is the area of the fetal head between the orbital ridge and the anterior fontanelle, known as the brow or mentoccipital diameter. Because this diameter measures approximately 13.5 centimeters, it is the longest anteroposterior diameter of the fetal skull. Passing this wide measurement through the normal maternal pelvis is exceptionally difficult, making spontaneous vaginal delivery nearly impossible in a persistent brow presentation.

Most cases are unstable during early labour. The presentation often starts as an intermediate attitude of the head and may spontaneously convert either into a favourable vertex presentation with further flexion or into a face presentation with further extension. Midwives must understand that a persistent brow presentation represents mechanical dystocia, requiring careful evaluation in a well-equipped maternity unit.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Any condition that prevents normal flexion or encourages extension of the fetal head can result in a brow presentation. These factors may be maternal, fetal, or placental in origin.

  • Contracted pelvis: A narrow pelvic brim prevents the normal engagement of the smaller vertex diameters.
  • Fetal macrosomia: A large baby has less room inside the uterine cavity to adjust its head attitude properly.
  • Multiparity: Laxity of the abdominal wall and uterine muscles allows the fetus to lie in unstable positions.
  • Placenta praevia: Low-lying placental tissue occupies space in the lower segment, preventing the fetal head from descending and flexing normally.
  • Fetal anomalies: Structural abnormalities like anencephaly or neck masses alter normal head posture.

Diagnosis

Discovering a brow presentation early prevents prolonged obstructed labour. Midwives rely on abdominal and vaginal examinations during labour to identify the condition.

Abdominal palpation often reveals an unusually high head that feels very prominent on both sides. The occiput is easily felt at a higher level than the chin, and a deep depression or groove may be palpable between the occiput and the back.

Vaginal examination is definitive. The midwife feels the large anterior fontanelle, the orbital ridges, the root of the nose, and the eyes. The mouth and chin are out of reach, which distinguishes it from a face presentation. Extensive caput succedaneum and marked moulding frequently develop over the brow area due to the tight fit, making identification via palpation confusing if the examination is delayed.

Mechanism of Labour and Clinical Course

When a brow presentation persists, the course of labour becomes abnormally prolonged. Engagement rarely occurs because the mentoccipital diameter is too wide for the pelvic inlet. If the pelvis is unusually roomy and the fetus is very small, a rare mechanism of labour may happen.

The brow descends with the frontal bone acting as the leading part. As it hits the pelvic floor, internal rotation of the brow occurs to bring it anteriorly under the pubic arch. A degree of flexion and extension then allows the head to sweep over the perineum, but this requires extreme stretching of maternal tissues and severe moulding of the fetal head. In the vast majority of cases, progress stops entirely, resulting in secondary arrest of dilation and deep transverse arrest.

Management

Active management is crucial upon diagnosing a persistent brow presentation to protect both mother and baby from severe trauma. Midwives must recognize their limitations and arrange prompt referral to a specialist obstetric unit.

  • Expectant approach: If the presentation is diagnosed early in first stage and membranes are intact, conservative observation is sometimes maintained as the head may spontaneously convert to a vertex or face presentation.
  • Caesarean section: This is the safest and most common route of delivery for a persistent brow presentation, especially if the fetus is of normal size and the pelvis is average.
  • Manual conversion: Rarely attempted by skilled specialists under tocolysis, converting a brow to a vertex or face presentation carries high risks of cord prolapse and uterine rupture.
  • Instrumental delivery: Forceps or vacuum extraction are strictly contraindicated for a persistent brow presentation at the pelvic brim.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

The mechanical strain of a brow presentation poses serious risks to maternal and perinatal well-being. Prolonged labour and obstructed passages increase the likelihood of severe outcomes.

For the mother, the main hazards include uterine rupture from prolonged contractions against an unyielding obstruction, postpartum haemorrhage due to uterine atony, and extensive soft tissue trauma including vesicovaginal fistulae from prolonged pressure necrosis.

For the baby, perinatal mortality and morbidity are significantly elevated. Fetal distress and hypoxia arise from prolonged cord compression and reduced placental perfusion. Severe caput succedaneum, extensive facial bruising, and intracranial haemorrhage can also occur due to the intense pressure exerted by the bony pelvis during attempted descent.
