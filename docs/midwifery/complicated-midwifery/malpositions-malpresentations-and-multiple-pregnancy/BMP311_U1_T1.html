Occipito-Posterior Position

Understanding Occipito-Posterior Position

In an occipito-posterior (OP) position, the baby faces forward toward the mother's abdomen, while the back of the head (the occiput) points toward the mother's spine and the right or left side of her pelvis. Midwives commonly refer to this as a "sunny-side up" baby. Unlike the ideal occipito-anterior position where the baby's head tucks neatly into a flexed posture, the OP position often results in deflexion of the fetal head. This wider diameter makes it harder for the baby to pass smoothly through the birth canal, frequently leading to longer labors and intense back pain for the mother.

Causes and Contributing Factors

Certain pelvic shapes and uterine conditions encourage the fetal head to engage posteriorly.

  • Android pelvis: A funnel-shaped pelvis with a narrow forepelvis naturally steers the occiput toward the wider posterior space of the sacrum.
  • Platypelloid or anthropoid pelvis: These uncommon pelvic dimensions can also discourage anterior rotation.
  • Weak abdominal muscles: Often seen in grand multiparous women, poor tone allows the uterus to sag forward, failing to press the baby's back into the anterior position.
  • Placental placement: An anterior placenta may prevent the baby's back from rotating forward naturally.

Clinical Identification During Antenatal and Labor Care

Detecting an OP position early helps midwives prepare for potential labor challenges. During abdominal palpation, you may notice a flat or depressed area around the mother's umbilicus where the baby's back should be. Fetal limbs are easily felt all over the anterior abdomen, and fetal heart sounds are heard far to the flank, rather than close to the midline.

During vaginal examination, the posterior fontanelle is found near one of the sacroiliac joints, while the anterior fontanelle is easily reached near the opposite side of the pelvis. Midwives should always note severe, persistent lower back pain during early labor as a strong clinical indicator of a posterior position.

Mechanism of Labor

The journey of an OP baby through the pelvis requires an extra half-circle of rotation to be born vaginally.

  1. Engagement: The head enters the pelvis in a slight extension with the occiput pointing posteriorly.
  2. Descent and Flexion: As contractions continue, the head descends and slowly achieves better flexion.
  3. Internal Rotation of 135 Degrees: Instead of the short 45-degree anterior rotation, the occiput must rotate forward across three-eighths of a circle (135 degrees) to reach the pubic symphysis.
  4. Long Internal Rotation Failure: Sometimes rotation stops completely, resulting in a "deep transverse arrest" or persistent occipito-posterior position where delivery may require operative assistance.

Potential Complications for Mother and Baby

Labor with a persistent occipito-posterior position carries higher risks for both mother and infant. Mothers often suffer from prolonged first and second stages of labor due to inefficient uterine contractions and slow cervical dilation. The relentless pressure on the sacrum causes severe backache, often referred to as "back labor."

Furthermore, rates of perineal trauma, postpartum hemorrhage, and operative deliveries—such as forceps, vacuum extraction, or cesarean section—rise significantly. For the baby, prolonged compression can lead to fetal distress, low Apgar scores at birth, and extensive caput succedaneum or cephalhematoma due to prolonged engagement in a tight pelvic space.

Midwifery Management and Supportive Care

Caring for a woman with an OP position requires patience, physical relief measures, and vigilant monitoring. Encourage maternal mobility and upright positioning, such as leaning forward on a birth ball, kneeling on all fours, or the Sims position on the side opposite the fetal back. These postures use gravity to help the heavy fetal spine swing forward into an anterior position.

Provide continuous emotional encouragement and physical comfort, utilizing lower back massage and warm compresses. Closely monitor maternal vital signs, progress of cervical dilation using partograph records, and fetal well-being via intermittent auscultation or cardiotocography. Prepare for potential operative delivery if labor stalls or signs of fetal compromise appear.
