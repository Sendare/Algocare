Classification of Hypertensive Disorders in Pregnancy

Introduction to Blood Pressure Changes in Pregnancy

Normal physiological adaptations in pregnancy lead to significant cardiovascular changes. Early in the first trimester, systemic vascular resistance drops markedly due to progesterone-induced smooth muscle relaxation. This vasodilatory effect causes maternal blood pressure to fall, reaching its lowest point around the second trimester. Midwives must expect this natural dip when evaluating antenatal records. By the third trimester, blood pressure gradually returns to pre-pregnancy baseline levels. Failing to account for this normal drop can lead to missed diagnoses if a rising third-trimester reading is mistakenly assumed to be normal.

Standard Classification Framework

Classifying hypertensive disorders accurately guides clinical management and protects both mother and baby. The global standard categorizes these conditions into four main groups based on onset timing and clinical features:

  1. Chronic Hypertension: Present before pregnancy or diagnosed before 20 weeks gestation.
  2. Gestational Hypertension: New-onset blood pressure elevation occurring after 20 weeks without proteinuria.
  3. Preeclampsia-Eclampsia: New-onset hypertension after 20 weeks accompanied by signs of end-organ damage.
  4. Superimposed Preeclampsia: Worsening hypertension or new organ damage in a woman with pre-existing chronic hypertension.

Diagnostic Criteria Overview

Accurate measurement forms the bedrock of safe maternity care. Hypertension in pregnancy is formally diagnosed when systolic blood pressure reaches 140 mmHg or higher, or diastolic blood pressure reaches 90 mmHg or higher, confirmed on two separate readings at least four hours apart. Severe hypertension is defined as readings of 160/110 mmHg or higher, requiring urgent medical intervention within minutes to prevent stroke. Midwives must ensure proper cuff size and maternal positioning during every assessment to avoid false readings.

Chronic Hypertension: Identification and Management Prior to 20 Weeks

Chronic hypertension involves elevated blood pressure identified before conception or prior to the 20th week of pregnancy. Women with this condition carry an elevated baseline risk for developing superimposed preeclampsia. Midwives should review existing antihypertensive medications early in the first trimester, as certain agents like ACE inhibitors are teratogenic and must be swapped for pregnancy-safe alternatives such as labetalol or alpha-methyldopa. Regular monitoring of baseline renal function and baseline proteinuria is essential.

Gestational Hypertension: New-Onset Blood Pressure Elevation

Gestational hypertension is characterized by high blood pressure readings arising after 20 weeks of gestation in a woman with previously normal blood pressure. Unlike preeclampsia, it occurs in the absence of significant proteinuria or biochemical markers of end-organ dysfunction. While many women experience benign clinical courses, gestational hypertension can progress to preeclampsia. Therefore, close surveillance including weekly blood pressure checks, symptom screening, and fetal well-being assessments remains standard midwifery practice.

Preeclampsia-Eclampsia: Multisystem Involvement and Severity Grading

Preeclampsia is a multisystem disorder characterized by widespread endothelial dysfunction. Diagnosis requires new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks combined with proteinuria, or in its absence, evidence of other end-organ involvement such as thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary edema, or new-onset cerebral disturbances.

Severe features include:

  • Blood Pressure: Sustained readings of 160/110 mmHg or higher.
  • Neurological: Persistent blinding headaches, visual blurring, or clonus.
  • Hepatic: Severe right upper quadrant or epigastric pain.
  • Hematological: Platelet count dropping below 100,000/microliter.

Progression to convulsions defines eclampsia, an obstetric emergency.

Superimposed Preeclampsia on Chronic Hypertension

Superimposed preeclampsia occurs when a woman with pre-existing chronic hypertension develops new or worsening systemic complications after 20 weeks gestation. Diagnosing this condition can be challenging because baseline blood pressure is already high and baseline proteinuria may exist. Midwives look for sudden, sharp increases in blood pressure that are difficult to control, new-onset heavy proteinuria, or acute laboratory abnormalities such as falling platelets or rising liver enzymes. This combination carries a high risk of adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes.

Implications for Midwifery Triage and Assessment

Early recognition of hypertensive disorders saves lives. Midwives in antenatal clinics and triage units must routinely screen every pregnant woman at each visit for subjective warning signs, including severe frontal headaches, visual disturbances like flashing lights, and epigastric pain. Prompt escalation, accurate blood pressure measurement, and immediate referral when severe features arise are core competencies. Maintaining situational awareness during every shift ensures that subtle signs of deterioration are caught before overt eclampsia or stroke occurs.
