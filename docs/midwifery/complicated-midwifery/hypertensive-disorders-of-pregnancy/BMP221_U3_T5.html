Eclampsia

Understanding Eclampsia in the Nigerian Context

Eclampsia remains a major public health challenge across Nigeria, contributing significantly to maternal mortality rates. It is defined as the onset of generalized tonic-clonic seizures in a woman with pre-eclampsia, which cannot be attributed to other neurological causes. In many local settings, delays in recognizing warning signs and reaching a healthcare facility worsen outcomes. Midwives must understand that eclampsia can occur antepartum, intrapartum, or postpartum. Cultural beliefs sometimes attribute these sudden fits to spiritual manipulation or poisoning, leading to dangerous delays before seeking orthodox medical care. Effective midwifery practice in Nigeria therefore requires combining clinical vigilance with compassionate community education to dispel these myths.

Recognizing the Stages of Convulsion

An eclamptic seizure follows a distinct pattern that every attending midwife must be able to identify quickly. Recognizing these phases helps prepare the team for immediate intervention.

  • Prodromal Stage: The patient may display sudden restlessness, twitching of the facial muscles, or a vacant stare.
  • Tonic Stage: The body becomes rigid, skeletal muscles contract violently, the eyes roll upward, and breathing temporarily stops, causing cyanosis.
  • Clonic Stage: Alternating contraction and relaxation of the muscles take over, producing vigorous jerking movements, frothing at the mouth, and noisy breathing.
  • Coma Stage: The muscular activity subsides, leaving the patient unconscious, sluggish, or deeply asleep, with slow recovery of full awareness.

Emergency First Aid for Eclamptic Fits

When a woman convulses in the ward or labor room, immediate panic must be replaced by structured emergency actions. The main goals are protecting the patient from physical injury and maintaining an open airway.

  1. Call for help: Shout for assistance while staying with the patient.
  2. Position safely: Turn the woman onto her left lateral side to prevent aspiration of vomit or saliva and reduce inferior vena cava compression.
  3. Clear the airway: Gently suction secretions if equipment is immediately available, but never force objects or fingers between clenched teeth.
  4. Protect from trauma: Ensure padded bed rails are up so she does not flail against hard metal frames.
  5. Administer oxygen: Provide high-flow oxygen via a face mask once the convulsion stops.

Comprehensive Medical and Midwifery Care

Once the acute seizure is managed, stabilization requires a coordinated medical and nursing protocol. Magnesium sulfate is the gold-standard anticonvulsant used to prevent recurrent fits. Midwives must monitor the patient closely for signs of magnesium toxicity, checking urine output, patellar reflexes, and respiratory rate before every maintenance dose. Blood pressure must be controlled using safe antihypertensives like oral labetalol or nifedipine to prevent stroke or cardiac overload. Fluid balance charts are mandatory because oliguria is common and fluid overload can quickly trigger pulmonary edema. Continuous fetal monitoring should be established once the mother is stable, keeping in mind that uterine hyperactivity often accompanies eclampsia.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Eclampsia places both mother and baby at extreme risk of morbidity and mortality. Maternal complications span multiple organ systems and require intense vigilance:

  • Cerebral: Intracranial hemorrhage, cerebral edema, and stroke.
  • Cardiopulmonary: Pulmonary edema, cardiac arrest, and severe hypertension.
  • Hematological: Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and thrombocytopenia.
  • Renal: Acute kidney injury and tubular necrosis.

Fetal risks are equally alarming. Placental abruption frequently occurs due to sudden uterine vessel changes, cutting off oxygen supply. This leads directly to fetal distress, intrauterine growth restriction, premature birth, and stillbirth.

Postnatal Surveillance and Long-Term Outlook

The danger of eclampsia does not end with the delivery of the baby and placenta. Postnatal surveillance is critical because seizures can still occur up to 48 hours or even days after childbirth. Midwives must continue monitoring blood pressure, neurological status, and lochia in the postpartum ward. Blood pressure usually normalizes within weeks, but women who experience eclampsia face a significantly higher lifetime risk of chronic hypertension and cardiovascular disease. Discharge counseling must emphasize healthy diet, regular exercise, blood pressure checks at local clinics, and prompt reporting of persistent headaches or visual disturbances.
