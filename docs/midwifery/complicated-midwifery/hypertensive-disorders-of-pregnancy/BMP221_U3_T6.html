HELLP Syndrome

Defining HELLP Syndrome and Its Pathophysiology

HELLP syndrome is a severe complication of pregnancy associated with severe preeclampsia, though it can occasionally develop without high blood pressure or proteinuria. The acronym stands for Hemolysis (H), Elevated Liver enzymes (EL), and Low Platelet count (LP).

  1. H (Hemolysis): Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia occurs when red blood cells are damaged as they pass through small, constricted blood vessels, leading to the formation of schistocytes and elevated bilirubin levels.
  2. EL (Elevated Liver Enzymes): Fibrin deposition in the liver hepatic sinusoidal spaces obstructs blood flow, causing hepatocellular necrosis and the release of AST and ALT into the bloodstream.
  3. LP (Low Platelets): Platelets are rapidly consumed at the sites of damaged vascular endothelium, resulting in thrombocytopenia with counts dropping below 100,000/μL.

This cascade stems from systemic endothelial dysfunction and vasospasm, creating a dangerous multi-organ threat for both mother and fetus.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Criteria

The clinical picture of HELLP syndrome can be deceptive because symptoms frequently mimic common, benign discomforts of late pregnancy. Midwives must maintain a high index of suspicion. Patients frequently complain of right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, which is caused by stretching of Glisson's capsule around the liver. Nausea, vomiting, and extreme fatigue are also common early warning signs.

Diagnosis relies primarily on laboratory blood investigations rather than blood pressure readings alone. Complete blood counts reveal microangiopathic hemolytic anemia with schistocytes on a peripheral blood smear and a low platelet count. Liver function tests show markedly elevated AST and ALT levels. Serum lactate dehydrogenase rises due to red blood cell destruction. A diagnosis is established when all three components of the syndrome are present, though partial variants also demand urgent intervention.

Emergency Stabilization and Immediate Management

Once HELLP syndrome is identified, stabilization must happen rapidly in a tertiary care setting. The primary goals are preventing maternal seizures, controlling dangerously high blood pressure, and correcting severe clotting abnormalities.

  • Magnesium Sulfate: Administered intravenously to prevent eclamptic convulsions and protect the fetal brain.
  • Antihypertensive Therapy: Agents such as hydralazine or labetalol are given if blood pressure exceeds 160/110 mmHg to prevent stroke.
  • Blood Product Support: Platelet transfusions are reserved for patients with active bleeding, extremely low counts, or those preparing for emergency surgical delivery.

Fluid balance must be carefully monitored because damaged capillary membranes put these patients at high risk for pulmonary edema.

Indications and Timing for Delivery

Delivery is the definitive cure for HELLP syndrome. Pregnancy prolongation is rarely safe once the diagnosis is confirmed, regardless of gestational age.

If the pregnancy has reached 34 weeks gestation or beyond, or if there is evidence of fetal distress, liver infarction, renal failure, or suspected placental abruption, delivery is expedited immediately. For gestations between 24 and 34 weeks, a brief delay of 24 to 48 hours may be considered solely to administer corticosteroids for fetal lung maturation, provided the maternal condition remains stable under intensive monitoring.

The mode of delivery depends on cervical favorability, gestational age, and coagulation status. Cesarean section is often necessary if the cervix is unfavorable and the platelet count is critically low, provided adequate blood products are available to cover the surgical intervention.

Postpartum Care and Complication Prevention

The danger does not end with the birth of the baby. The postpartum period is a high-risk window because HELLP syndrome can temporarily worsen before it begins to improve. Midwives must continue magnesium sulfate infusions for at least 24 hours after delivery to prevent postpartum eclampsia.

Laboratory values—particularly platelet counts and liver enzymes—must be monitored daily until they show a clear upward and resolving trend. Coagulation profiles help track recovery. Midwives must remain vigilant for life-threatening complications such as subcapsular liver hematoma rupture, acute kidney injury, and postpartum hemorrhage due to underlying thrombocytopenia. Patient education on warning signs such as persistent headache, visual changes, or right upper quadrant pain is vital before discharge.
