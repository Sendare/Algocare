Caesarean Section

Introduction and Scope of Caesarean Sections in Nigeria

A Caesarean section (CS) involves delivering a baby through surgical incisions made in the abdominal wall and the uterus. Across Nigeria, the rate of caesarean births has been rising, especially in urban tertiary health centers. Midwives encounter this procedure frequently, serving as the first point of contact for women who present with pregnancy complications. While a vaginal birth remains the primary goal for most healthy pregnancies, a timely CS saves lives when complications arise that threaten the mother or the baby.

Access to safe surgical delivery varies widely between rural primary health posts and well-equipped urban teaching hospitals. Midwives working in secondary and tertiary facilities must understand the local factors influencing surgical uptake, including religious beliefs, cultural perceptions surrounding surgical delivery, and financial constraints under the National Health Insurance Authority or out-of-pocket payment systems. Recognizing these barriers helps midwives provide empathetic counseling that eases fear and encourages prompt acceptance of medical advice.

Indications: Elective Versus Emergency Procedures

Caesarean sections are categorized based on urgency and clinical necessity. An elective CS is planned well in advance for specific maternal or fetal conditions where labor is either unsafe or contraindicated. An emergency CS is performed when unexpected complications arise during pregnancy or labor that demand immediate delivery.

Common indications fall into distinct clinical categories:

  • Elective Indications: Previous classical caesarean scar, persistent breech presentation near term, major placenta praevia covering the internal os, and well-controlled maternal viral infections where indicated.
  • Emergency Indications: Cephalopelvic disproportion, prolonged or obstructed labor, severe fetal distress, cord prolapse, and placental abruption with heavy bleeding.

Pre-Operative Preparation and Nursing Actions

Thorough pre-operative preparation reduces surgical risks and ensures the mother is physically and mentally ready. The midwife coordinates baseline investigations, including a packed cell volume to check for anemia, blood grouping and cross-matching in case a transfusion is needed, and urinalysis.

Key steps in pre-operative care include:

  1. Informed Consent: Ensure the procedure, risks, and benefits are fully explained and documented.
  2. Fasting Status: Confirm the patient has kept strictly nil per os for clear liquids and solids as ordered to prevent aspiration under anesthesia.
  3. Hygiene and Hair Removal: Perform abdominal shaving or clipping only when necessary, and ensure a clean bath with antiseptic soap.
  4. Bladder Care: Insert an indwelling urinary catheter to keep the bladder empty and out of the surgical field.
  5. Pre-medication: Administer prescribed antacids or prophylactic antibiotics right on schedule.

Overview of the Intra-Operative Process and Midwife's Role

The operating theater is a multidisciplinary environment where the midwife acts as a vital advocate for both mother and baby. Once the patient is positioned on the operating table with a slight left lateral tilt to prevent aortocaval compression, the anesthesia team administers either regional anesthesia, such as spinal or epidural, or general anesthesia.

The surgical team creates a transverse lower uterine segment incision, known as a Kerr incision, because it heals well and carries a lower risk of rupture in future pregnancies. As soon as the baby is delivered, the midwife receives the newborn, clears the airway if necessary, assesses the Apgar score, and initiates immediate thermal care. Meanwhile, the surgical team repairs the uterine and abdominal layers while administering oxytocin to encourage uterine contraction and minimize blood loss.

Immediate and Routine Post-Operative Care

Post-operative recovery begins in the recovery room or high-dependency unit, where vital signs are monitored every fifteen minutes until stable. Midwives watch closely for primary postpartum hemorrhage by checking the firmness of the uterine fundus and the amount of vaginal bleeding.

Routine post-operative monitoring includes:

  • Vital Signs: Assessing blood pressure, pulse, respiration rate, and oxygen saturation regularly.
  • Pain Management: Administering prescribed analgesics through regular schedules or patient-controlled devices.
  • Fluid Balance: Tracking intravenous fluids and urine output from the catheter until it is removed, usually on the first post-operative day.
  • Early Ambulation: Encouraging assisted sitting and walking as soon as sensation returns to the lower limbs to prevent venous thromboembolism.

Potential Complications and Emergency Response

Although routine in modern practice, a caesarean section is major abdominal surgery carrying inherent risks. Midwives must remain vigilant to detect early signs of complications so that medical interventions can start promptly.

Post-Natal Recovery, Breastfeeding Support, and Discharge Planning

Recovery continues after the mother leaves the hospital. Midwives guide the mother through wound care, dietary advice rich in iron and protein, and emotional adjustments. Establishing successful breastfeeding soon after surgery helps promote infant bonding and stimulates uterine contraction through natural oxytocin release.

Comprehensive discharge planning addresses several practical needs:

  • Wound Care Education: Teaching the mother how to keep the incision clean and dry, and when to report redness or pus.
  • Breastfeeding Assistance: Helping with comfortable nursing positions that avoid putting pressure on the abdominal wound, such as the football hold.
  • Family Planning Counseling: Discussing safe postpartum contraceptive choices and the recommended birth-spacing interval of at least two years before another pregnancy.
  • Follow-up Appointments: Scheduling a postnatal check at two and six weeks post-surgery.
