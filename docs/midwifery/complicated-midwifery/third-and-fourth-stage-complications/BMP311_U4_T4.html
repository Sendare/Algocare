Obstetric Trauma

Introduction to Obstetric Trauma and Risk Factors

Obstetric trauma refers to physical injury sustained by the maternal reproductive tract during childbirth, most commonly occurring during the second and third stages of labor. While some degree of stretching and minor tearing is expected, severe trauma can lead to significant hemorrhage, infection, and long-term pelvic floor dysfunction if not promptly recognized and managed.

  • Macrosomia: Delivery of a baby weighing over 4,000 grams stretches the birth canal beyond normal physiological limits.
  • Instrumental Delivery: The use of forceps or vacuum extractors increases mechanical stress on vaginal and perineal tissues.
  • Rapid Expulsion: Uncontrolled, precipitate labor does not allow sufficient time for maternal tissues to stretch gradually.
  • Persistent Occiput Posterior: Malpositions change the presenting diameter, forcing wider engagement through the pelvis.
  • Previous Scarring: Prior perineal surgeries or extensive healing from FGM create rigid, non-compliant scar tissue prone to tearing.

Classification and Management of Perineal Lacerations

Perineal tears are graded into four distinct categories based on anatomical depth. Proper classification dictates whether simple suturing or specialized surgical repair is required.

  1. First-Degree: Involves the vaginal mucosa and skin of the fourchette or perineum. Minor tears without bleeding often heal well without stitches.
  2. Second-Degree: Extends deeper to involve the perineal muscles and fascia, but leaves the anal sphincter intact. Requires layered anatomical closure.
  3. Third-Degree: Involves the perineal muscles and disrupts the anal sphincter complex. Subdivided into parts based on whether less or more than half of the external sphincter is torn.
  4. Fourth-Degree: The most severe tear, extending completely through the rectal mucosa, exposing the lumen of the rectum.

A common mistake in busy labor wards is performing a hasty visual inspection without a digital rectal examination, which frequently misses third and fourth-degree extensions.

Cervical Tears and High Vaginal Lacerations

Not all birth trauma occurs at the perineum. Cervical tears and high vaginal lacerations often present with bright red postpartum hemorrhage despite a well-contracted uterus.

Cervical lacerations typically occur at the lateral angles of the cervix, often as a result of operative delivery performed before the cervix is fully dilated, or from precipitate expulsion. Midwives should suspect a cervical tear when heavy bleeding persists while the fundus remains firm and hard.

High vaginal lacerations frequently involve the sulci and can extend deep into the paravaginal tissues, often resulting from rotational instrumental deliveries. Visualizing these tears requires good lighting, proper retraction, and sponge holding forceps to systematically inspect the entire vaginal vault. Repair demands careful placement of the first suture well above the apex of the tear to secure hidden bleeding vessels.

Genital Tract Hematomas

Hematomas develop when blood vessels beneath the vaginal mucosa or vulvar skin tear while the surface epithelium remains intact. Blood collects in the loose connective tissue spaces, creating a painful, swollen mass.

  • Vulvar Hematomas: Typically originate from injury to branches of the internal pudendal artery. Present with intense, sharp perineal pain and a visible, discolored swelling.
  • Vaginal Hematomas: Less visible externally, presenting with pressure in the vagina, rectal tenesmus, or urinary retention due to urethral compression.
  • Retroperitoneal Hematomas: Rare but life-threatening, resulting from bleeding high above the pelvic diaphragm, often presenting with profound shock and minimal external signs.

Disproportionate, excruciating pain that is out of proportion to the physical findings of a routine repair is the classic hallmark of a developing hematoma.

Clinical Assessment and Immediate Identification

Systematic evaluation immediately following the third stage of labor is essential to catch hidden injuries. Midwives must never assume the genital tract is intact simply because an episiotomy was not performed.

Inspection begins with positioning the woman comfortably in the dorsal lithotomy position with adequate lighting. The vulva, perineum, and labia are inspected for swelling, abrasions, and tears. Sterile gauze is used to gently wipe away clots so the exact extent of lacerations can be visualized. A digital rectal examination is mandatory if a deep second-degree tear or suspected third- or fourth-degree tear is identified, assessing sphincter tone and mucosal integrity.

Vital signs must be monitored closely. Tachycardia and a falling blood pressure can be the earliest indicators of concealed blood loss inside a hematoma or from an unmanaged high vaginal tear, long before external pooling becomes obvious.

Surgical Repair and Intraoperative Management

Surgical repair of obstetric trauma aims to restore normal anatomy, achieve hemostasis, and minimize pain and scarring. Adequate local anesthesia, such as a pudendal block or local infiltration of lidocaine, is established before suturing begins.

For perineal tears, repair proceeds from the apex of the vaginal component downward, carefully approximating the mucosal edges and deep muscles using absorbable synthetic sutures like polyglactin 910. Anal sphincter tears require precise edge-to-edge approximation or overlapping techniques to preserve continence. For small hematomas, conservative ice pack management may suffice, but large or expanding hematomas require surgical evacuation, identification of the bleeding vessel, ligation, and placement of a temporary drain.

Aseptic technique is strictly maintained throughout to prevent ascending pelvic infections.

Post-Repair Nursing Care and Prevention of Long-Term Sequelae

Comprehensive postnatal care after obstetric trauma focuses on promoting wound healing, managing pain, preventing infection, and averting long-term complications such as dyspareunia, urinary incontinence, and anal incontinence.

  • Hygiene: Instruct the mother on perineal hygiene, including regular washing with clean water from front to back and frequent maternity pad changes.
  • Pain Relief: Administer prescribed analgesics such as paracetamol or ibuprofen, and recommend cold packs during the first twenty-four hours to reduce edema.
  • Bowels: Prescribe stool softeners like lactulose to prevent straining during defecation, especially following third- and fourth-degree repair.
  • Pelvic Floor Exercises: Encourage early, gentle pelvic floor muscle training once comfortable to restore tone.

Midwives play a crucial role in providing psychological reassurance, as traumatic births can leave mothers feeling anxious about future pregnancies and intimate relationships.
