Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)

Understanding DIC and Obstetric Triggers

Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation, commonly known as DIC, is not a primary disease itself. Instead, it is a severe secondary complication where the body's normal blood clotting system becomes wildly overactive. In obstetric practice, midwives must understand that pregnancy creates a naturally hypercoagulable state to protect women from postpartum hemorrhage. When certain pregnancy complications strike, this protective mechanism spins dangerously out of control, causing widespread clotting followed by catastrophic bleeding.

Major obstetric triggers include:

  • Placental abruption: Premature separation releases tissue factor into the maternal circulation.
  • Amniotic fluid embolism: Fetal cells and debris enter maternal blood vessels, initiating rapid clotting.
  • Severe pre-eclampsia and HELLP syndrome: Endothelial damage triggers widespread platelet aggregation.
  • Prolonged dead fetus syndrome: Retained dead tissue slowly releases thromboplastic substances over weeks.
  • Postpartum hemorrhage and shock: Prolonged tissue hypoperfusion leads to metabolic acidosis and consumption of clotting factors.

The Pathophysiological Stages

The development of DIC follows a distinct two-phase pathological sequence that tricks the body's natural healing systems. First is the clotting phase, triggered by massive amounts of tissue factor entering the bloodstream. Tiny blood clots form everywhere inside the microvasculature, cutting off oxygen and nutrients to vital organs like the kidneys and brain. This widespread microvascular thrombosis consumes all available platelets and clotting factors.

Once the body's reserves of platelets and fibrinogen are entirely depleted, the condition enters its second phase: the bleeding phase (or consumption coagulopathy). At the same time, the body tries to dissolve the initial widespread clots by activating the fibrinolytic system. Because there are no clotting factors left to seal blood vessel walls, the patient experiences severe, uncontrolled hemorrhage from every possible site, including venipuncture wounds, surgical incisions, and mucous membranes.

Recognizing Clinical Manifestations

Midwives must maintain high clinical suspicion because DIC presents with a confusing mix of both clotting and bleeding signs. Clinical features depend entirely on whether microvascular thrombosis or severe hemorrhage is currently dominating the patient's presentation.

  • Oozing from puncture sites: Continuous trickling of blood from intravenous cannulation sites or surgical wounds is often the earliest visible warning sign.
  • Petechiae and purpura: Tiny red or purple dots appear on the skin due to minor subcutaneous bleeding from capillary rupture.
  • Organ dysfunction: Decreased urine output (oliguria) signals early renal ischemia caused by microthrombi.
  • Hemodynamic instability: Tachycardia, tachypnea, and severe hypotension develop rapidly as blood volume drops.
  • Gastrointestinal bleeding: Hematemesis or melena may occur in severe, prolonged cases.

Laboratory Diagnostics

Diagnosis relies on both clinical observations and rapid laboratory confirmation. Midwives must understand that waiting for lab results before starting emergency care is dangerous; however, baseline blood panels are vital to confirm the diagnosis and guide ongoing blood product replacement.

Resuscitation and Immediate Stabilization

The absolute golden rule in managing obstetric DIC is to treat the underlying trigger immediately. No amount of blood transfusion will stop the bleeding if a retained dead fetus, retained placenta, or active placental abruption remains inside the uterus.

Simultaneously, aggressive resuscitation must begin:

  1. Secure large-bore intravenous access: Insert two large-bore cannula lines (16G or 18G) to allow rapid fluid and blood administration.
  2. Administer oxygen: Provide high-flow oxygen via a face mask to maintain maternal tissue oxygenation and prevent multi-organ failure.
  3. Activate massive transfusion protocols: Request packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitate from the blood bank without delay.
  4. Monitor vital signs: Assess blood pressure, pulse, and respiratory rate every 15 minutes to evaluate response to fluid resuscitation.

Clinical Management and Blood Component Therapy

Medical management focuses on replacing what has been lost using specific blood components guided by laboratory results and clinical bleeding. Midwives work closely with medical officers and hematologists to ensure accurate replacement therapy.

  • Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP): Administered to replace depleted coagulation factors.
  • Platelet Concentrates: Given if the platelet count drops below 50,000/µL and active bleeding is present.
  • Cryoprecipitate: Rich in fibrinogen and factor VIII, used specifically when fibrinogen levels fall below critical thresholds.
  • Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBCs): Used to maintain adequate oxygen-carrying capacity and restore hemoglobin levels.

Heparin is rarely used in established obstetric DIC because the patient is already bleeding profusely, though it may occasionally be considered in very early or chronic cases under specialist supervision.

Midwifery Monitoring and Nursing Care

Continuous, vigilant monitoring is the cornerstone of safe nursing care for a woman with DIC. The midwife acts as the patient's primary line of defense by detecting subtle clinical changes before they escalate into cardiac arrest.

Maintain strict fluid balance charts. Every milliliter of urine output must be measured; an output dropping below 30 mL/hour signals failing kidney perfusion. Inspect all surgical wounds, episiotomies, and intravenous insertion sites every 15 to 30 minutes for fresh bleeding or hematoma formation. Provide constant psychological reassurance to both the mother and her family, as the sudden emergency and intensive care environment cause immense fear. Finally, ensure all blood component transfusions are checked meticulously against patient identification bands to prevent fatal transfusion reactions during an already fragile medical crisis.
