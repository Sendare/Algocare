Obstetric Shock

Understanding Obstetric Shock in Nigerian Maternity Practice

Obstetric shock remains a leading contributor to maternal mortality across Nigerian health facilities, spanning primary primary healthcare clinics to tertiary teaching hospitals. It is defined as a life-threatening clinical state characterized by inadequate tissue perfusion and cellular oxygenation, uniquely triggered by complications of pregnancy, childbirth, or the puerperium.

In our local context, the most prevalent aetiologies include postpartum hemorrhage from uterine atony, retained placental tissue, ruptured uterus following obstructed labor, severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia, and septic abortion. Recognizing these root causes shapes how midwives approach prevention and timely referral. When blood volume or vascular tone drops precipitously, vital organs like the brain, kidneys, and heart are deprived of oxygen, setting off a cascade that can rapidly lead to multi-organ failure if emergency care is delayed.

Pathophysiology and Stages of Shock

Understanding the physiological breakdown helps midwives intervene before irreversible damage occurs. Obstetric shock generally transitions through distinct phases:

  1. Compensated Shock: Blood pressure remains normal due to compensatory mechanisms like vasoconstriction and tachycardia, but peripheral blood flow is already reduced to protect vital organs.
  2. Decompensated Shock: Compensatory mechanisms fail, blood pressure drops sharply, and perfusion to vital organs becomes severely compromised.
  3. Irreversible Shock: Prolonged cellular hypoxia causes permanent organ damage, microvascular clotting, and death, even if circulation is eventually restored.

As blood volume diminishes, the body shunts blood away from the skin, kidneys, and gut to maintain perfusion to the heart and brain. This explains why an early sign of shock is cool, pale skin rather than an immediate drop in blood pressure.

Recognizing Early Warning Signs and Clinical Presentation

Midwives must never wait for a crashing blood pressure reading to diagnose shock. Relying solely on a blood pressure reading of 90/60 mmHg can be dangerously misleading, especially in young women whose strong vascular tone maintains normal pressure despite losing significant blood volume.

  • Tachycardia: A heart rate exceeding 100 beats per minute is often the very first indicator of blood loss or developing shock.
  • Mental Status Changes: Restlessness, anxiety, confusion, or a feeling of impending doom indicate decreased cerebral oxygenation.
  • Capillary Refill Time: Prolonged refill exceeding three seconds points to poor peripheral perfusion.
  • Tachypnea: Rapid, shallow breathing occurs as the body tries to compensate for metabolic acidosis and low oxygen levels.
  • Oliguria: Urine output falling below 30 mL per hour signals that renal perfusion is failing.

Emergency Resuscitation and Fluid Management

Immediate resuscitation follows the ABCDE approach, with rapid stabilization of airway, breathing, and circulation. For hypovolemic obstetric shock, gaining prompt intravenous access using two large-bore cannulas (16G or 18G) is paramount for aggressive fluid replacement and blood sampling.

Crystalloid fluids such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate should be infused warmly and rapidly, but midwives must remain cautious against fluid overload, which can trigger pulmonary edema. If blood loss is massive, whole blood or packed red blood cells must be requested urgently. While awaiting cross-matched blood, type-specific or O-negative blood can be lifesaving. Monitoring urine output via an indwelling catheter provides an objective guide to how well resuscitation is restoring organ perfusion.

Specific Clinical Management Based on Underlying Cause

Resuscitation alone is insufficient unless the underlying trigger of the shock is halted. If hemorrhage is the cause, bimanual uterine compression, administration of uterotonics like oxytocin, ergometrine, or misoprostol, and timely evacuation of retained products of conception are necessary. For septic shock originating from chorioamnionitis or infected retained tissue, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics must be commenced alongside surgical source control.

When treating anaphylactic shock from medications or septic shock from severe pelvic infection, vasoactive drugs may be needed under medical direction to support vascular tone. Midwives must work hand-in-hand with obstetricians to ensure that surgical interventions, such as laparotomy for a ruptured uterus, are not delayed while waiting for medical stabilization alone.

Multidisciplinary Team Roles and Emergency Preparedness

Managing obstetric shock successfully demands seamless teamwork between midwives, obstetricians, anesthetists, laboratory scientists, and blood bank personnel. In Nigerian maternity units, drills and clear communication protocols save lives by reducing confusion during critical moments.

The SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) communication tool helps midwives convey urgent clinical details concisely during referrals or handovers. Emergency trolley checks, readily available blood bank contacts, and functional suction and oxygen units form the bedrock of facility preparedness. Midwives serve as the coordinators of care on the ward, often initiating the first response before the wider emergency team arrives.
