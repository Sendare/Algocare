Prevention and Control of Occupational Diseases

Understanding Occupational Diseases in Midwifery

An occupational disease is any chronic ailment or physical condition caused directly by repeated exposure to hazards linked to a person's specific work environment. Unlike sudden injuries from slips or falls, these illnesses develop gradually over months or years of daily exposure.

In the context of midwifery and healthcare delivery in Nigeria, practitioners face diverse workplace hazards daily. Midwives are uniquely vulnerable due to close bodily contact with patients, handling of sharp medical instruments, exposure to infectious body fluids, and the physical strain of prolonged standing or lifting laboring mothers. Recognizing these risks is the first step toward safeguarding professional longevity and maternal health service delivery.

The Hierarchy of Controls Framework

The hierarchy of controls is a systematic framework used in occupational health to determine the most effective ways to minimize workplace hazards. Arranged from the most reliable to the least reliable, the standard levels are:

  1. Elimination: Physically removing the hazard entirely from the workplace.
  2. Substitution: Replacing a hazardous material or process with a safer alternative.
  3. Engineering Controls: Isolating workers from the hazard through physical changes to the environment.
  4. Administrative Controls: Changing the way people work through policies, training, and scheduling.
  5. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Wearing specialized gear as a final barrier against remaining risks.

Management must always prioritize top-tier interventions rather than relying solely on individual protective gear.

Elimination, Substitution, and Engineering Controls

Practical application of the top three control tiers in a maternity unit involves concrete operational changes. Elimination might mean banning the manual recap of used injection needles entirely. Substitution could involve replacing toxic surface disinfectants with safer enzymatic cleaners that do not irritate respiratory tracts.

Engineering controls are particularly vital in preventing bloodborne pathogen transmission and repetitive strain injuries. Examples include:

  • Safety-engineered needles: Devices with built-in shields that snap over the sharp tip immediately after use.
  • Sharps disposal containers: Rigid, puncture-resistant boxes mounted at eye-level and easy reach in every labor room.
  • Hydraulic beds and lifting aids: Mechanical devices designed to assist in positioning heavy or immobile patients safely.

Administrative Controls and Safe Work Practices

When hazards cannot be completely removed or isolated through physical design, administrative controls alter work practices to reduce exposure time and intensity. In busy Nigerian healthcare facilities, these measures help manage fatigue and infection risks.

Key administrative strategies include strict adherence to hand hygiene protocols, scheduling regular staff rotation away from high-risk infectious wards, limiting consecutive shift lengths to prevent severe mental and physical exhaustion, and conducting mandatory safety training. Clear policies regarding needle safety and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) pathways ensure that every midwife knows immediate action steps following an accidental exposure incident.

The Role and Limitations of Personal Protective Equipment

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) serves as the last line of defense against occupational hazards. For midwives, standard PPE includes non-sterile examination gloves, heavy-duty utility gloves for instrument decontamination, surgical masks, eye visors, waterproof aprons, and closed protective footwear.

Despite its visibility, PPE has major limitations. It does not eliminate the hazard at the source. If PPE is torn, poorly fitted, or removed incorrectly, the worker remains fully exposed. Furthermore, poor compliance due to discomfort or equipment shortages in resource-constrained settings often undermines its effectiveness. PPE should always supplement, never replace, higher-level control measures.

Monitoring, Surveillance, and Health Programs

Comprehensive occupational health requires ongoing oversight through monitoring and medical surveillance. Workplace monitoring assesses environmental hazards such as noise levels, chemical vapor concentrations, or infection rates within specific hospital wards.

Medical surveillance involves tracking the health status of individual workers over time. This includes pre-employment medical examinations, routine immunizations (such as Hepatitis B, tetanus, and influenza vaccines), baseline screenings for tuberculosis, and immediate post-exposure evaluations. Establishing clear reporting systems for workplace injuries ensures prompt medical intervention and helps hospital management identify recurring safety gaps.
