Occupational Hazards: Physical, Chemical, Biological, Ergonomic, Psychosocial

Introduction to Occupational Health in Midwifery

Midwifery practice involves caring for women and newborns, but this noble duty exposes healthcare workers to various workplace hazards. Occupational health focuses on protecting the physical, mental, and social well-being of all workers. In Nigerian healthcare facilities, midwives often face challenging environments with resource constraints, increasing their vulnerability to workplace injuries and infections. Understanding these hazards is the first step toward effective prevention and maintaining a safe clinical workspace.

Physical Hazards

Physical hazards are environmental factors in the workplace that can harm the body without necessarily touching it directly. In maternity units, midwives encounter several common physical stressors:

  • Radiation: Exposure to X-rays during theatre procedures or portable ultrasounds if safety shields are ignored.
  • Noise: Continuous sounds from fetal monitors, alarms, and busy ward machinery which can cause fatigue and stress.
  • Extreme Temperatures: Poorly ventilated labor rooms that get excessively hot, leading to dehydration and exhaustion.
  • Slips, Trips, and Falls: Wet floors from amniotic fluid, blood, or cleaning water in delivery suites.

Chemical Hazards

Midwives frequently handle various chemical agents for cleaning, sterilization, and medical treatments. Inhaling fumes or direct skin contact can lead to skin irritation, respiratory issues, or systemic toxicity.

  • Glutaraldehyde and Chlorine: Used for instrument disinfection; strong vapors can irritate the eyes and airways.
  • Ethylene Oxide: Used for gas sterilization of equipment, known to be a potent irritant and potential carcinogen.
  • Latex and Medications: Cytotoxic drugs used in complicated obstetrics and powdered gloves can trigger severe allergic reactions or contact dermatitis.

Biological Hazards

Biological hazards are living micro-organisms that can cause infectious diseases in healthcare personnel. Midwives are at high risk due to daily contact with blood, amniotic fluid, and other body fluids during childbirth and postpartum care.

  • Bloodborne Pathogens: Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV transmitted through sharps injuries or splashes onto mucous membranes.
  • Airborne Infections: Tuberculosis contracted from coughing patients attending antenatal clinics.
  • Contact Infections: Puerperal sepsis organisms or resistant bacterial strains passed between patients and staff if hand hygiene lapses occur.

Ergonomic Hazards

Ergonomics involves fitting the job and workspace to the worker. Poor ergonomics leads to musculoskeletal disorders, especially back pain and joint strain among maternity staff.

  • Manual Lifting: Assisting heavy or unconscious patients onto delivery beds without lifting aids.
  • Awkward Postures: Bending over low delivery couches or neonatal resuscitation tables for extended periods.
  • Prolonged Standing: Conducting long labor shifts without adequate seating or rest breaks.

Psychosocial Hazards

Psychosocial hazards relate to the way work is designed, organized, and managed, as well as the economic and social contexts of the workplace. Midwifery is emotionally demanding, and high stress levels can result in burnout, anxiety, and depression.

  • Workload and Staff Shortages: Managing multiple active labors simultaneously due to low staff-to-patient ratios.
  • Trauma and Emergency: Witnessing adverse obstetric outcomes, stillbirths, or maternal mortality.
  • Violence and Abuse: Facing verbal or physical aggression from distressed relatives or patients.

Risk Assessment and Mitigation Strategies in Clinical Practice

Identifying hazards and taking proactive steps to control them keeps clinical staff safe. Risk assessment involves spotting potential dangers and rating their likelihood of causing harm. Mitigation relies on the hierarchy of controls: eliminating the hazard, substituting safer alternatives, using engineering controls, applying administrative policies, and wearing personal protective equipment (PPE).

  • Engineering Controls: Installing mechanical lifts and sharp disposal containers.
  • Administrative Controls: Rotating shifts and limiting continuous work hours to prevent fatigue.
  • PPE: Correctly using gloves, aprons, face masks, and eye protection during every delivery.
