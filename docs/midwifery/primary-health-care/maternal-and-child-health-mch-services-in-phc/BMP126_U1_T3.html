Intranatal and Postnatal Care Services in the Community

Introduction to Community-Based Maternal Care

Community-based maternal care brings essential health services directly to women where they live, bridging the gap between remote villages and formal health facilities. In Nigeria's primary health care system, this approach relies heavily on Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs), midwives, and traditional birth attendants working together. By delivering care at the grassroots level, health workers can monitor pregnancies, attend deliveries, and provide early postpartum support without requiring families to travel long, expensive distances. This framework aims to reduce high maternal and neonatal mortality rates by ensuring that basic interventions reach every pregnant woman, regardless of her socio-economic status or geographical location.

Intranatal Care Services at the Primary Level

Intranatal care encompasses the skilled management and support provided to a woman and her baby throughout labor and delivery. At a primary health care facility, the primary goal is a safe delivery resulting in a healthy mother and baby while remaining vigilant for any sign of deviation from normal labor. Midwives and trained attendants monitor uterine contractions, fetal heart rates, and maternal vital signs systematically. Clean delivery practices are strictly enforced to prevent puerperal sepsis and neonatal tetanus. Mothers also receive emotional reassurance, encouragement, and pain relief guidance during this intense physiological process.

Managing Labour and Delivery in Primary Health Care Settings

Managing labor effectively in a primary care clinic requires continuous observation and prompt decision-making. The partograph is the essential tool used by midwives to plot cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, and maternal condition over time. This visual graph helps detect prolonged labor early before complications like obstructed labor or uterine rupture occur.

  • First Stage: Monitored by regular assessment of contractions and cervical dilation using the partograph.
  • Second Stage: Focuses on safe maternal pushing, monitoring the fetal heart rate after every contraction, and preparing for immediate newborn care.
  • Third Stage: Involves active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) using a uterotonic drug, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage to prevent postpartum hemorrhage.

Postnatal Care Services in the Community

Postnatal care begins immediately after the placenta is delivered and extends through the first six weeks of life, a period often called the puerperium. Community midwives and health workers visit mothers at home or see them at the clinic to check for postpartum hemorrhage, uterine involution, and perineal healing. Mental well-being is also evaluated, as postpartum blues and depression are common yet frequently overlooked. Newborn assessments are conducted simultaneously to check breathing, warmth, and feeding ability. Ensuring early and exclusive breastfeeding gives the newborn the best possible start in life while protecting against common childhood infections.

Early Detection and Referral of Obstetric Complications

Not all pregnancies and deliveries follow a normal course, making the early recognition of danger signs a critical competency for community health workers. Midwives must educate pregnant women and their families to recognize symptoms that require immediate medical attention. When complications arise beyond the scope of a primary health care clinic, an efficient referral system must be activated without delay. Delays in reaching a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility are a major cause of maternal mortality in Nigeria.

  • Maternal Danger Signs: Severe vaginal bleeding, persistent headache with blurred vision, high fever, convulsions, and severe abdominal pain.
  • Newborn Danger Signs: Difficulty breathing, inability to suck or feed, lethargy, and hypothermia or fever.

Community Engagement and Follow-up for Mother and Newborn

Sustainable maternal health outcomes depend heavily on active community participation and robust follow-up systems. Community leaders, traditional rulers, and women's groups must be engaged as advocates for facility delivery and antenatal attendance. Volunteer health workers and outreach teams bridge the communication gap, tracking defaulters who miss their postnatal appointments or immunization schedules. Building trust between traditional communities and formal health institutions ensures that families accept modern midwifery practices while preserving culturally safe support structures. Ultimately, shared responsibility within the community protects both mother and child long after the initial birth event.
