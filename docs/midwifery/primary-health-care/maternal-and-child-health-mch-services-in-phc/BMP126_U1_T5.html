Childhood Diseases Management: Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI)

Introduction to Childhood Diseases Management in Primary Health Care

Primary health care serves as the first line of defense for children in Nigerian communities. Many under-five mortality cases stem from preventable or easily treatable conditions like malaria, diarrhea, pneumonia, measles, and malnutrition. Managing these illnesses requires a shift from treating single symptoms to looking at the whole child. Facilities at the primary level often face drug stockouts, irregular power supply, and heavy patient loads, making structured guidelines essential for clinical decision-making. Midwives and community health workers need dependable protocols to catch severe illness early before referral becomes impossible due to distance or transport costs.

Understanding the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness Strategy

Developed by the World Health Organization and UNICEF in the mid-1990s, the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) strategy addresses the major causes of childhood death globally. Traditional training often taught health workers to treat each disease in isolation, which failed when a child presented with several problems at once. IMCI combines preventive and curative measures to lower death rates, reduce the frequency and severity of illnesses, and improve normal growth and development. The strategy views the sick child holistically, ensuring no major danger sign is missed during a busy clinic day.

The Three Main Pillars of the IMCI Approach

The IMCI framework rests on three distinct pillars that work together to protect child health:

  1. Case Management Skills: Improving the clinical skills of health workers to assess, classify, and treat sick children using standard guidelines.
  2. Health Systems Strengthening: Enhancing the overall health system, including drug availability, supervision, and organization of health facilities.
  3. Family and Community Practices: Improving community and household practices regarding nutrition, hygiene, home care, and seeking care promptly.

Neglecting any single pillar weakens the entire strategy. For instance, perfect clinical diagnosis fails if essential medicines are missing from the clinic dispensary or if parents delay bringing the child in.

Identifying General Danger Signs in Under-Five Patients

Before assessing specific disease categories, every under-five child must be checked for general danger signs. The presence of any danger sign indicates a severe disease that requires urgent hospital referral or immediate emergency treatment.

  • Inability to drink or breastfeed: The child cannot suck or swallow anything.
  • Vomiting everything: The child brings up everything consumed, keeping no fluids or food down.
  • Convulsions: The child is having fits during the current illness or has had one right now.
  • Lethargy or unconsciousness: The child is unusually sleepy, floppy, or does not wake up when stimulated.

Assessing and Classifying Common Childhood Conditions

Following the check for general danger signs, the health worker assesses specific symptom groups using a color-coded triage system. The core symptoms evaluated in every sick child include cough or difficult breathing, diarrhea, fever, and ear problems. Nutritional status and immunization status are checked during every visit.

PinkUrgent pre-referral treatment and hospital transferSevere disease requiring inpatient care
YellowSpecific medical treatment and home care adviceModerate illness needing outpatient drugs
GreenSimple home management and supportive careMild condition requiring no specific drugs

This color-coding ensures that life-threatening illness is recognized instantly without waiting for complex laboratory results.

Treatment Actions and Counseling the Caretaker

Once classification is complete, the health worker administers the first dose of oral medication in the clinic, teaches the caretaker how to give home treatments, and explains when to return immediately. Communication must be clear and jargon-free. For caretakers giving oral drugs at home, the health worker must demonstrate how to measure doses using clean spoons or oral syringes, rather than vague measurements like 'half a cup.' Counseling also covers feeding practices during illness, increasing fluid intake, and continuing breastfeeding. Caretakers are given specific warning signs that mean they must bring the child back without waiting for the scheduled follow-up date.

Strengthening Health Systems and Community-Based IMCI

Facility-based care alone cannot reach every child who needs help in Nigeria. Community-Based IMCI (C-IMCI) extends life-saving interventions directly into villages and households by training community health extension workers and volunteers. These community agents teach mothers recognize danger signs early, promote exclusive breastfeeding, ensure children sleep under insecticide-treated nets, and provide prompt care-seeking. Strengthening health systems ensures these community efforts link smoothly with primary health centers that have reliable supplies of oral rehydration salts, zinc, amoxicillin, and artemisinin-based combination therapy.
