Waste Disposal Systems: Refuse, Excreta and Liquid Waste Management

Introduction to Environmental Health and Waste Management

Environmental health forms the bedrock of safe maternity care. In Nigerian health facilities and communities, poor sanitation directly contributes to maternal and neonatal morbidity. Waste management encompasses the safe handling, collection, treatment, and final disposal of refuse, human excreta, and liquid waste. When waste is left unmanaged, it creates breeding grounds for vectors like mosquitoes and houseflies, directly threatening vulnerable mothers and newborns.

Refuse Collection and Solid Waste Management

Solid waste, or refuse, includes anything discarded from domestic, commercial, and medical sources. In healthcare facilities, refuse is categorized into domestic waste and hazardous medical waste. Proper management involves segregation at the point of generation, safe temporary storage in covered bins, and appropriate final disposal methods such as municipal collection, controlled tipping, incineration, or deep burial for sharp items.

Excreta Disposal Systems

Human excreta contains numerous pathogens capable of causing waterborne and fecal-oral diseases such as cholera, typhoid, and dysentery. Safe disposal systems break this transmission cycle.

  • VIP Latrine (Ventilated Improved Pit): A pit latrine equipped with a vent pipe and fly screen to control odors and insects.
  • Water-Closet System: Flushes human waste through a plumbing network into a septic tank or municipal sewage system.
  • Bucket Latrine: An outdated and hazardous system involving manual removal of excreta, which is strongly discouraged due to severe public health risks.

Liquid Waste and Wastewater Management

Liquid waste comprises sewage (blackwater from toilets), sullage (greywater from bathing, laundry, and kitchen sinks), and medical liquid waste from clinical procedures. Improper discharge of wastewater creates stagnant pools that serve as primary breeding sites for malaria-carrying mosquitoes. Health facilities must use functional soakaway pits, septic tanks, or proper drainage channels to manage liquid waste safely.

Public Health Risks of Poor Sanitation

Poor sanitation triggers a cascade of community health problems. Contaminated water sources and unmanaged refuse lead directly to recurrent outbreaks of cholera, diarrhea, typhoid, and soil-transmitted helminthiases. For pregnant women, repeated bouts of diarrheal disease cause severe dehydration, electrolyte imbalance, and nutritional deficiencies, which significantly elevate the risk of miscarriage, premature labor, and low birth weight.

The Role of the Midwife in Promoting Environmental Hygiene

Midwives are trusted health educators within their communities. Beyond managing labor and delivery, a midwife must model and advocate for high standards of environmental sanitation. This involves teaching pregnant women about safe drinking water, proper handwashing, and sanitary waste disposal during antenatal health talks. Midwives also ensure that their delivery suites maintain strict infection prevention protocols to protect mothers from puerperal sepsis.
