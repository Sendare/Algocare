Role of Community Health Committees and Village Health Workers

Introduction to Grassroots Healthcare Structures

Grassroots healthcare structures form the foundation of primary health care delivery, particularly in rural and underserved Nigerian communities. These local structures bring health promotion and basic disease prevention directly to the doorsteps of families who might otherwise travel many kilometers to reach a formal health facility. At the heart of this grassroots network are Community Health Committees (CHCs) and Village Health Workers (VHWs). CHCs are voluntary bodies composed of respected local leaders, traditional rulers, religious figures, and women's group representatives who guide health activities in their domain. VHWs are trusted community members chosen by their own people to receive basic health training. Together, they act as the first line of defense against preventable illnesses, ensuring that health decisions reflect local realities and cultural values rather than top-down mandates.

Functions of Community Health Committees

Community Health Committees carry out vital administrative, mobilization, and oversight duties to keep primary health care running smoothly. Their primary responsibilities include:

  • Health Planning: Identifying the most pressing health needs of the village through open community dialogues.
  • Facility Maintenance: Mobilizing local labor and materials to keep primary health care clinics clean, safe, and functional.
  • Resource Mobilization: Gathering community contributions, whether financial or material, to support emergency transport or facility repairs.
  • Grievance Resolution: Serving as a bridge between patients and healthcare staff to resolve complaints and build mutual trust.

By taking ownership of these duties, CHCs ensure that health facilities remain accountable to the people they serve.

Roles and Training of Village Health Workers

Village Health Workers are the daily foot soldiers of community health. Because they share the language, culture, and daily struggles of their neighbors, they deliver health education without the intimidation factor sometimes associated with clinical staff. Their core training covers basic home-based care, recognizing danger signs in pregnancy, promoting exclusive breastfeeding, and managing simple childhood illnesses like uncomplicated diarrhea using oral rehydration therapy. VHWs do not diagnose complex diseases or prescribe restricted medications; instead, their training emphasizes early detection and prompt referral. When a VHW recognizes a severe postpartum hemorrhage or a lethargic, feverish infant, they immediately guide the family to the nearest primary health center, saving precious time.

Bridging the Gap Between Communities and Formal Health Systems

A major barrier to maternal and child survival in Nigeria is the cultural and communication disconnect between rural populations and formal biomedical health workers. Midwives and doctors often speak different languages, hold different beliefs about disease causation, and operate within rigid bureaucratic frameworks. VHWs and CHCs act as cultural translators. They explain hospital procedures to frightened villagers and, conversely, educate formal health workers on local customs and naming ceremonies that affect healthcare utilization. This dual advocacy builds trust, reduces the fear of facility-based childbirth, and ultimately increases antenatal care attendance and institutional delivery rates.

Mobilizing Local Resources for Maternal and Child Health

Sustainable community health relies on internal resourcefulness rather than permanent external funding. CHCs lead initiatives to pool community assets specifically for maternal and child health emergencies. Common local mobilization strategies include creating community emergency transport schemes—such as motorcycles or bicycles designated to carry laboring women—and establishing local drug revolving funds to ensure basic supplies never run out. Communities also contribute food or housing incentives to attract and retain VHWs and resident midwives in remote areas. When community members invest their own resources, they take pride in the health post's success and ensure its long-term survival.

Challenges and Sustainability in Community-Led Health Initiatives

Despite their immense value, community-led health initiatives face persistent hurdles that threaten their continuity. Volunteer burnout is a major challenge; VHWs often work without financial pay, leading to frustration when economic pressures mount. Political interference from local elites can also hijack CHC leadership, turning health committees into tools for personal influence rather than public service. Additionally, poor integration between primary health care boards and grassroots committees leaves VHWs feeling isolated and undersupplied. To achieve true sustainability, local governments must provide regular supportive supervision, continuous refresher training, and token incentives or non-monetary recognition to keep community health workers motivated.
