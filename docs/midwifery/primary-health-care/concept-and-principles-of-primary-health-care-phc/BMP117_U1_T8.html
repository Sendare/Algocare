National Health Policy and Strategy for PHC in Nigeria

Introduction to Nigeria's National Health System

Nigeria operates a three-tier health system structured around the federal, state, and local government tiers. This concurrent responsibility aims to bring healthcare services closer to the populace, particularly those living in rural and underserved areas.

  • Federal Tier: Responsible for overall policy formulation, coordination, regulation, and provision of tertiary care through teaching hospitals and federal medical centres.
  • State Tier: Manages secondary healthcare services through general hospitals and provides technical support to primary healthcare facilities within the state.
  • Local Government Tier: Directly responsible for delivering primary healthcare services through local government health authorities and primary health centres.

Despite this clear constitutional division, the system faces chronic fragmentation, poor referral linkages between tiers, and a lack of synchronized service delivery. Midwives working at the grassroots must navigate these structural bottlenecks daily to ensure mothers and infants receive timely care during obstetric emergencies.

The National Health Policy Framework

The National Health Policy serves as the compass guiding health development in Nigeria. First formulated in 1988 and revised periodically, the policy adopts Primary Health Care (PHC) as the bedrock for achieving universal health coverage. It emphasizes equity, social justice, community involvement, and the use of appropriate technology.

Midwives and other healthcare providers must align their clinical practices with the strategic thrusts of this policy, which prioritize maternal and child health, communicable disease control, and health promotion. The policy framework also establishes minimum standards for health facilities, ensuring that even a basic maternity clinic in a remote village meets specific structural and staffing criteria to safeguard patient safety.

PHC Implementation Strategies in Nigeria

Translating PHC policy into practical action requires deliberate implementation strategies tailored to the Nigerian socio-cultural landscape. The establishment of the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA) in 1992 was a milestone designed to drive grassroots health development.

Key implementation strategies include:

  • Ward-Based Health System: Bringing services down to the political ward level.
  • Integrated Management of Childhood Illnesses (IMCI): Combining treatment protocols for common childhood killers.
  • Midwives Service Scheme (MSS): Deploying unemployed and retired midwives to rural PHC facilities to curb maternal mortality.
  • Routine Immunization Outreach: Regular mobile campaigns to reach children in hard-to-reach settlements.

The Role of the Ward Health System

The Ward Health System (WHS) is the operational arm of the National Health Policy designed to revitalize PHC at the grassroots. A political ward serves as the basic unit for health planning and service delivery. Each ward is expected to have a functional primary health centre supported by subordinate health posts.

At the heart of the WHS is the Village Health Worker (VHW) and Traditional Birth Attendant (TBA) linkage, supervised by professional midwives and community health extension workers. This structure ensures that health promotion, disease surveillance, and basic curative services reach every household. When community members participate in managing their ward health committee, trust in the health facility grows, leading to higher utilization of antenatal care and institutional delivery services.

Intersectoral Collaboration and Community Participation

Primary health care cannot succeed in isolation. Intersectoral collaboration requires the health sector to work hand in hand with agriculture, education, water resources, and local works. For instance, reducing childhood malnutrition requires agricultural policies that boost food production and educational programs that teach mothers proper weaning diets.

Equally important is community participation. Communities must be actively involved in identifying their health problems, planning interventions, and evaluating health programmes. When community members view a health facility as their own project rather than a government imposition, they protect it, maintain it, and utilize its services during pregnancy and childbirth without hesitation.

Financing and Resource Allocation for PHC

Inadequate and unpredictable funding remains a major hurdle for primary healthcare delivery in Nigeria. Traditionally, PHC relies heavily on government budgetary allocations, which often suffer from bureaucratic delays and low releases.

To bridge this gap, newer financing mechanisms have emerged:

  • Basic Health Care Provision Fund (BHCPF): Designed to finance a basic minimum package of health services, emergency medical treatment, and primary facility revitalization.
  • State Health Insurance Schemes: Promoting prepayment and pooling of risks to protect vulnerable households from out-of-pocket catastrophic health spending.
  • Community-Based Health Insurance: Local risk-pooling initiatives managed by community groups to support maternal and child care costs.

Transparent resource allocation is vital to ensure that drugs and equipment actually reach rural primary health centres instead of getting trapped at administrative levels.

Current Challenges and Reforms in National Health Strategy

Despite numerous policy documents and interventions, Nigeria's primary health system continues to grapple with persistent challenges. These include acute staff shortages, brain drain of skilled midwives, dilapidated infrastructure, irregular supply of essential medicines, and poor data collection systems.

Recent reform efforts focus on revitalising thousands of PHC facilities across the federation, strengthening supply chain management through electronic tracking, and enforcing accountability frameworks. Midwives must stay informed about these evolving strategies, as policy shifts directly impact staffing ratios, drug availability, and clinical protocols at the frontline of care.
