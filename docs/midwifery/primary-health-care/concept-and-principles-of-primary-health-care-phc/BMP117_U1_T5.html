Health Promotion

Defining Health Promotion within Primary Health Care

Health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health. Unlike disease prevention, which focuses on specific illnesses and stopping them before they start, health promotion takes a much broader, positive view of well-being. It looks at health as a resource for everyday life, not just the objective result of living without sickness.

Within the framework of Primary Health Care (PHC), health promotion serves as a foundational pillar. It shifts the healthcare delivery model away from a purely medical approach where the doctor or midwife fixes the patient. Instead, it places the power back into the hands of individuals and communities. Midwives working at the primary health level in Nigeria use health promotion daily when they talk to pregnant women about good nutrition, clean water, and mental well-being, treating the community as active partners rather than passive recipients of care. Core Action Areas of the Ottawa Charter

The Ottawa Charter for Health Promotion, established in 1986, remains the global blueprint for public health practice. It outlines five core action areas that midwives and other healthcare providers must integrate into their daily work:

  1. Build healthy public policy: Urging policymakers to make health a priority across all sectors, such as advocating for safe maternity transport roads.
  2. Create supportive environments: Shaping physical and social surroundings to make healthy choices the easiest choices, like promoting clean community water sources.
  3. Strengthen community action: Empowering local groups to set their own health priorities and take charge of local health initiatives.
  4. Develop personal skills: Providing information and education so individuals can gain life skills to manage their health.
  5. Reorient health services: Moving clinical services away from a narrow curative focus toward prevention and health promotion.

In Nigerian primary health centres, reorienting health services means spending as much time teaching mothers how to prevent malnutrition as treating children who already have it. [[LEARN_MORE]]

Social Determinants of Health in the Nigerian Context

People do not live in a vacuum, and healthcare alone cannot guarantee wellness. The Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) are the non-medical conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age. They include economic stability, education access, social community context, neighborhood environment, and healthcare access.

In Nigeria, these determinants heavily shape maternal and child health outcomes. Poverty limits a pregnant woman's ability to afford prescribed haematinics or pay for transport to a health facility during labor. Low female literacy rates in certain regions hinder a mother's capacity to understand health messages or recognize danger signs of pregnancy complications. Cultural norms and gender inequality often restrict a woman's autonomy, preventing her from making prompt decisions about her own reproductive health. Midwives must look beyond the clinical diagnosis and understand these underlying social realities to offer meaningful care. [[LEARN_MORE]]

Strategies for Community Empowerment and Participation

Community empowerment means local people take charge of their own development and health solutions. When health programs are imposed from above without community input, they frequently fail once external funding stops.

Effective community participation relies on several key strategies:

  • Stakeholder mapping: Identifying traditional rulers, religious leaders, market women leaders, and youth groups who hold influence.
  • Dialogue and town hall meetings: Creating safe spaces for community members to voice their actual health needs rather than assuming what they need.
  • Village Health Committees (VHCs): Establishing local leadership structures to oversee primary health centre activities and manage local health resources.
  • Capacity building: Training local volunteers, such as traditional birth attendants or youth peer educators, to support primary healthcare outreach.

When a community helps plan a immunization campaign, turnout is consistently higher because the people feel ownership over the process. [[LEARN_MORE]]

The Role of the Midwife as a Health Educator and Advocate

The midwife is often the most trusted health professional in a rural Nigerian community. This unique position transforms the midwife into both an educator and an advocate.

As a health educator, the midwife translates complex medical information into simple, culturally relevant advice. During antenatal classes, postnatal visits, and immunisation outreaches, the midwife teaches families about exclusive breastfeeding, birth preparedness, family planning, and hygiene. Good education changes behaviors by helping clients understand the 'why' behind health recommendations, rather than just giving blind instructions.

As an advocate, the midwife speaks up for vulnerable populations who lack a voice in the health system. This involves lobbying local government for better maternity supplies, defending a teenage mother against stigma, or mobilizing transport when a woman in obstructed labor needs emergency referral. Midwifery advocacy bridges the gap between distressed community members and unresponsive bureaucratic systems. [[LEARN_MORE]]
