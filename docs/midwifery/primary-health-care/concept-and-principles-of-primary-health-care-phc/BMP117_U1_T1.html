Definition, Historical Background and Alma-Ata Declaration

Defining Primary Health Care: Core Concepts and Philosophy

Primary Health Care (PHC) is not merely the lowest level of health services, but a holistic approach to health and well-being centered on individuals, families, and communities. Defined by the World Health Organization, it addresses the broader social determinants of health and focuses on the complete physical, mental, and social well-being of populations rather than just the absence of disease.

At its philosophical core, PHC rests on two main pillars: social justice and equity. This means healthcare must be universally accessible to everyone, regardless of their ability to pay, social status, or geographical location. In the context of Nigerian healthcare, this philosophy challenges the urban-biased model of curative medicine by prioritizing rural communities where access has traditionally been limited.

Historical Background and Evolution of Global Health Systems

Before the concept of PHC emerged, global health systems were heavily focused on vertical programs—disease-specific campaigns that targeted single conditions like malaria, smallpox, or tuberculosis. While these campaigns achieved notable victories, they operated independently of community needs and neglected basic health infrastructure. Hospitals were concentrated in big cities, leaving rural populations entirely unserved.

During the mid-20th century, health planners realized that building more hospitals did not automatically improve the health of the general population. Communities needed basic preventive care, clean water, and nutritional education close to where they lived. This realization sparked a global movement to shift away from expensive, hospital-centered curative care toward a community-based approach.

The 1978 Alma-Ata Declaration: A Turning Point in Healthcare

In September 1978, delegates from 134 nations and numerous international organizations gathered in Alma-Ata, Kazakhstan (then part of the Soviet Union), for an international conference on Primary Health Care. Jointly sponsored by the WHO and UNICEF, the resulting Alma-Ata Declaration boldly proclaimed that health is a fundamental human right and that the attainment of the highest possible level of health is a worldwide social goal.

The conference famously established the goal of 'Health for All by the Year 2000'. It challenged governments to restructure their national health budgets to prioritize primary prevention over expensive tertiary hospitals. Although the ambitious target date passed without global fulfillment, the declaration fundamentally altered international health policy and remains the guiding light for public health nursing and midwifery today.

Key Principles and Elements of the Alma-Ata Vision

The Alma-Ata Declaration outlined specific operational pillars to make health universally attainable. These principles guide how health services should be designed and delivered at the grassroots level.

  • Universal Accessibility: Services must be fully available to everyone without discrimination.
  • Community Participation: Local people must be actively involved in planning, implementing, and managing their own health programs.
  • Intersectoral Collaboration: Health cannot be achieved by the health sector alone; agriculture, education, housing, and public works must cooperate.
  • Appropriate Technology: Methods, equipment, and procedures must be scientifically sound, culturally acceptable, and affordable for the local community.

To fulfill these principles, the declaration listed essential components including health education, proper nutrition, safe water, maternal and child health care, immunization, and treatment of common diseases.

Relevance of Primary Health Care and Alma-Ata to Modern Midwifery in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the principles of Alma-Ata find daily expression in the primary health care system, where midwives serve as frontline healthcare providers. Midwifery practice in rural primary health care centres (PHCCs) directly embodies the Alma-Ata vision by bringing maternal and child health services closer to the grassroots.

Midwives do not just manage normal deliveries; they practice health education, promote exclusive breastfeeding, administer immunizations, and screen for antenatal complications. Recognizing that maternal mortality remains a pressing challenge in Nigeria, integrating the PHC approach empowers midwives to work alongside community leaders and traditional birth attendants. This collaborative strategy ensures that pregnant women in remote areas receive timely care, proving that the 1978 Alma-Ata ideals remain urgently relevant to Nigerian midwifery education and clinical practice.
