Accessibility

Understanding Accessibility in Primary Health Care

Accessibility in Primary Health Care (PHC) means that essential health services must be universally available and reachable by all individuals and families in a community, without discrimination or undue hardship. Within the framework established at Alma-Ata, this principle forms a core pillar of community-based health delivery. True access goes beyond merely building a health post in a village; it ensures that the services provided are affordable, culturally acceptable, and physically within reach. For midwifery students practicing in Nigeria, understanding this concept helps explain why pregnant women sometimes bypass nearby facilities to travel further distances where they feel more welcome or financially secure. When health systems remove barriers, utilization rates for antenatal care and routine immunization naturally improve.

Geographical and Financial Accessibility

Physical distance and cost remain the two most formidable barriers to healthcare utilization across rural and peri-urban Nigeria.

  • Geographical Accessibility: Refers to the physical distance, road conditions, and availability of transport between the patient's home and the health facility. In many rural settlements, seasonal flooding or lack of motorized transport turns a simple journey into a major obstacle for a woman in labor.
  • Financial Accessibility: Relates to the direct and indirect costs of care, including consultation fees, laboratory investigations, transport fare, and the price of prescribed drugs. Out-of-pocket spending often forces families to delay seeking care until obstetric emergencies become life-threatening.

Equity in Health Service Distribution

Equity ensures that healthcare resources are distributed fairly based on health needs rather than the ability to pay or social standing. In many developing regions, health facilities tend to cluster in affluent urban centers, leaving rural and marginalized populations underserved. Midwives must advocate for fair resource allocation so that remote communities receive adequate supplies of uterotonics, vaccines, and basic obstetric equipment. Achieving equity does not mean treating everyone identically; it means providing targeted support to disadvantaged groups to bridge the health outcome gap.

Cultural and Social Barriers to Healthcare Utilization

Even when a clinic is nearby and affordable, cultural norms and social dynamics can prevent women from utilizing maternity services. Traditional beliefs regarding pregnancy complications, reliance on traditional birth attendants, and gender dynamics where husbands make the final healthcare decisions heavily influence health-seeking behavior. Midwives working in Nigerian communities must engage respectfully with local leaders, traditional birth attendants, and family decision-makers. Building bridges through respectful maternity care and community dialogue helps transform resistance into active health participation.

Strategies for Improving Maternal and Child Health Access in Nigeria

To overcome access hurdles, Nigeria employs several multi-sectoral strategies aimed at strengthening PHC delivery. Task shifting allows community health workers and midwives to manage uncomplicated deliveries and administer essential medications where doctors are scarce. Mobile health clinics bridge geographical gaps by bringing immunization and antenatal care directly to nomadic or hard-to-reach settlements. Furthermore, health insurance schemes designed for the informal sector help mitigate catastrophic out-of-pocket expenditures for pregnant mothers and newborns.
