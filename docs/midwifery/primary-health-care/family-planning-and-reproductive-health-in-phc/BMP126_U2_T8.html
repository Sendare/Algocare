Management of Infertility and Prevention of STIs/HIV in PHC

Introduction to Reproductive Health in Primary Health Care

Reproductive health within primary health care forms the bedrock of community wellness in Nigeria. At the grass-roots level, midwives and community health extension workers manage everything from safe motherhood to family planning. Infertility and sexually transmitted infections present major challenges that require integrated, respectful care rather than isolated disease treatment. Addressing these conditions early prevents long-term physical suffering and profound social stigmatization, particularly for women in vulnerable settings.

Differentiating Primary and Secondary Infertility

Infertility in the Nigerian clinical context is defined as the inability to achieve a clinical pregnancy after twelve months or more of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse. Midwives must distinguish between two main clinical presentations:

  • Primary infertility: A couple has never achieved a clinical pregnancy.
  • Secondary infertility: A couple has experienced at least one prior documented pregnancy, regardless of whether it resulted in a live birth, miscarriage, or ectopic gestation.

In many Nigerian communities, secondary infertility is exceptionally common due to a high burden of untreated postpartum infections, unsafe abortions, and pelvic inflammatory disease.

Initial Assessment and Clinical Management at the PHC Level

When a couple presents with fertility concerns at a primary health care facility, the midwife begins with a comprehensive joint history. This includes detailed menstrual charting, sexual frequency, previous pelvic infections, and past surgical procedures. Both partners must be evaluated concurrently to avoid placing the psychological burden solely on the woman. Initial basic investigations available at some primary centers or through vetted local laboratories include semen analysis for the male partner and basic hormonal assays or ovulation tracking for the female partner.

Identifying Criteria for Specialist Referral

Primary health care midwives must recognize their clinical limits. While history taking, basic counseling, and preliminary infection screening occur at the PHC level, complex cases require formal referral to a secondary or tertiary facility. Indications for timely referral include documented bilateral tubal blockage, severe male factor abnormalities like azoospermia, persistent anovulation unresponsive to basic primary care, and suspected uterine anomalies such as severe fibroids or intrauterine adhesions.

Primary Prevention and the Link Between STIs, HIV, and Infertility

Sexually transmitted infections and human immunodeficiency virus act as major biological drivers of reproductive failure. Pathogens like *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* ascend into the upper female reproductive tract silently, causing pelvic inflammatory disease. The resulting scarring destroys tubal architecture and damages ciliated epithelium. Primary prevention therefore serves a dual purpose: protecting individuals from communicable viral and bacterial agents while preserving their future reproductive potential.

Syndromic Management and Partner Notification Protocols

In resource-constrained primary care settings, laboratory confirmation for every sexually transmitted infection is often unavailable. Syndromic management bridges this gap by treating groups of symptoms using standardized clinical flowcharts. Midwives treat patients immediately based on reported complaints like abnormal vaginal discharge or urethral discharge. Effective treatment remains incomplete without partner notification and concurrent treatment to prevent immediate reinfection and break ongoing community transmission chains.

Counseling and Psychosocial Support for Affected Couples

Infertility and chronic infections carry heavy emotional and social consequences in Nigeria, where childbearing holds immense cultural value. Affected couples frequently face anxiety, depression, marital discord, and social ostracization. Midwives must provide a safe, confidential environment for emotional expression. Counseling should dispel harmful cultural myths, alleviate guilt, and involve male partners actively to reduce the unfair social blame often directed solely at women.
