Permanent

Introduction to Permanent Methods of Contraception

Permanent methods of contraception, commonly known as surgical sterilization, are designed for individuals and couples who have completed their desired family size and want a permanent end to childbearing. Unlike barrier or hormonal methods, sterilization involves a minor surgical procedure to block or seal the reproductive tracts, preventing the union of sperm and egg.

For female clients, the procedure typically involves blocking the fallopian tubes, while male sterilization, known as a vasectomy, involves sealing the vas deferens. Midwives working in primary health care facilities must understand that these methods are meant to be irreversible. Although surgical reversals are sometimes possible, they are expensive, complex, and carry a very low success rate. Therefore, careful client selection and thorough counseling are absolute prerequisites before any permanent method is scheduled.

Informed Consent and Counseling in Primary Health Care

Counseling for permanent contraception requires absolute clarity, patience, and neutrality. Midwives must ensure the client makes a voluntary choice free from pressure by partners, family members, or health workers.

Before signing the consent form, the midwife must cover several crucial points:

  1. Permanence: Emphasize that the procedure is permanent and non-negotiable.
  2. Alternatives: Discuss other highly effective long-acting reversible contraceptives like IUDs and implants.
  3. Surgical Risks: Explain minor and major risks, including bleeding, infection, and rare anesthetic complications.
  4. Failure Rate: Inform the client that while highly effective, no method is one hundred percent foolproof.
  5. No STI Protection: Remind the client that sterilization does not protect against HIV or other sexually transmitted infections.

Adequate informed consent documentation safeguards both the client's rights and the legal standing of the healthcare provider.

Female Sterilization: Tubal Ligation Techniques

Female sterilization, commonly referred to as tubal ligation, involves sealing or cutting the fallopian tubes to prevent fertilization. In primary health care settings or district hospitals, this is typically performed via minilaparotomy or laparoscopy.

  • Minilaparotomy: A small surgical incision, usually about two to three centimeters, is made in the lower abdomen, particularly during the postpartum period when the uterus is still elevated. The fallopian tubes are brought to the surface, tied, and a segment is removed using techniques such as the Pomeroy method.
  • Laparoscopy: A specialized lighted tube is inserted through a small navel incision to view the pelvic organs, allowing the surgeon to apply clips, rings, or electrocoagulation to block the tubes.

Postpartum tubal ligation is often performed within 48 hours after childbirth because the fallopian tubes are easily accessible near the top of the enlarged uterus.

Male Sterilization: Vasectomy Procedures

A vasectomy is a safe, simple, and highly effective permanent contraceptive method for men. The procedure involves cutting, tying, or sealing the vas deferens, the tubes that carry sperm from the testicles to the urethra.

Performed under local anesthesia, the surgeon makes a tiny puncture or incision in the upper scrotum to access, lift, and seal the tubes. Traditional vasectomies use a small scalpel, while the no-scalpel vasectomy (NSV) technique uses specialized instruments to puncture the skin, resulting in less bleeding, fewer stitches, and faster healing.

Midwives must counsel male clients that a vasectomy does not immediately stop pregnancy. Sperm remain stored above the surgical block. The client must use a backup contraceptive method, such as condoms, until a post-procedure semen analysis confirms that no sperm remain in the ejaculate, usually after about twenty ejaculations or three months.

Immediate Post-Procedure Care and Client Instructions

Following any sterilization procedure, proper wound care and rest prevent complications. Midwives play a key role in monitoring clients in the immediate recovery ward before discharge.

Key discharge instructions include:

  • Wound Care: Keep the incision clean and dry for at least forty-eight hours. Watch for signs of infection such as spreading redness, pus, or severe worsening pain.
  • Activity Restriction: Avoid heavy lifting, strenuous farm work, or rigorous exercise for at least a week.
  • Analgesia: Provide simple pain relievers like paracetamol to manage mild surgical site discomfort.
  • Follow-up Visit: Schedule a routine checkup after seven to ten days for wound inspection and suture removal if non-dissolvable threads were used.

Reassure the client that sterilization does not alter sexual pleasure, libido, or hormonal balance, as ovaries and testicles continue to function normally.

Addressing Common Community Myths and Misconceptions

In many Nigerian communities, deep-seated myths surrounding permanent contraception hinder uptake. Midwives must proactively address these misconceptions during community health talks and antenatal sessions.

Common myths include:

  • Myth: Sterilization causes physical weakness, making a person unable to work on the farm or walk long distances.

*Fact*: The surgery is minor and does not cause long-term physical weakness or loss of muscle strength once healed.

  • Myth: Tubal ligation or vasectomy causes madness or mental illness.

*Fact*: The reproductive organs are entirely separate from the brain and central nervous system; surgery has no effect on mental health.

  • Myth: A vasectomy is the same as castration (removal of the testicles).

*Fact*: The testicles remain intact and continue producing male hormones; only the sperm transport tubes are blocked.
