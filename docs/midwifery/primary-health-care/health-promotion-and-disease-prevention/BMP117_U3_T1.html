Concepts of Health Promotion, Disease Prevention and Levels of Prevention (Primary, Secondary, Tertiary)

Understanding Health Promotion in Midwifery

Health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over, and to improve, their health. Unlike disease prevention which focuses on a specific illness, health promotion takes a holistic view of well-being. It looks at the social, economic, and environmental factors that shape health. In Nigerian midwifery practice, this means looking beyond the antenatal clinic walls to consider how poverty, nutrition, housing, and community support affect a pregnant woman and her baby. Midwives do not just treat sickness; they build capacity. They empower women, families, and communities to make healthy choices, advocate for clean water, and access proper nutrition. Health promotion relies heavily on education and empowerment rather than clinical intervention alone. When a midwife teaches a women's group about balanced diets using local food stuffs like beans and greens, she is engaging in health promotion. The goal is to create conditions where healthy choices are the easiest and most accessible choices for mothers and children.

Core Disease Prevention Concepts

Disease prevention targets specific pathologies and aims to stop the onset of a disease, reduce its progression, or limit its long-term damage. While health promotion aims to increase well-being, prevention is more clinical and specific to known health threats. It rests on the understanding of the natural history of diseases—from the pre-pathogenesis phase (before a person gets sick) to the clinical phase (when symptoms appear). Midwives apply these concepts daily by identifying risk factors in pregnant women, such as a history of gestational diabetes or sickle cell trait, and taking targeted steps to block the disease process. Prevention acknowledges that many conditions have predictable pathways. By interrupting these pathways early, midwives protect mothers and newborns from severe morbidity and mortality.

Primary Prevention: Stopping Illness Before It Starts

Primary prevention acts upstream to prevent a disease or condition from ever occurring. It targets healthy populations or individuals before any pathology begins.

  • Immunization: Administering tetanus toxoid to pregnant women to prevent neonatal tetanus.
  • Chemoprophylaxis: Giving routine malaria intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp-SP) and folic acid supplements.
  • Health Education: Teaching safe water practices, exclusive breastfeeding, and proper nutrition to prevent nutritional deficiencies.
  • Environmental Control: Promoting the use of insecticide-treated bed nets to stop mosquito bites before malaria infection starts.

In Nigeria, primary prevention is the cornerstone of reducing high maternal and infant mortality rates, as stopping infections and nutritional deficits saves countless lives before clinical complications ever develop.

Secondary Prevention: Early Detection and Screening

Secondary prevention focuses on early diagnosis and prompt treatment of a disease that is already present in the body, often before symptoms are noticeable. The goal is to halt the pathological process, cure the disease, or slow its progression to prevent complications.

  • Routine Screening: Checking blood pressure at every antenatal visit to catch gestational hypertension early.
  • Diagnostic Testing: Using urine dipsticks for proteinuria or blood tests for gestational diabetes and HIV.
  • Cervical Cancer Screening: Performing visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) for women of reproductive age.

Catching conditions early in Nigerian clinics prevents severe emergencies like eclampsia or late-stage disease complications. Midwives must ensure that screening is always linked to immediate, accessible treatment; otherwise, knowing a diagnosis without being able to treat it offers little clinical value.

Tertiary Prevention: Managing Established Disease and Rehabilitation

Tertiary prevention steps in when a disease is well-established, symptomatic, or has already caused damage. The aim is to reduce impairment, minimize suffering, prevent long-term complications, and restore the individual to their highest possible level of functioning. In maternity care, this level is critical when complications arise during childbirth.

  • Emergency Management: Performing a blood transfusion or emergency cesarean section for severe postpartum hemorrhage or obstructed labor.
  • Rehabilitation: Supporting a mother and baby after severe birth asphyxia or managing long-term physical trauma like obstetric fistula.
  • Palliative Support: Managing chronic conditions to prevent permanent organ failure.

Tertiary prevention requires skilled clinical teams, functional operating theatres, and robust referral networks across Nigerian health facilities. It ensures that mothers and babies who experience severe complications survive and regain their quality of life.

Integrating the Levels of Prevention in Nigerian Practice

Effective maternal and child healthcare in Nigeria requires weaving primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention together seamlessly. A community midwife cannot rely on just one level; she must practice across the entire spectrum. During antenatal care, she provides primary prevention through nutritional counseling and malaria prophylaxis, secondary prevention through blood pressure screening and urinalysis, and prepares for tertiary prevention by identifying high-risk mothers who need delivery at a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility. Resource constraints in primary health centers often make secondary and tertiary care difficult, highlighting why robust primary prevention remains our strongest shield. When all three levels work together in the primary healthcare system, maternal and neonatal morbidity drop significantly, giving every Nigerian child a healthier start.
