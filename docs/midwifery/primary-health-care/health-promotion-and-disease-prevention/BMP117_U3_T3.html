Immunization and Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) Schedules

Introduction to Immunization in Nigerian Midwifery Practice

Immunization remains one of the most cost-effective public health interventions available to midwives in Nigeria. By introducing weakened or killed antigens into the body, vaccines stimulate the immune system to produce specific antibodies without causing the actual disease. Midwives encounter mothers and newborns at critical touchpoints—during antenatal care, labor, and postnatal clinics—making them frontline providers for disease prevention. Understanding how immunity develops protects vulnerable infants from severe childhood morbidity and mortality across urban and rural health facilities alike.

Principles of Active and Passive Immunization

Immunity is acquired through two primary mechanisms: active and passive.

  • Active Immunity: Generated when the body's own immune system responds to an antigen, either naturally through an infection or artificially via vaccination. It provides long-lasting protection because memory cells are formed.
  • Passive Immunity: Acquired when pre-formed antibodies are transferred from an external source, such as maternal antibodies crossing the placenta to the fetus or receiving therapeutic immunoglobulin injections. It offers immediate but temporary protection, lasting only weeks or months.

Overview of the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) in Nigeria

The Expanded Programme on Immunization was established globally by the World Health Organization in 1974 and launched in Nigeria in 1979. Its primary goal is to make life-saving vaccines accessible to all children, particularly infants under one year of age, and pregnant women. In Nigeria, the National Primary Health Care Development Agency coordinates this effort. Over the decades, the schedule has expanded from a few basic vaccines to include newer formulations like pneumococcal conjugate and rotavirus vaccines to combat major causes of childhood mortality.

Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) Schedule in Nigeria

The Nigerian routine immunization schedule dictates exact ages when infants and mothers must receive specific doses. Adhering to this timeline ensures optimal protection before children are exposed to common environmental pathogens.

  • At Birth: BCG, Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV 0), and Hepatitis B birth dose.
  • 6 Weeks: OPV 1, Pentavalent 1 (DTP-HebB-Hib), Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine (PCV 1), and Rotavirus 1.
  • 10 Weeks: OPV 2, Pentavalent 2, PCV 2, and Rotavirus 2.
  • 14 Weeks: OPV 3, Pentavalent 3, PCV 3, Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV), and Rotavirus 3 (where applicable).
  • 9 Months: Measles 1, Yellow Fever, and Meningitis vaccine.
  • 15 Months: Measles 2 and Vitamin A supplementation.

Target Diseases and Vaccine Administration Routes

Midwives must master the correct administration routes and anatomical sites for each vaccine to ensure efficacy and minimize adverse tissue reactions. Incorrect injection depths can lead to vaccine failure or sterile abscess formation.

  • Intradermal (ID): BCG is given on the right upper arm.
  • Intramuscular (IM): Pentavalent and PCV are given in the anterolateral aspect of the mid-thigh.
  • Subcutaneous (SC): Measles and Yellow Fever are administered in the upper arm.
  • Oral: OPV and Rotavirus drops are dispensed directly into the infant's mouth.

Cold Chain Management and Vaccine Handling

The cold chain is a temperature-controlled supply chain that ensures vaccines maintain their potency from the manufacturer to the point of administration. Exposing vaccines to extreme heat or freezing temperatures destroys their active biological components permanently. Midwives manage local cold chain equipment, including vaccine refrigerators, cold boxes, and vaccine carriers with ice packs. Routine temperature monitoring twice daily is mandatory to detect equipment failures before vaccine stocks are compromised.

Overcoming Barriers and Promoting Vaccine Uptake in Communities

Despite the proven benefits of immunization, various cultural, geographical, and logistical barriers hinder high coverage rates across Nigeria. Midwives frequently encounter vaccine hesitancy rooted in misinformation, religious skepticism, or fear of adverse side effects. Addressing these challenges requires culturally sensitive health education, active community engagement with local leaders, and robust tracking systems for defaulters. Midwives must build trusting relationships with families to dispel myths and emphasize that completing the immunization schedule is vital for child survival.
