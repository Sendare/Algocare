Cleft Lip and Palate

Understanding Cleft Lip and Palate: Embryology and Anatomy

Cleft lip and cleft palate are among the most common congenital malformations encountered in neonatal practice across Nigeria. They arise from a failure of normal tissue fusion during early embryonic development, specifically between the fourth and tenth weeks of intrauterine life. A cleft lip occurs when the frontonasal and maxillary processes fail to merge properly, which can be unilateral or bilateral, and incomplete or complete reaching the nostril. A cleft palate develops independently when the palatal shelves fail to fuse in the midline, creating an open communication between the oral and nasal cavities. While some cases run in families, most occurrences are multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of genetic predispositions and environmental influences such as maternal nutritional status, folic acid deficiency, and exposure to certain teratogens during early pregnancy.

Initial Assessment and Clinical Presentation in the Newborn

When a baby is born with an orofacial cleft, immediate and thorough physical examination is essential. Midwives should gently inspect both the external lip structures and use a penlight with a finger cot to palpate the hard and soft palates. A hidden or submucous cleft palate might present subtly with a bifid uvula, a zona pellucida (a bluish midline band), or a notched posterior hard palate. Beyond the visible gap, the newborn must be checked carefully for other associated anomalies, as orofacial clefts can sometimes form part of broader genetic syndromes. Documenting the exact extent, width, and involvement of the alveolar ridge helps the surgical team plan future interventions.

Feeding Difficulties Associated with Orofacial Clefts

Feeding a newborn with an orofacial cleft presents unique challenges because normal infant feeding relies on the baby's ability to create intra-oral negative pressure. When there is a gap in the lip or an open palate, the infant cannot form a proper seal around the nipple or generate the suction needed to draw milk effectively. This often leads to prolonged feeding times, excessive air swallowing, nasal regurgitation, fatigue, and poor weight gain. Frustration during feeds is common for both the baby and the mother, making empathetic support and practical feeding strategies vital from day one.

Specialized Feeding Techniques and Equipment

  • Modified Bottles: Use squeezable bottles or specialized cleft feeding bottles that allow the caregiver to control the flow of milk manually.
  • Upright Positioning: Hold the infant in an upright or semi-upright posture during feeds to reduce nasal regurgitation and the risk of aspiration.
  • Frequent Burping: Pause often to burp the baby, as these infants swallow large amounts of air while feeding due to the open palate.
  • Expressed Breast Milk: Encourage mothers to express breast milk when direct latching is not possible, ensuring the baby still receives essential immunological benefits.

Psychological Support and Counseling for Parents

The birth of a baby with a visible facial anomaly can trigger a profound emotional shock for parents, often accompanied by grief, anxiety, guilt, and fear of societal stigma. In many Nigerian communities, cultural beliefs and superstitions surrounding birth defects can worsen maternal distress and isolation. Midwives play a central role in offering compassionate, non-judgmental listening and accurate medical information. Reassuring parents that the condition is correctable through surgery helps build hope. Connecting families with parent support groups or mothers who have walked a similar path provides practical encouragement and reduces feelings of loneliness.

Preoperative Care and Multidisciplinary Preparation

Preparing an infant for surgical repair requires careful coordination among a multidisciplinary team including pediatricians, plastic surgeons, speech therapists, and midwives. Preoperative optimization focuses on ensuring good nutritional status, adequate weight gain, and freedom from any respiratory tract infections. Midwives educate parents on postoperative care expectations, such as arm restraints to prevent the baby from touching the surgical site and the transition to spoon or cup feeding depending on the surgeon's protocols. Timing of surgery varies, with cleft lip repair typically performed around three to six months of age, and cleft palate repair usually scheduled later before speech development peaks.
