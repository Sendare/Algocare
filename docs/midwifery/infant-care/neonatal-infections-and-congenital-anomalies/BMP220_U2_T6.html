Clubfoot (Talipes)

Understanding Congenital Talipes Equinovarus (Clubfoot)

Congenital talipes equinovarus, commonly known as clubfoot, is a structural birth defect where a newborn's foot is twisted inward and downward. The muscles, tendons, and ligaments on the inside and back of the leg are shorter and tighter than usual, pulling the foot into this abnormal position. It can affect one foot (unilateral) or both feet (bilateral). In Nigerian clinical practice, midwives frequently encounter this during routine neonatal head-to-toe examinations at birth. While the exact cause is not always clear, it often results from a combination of genetic and environmental factors. It is essential to distinguish this structural deformity from postural clubfoot, which is flexible and corrects easily with gentle passive stretching, unlike true congenital clubfoot which is rigid.

Recognition and Physical Assessment

Recognizing clubfoot requires a careful physical examination of the newborn's lower extremities immediately after delivery. Midwives should evaluate the flexibility, alignment, and skin creases of the affected limb.

  • Equinus: The heel is drawn up and cannot be brought down to a flat position.
  • Varus: The heel is tilted inward toward the midline of the body.
  • Cavus: There is an abnormally high arch in the midfoot.
  • Adductus: The forefoot is turned inward relative to the hindfoot.

arreling these features together creates the classic rigid deformity. Palpation also helps determine if the calf muscle on the affected side is smaller, which is a common associated finding.

Immediate Care and Parental Support

When a midwife identifies a newborn with clubfoot in a primary health centre or maternity unit, immediate care focuses on gentle reassurance and preventing secondary injury. Parents often experience shock, guilt, or anxiety when they see the deformity. Midwives play a crucial role in offering compassionate psychological support, explaining that the condition is correctable without immediate emergency surgery. Avoid forceful manipulation or applying improvised tight bandages, as these can damage fragile newborn skin and restrict circulation. Document the findings clearly in the birth record and prepare the family for specialized orthopedic care.

Referral for Serial Casting (Ponseti Method)

The gold standard treatment for clubfoot is the Ponseti method, which relies on serial casting. Midwives must ensure timely referral of the newborn to a specialized pediatric orthopedic clinic, ideally within the first few weeks of life while the ligaments are still supple.

  1. Gentle Manipulation: The specialist gently stretches the tight ligaments to correct the deformities step by step.
  2. Long-Leg Plaster Cast: A well-molded cast is applied from the toes up to the upper thigh to hold the foot in its newly corrected position.
  3. Weekly Changes: The baby returns every week for cast removal, gentle stretching, and application of a new cast to gradually align the bones.
  4. Tenotomy: A minor outpatient procedure to release the tight Achilles tendon is often performed near the end of casting.

Early referral prevents the deformity from becoming rigid and harder to treat.

Long-Term Management and Bracing

Once serial casting is successfully completed, the treatment phase is not entirely over. Because clubfoot has a high natural tendency to relapse, infants must wear a foot abduction brace (boots connected by a metal bar) for several years. Initially, the baby wears the brace nearly 23 hours a day for a few months, and later only during nighttime and naps up to age four or five. Midwives supporting families in the community must emphasize compliance with bracing schedules. Encouraging parents during home visits helps prevent relapse and ensures the child achieves normal mobility, allowing them to run and play like their peers.
