Ophthalmia Neonatorum and Prevention

Understanding Ophthalmia Neonatorum

Ophthalmia neonatorum is formally defined as any form of conjunctivitis occurring within the first 28 days of life. In Nigerian clinical practice, this condition remains a significant neonatal health concern due to the high prevalence of untreated maternal sexually transmitted infections. The newborn acquires the infection primarily during passage through an infected birth canal. Early identification is vital because severe forms, particularly those caused by bacteria, can rapidly progress to corneal ulceration and permanent blindness if treatment is delayed.

Etiological Agents and Pathophysiology

  • Neisseria gonorrhoeae: A gram-negative diplococcus responsible for hyperacute, severe purulent conjunctivitis with a rapid onset.
  • Chlamydia trachomatis: The leading cause of inclusion conjunctivitis in newborns, typically presenting with a slightly longer incubation period.
  • Herpes Simplex Virus: Can cause conjunctivitis associated with systemic signs and characteristic skin vesicles around the eye.
  • Chemical agents: Caused by the irritant effect of prophylactic eye medications, usually appearing within hours of instillation and resolving spontaneously.

Clinical Presentation and Pathogen Differentiation

Different pathogens present with distinct timelines and discharge characteristics. Gonococcal conjunctivitis usually manifests within two to four days after birth, marked by eyelid swelling and profuse, thick pus. Chlamydial infections emerge later, typically between five and fourteen days, showing mild to moderate discharge. Chemical conjunctivitis appears almost immediately after birth following prophylaxis but clears within a day without specific treatment. Careful history-taking regarding the onset day guides empirical midwifery assessment.

Credé's Prophylaxis and Eye Care

Routine ocular prophylaxis is a standard component of immediate newborn care designed to prevent gonococcal ophthalmia. Midwives administer antimicrobial agents into the conjunctival sac shortly after delivery.

  1. Tetra 1% Eye Ointment: Widely used in Nigerian facilities due to availability and stability.
  2. Erythromycin 0.5% Ointment: Effective alternative providing broad coverage without causing significant chemical irritation.
  3. Povidone-Iodine 2.5%: Aqueous solution increasingly studied and utilized in low-resource settings due to broad antimicrobial spectrum.

Medical Treatment Protocols

Confirmed cases require prompt, pathogen-specific medical management. Gonococcal conjunctivitis demands systemic antibiotic therapy, typically a single injection of ceftriaxone, combined with frequent saline eye irrigations to clear purulent exudate. Chlamydial conjunctivitis is managed with oral systemic erythromycin syrup, as topical treatment alone often fails to eradicate the nasopharyngeal carriage. Midwives must ensure complete adherence to prescribed systemic courses to prevent systemic complications such as pneumonia.

Antenatal Screening and Prevention

Preventing ophthalmia neonatorum begins well before delivery during routine antenatal care. Midwives should screen pregnant women for sexually transmitted infections during booking visits and treat any identified cases promptly. Partner notification and treatment are equally important to prevent reinfection before the third trimester concludes. Effective antenatal management drastically reduces the microbial load in the birth canal, safeguarding the newborn from severe ocular exposure.

Nursing Care and Complication Management

Nursing management focuses on gentle, frequent eye irrigation using sterile normal saline to prevent the accumulation of crusts and discharge. Midwives must wear gloves and practice strict hand hygiene before and after handling the infant to prevent nosocomial spread. Regular inspection of the cornea for clouding or ulceration is critical. If corneal damage is suspected, an urgent ophthalmology referral must be arranged to save the infant's sight.
