Exclusive Breastfeeding Promotion and UNICEF/WHO Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative

Introduction to Infant Nutrition and Optimal Feeding Practices

Optimal infant feeding forms the bedrock of child survival, healthy growth, and long-term development. In the early stages of life, breast milk provides every nutrient an infant requires, perfectly tailored to immature digestive and renal systems. Global public health bodies uniformly recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life. This means the infant receives only breast milk without any additional water, other liquids, or solid foods, save for necessary medicinal drops or syrups.

Following this six-month window, nutritionally adequate and safe complementary foods must be introduced while breastfeeding continues up to two years of age or beyond. In Nigeria and many sub-Saharan settings, early introduction of water or herbal teas remains a deeply rooted cultural practice. Midwives must understand these local realities to provide respectful, effective education that champions the physiological superiority of unadulterated breast milk.

Nutritional and Immunological Benefits of Breast Milk

Breast milk is a dynamic biological fluid that changes in composition to match the evolving needs of the growing infant.

  • Colostrum: The yellowish, thick milk produced in late pregnancy and the first few days postpartum. It is exceptionally rich in proteins, fat-soluble vitamins, and white blood cells.
  • Immunoglobulins: Particularly secretory IgA, which coats the infant's gut mucosa, creating a physical barrier against enteric pathogens.
  • Macronutrients: Precisely balanced proportions of whey and casein proteins, lactose for brain development, and essential fatty acids.
  • Growth Factors: Bioactive compounds that stimulate the maturation of the infant's gastrointestinal tract, lowering the risk of necrotizing enterocolitis.

Formula milk attempts to mimic these components in a laboratory, but it completely lacks living maternal immune cells, active enzymes, and customized antibodies.

UNICEF/WHO Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative (BFHI)

Launched in 1991, the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative is a global effort by UNICEF and the WHO to ensure that maternity facilities worldwide create a healthcare environment that protects, promotes, and supports breastfeeding. The initiative addresses systemic hospital practices that historically undermined breastfeeding initiation, such as routine separation of mothers and babies, early supplementation with infant formula, and the distribution of free commercial milk samples.

By designating facilities as 'Baby-Friendly,' health systems commit to eliminating maternity ward policies that interfere with lactation. Midwives serve as the frontline implementers of these standards, ensuring that institutional routines align with infant nutritional needs rather than commercial or administrative convenience.

The Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding

The operational core of the Baby-Friendly Hospital Initiative rests on the Ten Steps to Successful Breastfeeding. These clinical guidelines govern maternal and newborn care in maternity wards.

  1. Hospital Policies: Comply routinely with written breastfeeding policies communicated to staff and parents.
  2. Staff Competency: Train health care staff in knowledge and skills necessary to implement this policy.
  3. Antenatal Care: Discuss the management and benefits of breastfeeding with pregnant women and their families.
  4. Immediate Care: Facilitate immediate, uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact and support mothers to initiate breastfeeding as soon as possible after birth.
  5. Support Positioning: Support mothers to initiate and maintain breastfeeding and manage common difficulties.
  6. Exclusive Milk: Do not provide breastfed newborns any food or fluids other than breast milk, unless medically indicated.
  7. Rooming-In: Enable mothers and infants to remain together 24 hours a day.
  8. Responsive Feeding: Support mothers to recognize and respond to their infants' cues for feeding.
  9. Counseling on Bottles: Counsel mothers on the use and risks of feeding bottles, teats, and pacifiers.
  10. Discharge Support: Coordinate discharge so that parents and their infants have timely access to ongoing support and care.

Overcoming Common Early Initiation Challenges in Clinical Practice

Even with optimal hospital policies, mothers frequently encounter physical and psychological hurdles during the first few days postpartum. Midwives must recognize these challenges and offer prompt, evidence-based solutions.

  • Engorged Breasts: Caused by vascular congestion and fluid accumulation before milk 'comes in.' Manage with warm compresses before feeds, gentle massage, and expressing a small amount of milk to soften the areola.
  • Sore or Cracked Nipples: Almost always the result of incorrect infant latching. Correct the baby's position so a large portion of the lower areola is in the baby's mouth rather than just the nipple.
  • Perceived Insufficient Milk: Many primiparous mothers worry their milk supply is inadequate during the physiological lag before copious milk production. Reassure the mother, check baby output (wet diapers), and encourage frequent suckling to stimulate prolactin.

Addressing these issues early prevents premature introduction of water or formula and builds maternal confidence.

The Role of the Midwife in Counseling and Community Support

Midwifery care extends far beyond the delivery suite. Midwives act as vital links between institutional maternity care and community life. Antenatal education classes provide the ideal setting to debunk myths regarding colostrum discard, prelacteal feeding, and water supplementation.

When women are discharged, community follow-up and mother support groups help sustain exclusive breastfeeding against societal pressures from family members or aggressive marketing of breast milk substitutes. Midwives must engage grandmothers, spouses, and community leaders, as these influential figures often dictate infant feeding decisions in Nigerian households. Culturally sensitive communication ensures that messages are received respectfully and implemented effectively at home.
