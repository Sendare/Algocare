Expressing and Storage of Breast Milk

Indications and Preparation for Expressing Breast Milk

Expressing breast milk allows mothers to maintain their milk supply and feed their babies even when separated. Common reasons include returning to work, managing painful or engorged breasts, feeding a premature or sick baby in the neonatal unit, or relieving blocked milk ducts.

Before starting any expression method, meticulous preparation protects the baby from harmful germs.

  • Hand hygiene: Wash hands thoroughly with soap and clean running water for at least twenty seconds.
  • Equipment check: Inspect all pump parts or containers to ensure they are clean, dry, and free from cracks or mold.
  • Environment: Choose a clean, quiet space to help relax, which naturally triggers the oxytocin reflex needed for milk flow.
  • Breast preparation: Gently massage the breasts to stimulate milk let-down, though routine washing of the breasts before every expression is unnecessary.

Techniques for Expressing Breast Milk

Mothers can express milk using either hand expression or mechanical pumps, depending on their resources and needs.

Hand expression is a valuable skill that requires no equipment, making it ideal for immediate postpartum use, engorgement, or emergencies. The mother places her thumb and fingers on the edge of the areola, presses back toward the chest wall, and compresses gently together, rhythmic rolling without sliding on the skin.

Mechanical pumps—manual or electric—use suction to draw milk from the ducts. Single pumps are suitable for occasional use, while double electric pumps are more efficient for mothers who express frequently or need to establish a strong milk supply after preterm delivery. Suction levels should remain comfortable and never cause sharp pain or nipple trauma.

Container Selection and Hygiene Practices

Selecting the right storage vessel prevents contamination and preserves the nutritional and immunological properties of breast milk. Food-grade glass or hard plastics free from bisphenol-A (BPA) are the safest choices. Disposable breast milk storage bags designed specifically for freezing are also acceptable, provided they are sealed securely and handled with care to avoid punctures.

Containers must be washed thoroughly in hot, soapy water and rinsed well after every use. For infants who are newborn, premature, or immunocompromised, sanitizing equipment daily by boiling or using a steam sterilizer is standard practice. Always label every container clearly with the exact date and time of expression to ensure older milk is used first.

Storage Guidelines for Different Environments

Breast milk is a living fluid with dynamic anti-infective properties, but its safety depends heavily on storage temperature. Freshly expressed milk can safely stay at room temperature for up to four hours under clean conditions. When placed in a cooler bag with ice packs, it remains stable for up to twenty-four hours.

Inside a standard refrigerator (4°C or colder), milk stays fresh for up to four days. For longer storage, a deep freezer maintains quality for six to twelve months, though using it within six months is ideal to preserve maximum vitamin content. Always store milk toward the back of the refrigerator or freezer where temperatures remain most stable.

Thawing, Warming, and Safe Handling of Stored Milk

Proper thawing and warming protect the nutritional value of stored breast milk and prevent accidental burns to the baby. Always use the oldest stored milk first. Thaw frozen milk by placing it in the refrigerator overnight or by running the container under warm tap water.

Never use a microwave to thaw or warm breast milk. Microwaves heat liquids unevenly, creating dangerous hot spots that can severely burn an infant's mouth, and high heat destroys precious protective antibodies. Once milk is thawed, swirl it gently to mix the separated fat layer back in. Discard any leftover milk from a feeding once the baby has finished eating.

Troubleshooting Common Expression and Storage Challenges

Mothers often encounter hurdles such as low milk output, clogged ducts, or changes in milk odor. Low expression volume can stem from stress, incorrect flange size, or poor pump suction. Reassuring the mother, encouraging skin-to-skin contact, and checking equipment settings often restores normal flow.

Sometimes stored milk develops a soapy smell or blue tint; this is normal and caused by enzyme activity breaking down fats, and babies usually drink it without issue. However, if milk smells sour or rancid, it has spoiled and must be discarded. Emphasize that slight variations in milk color based on the mother's diet are entirely normal.
