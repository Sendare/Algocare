Mastitis

Understanding Mastitis in Lactation

Mastitis is an inflammation of the breast tissue that can involve an infection, most commonly affecting breastfeeding women within the first few weeks postpartum, though it can occur at any time during lactation. When milk is trapped or stagnant inside the breast ducts, it creates an ideal environment for bacteria to multiply, leading to painful swelling and systemic symptoms. In Nigerian clinical practice, midwives frequently see mothers presenting with this condition when early breastfeeding challenges are ignored or poorly managed. Left untreated, simple inflammation can rapidly progress to a bacterial infection, causing significant distress to the mother and threatening the continuation of exclusive breastfeeding. Recognizing the distinction between non-infectious milk stasis and true infectious mastitis is vital for providing appropriate care in primary health centers.

Underlying Causes and Risk Factors for Nigerian Nursing Mothers

Several local and physiological factors contribute to the incidence of mastitis among nursing mothers in Nigeria. Poor infant attachment remains the leading culprit, as an ineffective latch prevents the baby from efficiently removing milk from all parts of the breast. Other common triggers include infrequent feeds, sudden weaning, pressure from tight clothing or wrappers, and maternal fatigue.

  • Ineffective Latch: Causes incomplete drainage of specific breast segments.
  • Infrequent Feedings: Prolonged intervals between feeds allow milk to pool and pressure to build up.
  • Localized Pressure: Tight blouses, heavy wrappers tied tightly across the chest, or carrying a baby too firmly against the breast can obstruct milk ducts.
  • Maternal Exhaustion: High stress and physical tiredness can weaken the mother's immune response, making her more susceptible to infection.

Recognition and Management of Mastitis

Clinical presentation of mastitis typically includes a painful, swollen, red area on the breast, often accompanied by flu-like symptoms such as high fever, chills, body aches, and fatigue. Midwives must assess the client thoroughly, evaluating both local breast signs and systemic well-being. Management centers on effective milk removal, pain relief, and, when an infection is present, appropriate antibiotic therapy.

Encouraging the mother to continue breastfeeding from the affected side is crucial, as the baby is the most efficient pump for clearing blocked ducts. Simple analgesics like paracetamol help manage pain and fever. If symptoms persist beyond twenty-four hours of effective milk removal, or if the mother shows signs of systemic toxicity, prescription antibiotics targeting common skin flora such as Staphylococcus aureus should be initiated promptly without advising the mother to stop nursing.

Differential Diagnosis: Engorgement, Blocked Ducts, and Abscesses

Midwives must carefully differentiate mastitis from other common postpartum breast conditions to ensure proper treatment.

Misdiagnosing an abscess as simple mastitis delays necessary surgical referral, while treating engorgement with antibiotics is unnecessary and inappropriate.

Supportive Care and Breastfeeding Continuity

Providing holistic support to a mother with mastitis helps prevent premature cessation of breastfeeding. Midwives should reassure mothers that breast milk remains safe for the baby to consume, even when an infection or mild pus is present. Discontinuing breastfeeding abruptly only worsens engorgement and increases the risk of abscess formation. Practical comfort measures include applying warm compresses before feeding to encourage milk flow, and cool compresses afterward to reduce inflammation. Encouraging adequate fluid intake, balanced nutrition, and strict bed rest for the first couple of days accelerates recovery. Family members, especially partners and mothers-in-law, should be involved in counseling sessions to help take over household chores, allowing the nursing mother the physical rest she desperately needs.

Preventive Strategies and Patient Education in Community Practice

Community midwifery programs play a pivotal role in preventing mastitis through antenatal and postnatal education. Midwives must teach expectant mothers proper positioning and latch techniques before delivery, reinforcing these lessons during home visits or clinic follow-ups. Mothers should understand the importance of feeding on demand, avoiding rigid feeding schedules, and varying nursing positions to ensure all quadrants of the breast are thoroughly drained. Antenatal assessments should also check for flat or inverted nipples so that corrective measures can be introduced early. Empowering women to recognize early signs of milk stasis allows them to seek help immediately, transforming potential emergencies into easily managed minor issues.
