Physiology of Lactation and Composition of Breast Milk

Hormonal Control and the Physiology of Lactation

Lactation is primarily regulated by a delicate balance of hormones acting on the breast tissue throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period. During pregnancy, high levels of estrogen and progesterone stimulate the growth and branching of the ductal system and the development of the alveoli, while simultaneously suppressing full milk production. Following delivery, the expulsion of the placenta causes a sharp drop in estrogen and progesterone levels. This sudden hormonal withdrawal removes the block on lactation, allowing prolactin to stimulate milk synthesis. Meanwhile, oxytocin triggers the contraction of myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli to eject the milk. Together, these hormones ensure that milk is both produced and delivered effectively in response to the infant's demand.

Stages of Lactogenesis: From Pregnancy to Established Milk Flow

Lactogenesis occurs in distinct physiological phases from late gestation through the early weeks postpartum:

  1. Lactogenesis I (Secretory Differentiation): Begins around mid-pregnancy. The breasts develop secretory characteristics, and small amounts of colostrum are produced under the influence of placental hormones.
  2. Lactogenesis II (Secretory Activation): Triggered by the delivery of the placenta and the resulting drop in progesterone. This phase marks the onset of copious milk production, commonly referred to as 'the milk coming in,' usually occurring between 48 to 72 hours after birth.
  3. Lactogenesis III (Galactopoiesis): The maintenance stage of milk production. It relies on autocrine control, meaning milk supply is sustained primarily by frequent removal of milk through breastfeeding or expression.

The Neuroendocrine Reflexes: Prolactin and the Let-Down Reflex

Successful breastfeeding relies on two distinct neuroendocrine pathways: the prolactin reflex (supply line) and the oxytocin reflex (delivery system).

  • Prolactin Reflex: Infant suckling stimulates nerve endings in the nipple and areola. These signals travel up the spinal cord to the hypothalamus, which signals the anterior pituitary gland to release prolactin into the bloodstream. Prolactin drives the secretory cells to manufacture the next feed's milk.
  • Oxytocin Reflex (Let-Down): Sensory input also stimulates the posterior pituitary gland to release oxytocin. Oxytocin causes tiny muscle-like myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli to contract, squeezing milk down into the milk ducts so the baby can easily swallow it.

Nutritional Profile and Composition of Breast Milk

Human milk is a dynamic fluid tailored perfectly to infant growth and development. It contains water, carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, and minerals in ideal proportions. Lactose is the primary carbohydrate, supplying vital energy and promoting the absorption of calcium and iron while supporting healthy gut flora. Proteins are divided into whey and casein, with whey predominating in early milk for easier digestion and superior immune protection through immunoglobulin A (IgA). Lipids provide over half of the infant's caloric intake and deliver essential fatty acids crucial for brain and retinal development. Furthermore, bioactive factors and white blood cells protect the newborn against infections while their immune system matures.

Colostrum versus Mature Milk: Nutritional Transitions

Breast milk transforms over time to match the growing infant's changing physiological needs. Colostrum, secreted during the first few days after birth, is a thick, yellowish fluid packed with concentrated proteins, fat-soluble vitamins, and high levels of secretory IgA to coat the baby's gut. It acts as a natural laxative to help clear meconium. As days pass, milk transitions through a transitional phase before settling into mature milk, which appears thinner and whiter, featuring higher concentrations of fats and carbohydrates to support rapid physical growth.

Foremilk and Hindmilk Variations During a Single Feed

Milk composition also shifts dynamically during a single feeding session. Foremilk is the milk that flows at the start of a feed; it has a higher water content, lower fat volume, and quenches the infant's thirst. As the nursing session continues and the breast empties, fat droplets that previously clung to the walls of the alveoli are swept into the milk stream, creating hindmilk. Hindmilk is rich in fat and calories, signaling satiety and helping the infant gain appropriate weight. This variation highlights why letting an infant finish one breast completely before offering the second is clinically important.
