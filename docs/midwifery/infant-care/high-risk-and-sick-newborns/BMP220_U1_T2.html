Small for Gestational Age (SGA)

Understanding Small for Gestational Age Infants

A Small for Gestational Age (SGA) infant is a newborn whose birth weight lies below the 10th percentile for their gestational age on standard growth charts. This means compared to babies born at the exact same week of pregnancy, an SGA infant weighs less than 90 percent of them.

It is vital to separate SGA from premature birth, though a baby can be both. Prematurity refers strictly to being born before 37 completed weeks of pregnancy, regardless of weight. An SGA baby, however, can be born full-term at 39 weeks but simply failed to grow adequately inside the womb. Midwives in Nigeria use reliable last menstrual period dating or early ultrasound scans alongside accurate birth weight measurements to properly identify these infants.

Symmetrical Versus Asymmetrical Growth Restriction

Intrauterine growth restriction leading to an SGA infant generally manifests in two distinct patterns: symmetrical and asymmetrical.

  • Symmetrical SGA: All parts of the body are uniformly small. Head circumference, length, and weight are all below the 10th percentile. This usually results from early-onset insults such as chromosomal abnormalities, severe maternal chronic infections, or first-trimester malnutrition.
  • Asymmetrical SGA: The infant's head and brain are spared, while the body and abdomen are thin and wasted. This occurs due to late-onset placental insufficiency where the fetus redirects its limited oxygen and nutrients primarily to the brain and heart at the expense of liver glycogen and subcutaneous fat.

Common Etiologies and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

Several maternal, placental, and fetal factors contribute to the incidence of SGA newborns across Nigerian healthcare facilities. Identifying these factors during antenatal booking helps midwives anticipate complications.

  • Maternal Nutrition and Health: Chronic maternal malnutrition, severe maternal anemia, adolescent pregnancies, and chronic illnesses like hypertension or renal disease limit nutrient delivery.
  • Infections: Malaria in pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections such as syphilis heavily impact placental function and fetal growth.
  • Substance Use: Maternal exposure to environmental toxins, tobacco smoke, and local substances can restrict fetal development.
  • Placental Factors: Poor placentation, placental infarctions, and chronic uteroplacental insufficiency directly starve the developing fetus.

Clinical Assessment and Identification at Birth

Accurate assessment in the labor ward or immediate postnatal room ensures that every SGA infant receives targeted care. Midwives must perform a comprehensive physical examination beyond simply placing the baby on a weighing scale.

Signs of an SGA infant include a scaphoid or sunken abdomen, loose and dry skin with reduced subcutaneous fat, wide skull sutures due to poor bone mineralization, and an alert, anxious facial expression often described as an "old man" look. Midwives should immediately plot the birth weight, length, and head circumference on standard growth curves to verify the classification and check for asymmetry.

Immediate Neonatal Complications and Risks

Small for Gestational Age infants face several critical physiological challenges in the transition to extrauterine life.

  • Hypoglycemia: Depleted glycogen stores and reduced fat reserves make these infants extremely vulnerable to low blood sugar levels.
  • Polycythemia: Chronic intrauterine hypoxia stimulates excess red blood cell production, leading to thick, sluggish blood that can cause microvascular stasis and jaundice.
  • Hypothermia: A high surface-area-to-mass ratio combined with absent brown adipose tissue makes maintaining core body temperature difficult.
  • Birth Asphyxia: Poor placental reserve during labor leaves them susceptible to acute oxygen deprivation.

Midwifery Management in the Delivery Room and Ward

Midwifery care for an SGA infant focuses on preventing complications through prompt intervention, rigorous monitoring, and supportive therapy.

  • Thermal Regulation: Immediately dry the infant, place them skin-to-skin with the mother, or admit them to a warm incubator to prevent cold stress.
  • Glucose Monitoring: Test blood glucose levels within the first hour of life and feed the baby early with expressed breast milk or formula if breastfeeding is delayed.
  • Resuscitation Readiness: Ensure skilled personnel and functional resuscitation equipment are available during delivery, as these infants tolerate labor stress poorly.
  • Infection Prevention: Practice strict hand hygiene and umbilical cord care, since malnourished newborns have compromised immune defenses.

Long-Term Growth and Developmental Outcomes

While many SGA infants catch up in growth during their first two years of life, some face long-term health implications. Symmetrical SGA infants who experienced early insults may remain short in stature throughout childhood. Furthermore, epidemiological evidence links low birth weight with an increased risk of developing metabolic syndrome, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes in adult life—a phenomenon known as fetal programming. Midwives play a role in counseling parents on optimal infant nutrition, routine immunizations, and regular pediatric follow-ups to support optimal developmental milestones.
