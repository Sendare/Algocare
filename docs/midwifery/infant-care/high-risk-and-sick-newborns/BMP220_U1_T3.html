Care of the Low Birth Weight and Preterm Infant

Understanding the Vulnerable Newborn: Defining Preterm and Low Birth Weight

In midwifery practice, classifying small newborns correctly dictates their immediate care pathways. A preterm infant is born alive before 37 completed weeks of gestation, categorized further into late preterm, very preterm, and extremely preterm. Conversely, low birth weight (LBW) describes any baby born weighing less than 2,500 grams, regardless of gestational age. This group includes both infants born prematurely and those who suffered from intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) while carrying to term.

Physiologically, these infants face severe systemic immaturity. Their skin is thin, brown fat stores are minimal, and surfactant deficiency makes their lungs prone to collapse. Midwives must recognize that an infant can be LBW without being preterm, or preterm without falling strictly into the lowest weight tiers, though many experience both challenges simultaneously. Accurate gestational age estimation through the mother's last menstrual period and early ultrasound, combined with precise birth weighing within the first hour of life, remains the foundation of safe neonatal nursing.

Maintaining Thermal Stability and Preventing Cold Stress

Preterm and low birth weight infants lose heat rapidly due to a high surface area-to-body-mass ratio, absence of insulating subcutaneous fat, and an inability to shiver. When a newborn's core temperature drops below 36.5°C, they enter a state of cold stress. To compensate, the infant breaks down brown adipose tissue, consuming massive amounts of oxygen and glucose. This metabolic response quickly triggers severe hypoxia, hypoglycemia, and metabolic acidosis.

Midwives prevent cold stress using a sequence known as the warm chain:

  • Warm delivery room: Ensure the ambient temperature is at least 25°C with no drafts.
  • Immediate drying: Wipe the wet newborn thoroughly at birth and discard wet linen.
  • Skin-to-skin contact: Place the stable baby directly on the mother's chest under warm covers.
  • Appropriate clothing: Dress the baby in a cap and light wrappers, avoiding unnecessary bathing until thermal stability is well established.

Nutritional Needs and Feeding Support Strategies

Feeding the vulnerable infant requires balancing high metabolic demands with immature gastrointestinal function. Maternal breast milk remains the gold standard, offering protective immunological factors alongside easily digestible nutrients. However, extremely preterm infants lack a coordinated suck-swallow-breathe reflex until roughly 34 weeks gestation.

Midwives utilize various feeding methods depending on the infant's gestational age and clinical stability:

  • Expressing and cup feeding: Suitable for infants who can swallow but cannot coordinate sucking at the breast.
  • Nasogastric or oro-gastric tube feeding: Necessary for infants under 32 weeks or those experiencing respiratory distress.
  • Fortification: Adding human milk fortifiers to express breast milk to supply extra protein, calcium, and calories for rapid catch-up growth.

Always check gastric residual volumes before administering a new tube feed to assess digestive tolerance and prevent necrotizing enterocolitis.

Continuous Physiological Monitoring and Early Warning Signs

A stable exterior can mask rapid physiological deterioration in a preterm infant. Midwives must monitor vital signs frequently, watching closely for signs of respiratory distress, such as nasal flaring, intercostal retractions, and grunting. Apnea—pauses in breathing lasting longer than 20 seconds, or shorter pauses accompanied by bradycardia and cyanosis—is a frequent emergency in infants born before 32 weeks.

Pulse oximetry helps maintain safe oxygen saturation targets, preventing both hypoxia and hyperoxia, which can cause retinopathy of prematurity. Regular blood glucose checks are equally vital, as preterm infants exhaust their glycogen stores quickly. Any sudden lethargy, poor peripheral perfusion, or temperature instability should prompt immediate clinical escalation.

Infection Prevention in the Neonatal Unit

Preterm infants possess immature immune systems with low levels of circulating immunoglobulins and fragile skin barriers that allow bacteria to enter easily. Neonatal sepsis can progress from subtle lethargy to fatal septic shock within hours. Strict hand hygiene before touching any infant is the single most effective intervention to break the chain of transmission.

Midwives must maintain aseptic technique during invasive procedures like umbilical catheter insertion, intravenous cannulation, and endotracheal suctioning. Incubators and radiant warmers require strict, regular disinfection protocols. Furthermore, promoting exclusive breastfeeding supplies maternal antibodies that protect the infant's mucosal surfaces against opportunistic pathogens.

Implementing Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC)

Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) is a proven, evidence-based practice for stable low birth weight infants. It involves continuous skin-to-skin contact between the infant and the mother, father, or another designated family member, placing the baby upright between the mother's breasts in a specialized wrap.

KMC stabilizes the infant's body temperature as effectively as an incubator, regulates heart and respiratory rates, reduces stress hormones, and encourages successful breastfeeding. Midwives play a central role in positioning the infant correctly with the head turned to one side, airway clear, and hips flexed in a frog-like position. Educating the family transforms them into active partners in the daily care of their vulnerable newborn.

Preparing for Discharge and Family-Centered Care

Discharge planning for a preterm or low birth weight infant begins on the day of admission. Transitioning home requires confirming that the infant maintains a stable temperature in normal clothing, feeds effectively to achieve steady weight gain, and remains free of apnea or severe infections. Parents must demonstrate confidence in handling the baby, recognizing danger signs, and administering any necessary medications.

Midwives coordinate follow-up appointments, immunizations, and developmental screenings. Empowering the family through clear communication reduces anxiety and ensures that the vulnerable infant continues to thrive long after leaving the hospital environment.
