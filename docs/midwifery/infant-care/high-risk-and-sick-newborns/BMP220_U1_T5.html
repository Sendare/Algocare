Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS) and Oxygen Therapy

Introduction to Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome

Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome, commonly known as RDS, primarily affects preterm infants whose lungs are not yet fully developed. The main trigger behind this condition is a deficiency of pulmonary surfactant, a soapy substance that keeps tiny air sacs open. Without enough surfactant, the alveoli collapse every time the baby exhales, making every breath a massive struggle. In Nigerian neonatal units, premature delivery remains a major challenge due to limited access to advanced antenatal care, meaning midwives frequently encounter babies fighting for breath within minutes of birth. Recognizing the risk factors, especially extreme prematurity and diabetic mothers, allows delivery room staff to prepare specialized resuscitation equipment immediately.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

Understanding why the lungs fail in RDS requires looking closely at micro-anatomy. The immature type II pneumocytes fail to synthesize and secrete adequate surfactant. As surface tension inside the alveoli skyrockets, widespread atelectasis occurs. This leads to severe ventilation-perfusion mismatch, hypoxia, and hypercapnia. The body responds by constricting pulmonary blood vessels, causing ischemia of the lung lining and plasma leakage. This leaked fluid mixes with cellular debris to form hyaline membranes that line the terminal air spaces, further blocking oxygen exchange and locking the newborn into a vicious cycle of worsening respiratory failure.

Clinical Signs and Symptoms

A newborn with RDS displays unmistakable signs of respiratory struggle that every midwife must spot instantly.

  • Tachypnea: Respiratory rate consistently above 60 breaths per minute as the baby tries to compensate for poor oxygen intake.
  • Intercostal and subcostal retractions: Visible sinking of the skin around and between the ribs due to negative pressure generated against stiff lungs.
  • Expiratory grunting: A characteristic sound made when the baby exhales against a partially closed glottis to maintain residual lung volume.
  • Nasal flaring: Widening of the nostrils with each breath to minimize airway resistance.
  • Cyanosis: Bluish discoloration of the tongue, lips, and core body, indicating dangerously low blood oxygen.

Immediate Nursing Assessment and Scoring Systems

Rapid evaluation saves lives in the labor ward or neonatal intensive care unit. Midwives use scoring tools to quantify the severity of respiratory distress and guide urgent interventions. The Silverman-Andersen Score evaluates five distinct clinical features: chest movement, intercostal retraction, xiphoid retraction, lower jaw movement, and expiratory grunt. Each item scores from 0 to 2, where a higher total score points to severe respiratory failure requiring immediate respiratory support. Concurrently, continuous pulse oximetry provides objective data on peripheral oxygen saturation, though clinical observation of chest wall movement remains paramount in resource-limited settings.

Oxygen Administration and Monitoring

Oxygen is a vital drug in neonatal care, but it must be handled with precision. When administering supplemental oxygen to a distressed newborn, the goal is to maintain target oxygen saturations, typically between 88% and 95% for preterm infants, avoiding both hypoxia and hyperoxia. Midwives utilize devices such as nasal prongs, head boxes, or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) circuits. Blenders must be used to mix medical air and oxygen accurately. Regular calibration of equipment and continuous monitoring with pulse oximetry prevent accidental over-oxygenation, which carries severe long-term risks for vulnerable tissues.

Complications of Oxygen Therapy in Preterm Neonates

While life-saving, excessive or poorly monitored oxygen therapy poses major hazards to the preterm neonate.

ComplicationUnderlying MechanismClinical Impact
Retinopathy of Prematurity (ROP)High oxygen levels halt normal blood vessel growth in the retina, followed by abnormal vessel proliferation leading to retinal detachment.Potential blindness if not screened and treated early.
Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia (BPD)Barotrauma, volutrauma, and oxygen toxicity cause chronic inflammation and scarring of immature lung tissue.Long-term oxygen dependency and recurrent respiratory infections.

Midwives play a critical role in advocating for target saturations and ensuring routine eye screening for at-risk infants before discharge.

Supportive Care and Prevention Strategies

Preventing RDS and managing affected infants requires a comprehensive approach that extends beyond the delivery room. Antenatal corticosteroids administered to mothers in preterm labor accelerate fetal lung maturation and dramatically reduce the severity of RDS. Once the baby is born, maintaining neutral thermal environment prevents cold stress, which worsens metabolic acidosis and increases oxygen demand. Gentle handling, minimal stimulation, and careful fluid management protect the fragile cerebral and pulmonary systems. Through vigilant nursing care, proper positioning, and family involvement, midwives help bridge the gap toward healthy neonatal survival.
