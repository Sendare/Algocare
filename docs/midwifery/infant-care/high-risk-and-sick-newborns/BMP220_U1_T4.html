Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) Principles and Implementation

Introduction and Physiological Rationale of Kangaroo Mother Care

Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC) is an evidence-based intervention for preterm and low birth weight infants, characterized by continuous skin-to-skin contact between the mother and her baby, exclusive breastfeeding, and early discharge. In Nigeria, where facility neonatal intensive care units are often overcrowded and resource-constrained, KMC serves as a life-saving, cost-effective alternative that bridges the gap in newborn survival.

Physiologically, the preterm infant struggles with thermoregulation due to a lack of brown adipose tissue and inadequate glycogen stores. The mother's chest acts as a natural incubator, maintaining the infant's core temperature through thermal synchrony. Skin-to-skin contact stabilizes the newborn's heart rate and respiratory pattern, drastically reducing episodes of apnea of prematurity. Furthermore, this intimate proximity colonizes the infant's skin with maternal flora rather than hospital-acquired pathogens, offering robust immunological protection.

Core Components: Skin-to-Skin Contact and Positioning

The foundational element of KMC is continuous, prolonged skin-to-skin contact (SSC). The infant, dressed only in a diaper, cap, and socks, is placed upright between the mother's breasts in a frog-like flexion posture.

  • Positioning: The baby's abdomen should rest against the mother's epigastric region, head turned to one side with the neck slightly extended to maintain a patent airway. Avoid extreme flexion or hyperextension of the neck.
  • Support: A secure binder or wrapper must firmly hold the infant against the maternal chest, supporting the buttocks and back so the weight is distributed evenly.
  • Duration: SSC should be maintained 24 hours a day, interrupted only for brief hygiene changes. If the mother is fatigued, other family members can share the practice.

Core Component: Exclusive Breastfeeding and Nutrition

Nutrition in KMC is inextricably linked to the physical closeness of mother and child. Proximity stimulates maternal prolactin and oxytocin reflexes, enhancing milk production and let-down.

Preterm infants possess immature suck-swallow-breathe coordination and may tire easily at the breast. Midwives must guide mothers on alternative feeding methods such as expressed breast milk (EBM) given via cup, spoon, or nasogastric tube until the infant is mature enough to latch consistently. Frequent, small-volume feeds are prioritized over rigid schedules to accommodate small gastric capacities and prevent hypoglycemia.

Preparing the Mother and Family for KMC Practice

Successful KMC implementation requires comprehensive preparation of the mother, her partner, and the wider household. Midwives must demystify the practice, addressing cultural myths or fears regarding the vulnerability of small babies.

Counseling should cover personal hygiene, nutrition and hydration for the nursing mother, and instructions on recognizing danger signs. Involving fathers and grandmothers creates a supportive domestic environment, ensuring the mother gets adequate rest and assistance with household chores while she remains in the KMC position for extended hours.

Monitoring, Complications, and Discharge Criteria

Infants receiving KMC require vigilant clinical monitoring by both mothers and healthcare providers. Midwives must teach mothers how to track infant breathing rates, color, temperature, and feeding tolerance.

Discharge from the facility is considered when the infant can maintain stable body temperature in ambient room clothing, shows consistent weight gain for at least three consecutive days, and is breastfeeding well. Structured community follow-up visits ensure continued growth tracking and psychological support for the family.
