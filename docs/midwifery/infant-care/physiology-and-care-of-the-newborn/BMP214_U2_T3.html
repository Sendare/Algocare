Umbilical Cord Care and Infection Prevention

Anatomy and Normal Physiology of the Newborn Umbilicus

At birth, the human umbilical cord contains three distinct blood vessels: two thick-walled arteries and one large, thin-walled vein. These vessels are surrounded by Wharton's jelly, a specialized mucous connective tissue that prevents compression of the cord in utero. Immediately following birth and the application of a cord clamp, circulation through these vessels ceases.

Over the first few days of life, the remaining cord stump undergoes a natural process of ischemic necrosis, commonly known as mummification or dry gangrene. The tissue dehydrates, turns brown, and eventually black. Separation typically occurs between 5 to 15 days postpartum, leaving a small, healing umbilical wound that epithelizes rapidly. Midwives must understand that a moist, foul-smelling stump or bleeding after separation falls outside normal physiological parameters.

Standard Protocols for Umbilical Cord Care

Standard clinical management of the newborn umbilical cord focuses on keeping the area clean and allowing natural drying to occur. Midwives should instruct mothers to wash their hands thoroughly with soap and water before handling the baby's cord area.

  • Cleanliness: Wash the cord stump gently only when soiled with urine or stool using clean water and mild soap, then dry thoroughly with a clean cloth.
  • Air exposure: Keep the top of the diaper folded below the umbilicus to allow air circulation and speed up drying.
  • Avoid harmful substances: Do not apply traditional concoctions, herbs, toothpaste, or dirty oils to the fresh cord stump, as these introduce harmful bacteria.

Dry Cord Care Versus Topical Antiseptics in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Global health guidelines, including those from the World Health Organization, recommend dry cord care—leaving the cord clean and dry without applying any substance—for newborns born in health facilities with low neonatal mortality rates. However, in community settings across Nigeria where home delivery rates remain significant and neonatal tetanus and omphalitis pose severe threats, the application of 4% chlorhexidine digluconate gel to the umbilical stump is strongly recommended.

Chlorhexidine significantly reduces severe umbilical infections and neonatal mortality in settings with high risk of bacterial contamination. Midwives practising in rural Nigerian primary healthcare clinics must distinguish between urban facility protocols and high-risk community guidelines, ensuring that chlorhexidine is applied once daily during the first week of life where indicated.

Identifying Early Signs of Omphalitis and Cord Infection

Omphalitis is a serious bacterial infection of the umbilical stump and surrounding abdominal wall tissue. Midwives must teach mothers and recognize clinical red flags early, as localized infection can rapidly progress to systemic sepsis and neonatal death.

  • Periumbilical erythema: Redness spreading outward from the base of the cord onto the skin of the abdomen.
  • Purulent discharge: Pus or cloudy, foul-smelling fluid draining from the stump.
  • Local warmth and tenderness: The skin around the umbilicus feels hot to the touch, and the baby cries when the area is pressed.
  • Systemic signs: Fever, lethargy, poor feeding, and sometimes hypothermia or jaundice.

Infection Prevention Strategies in Hospital and Community Settings

Preventing umbilical infections requires strict adherence to infection control principles across all levels of care. In the labour ward and postnatal unit, healthcare providers must practice proper hand hygiene before and after handling any newborn. Instruments used for cutting the umbilical cord must be thoroughly sterilized, and clean delivery kits must be distributed to pregnant women anticipating home births.

Furthermore, health workers must promote hygienic practices during bathing, ensuring that newborns are not immersed in unboiled water until the umbilical stump has completely fallen off and the wound has healed. Community health extension workers and traditional birth attendants must receive continuous training on clean cord cutting and the avoidance of harmful cord applications.

Counseling Mothers on Hygienic Cord Practices at Home

Effective maternal counseling bridges the gap between clinical instruction and home-based newborn care. Midwives should conduct practical demonstrations during antenatal and postnatal education sessions, showing mothers how to fold diapers away from the umbilicus and how to perform gentle cleaning when necessary.

Midwives must also empower mothers to recognize danger signs and seek prompt medical attention rather than relying on home remedies when complications arise. Clear, respectful communication ensures that mothers feel confident in caring for their babies without fear or the pressure of unscientific cultural practices.
