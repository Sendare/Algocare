Eye Care and Prophylaxis in the Newborn

Introduction to Newborn Ophthalmic Health

A newborn's eyes are uniquely vulnerable during the journey through the birth canal. Exposure to maternal micro-organisms such as Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis can lead to severe ocular infections. Without preventative care, these infections may progress rapidly, causing corneal ulceration, scarring, and permanent visual impairment or blindness. Routine ophthalmic prophylaxis is therefore a critical component of immediate newborn care in Nigerian maternity settings, designed to safeguard infant sight right after birth.

Understanding Neonatal Conjunctivitis

Neonatal conjunctivitis, also known as ophthalmia neonatorum, is any eye infection occurring within the first 28 days of life. It presents with redness, swelling of the eyelids, and discharge.

  • Chemical Conjunctivitis: Usually caused by the irritant effect of eye prophylaxis itself. It appears within hours of administration and resolves spontaneously within 24 to 48 hours.
  • Gonococcal Conjunctivitis: Caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae. It typically appears 2 to 5 days after birth, presenting with severe purulent discharge and rapid tissue destruction.
  • Chlamydial Conjunctivitis: Caused by Chlamydia trachomatis. It usually manifests 5 to 14 days postpartum with mild to moderate discharge and eyelid swelling.

Routine Eye Care and Cleansing Practices

Immediate care starts with gentle cleansing of the newborn's eyelids. Using a sterile cotton swab or gauze moistened with sterile water or normal saline, midwives wipe each eye from the inner canthus outward. A fresh swab must be used for each eye to prevent cross-contamination. This procedure removes maternal blood, amniotic fluid, and debris, allowing for a clear assessment of the eye structures and ensuring that subsequent prophylactic medications can make direct contact with the conjunctival membranes.

Ocular Prophylaxis Protocols and Agents

Global and national health guidelines mandate eye prophylaxis for all newborns regardless of the mother's screening status.

  1. Tetracycline 1% Ointment: Widely used due to its broad-spectrum coverage and low irritation profile. Effective against both gonococcal and chlamydial organisms.
  2. Erythromycin 0.5% Ointment: Another safe, well-tolerated option that prevents chlamydial ophthalmia, though slightly less effective against severe gonococcal strains compared to past standards.
  3. Silver Nitrate 1% Solution: Historically significant, but rarely used today because it frequently causes severe chemical conjunctivitis.

Administration Technique for Eye Medications

Proper technique ensures the medication reaches the conjunctival sac where it can work effectively. The midwife holds the infant securely, gently holding the lower lid down to form a small pocket. The tube or container is held without touching the eyelids or lashes to maintain sterility. A thin ribbon of ointment or a single drop of solution is applied along the lower conjunctival sac from the inner canthus to the outer canthus. After application, the midwife closes the eye gently for a few seconds to distribute the medication and wipes away any excess with a clean gauze.

Side Effects and Nursing Management

While essential, eye prophylaxis can cause temporary side effects. Mild inflammation, slight eyelid edema, and temporary blurred vision are common immediately following application. Midwives must monitor the infant closely during the transition period. If heavy discharge, persistent redness, or swelling develops after 24 hours, the midwife must report it immediately for medical evaluation. Parents should be reassured that mild, short-lived blurriness is normal and does not permanently affect their baby's developing vision.

Parental Education and Follow-Up Care

Empowering parents with correct knowledge ensures continued monitoring at home. Midwives should explain why eye prophylaxis is given and what normal side effects to expect. Parents must learn to recognize warning signs such as thick discharge, excessive tearing, or increasing swelling after the first day. Instructions should emphasize good hand hygiene before touching the baby's face. Finally, midwives should remind parents to attend routine postnatal and immunization visits where infant vision screening is routinely performed.
