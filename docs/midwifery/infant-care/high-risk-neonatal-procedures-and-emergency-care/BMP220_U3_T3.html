Management of Hypothermia

Understanding Newborn Temperature Regulation

Newborn babies lose heat four times faster than adults do. Their bodies have a large surface area compared to their small weight, and their skin is very thin with blood vessels close to the surface. Furthermore, newborns cannot shiver to generate warmth because their nervous systems are still immature. Instead, they rely on a special type of fat called brown adipose tissue, often called brown fat, which sits around the neck, chest, and between the shoulder blades. When a baby gets cold, hormones trigger this fat to burn and produce heat, a process called non-shivering thermogenesis.

Heat leaves the baby's body through four main routes:

  • Conduction: Direct transfer of heat when the baby touches a cold surface, like an unheated scale or a cold metal table.
  • Convection: Heat loss to moving air currents, such as an open window or an air conditioner blowing directly on the cot.
  • Radiation: Transfer of heat to cooler nearby objects or walls without direct contact, like a cold exterior wall.
  • Evaporation: Heat loss when amniotic fluid or bathwater turns to gas on the wet skin.

Cold Stress Pathophysiology

When a newborn's core temperature drops slightly below normal, entering the cold stress range of 36.0°C to 36.4°C, the body immediately works overtime to stay warm. To keep organs functioning, the baby increases metabolic activity. This high demand for energy causes the baby to burn through available glucose rapidly, leading directly to hypoglycemia.

As the body struggles to maintain warmth, blood vessels in the skin constrict to redirect warm blood toward the brain and heart. This peripheral vasoconstriction makes the skin pale or mottled. At the same time, the baby breathes faster to take in more oxygen for this extra energy burn. However, if the cold continues, the body runs out of oxygen, causing severe hypoxia, metabolic acidosis, and respiratory distress. In premature or very small babies, this extra stress can also trigger bleeding inside the brain.

Recognizing Signs of Hypothermia in the Neonate

Detecting low body temperature early stops minor chilling from turning into a life-threatening emergency. Midwives must assess both the physical appearance and behavior of the newborn during routine checks. Hypothermia is classified by core temperature into mild (36.0°C - 36.4°C), moderate (32.0°C - 35.9°C), and severe (below 32.0°C).

Signs to look out for include:

  • Skin changes: Cool to the touch on the abdomen and trunk, pale, mottled, or bright red in early cold stress.
  • Behavioral shifts: Lethargy, weak cry, poor sucking reflex, and floppiness.
  • Respiratory signs: Grunting, nasal flaring, fast breathing, or periods of not breathing.
  • Metabolic signs: Low blood sugar and slow heart rate.

Temperature Monitoring Protocols

Regular temperature checks form the backbone of safe neonatal care. Midwives use low-reading clinical thermometers placed in the middle of the baby's axilla (armpit) for at least three minutes, ensuring the bulb touches dry skin and is not trapped against clothing. Rectal temperatures are generally avoided because they risk tearing the delicate rectal wall.

Monitoring frequency depends on the baby's condition and environment:

  • Healthy term newborns: Checked at birth, every thirty minutes until stable, then once every shift.
  • Preterm or sick newborns: Checked every two to four hours in an incubator or warmer.
  • During rewarming: Checked every fifteen to thirty minutes until the temperature normalizes.

Rewarming Techniques for Mild and Moderate Hypothermia

For babies with mild to moderate hypothermia without severe complications, slow and steady rewarming is the safest approach. Rapid warming can cause sudden low blood pressure and apnea.

The gold standard for mild hypothermia is Kangaroo Mother Care (KMC). Placing the naked baby chest-to-chest between the mother's breasts, covered with a warm blanket and a cap, uses the mother's body heat to stabilize the baby naturally. For babies in incubators, the air temperature should be increased slowly by 0.5°C to 1°C every hour until the baby's core temperature reaches 36.5°C. Hot water bottles or improvised heating devices should never touch the baby's skin directly because they easily cause severe burns.

Emergency Management of Severe Hypothermia

Severe hypothermia, where the core temperature drops below 32.0°C, is a medical emergency that requires immediate intervention in a neonatal intensive care unit. At this stage, the baby's organs begin to shut down, and the heart rhythm can become dangerously irregular.

Management StepAction
ResuscitationCheck airway, breathing, and circulation; provide supplemental oxygen if needed.
Active WarmingUse pre-warmed radiant warmers or incubators, adjusting the temperature gradually.
Blood Sugar CheckTest blood glucose immediately, as severe cold almost always drains glucose reserves, and start IV dextrose if low.
Infection ScreenDraw blood cultures and start prescribed antibiotics, since severe hypothermia often masks or mimics serious bacterial sepsis.

Preventive Strategies in the Labor Ward and Nursery

Preventing heat loss is much easier and safer than treating established hypothermia. The delivery room should be warm, draft-free, and kept at a minimum temperature of 25°C. Midwives must follow the warm chain principles from the moment of birth.

The warm chain involves ten interconnected steps:

  1. Provide a warm delivery room.
  2. Dry the baby immediately at birth with a clean, pre-warmed towel.
  3. Remove wet towels and wrap the baby in a dry, warm blanket.
  4. Initiate skin-to-skin contact with the mother.
  5. Encourage early breastfeeding.
  6. Delay routine bathing until the baby's temperature is fully stable for at least 24 hours.
  7. Keep appropriate clothing and a cap on the baby.
  8. Ensure mother and baby stay together without unnecessary separation.
  9. Transport babies in warm, dedicated transport incubators if movement is necessary.
  10. Train all healthcare staff on thermal protection protocols.
