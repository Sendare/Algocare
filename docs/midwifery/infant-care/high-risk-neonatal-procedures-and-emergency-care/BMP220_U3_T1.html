Assisting with Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) Procedures

Introduction to the Midwife's Role in NICU Procedures

Midwives working in specialized care settings often participate in emergency stabilization and invasive procedures for compromised newborns. While primary midwifery practice focuses on normal pregnancy and childbirth, an understanding of neonatal intensive care is vital for timely intervention during birth asphyxia or severe prematurity. The midwife acts as a crucial link between the obstetric team and the neonatologist, preparing equipment, positioning the infant, and maintaining a sterile field.

  • Pre-procedural assessment: Quick evaluation of the newborn's vital signs and respiratory effort.
  • Equipment preparation: Gathering specialized supplies like umbilical catheterization trays or endotracheal tubes before the medical team arrives.
  • Thermal regulation: Ensuring the radiant warmer is active to prevent rapid neonatal hypothermia during interventions.
  • Family communication: Providing calm, clear explanations to anxious parents while procedures are underway.

Preparing and Setting Up Monitoring Equipment

Continuous physiological monitoring is mandatory for any infant undergoing invasive procedures in the NICU. Midwives must ensure that all electronic monitors are calibrated, functional, and properly connected before the clinician begins.

Electrodes for electrocardiography must be placed carefully on the chest, avoiding the liver area and lead placement sites that interfere with chest compressions if resuscitation becomes necessary. Pulse oximetry sensors are typically wrapped around the right wrist or palm, providing pre-ductal oxygen saturation readings that reflect the oxygen reaching the brain and heart. Temperature probes require secure placement without exerting excessive pressure on delicate newborn skin. Regular checks of alarm limits prevent both false alarms and missed critical changes in heart rate or oxygen saturation.

Assisting with Vascular Access in the Critically Ill Neonate

Establishing reliable vascular access is essential for administering fluids, blood products, and medications. In emergency situations, peripheral veins can collapse rapidly, making alternative routes necessary.

  • Peripheral Intravenous (PIV) lines: Placed in scalp, hand, or foot veins for short-term fluid delivery.
  • Umbilical Venous Catheter (UVC): Inserted through the umbilical vein into the inferior vena cava for central access and emergency medications.
  • Umbilical Arterial Catheter (UAC): Inserted through one of the two umbilical arteries for continuous blood pressure monitoring and frequent arterial blood gas sampling.

The midwife assists by maintaining the sterile field, handing sterile instruments to the operator, securing the catheter once placed, and observing for signs of vascular compromise or bleeding in surrounding tissues.

Supporting Neonatal Intubation and Airway Management

Endotracheal intubation is performed to secure an airway, provide mechanical ventilation, or administer emergency intratracheal medications. The procedure requires precision, speed, and absolute teamwork between the clinician and the assisting midwife.

Before the procedure starts, the midwife prepares the laryngoscope with a working light source, selects the correct blade size (such as Miller 0 or 1 for term and preterm infants), and tests the suction apparatus. During insertion, the midwife may be asked to apply gentle cricoid pressure or monitor the infant's heart rate via the cardiac monitor. Bradycardia is a common sign of vagal stimulation or hypoxia during intubation attempts; if heart rate drops significantly, the procedure must be paused to ventilate the infant with a bag-valve-mask device.

Assisting with Chest Tube Insertion

A pneumothorax or pleural effusion can cause rapid respiratory decompensation in a neonate receiving respiratory support. Emergency chest tube insertion or needle aspiration decompresses the chest cavity.

The midwife helps position the infant, applies local antiseptic solutions, and prepares the underwater seal drainage system. Strict adherence to sterile technique is paramount because the pleural space is highly vulnerable to infection. Following insertion, the midwife monitors the drainage system for continuous bubbling (indicating an air leak) or fluid fluctuation, and ensures the tubing remains free of kinks or dependent loops that block drainage.

Infection Control and Patient Safety During Invasive Procedures

Newborn infants have immature immune systems, making them exceptionally vulnerable to nosocomial infections during invasive procedures. Midwives enforce strict infection control bundles to safeguard patient health.

  • Hand hygiene: Rigorous scrubbing with antimicrobial soap before touching equipment or the infant.
  • Maximum sterile barriers: Wearing sterile gowns, masks, caps, and sterile gloves, alongside using large sterile drapes.
  • Skin antisepsis: Using approved antiseptic solutions (such as chlorhexidine gluconate, with careful attention to avoiding chemical burns or systemic absorption in very low birth weight infants).
  • Sharps safety: Safely disposing of needles and scalpel blades immediately into puncture-resistant containers without recapping.

Post-Procedure Neonatal Care and Continuous Assessment

Once an invasive procedure concludes, the midwife's responsibilities shift toward stabilization, comfort, and ongoing observation. Immediate tasks include securing all lines, documenting the procedure accurately in the clinical record, and disposing of waste safely.

The midwife monitors vital signs every fifteen minutes initially, then hourly once stable. Pain assessment is critical; non-pharmacological comfort measures such as swaddling, non-nutritive sucking, or measured doses of sweet-tasting sucrose solutions are administered where indicated. Any sudden deterioration in color, perfusion, or respiratory effort must be reported immediately to the attending physician.
