Fluid and Electrolyte Management in Sick Infants

Physiological Principles of Neonatal Body Water and Solutes

Newborn babies, especially preterm infants, have a remarkably high total body water content, often reaching up to 80% or 90% of their body weight. Most of this water is extracellular, meaning it sits outside the cells, which makes sick infants very vulnerable to rapid dehydration and fluid overload. Shortly after birth, healthy term and preterm infants undergo a normal physiological contraction of extracellular fluid. This transition results in a natural weight loss of about 5% to 10% in term infants and up to 15% in premature infants during the first few days of life. Midwives and neonatal nurses must recognize that this early weight loss is normal and not an indication to aggressively force fluids. Total body water decreases gradually with gestational age and postnatal maturity, shifting the balance of intracellular and extracellular compartments as the infant grows.

Calculations of Maintenance Fluids

Calculating daily fluid requirements for sick neonates requires precise attention to birth weight, postnatal age, and insensible water losses. Insensible water loss occurs through the skin and respiratory tract and can be drastically increased by radiant warmers, phototherapy, and respiratory distress.

  • Day 1 of Life: Term infants usually require 60 to 80 mL/kg/day, while extremely low birth weight infants may need 80 to 100 mL/kg/day due to high skin permeability.
  • Subsequent Days: Fluid volume is typically increased by 10 to 20 mL/kg/day up to a maximum of 150 to 170 mL/kg/day.
  • Insensible Loss Factors: Phototherapy and radiant warmers add an extra 10 to 20 mL/kg/day to the baseline fluid calculation.

Electrolyte Balance and Sodium Management

Electrolytes maintain osmotic pressure and cellular function in newborn infants. Sodium is the primary extracellular cation determining extracellular fluid volume. During the first 24 to 48 hours of life, routine sodium administration is usually avoided in maintenance fluids because babies retain sodium and water initially. Once the physiological diuresis begins, sodium and potassium are added to intravenous solutions, typically around 2 to 3 mEq/kg/day each, depending on serum blood levels and urine output. Potassium should never be added to intravenous fluids until the infant has established adequate urine flow to prevent dangerous hyperkalemia.

Addressing Abnormal Fluid Losses and Shifts

Sick neonates frequently experience pathological fluid losses that go far beyond standard maintenance calculations. Conditions like necrotizing enterocolitis, massive third-space losses, surgical drainage, persistent vomiting, or severe diarrhea cause rapid depletion of intravascular volume. These abnormal losses must be replaced volume-for-volume, often using isotonic crystalloids such as normal saline. Midwives must track drainage from nasogastric tubes and stomas meticulously, replacing the exact volume lost over specified intervals to prevent hypovolemic shock. Conversely, conditions like oliguric renal failure or sepsis can cause acute fluid retention and generalized edema, requiring strict fluid restriction.

Monitoring Hydration Status and Urine Output

Accurate clinical assessment is the cornerstone of safe fluid therapy in the neonatal unit. Urine output is the single best bedside indicator of renal perfusion and hydration status. Normal urine output for a stable neonate ranges between 1 and 3 mL/kg/hour. An output dropping below 1 mL/kg/hour indicates oliguria, which signals dehydration, poor cardiac output, or acute kidney injury. Beyond urine measurement, clinicians rely on daily weight checks, capillary refill time, fontanelle tension, skin turgor, and mucous membrane moisture to evaluate fluid balance. Sudden weight gain without nutritional justification points directly to fluid retention.

Recognizing and Managing Complications of Infusion Therapy

Intravenous infusion therapy carries inherent risks, particularly in infants with fragile blood vessels and immature organ systems. Infiltration and extravasation occur when intravenous fluids leak into the surrounding subcutaneous tissue, causing painful swelling, tissue ischemia, and potential skin necrosis if the fluid contains vesicant medications. Nurses must inspect infusion sites hourly. Fluid overload can lead to congestive heart failure, pulmonary edema, and patent ductus arteriosus reopening. Electrolyte imbalances like hyponatremia from excessive free water or hyperglycemia from rapid glucose infusion are also common hazards that require constant laboratory surveillance.
