Management of Birth Asphyxia in Newborns

Understanding Birth Asphyxia and Immediate Recognition

Birth asphyxia occurs when a newborn fails to establish and maintain normal respiration immediately after birth, leading to progressive hypoxia, hypercapnia, and metabolic acidosis. In Nigerian delivery suites, recognizing this emergency quickly saves lives. Midwives must assess the newborn immediately using the Apgar scoring system at one and five minutes of life.

  • Primary Apnea: The infant stops breathing briefly after a period of distress, heart rate begins to fall, and stimulation or oxygen usually helps restart breathing.
  • Secondary Apnea: If hypoxia continues, the infant enters a deep sleep-like state where stimulation fails, blood pressure drops, and positive pressure ventilation is urgently required to save the baby.

Always check for a limp muscle tone, absent or gasping breathing, and a slow heart rate below 100 beats per minute right after delivery.

Neonatal Resuscitation Protocols

When a baby fails to breathe at birth, every second counts. Midwives must follow the structured steps of the newborn resuscitation algorithm, focusing heavily on establishing effective ventilation rather than spending excessive time giving chest compressions.

  1. Initial Steps: Provide a warm environment, dry the infant thoroughly, clear the airway if secretions block it, and gently stimulate the baby.
  2. Evaluation: Assess breathing, heart rate, and color within the golden first minute.
  3. Ventilation: If the baby is gasping, apneic, or has a heart rate below 100 beats per minute, initiate positive pressure ventilation using a self-inflating bag and mask with room air.
  4. Chest Compressions: Add chest compressions coordinated with ventilation only if the heart rate remains below 60 beats per minute despite 30 seconds of effective ventilation.

A common mistake in busy labor wards is delaying ventilation to administer drugs; adequate oxygenation through ventilation remains the absolute priority.

Supportive Management: Maintaining Thermoregulation and Glycemic Control

An asphyxiated newborn struggles to maintain normal body temperature and blood sugar levels, both of which worsen brain injury if neglected. Hypothermia increases oxygen consumption and metabolic acidosis, counteracting resuscitation efforts.

  • Thermoregulation: Place the baby under a pre-warmed radiant warmer, dry completely, remove wet linen, and use skin-to-skin contact once stable. Avoid cold drafts in the delivery room.
  • Glycemic Control: Severe stress depletes glycogen stores rapidly, leading to hypoglycemia. Test blood glucose early and frequently. Provide early enteral feeding with expressed breast milk or initiate intravenous glucose infusions if oral feeding is unsafe due to respiratory distress.

Keeping the neonate warm and normoglycemic protects the delicate central nervous system from secondary cellular damage during recovery.

Monitoring for Hypoxic-Ischemic Encephalopathy

Following birth asphyxia, brain tissue may suffer delayed injury known as hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). Midwives must monitor the baby closely over the first 72 hours of life for neurological changes.

HIE StageClinical SignsSeverity
Stage 1 (Mild)Hyperalert, jittery, normal muscle tone, overactive reflexesMild, usually recovers well
Stage 2 (Moderate)Lethargic, hypotonia, weak suck reflex, possible seizuresModerate, requires close monitoring
Stage 3 (Severe)Stupor, flaccid, absent reflexes, prolonged seizures or comaSevere, high risk of permanent damage

Watch carefully for subtle seizures such as eye-rolling, tongue-sucking, or bicycling movements of the legs, which are easy to miss in a busy nursery.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Ongoing Observation

Once the initial crisis passes and the baby breathes independently, comprehensive post-resuscitation care begins. This phase focuses on stabilizing vital organ systems to prevent multi-organ failure common after severe oxygen deprivation.

Monitor vital signs every 15 minutes for the first hour, then hourly until stable. Pay close attention to urine output, as renal impairment frequently follows asphyxia; a normal output is at least 1 to 2 milliliters per kilogram per hour. Keep intravenous fluids regulated accurately to prevent fluid overload, which can cause pulmonary edema or worsen brain swelling.

Document all resuscitation events, exact times of interventions, and the baby's response in the clinical record clearly and promptly.

Complications and Indicators for Neonatal Intensive Care Referral

Not all asphyxiated newborns can be managed at primary or secondary health facilities. Midwives must identify warning signs that demand urgent transfer to a specialized neonatal intensive care unit.

  • Persistent Seizures: Seizures that do not stop with first-line anticonvulsant therapy like phenobarbital.
  • Respiratory Failure: Continued need for high oxygen concentrations or mechanical ventilation.
  • Multi-Organ Dysfunction: Evidence of kidney failure, cardiac dysfunction, or severe coagulation disorders leading to bleeding.
  • Moderate to Severe HIE: Infants classified under Stage 2 or Stage 3 encephalopathy who may benefit from advanced neuroprotective care.

Stabilize the infant's airway, breathing, and circulation before arranging transport, and ensure a healthcare provider accompanies the baby with adequate thermal support.
