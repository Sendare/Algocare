Cardiac Cycle, Cardiac Output and Regulation

Introduction to Cardiovascular Mechanics

The human heart operates as a twin muscular pump, driving blood through the pulmonary and systemic circuits. Understanding this mechanical performance requires looking at how pressure gradients dictate blood flow. Blood moves passively from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure, and the heart valves ensure this flow remains strictly unidirectional, preventing backflow.

  • Right side: Manages the pulmonary circulation, pumping deoxygenated blood from the body to the lungs for gas exchange.
  • Left side: Drives the systemic circulation, pushing oxygen-rich blood out through the aorta to nourish all body tissues.

Myocardial fibers possess unique properties, including automaticity and rhythmicity, allowing them to generate electrical impulses and contract spontaneously without external nerve stimulation.

Understanding the Cardiac Cycle

The cardiac cycle encompasses all the mechanical and electrical events occurring during a single heartbeat. It is divided into two primary phases: systole, the period of contraction and emptying, and diastole, the period of relaxation and filling. At a normal resting heart rate of about 72 beats per minute, a single cycle lasts roughly 0.8 seconds. Interestingly, the heart spends more than half of this time in diastole, which is vital because coronary artery perfusion occurs primarily when the ventricles are relaxed.

Phases of Mechanical Activity in the Heart

A single heartbeat moves through distinct mechanical stages that coordinate valve action and chamber volume changes.

  1. Atrial systole: The atria contract to top off ventricular filling, forcing the remaining blood through the open atrioventricular valves.
  2. Isovolumetric contraction: The ventricles begin to contract, causing pressure to rise sharply and snap the AV valves shut, creating the first heart sound. All valves are temporarily closed.
  3. Ventricular ejection: Pressure in the ventricles exceeds aortic and pulmonary pressure, pushing the semilunar valves open so blood surges into the great vessels.
  4. Isovolumetric relaxation: Ventricular muscle relaxes, pressure drops below arterial pressure, and the semilunar valves snap shut, producing the second heart sound.

Defining and Calculating Cardiac Output

Cardiac output is the total volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per minute. It serves as the primary indicator of cardiovascular efficiency and tissue perfusion. Calculating this value requires two variables: heart rate, the number of beats per minute, and stroke volume, the amount of blood ejected per beat.

$$\text{Cardiac Output} (CO) = \text{Heart Rate} (HR) \times \text{Stroke Volume} (SV)$|

For a healthy adult at rest, a typical heart rate of 72 beats per minute and a stroke volume of about 70 milliliters yield a cardiac output of roughly 5 liters per minute. The body can alter this output dramatically during exercise or stress.

Factors Influencing Stroke Volume

Stroke volume is governed by three critical physiological factors: preload, afterload, and myocardial contractility.

  • Preload: The degree of stretch on the ventricular muscle fibers just before they contract. According to Frank-Starling's law of the heart, the more the cardiac muscle is stretched during filling, the stronger the subsequent contraction will be.
  • Afterload: The resistance the ventricles must overcome to push blood out into the systemic and pulmonary arteries. Conditions like chronic hypertension increase afterload, making the heart work harder.
  • Contractility: The inherent strength of the heart muscle's contraction independently of stretch. Positive inotropic agents, such as adrenaline or digitalis, increase contractility.

Physiological Regulation of Cardiac Function

The body constantly adjusts cardiac output to meet metabolic demands through neural and hormonal controls. The autonomic nervous system plays a dominant role here. The sympathetic nervous system releases noradrenaline, increasing both heart rate and contractility. Conversely, the parasympathetic nervous system via the vagus nerve acts through acetylcholine to slow down the resting heart rate.

Reflexes also fine-tune this control. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinuses and aortic arch monitor blood pressure changes, sending signals to the cardiac control center in the medulla oblongata to adjust autonomic output instantly.

Clinical Implications for Midwifery Practice

During pregnancy, maternal blood volume increases by nearly 40 to 50 percent to support the developing fetus. This physiological adaptation causes a proportional rise in cardiac output and stroke volume. Midwives must understand these normal hemodynamic shifts to differentiate them from pathological conditions like pre-eclampsia or peripartum cardiomyopathy. Monitoring pulse, blood pressure, and recognizing signs of maternal fatigue or fluid overload are vital assessment skills during both antenatal visits and labor management.
