Plasma

Introduction to Blood Plasma

Blood plasma is the pale yellow, liquid component of blood that normally holds the blood cells in whole blood in suspension. It makes up about 55% of the total body blood volume. Plasma is essentially an aqueous solution containing 92% water, 7% vital proteins, and 1% other solutes such as dissolved nutrients, waste products, and gases. When healthcare workers centrifuge a whole blood sample mixed with an anticoagulant, the heavier red blood cells settle to the bottom, leaving the clear plasma layer on top. Understanding this fluid base is essential for midwives managing maternal blood volume expansion during pregnancy.

Water Content and Fluid Balance

Water acts as the primary dissolving and suspending medium in plasma, accounting for about 92% of its total composition. This high water content allows blood to flow smoothly through complex vascular networks without creating excessive friction. Plasma acts as a mobile reservoir that constantly exchanges fluid with interstitial spaces to maintain proper hydration and tissue perfusion. In pregnant women, total body water and plasma volume increase dramatically to support the growing placental unit, which directly impacts maternal blood pressure regulation.

Plasma Proteins: Albumin, Globulins, and Fibrinogen

Plasma proteins are critical macromolecules synthesized mainly by the liver. They are divided into three major functional categories:

  • Albumin: The most abundant plasma protein, responsible for maintaining colloid osmotic pressure, which pulls fluid back into capillaries and prevents tissue swelling.
  • Globulins: Comprises alpha, beta, and gamma variants; alpha and beta transport lipids and fat-soluble vitamins, while gamma globulins act as antibodies supporting the immune system.
  • Fibrinogen: An essential soluble clotting factor that converts into insoluble fibrin strands during tissue injury to form a stable blood clot.

Electrolytes and Nutrient Transport

Plasma serves as the delivery highway for essential body salts and digested food molecules. Dissolved electrolytes—such as sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, chloride, and bicarbonate—maintain acid-base balance, nerve conduction, and muscle contraction. Alongside minerals, plasma transports glucose, amino acids, vitamins, and lipids absorbed from the digestive tract directly to hungry body tissues. Maintaining stable electrolyte levels in plasma is vital, as minor shifts can trigger cardiac arrhythmias or neurological disturbances in vulnerable patients.

Waste Removal and Respiratory Gas Transport

Beyond delivering nutrients, plasma acts as a cleanup system by carrying metabolic waste products away from active cells to excretory organs. Waste substances like urea, uric acid, creatinine, and breakdown products of bilirubin travel dissolved in plasma until filtered out by the kidneys or processed by the liver. Additionally, plasma plays a supportive role in respiratory gas exchange. While most oxygen attaches to hemoglobin inside red blood cells, a small fraction dissolves directly in plasma. Conversely, a significant amount of carbon dioxide travels through plasma as dissolved gas or converted bicarbonate ions on its way back to the lungs.

Clinical Significance in Nursing and Midwifery Practice

Clinical evaluation of plasma components guides critical medical decisions during labor, postpartum hemorrhage, and shock management. Infusions of fresh frozen plasma or specific plasma derivatives help restore clotting factors in women experiencing severe postpartum bleeding. Monitoring plasma electrolyte panels alerts clinicians to dangerous conditions such as pre-eclampsia or severe dehydration. Midwives must recognize how alterations in plasma volume and protein concentrations directly affect blood pressure, medication dosing, and fluid resuscitation strategies.
