Capillaries

Anatomy and Microscopic Structure of Capillaries

Capillaries are the tiniest blood vessels in the human body, forming the vital connection between arterioles and venules. Because their walls are only one cell layer thick, consisting of a simple squamous endothelium resting on a thin basal lamina, they are uniquely suited for rapid substance exchange. Red blood cells must often travel through these narrow vessels in single file, which maximizes their exposure to the surrounding tissue fluids. Lacking the thick tunica media and tunica externa found in arteries and veins, capillaries prioritize diffusion efficiency over structural strength.

Continuous, Fenestrated, and Sinusoidal: The Three Types of Capillaries

Not all capillaries are built the same way. Their structure matches the functional needs of the specific organ they supply.

  • Continuous capillaries: Have an uninterrupted endothelial lining with tight junctions, found in muscle, skin, and the blood-brain barrier.
  • Fenestrated capillaries: Contain small pores or windows (fenestrations) in the endothelial cells, allowing rapid filtration in sites like the kidneys and small intestine.
  • Sinusoidal capillaries: Feature large gaps, incomplete basement membranes, and giant clefts, permitting the passage of whole cells in organs like the liver, spleen, and bone marrow.

Microcirculation and Precapillary Sphincters

Blood flow through a capillary bed is not constant; it is meticulously regulated by local tissue needs. Metarterioles act as thoroughfare channels that connect arterioles directly to venules. Branching off these channels are true capillaries guarded by rings of smooth muscle known as precapillary sphincters. When the surrounding tissues require oxygen and nutrients, these sphincters relax, opening the capillary bed to perfusion. Conversely, when metabolic demand drops, the sphincters contract, shunting blood straight from the arterial side to the venous side without entering the delicate exchange beds.

Mechanisms of Exchange: Diffusion, Filtration, and Osmosis

Substances move across the capillary wall through distinct physical processes. Diffusion is the primary mechanism, moving lipid-soluble gases like oxygen and carbon dioxide straight through endothelial cell membranes, while water-soluble solutes pass through intercellular clefts or pores. Filtration is driven by capillary blood pressure, pushing water and small molecules out of the vessel into the interstitial space. Osmosis draws fluid back into the capillary as plasma proteins create a pulling pressure that counteracts outward hydrostatic forces.

Fluid Balance and Starling's Forces in the Capillary Bed

The movement of fluid across the capillary wall is governed by Starling's forces, which balance hydrostatic and osmotic pressures. On the arteriolar end of the capillary, outward forces exceed inward forces, causing net fluid filtration into the tissues. On the venular end, blood pressure drops significantly while plasma protein concentration remains high, creating a net inward force that pulls most of the fluid back into the circulation. Any leftover interstitial fluid is safely drained away by the lymphatic system to prevent swelling.

Clinical Correlates: Edema, Shock, and Vascular Permeability

Disruptions in normal capillary dynamics lead to distinct clinical pathologies. Edema develops when fluid filters out faster than it can be reabsorbed or drained by lymphatics, often caused by high venous pressure, starvation, or damaged vessels. In conditions like severe inflammation or allergic reactions, chemical mediators increase vascular permeability, allowing large proteins to leak into tissues and drag water along with them. If capillary beds fail to maintain adequate perfusion due to systemic blood pressure collapse, the patient enters shock, starving vital organs of oxygen.
