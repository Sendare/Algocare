Anatomy and Structure of the Heart, Heart Wall and Chambers

General Anatomy and Location of the Heart

The human heart is a remarkably powerful, hollow, muscular organ located snugly within the middle mediastinum of the thoracic cavity. It sits directly behind the sternum, resting obliquely between the lungs with its broad base directed upward and backward, while its pointed apex tilts downward and forward toward the left hip, typically striking the chest wall at the fifth intercostal space. Enclosing this vital pump is the pericardium, a sturdy double-layered fibro-serous sac that anchors the heart to surrounding structures, prevents overfilling with blood, and reduces friction as the organ beats continuously.

A common misconception among junior students is that the heart sits entirely on the left side of the chest; in reality, roughly two-thirds of its mass lies slightly to the left of the midline, with the remainder positioned centrally. The outer fibrous layer protects the heart, while the inner serous layer secretes a tiny amount of lubricating fluid into the pericardial cavity. Without this smooth lubrication, the constant motion of beating against the moist pleural membranes of the lungs would cause painful irritation with every single cardiac cycle.

The Three Layers of the Heart Wall

The wall of the heart is built from three distinct tissue layers, each serving a specialized structural and functional purpose.

  • Epicardium: The outermost layer, also known as the visceral layer of the serous pericardium, consists of a delicate epithelial membrane mixed with adipose tissue that cushions the blood vessels supplying the heart.
  • Myocardium: The middle and thickest layer, composed of specialized cardiac muscle tissue arranged in spiral bundles. This layer does the actual heavy lifting, generating the powerful rhythmic contractions that push blood through the circulatory system.
  • Endocardium: The innermost shiny lining made of simple squamous epithelium. It provides an ultra-smooth surface for blood to flow across without clotting, covering the heart chambers and extending seamlessly over the valves.

Midwives should note that infections can target these layers independently. For instance, inflammation of the inner lining is known medically as endocarditis, a serious condition that can permanently damage the delicate heart valves.

Interior Chambers: Atria and Ventricles

Internally, the heart is divided longitudinally by muscular partitions into four distinct chambers: two superior atria and two inferior ventricles.

The upper chambers act mainly as low-pressure receiving reservoirs, filling with blood returning from the veins and gently squeezing it down into the lower chambers. The ventricles, by contrast, are high-pressure discharge pumps. Because the left ventricle must push blood all the way around the entire systemic circuit through every organ in the body, its muscular wall is roughly three times thicker than that of the right ventricle.

Valves of the Heart and Direction of Blood Flow

To ensure that blood flows in only one direction and never leaks backward, the heart contains four one-way valves. These valves open and close passively in response to pressure changes created as the chambers contract and relax.

  1. Tricuspid Valve: Located between the right atrium and right ventricle, featuring three flexible cusps.
  2. Mitral (Bicuspid) Valve: Located between the left atrium and left ventricle, featuring two strong cusps.
  3. Aortic Semilunar Valve: Positioned at the exit of the left ventricle leading into the aorta.
  4. Pulmonary Semilunar Valve: Positioned at the exit of the right ventricle leading into the pulmonary trunk.

The atrioventricular (tricuspid and mitral) valves are anchored tightly to muscular projections inside the ventricles called papillary muscles by tough, fibrous strings known as chordae tendineae. When the ventricles contract, these strings pull taut to prevent the valve flaps from blowing backward into the atria under high pressure. Semilunar valves, shaped like little half-moons, catch backward-flowing blood after contraction and snap shut tightly.

Coronary Circulation and Blood Supply to the Myocardium

Although the heart chambers are constantly filled with blood, the thick muscular walls of the myocardium are far too dense to absorb nutrients directly from that internal flow. Instead, the heart relies on its own dedicated vascular network known as the coronary circulation.

Arterial blood supply originates from the base of the aorta right above the aortic valve via the left and right coronary arteries. These vessels branch extensively across the epicardium to deliver oxygen and glucose to every active muscle cell. After the tissue extracts what it needs, deoxygenated blood is collected by cardiac veins that merge into the coronary sinus, a large vascular channel that empties directly back into the right atrium.

When a coronary artery becomes blocked by a blood clot or atherosclerotic plaque, the downstream myocardial tissue is deprived of oxygen, leading to ischemia, chest pain known as angina, or tissue death called a myocardial infarction.
