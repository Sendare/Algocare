Blood Pressure Regulation and Factors Affecting Blood Pressure

Introduction to Blood Pressure in Clinical Practice

Blood pressure is the force exerted by circulating blood against the walls of the body's arteries. In clinical midwifery, measuring this force provides a vital window into the cardiovascular health of both pregnant and non-pregnant women. The measurement yields two values: systolic pressure, representing the arterial pressure when the heart beats and pushes blood out, and diastolic pressure, representing the pressure in the arteries when the heart rests between beats.

Recording these values accurately helps midwives detect cardiovascular stress, fluid retention issues, and hypertensive disorders unique to pregnancy, such as pre-eclampsia. Understanding these baseline definitions is the first step toward interpreting abnormal readings during antenatal and postnatal care.

Primary Factors Affecting Blood Pressure

Blood pressure does not stay fixed; it rises and falls depending on several underlying physiological components. The main determinants include:

  • Cardiac Output: The total volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute. If cardiac output increases, blood pressure naturally rises.
  • Peripheral Resistance: The friction and resistance blood encounters as it flows through narrow blood vessels. Constricted vessels increase resistance and elevate pressure.
  • Blood Volume: The total amount of fluid circulating within the vascular system. Severe dehydration lowers volume and pressure, whereas fluid overload increases both.
  • Blood Viscosity: The thickness of the blood. Thicker blood flows more sluggishly and requires higher pressure to move through the body.

Neural Mechanisms of Blood Pressure Regulation

The nervous system provides rapid, minute-to-minute adjustments to keep blood pressure stable through the action of specialized sensors called baroreceptors. Located in the carotid sinuses and the aortic arch, these stretch receptors constantly monitor the stretching of arterial walls caused by blood flow.

When blood pressure drops, baroreceptors send fewer signals to the brain's cardiovascular center. The brain responds by ramping up sympathetic nervous system activity, causing the heart rate to speed up and blood vessels to tighten. Conversely, if pressure climbs too high, increased signals prompt the brain to slow the heart rate and dilate blood vessels, bringing the pressure back down.

Hormonal and Renal Control via the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System

While neural controls act instantly, the kidneys provide long-term regulation through a hormonal cascade known as the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS). When the kidneys detect low blood pressure or reduced blood flow, they release an enzyme called renin into the bloodstream.

Renin converts angiotensinogen (produced by the liver) into angiotensin I. An enzyme in the lungs then converts angiotensin I into angiotensin II, a powerful vasoconstrictor that rapidly raises blood pressure. Additionally, angiotensin II stimulates the adrenal glands to release aldosterone, a hormone that tells the kidneys to reabsorb more sodium and water, increasing total blood volume and sustaining long-term pressure stability.

Nursing Assessment and Measurement Techniques in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Accurate blood pressure measurement is a fundamental clinical skill in Nigerian hospitals and primary health clinics. Midwives must ensure proper technique to avoid false readings that could lead to misdiagnosis. The patient should rest quietly for at least five minutes before the assessment.

Select a cuff of the correct size: one that encircles about eighty percent of the upper arm. A cuff that is too small will produce a falsely high reading, while one that is too large may give a falsely low reading. Inflate the cuff until the pulse disappears, then slowly deflate it while listening with a stethoscope over the brachial artery for the Korotkoff sounds. Regular calibration of aneroid sphygmomanometers and proper maintenance of digital devices are essential in resource-constrained environments to ensure ongoing reliability.
