ABO System

Introduction to the ABO System

The ABO blood group system is the most vital blood type classification system in human medicine. Discovered by Karl Landsteiner in the early 20th century, it explains why certain blood transfusions succeed while others trigger fatal reactions. This system relies on specific inherited markers present on the surface of red blood cells and matching proteins circulating in the liquid portion of the blood. Understanding this framework prevents catastrophic immune responses during medical emergencies, surgeries, and obstetric care across Nigerian hospitals.

Antigens and Antibodies: The Core Mechanism

The foundation of the ABO system rests on two biological components: antigens and antibodies.

  • Antigens (Agglutinogens): Chemical structures located on the outer membrane of red blood cells. Group A cells carry A antigens, Group B cells carry B antigens, AB cells carry both, and Group O cells carry neither.
  • Antibodies (Agglutinins): Natural proteins floating in the blood plasma. A person's plasma contains antibodies against the antigens their own red blood cells lack. For example, a person with Group A blood has anti-B antibodies.

When a foreign antigen enters the bloodstream—such as giving Type B blood to a Type A recipient—the matching antibodies immediately bind to those foreign cells, causing agglutination (clumping) and subsequent destruction of the red blood cells.

Blood Group Phenotypes and Genotypes

A person's blood type is inherited from their parents according to classical Mendelian genetics. The gene governing ABO blood groups has three primary alleles: $I^A$, $I^B$, and $i$.

Universal Donors and Universal Recipients

In emergency settings where time is critical and crossmatching cannot wait, specific blood types play unique roles.

  • Universal Red Cell Donor (Group O negative): Group O red blood cells lack both A and B antigens. Consequently, the recipient's plasma antibodies have nothing to attack, making O packed red cells safe for almost any patient.
  • Universal Plasma Recipient (Group AB): Group AB individuals have both A and B antigens on their red cells, meaning their plasma contains neither anti-A nor anti-B antibodies. Their plasma will not attack donor red blood cells from any ABO group.

Clinical Significance in Midwifery and Transfusion

Midwives and obstetric nurses frequently manage situations where blood group compatibility is a matter of life and death. Obstetric hemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal mortality in Nigeria, requiring rapid access to typed blood products. Furthermore, ABO incompatibility can occasionally occur between a mother and her foetus, although it is usually far less severe than Rhesus incompatibility because natural anti-A and anti-B antibodies are largely IgM molecules that do not cross the placenta as readily as IgG.

Handling Transfusion Reactions and ABO Incompatibility

Giving the wrong blood type triggers an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, a medical emergency requiring immediate action by healthcare staff.

  1. Stop the transfusion immediately: Turn off the IV line containing the incompatible blood product, but keep the vein open with normal saline using a new infusion set.
  2. Check identification: Verify patient wristbands, blood bag labels, and request forms to confirm clerical errors.
  3. Notify the physician and blood bank: Send fresh blood and urine samples to the laboratory to check for free hemoglobin and evidence of red cell breakdown.
  4. Support vital signs: Administer oxygen, maintain blood pressure with intravenous fluids, and monitor urinary output closely to protect kidney function against hemoglobin-induced tubular necrosis.
