Leucocytes

Introduction and Classification of Leucocytes

Leucocytes, commonly known as white blood cells, are vital cellular components of the cardiovascular and immune systems. Unlike red blood cells, leucocytes contain nuclei and lack hemoglobin, giving them a translucent appearance under a microscope. Their primary role is to protect the body against invading pathogens, foreign antigens, and abnormal cells.

Based on their microscopic appearance after staining, leucocytes are broadly divided into two main groups:

  • Granulocytes: Contain prominent visible granules in their cytoplasm and have lobed nuclei. This group includes neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils.
  • Agranulocytes: Lack prominent cytoplasmic granules and have non-lobed nuclei. This group includes lymphocytes and monocytes.

Neutrophils: The First Responders

Neutrophils are the most abundant leucocytes circulating in human blood, typically making up over half of the total white blood cell count. They feature a distinct multi-lobed nucleus, which earns them the alternate name polymorphonuclear leucocytes. When tissue injury or bacterial infection occurs, neutrophils are rapidly summoned to the site via chemical signaling called chemotaxis.

These cells act as professional phagocytes, engulfing and destroying invading bacteria using powerful antimicrobial enzymes contained within their granules. Due to their short lifespan, dead neutrophils accumulate at infection sites, forming the thick, whitish fluid commonly known as pus.

Eosinophils, Basophils, and Specialized Defenses

Eosinophils and basophils play specialized roles in host defense, particularly involving allergic responses and parasitic infections.

Eosinophils feature bilobed nuclei and bright red-orange granules when stained. They specialize in attacking parasitic worms that are too large for a single phagocyte to ingest, releasing destructive substances directly onto the parasite's surface. They also modulate allergic inflammatory responses.

Basophils are the least common granulocytes. Their dark purple granules contain histamine and heparin. Histamine promotes local blood vessel dilation and increased permeability during inflammation and allergic reactions, while heparin prevents inappropriate blood clotting within microvessels.

Lymphocytes and Adaptive Immunity

Lymphocytes are the master coordinators of the adaptive immune response, providing long-term immunity against specific pathogens. Unlike innate immune cells that react the same way to every threat, lymphocytes mount targeted attacks and retain memory of past infections.

The two primary types are B lymphocytes (B cells) and T lymphocytes (T cells). B cells mature in the bone marrow and differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibodies. T cells mature in the thymus gland and are responsible for cell-mediated immunity, directly destroying virus-infected cells or tumor cells, as well as helping regulate the overall immune response.

Monocytes and Macrophages: The Clean-Up Crew

Monocytes are the largest of all white blood cells, featuring a characteristic kidney-shaped or indented nucleus. After circulating briefly in the bloodstream, monocytes migrate into body tissues where they transform into active macrophages.

Macrophages are powerful phagocytes that engulf cellular debris, dead neutrophils, foreign particles, and worn-out erythrocytes. Beyond cleaning up tissue debris, macrophages act as antigen-presenting cells. They digest captured pathogens and display fragments of them on their surface to alert lymphocytes, effectively bridging innate and adaptive immunity.

Leucopoiesis and Clinical Interpretation

Leucopoiesis is the process of white blood cell production, which takes place in the red bone marrow under the stimulation of specific growth factors and cytokines called colony-stimulating factors.

In clinical practice, a complete blood count with differential provides critical diagnostic information:

  • Leucocytosis: An elevated total white blood cell count, commonly seen during acute bacterial infections, inflammation, or physical stress.
  • Leucopenia: A reduced white blood cell count, which leaves the patient vulnerable to opportunistic infections. This can be caused by viral infections, bone marrow suppression, or certain medications.

Midwives monitor these counts to detect early signs of postpartum infections or systemic complications in pregnant and puerperal women.
