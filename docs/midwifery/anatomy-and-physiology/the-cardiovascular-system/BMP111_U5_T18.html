Pulmonary Circulation

Introduction to Pulmonary Circulation

Pulmonary circulation is the dedicated circuit that moves deoxygenated blood away from the right side of the heart, through the lungs for gas exchange, and back to the left side of the heart. Unlike the systemic circulation, which supplies oxygen to the entire body under high pressure, the pulmonary circuit operates as a low-pressure system designed exclusively for efficient respiratory gas exchange.

Anatomical Pathway from the Right Ventricle to the Lungs

The journey of blood through the pulmonary circuit follows a precise anatomical sequence:

  1. Right Ventricle: Pumping chamber that ejects blood through the pulmonary semilunar valve.
  2. Pulmonary Trunk: A short, wide main vessel that receives blood from the right ventricle.
  3. Right and Left Pulmonary Arteries: The trunk bifurcates to carry deoxygenated blood to each respective lung. Notice the common student misconception: pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood, which is the exact opposite of systemic arteries.
  4. Lobar and Segmental Arteries: These main arteries branch repeatedly inside the lung tissue, matching the branching of the bronchial tree.
  5. Pulmonary Capillaries: A dense network wrapping tightly around the pulmonary alveoli for gas exchange.

Gas Exchange at the Alveolar-Capillary Membrane

Gas exchange occurs across the extremely thin alveolar-capillary membrane via simple diffusion. Carbon dioxide moves from the deoxygenated capillary blood into the alveolar space to be exhaled, while oxygen crosses from the alveoli into the blood to bind with hemoglobin in red blood cells. The vast surface area of millions of tiny alveoli ensures rapid and complete blood oxygenation during every respiratory cycle.

Return of Oxygenated Blood to the Left Atrium

Once gas exchange is complete, newly oxygenated blood collects in small venules that merge to form larger pulmonary veins. Typically, four pulmonary veins—two from the right lung and two from the left lung—drain directly into the left atrium of the heart. This marks the end of the pulmonary circulation circuit and prepares the blood for systemic distribution.

Pulmonary Vascular Resistance and Blood Pressure Dynamics

Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) measures the opposition to blood flow through the pulmonary vascular bed. Because pulmonary vessels have thin walls and can easily distend or recruit more pathways, normal PVR is remarkably low. Low resistance protects the fragile right ventricle from strain. When alveolar oxygen levels drop significantly, localized pulmonary blood vessels constrict to redirect blood to better-ventilated areas of the lung.

Clinical Implications of Pulmonary Circulation Impairment

When pulmonary circulation is blocked or disrupted, serious clinical consequences quickly follow. A blood clot traveling from the systemic veins can lodge in the pulmonary arterial tree, causing a pulmonary embolism that strains the right heart. Chronic lung diseases can also increase pulmonary vascular resistance, eventually leading to right-sided heart failure, a condition known clinically as cor pulmonale.
