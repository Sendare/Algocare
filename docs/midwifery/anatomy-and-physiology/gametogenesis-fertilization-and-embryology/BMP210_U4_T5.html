Development of Embryo, Fetus, and Fetal Membranes (Amnion and Chorion)

From Zygote to Blastocyst: Early Embryonic Development

The journey of human development begins the moment a sperm fertilizes an oocyte, forming a single-celled zygote. As this zygote travels down the Fallopian tube toward the uterine cavity, it undergoes rapid mitotic cell divisions known as cleavage. These divisions do not increase the overall size of the mass, but rather create smaller cells called blastomeres.

By the third day after fertilization, the embryo consists of a solid ball of about sixteen cells resembling a mulberry, which is termed the morula. Fluid soon enters the intercellular spaces of the morula, creating a fluid-filled cavity called the blastocoele. At this point, the structure becomes a blastocyst. The blastocyst differentiates into two distinct parts: an inner cell mass, or embryoblast, which will eventually form the actual body of the embryo, and an outer single layer of cells called the trophoblast, which contributes to the formation of the placenta and fetal membranes.

Development of Embryo: Organogenesis and Key Milestones

The embryonic period spans from the third week through the eighth week following fertilization. This phase is characterized by organogenesis, the critical window during which all major internal and external organs are laid down from the three primary germ layers: ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm.

  • Ectoderm: Gives rise to the nervous system, skin, hair, and enamel of the teeth.
  • Mesoderm: Forms the cardiovascular system, skeletal muscles, bones, kidneys, and reproductive organs.
  • Endoderm: Builds the epithelial linings of the digestive tract, respiratory system, liver, and pancreas.

By the end of the eighth week, the embryo has distinct human features, a beating heart that can be visualized via ultrasound, and rudimentary limb buds. Because organ systems are forming rapidly during this window, the embryo is exceptionally vulnerable to teratogens, infections, and nutritional deficiencies.

Transitioning to the Fetus: Growth and System Maturity

From the beginning of the ninth week until birth, the developing human is classified as a fetus. Unlike the embryonic period, which focuses primarily on organogenesis, the fetal period is defined by rapid body growth, tissue enlargement, and the functional maturation of organ systems already established.

Midwives monitor key developmental milestones across the remaining trimesters. By the end of the second trimester, around 24 weeks, the lungs begin producing surfactant, a vital substance that reduces surface tension in the alveoli and makes extrauterine survival possible, albeit with intensive neonatal care. Subcutaneous fat accumulates rapidly in the third trimester, smoothing out the wrinkled skin and regulating body temperature in preparation for birth.

Amnion: Structure, Fluid Dynamics, and Clinical Significance

The amnion is a thin, tough, transparent membranous sac that closely envelops the embryo and later the fetus. It forms early during embryonic development and fills with amniotic fluid, creating a fluid-filled cushioning environment.

FeatureAmnion Characteristic
CompositionInitially maternal serum filtrate, later largely fetal urine and pulmonary secretions
Volume at TermApproximately 500 to 1000 mL
Primary FunctionsCushions against trauma, allows symmetrical movement, maintains constant temperature, and prevents umbilical cord compression

Midwives assess amniotic fluid volume clinically because abnormal amounts signal underlying complications. Oligohydramnios (too little fluid) often relates to renal tract anomalies or uteroplacental insufficiency, while polyhydramnios (too much fluid) may indicate fetal swallowing obstructions or maternal gestational diabetes.

Chorion: Formation, Villi, and Placental Contribution

The chorion is the outermost fetal membrane that surrounds the amnion, embryo, and other membranes. It develops from the trophoblast cells of the blastocyst along with extraembryonic mesoderm. Its surface becomes covered in finger-like projections called chorionic villi.

These chorionic villi invade the decidua basalis of the maternal endometrium, establishing the essential structural foundation for the placenta. The villi contain fetal blood vessels that bring deoxygenated blood from the fetus close to maternal blood pools inside the intervillous spaces. This close proximity allows the efficient exchange of oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, and waste products without the maternal and fetal bloodstreams directly mixing.

Midwifery Implications of Membrane and Fetal Anomalies

Midwives must be vigilant in identifying deviations in membrane formation and fetal development during antenatal care. Premature rupture of membranes (PROM) or preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM) poses significant infection risks, such as chorioamnionitis, requiring careful monitoring of maternal temperature and fetal heart rates.

Structural anomalies involving the neural tube, such as spina bifida, or chromosomal variations often present with abnormal uterine size-to-date measurements or atypical biochemical screening results. Recognizing these warning signs allows midwives to initiate timely referrals to obstetric specialists, ensuring safe management and appropriate neonatal resuscitation readiness.
