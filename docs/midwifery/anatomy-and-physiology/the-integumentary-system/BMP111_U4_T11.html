Sweat Glands

Anatomy and Distribution of Sweat Glands

Sweat glands, scientifically known as sudoriferous glands, are small, tubular structures nestled within the dermis of the skin. They are distributed across almost the entire human body surface, though their density varies significantly depending on the anatomical site. For instance, the palms, soles, and forehead contain the highest concentrations.

Histologically, these glands consist of a secretory coil that produces the fluid and a duct that channels this fluid toward the skin surface. Understanding this basic architecture helps midwives appreciate how the skin responds to both internal and external environmental shifts throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period.

Eccrine Glands and Temperature Regulation

Eccrine glands are the most numerous sweat glands on the human body. Unlike other glands tied to hair follicles, eccrine glands open directly onto the skin surface through tiny pores. Their primary function is thermoregulation. When core body temperature rises—due to physical exertion, ambient heat, or fever—the hypothalamus signals these glands to release a watery, hypotonic fluid. As this moisture evaporates from the skin, it draws heat away from the body, cooling the blood circulating through superficial vessels. This evaporative cooling is a vital homeostatic mechanism for preventing dangerous heat illness.

Apocrine Glands and Hormonal Influences

Apocrine glands are larger sweat glands found mainly in specific regions containing hair follicles, such as the axillae (armpits) and the groin. Unlike eccrine glands, apocrine glands become active primarily at puberty under the influence of sex hormones, particularly androgens. They secrete a thick, milky fluid that contains proteins and lipids. When this secretion reaches the skin surface, resident skin bacteria break it down, producing characteristic body odor. Apocrine secretion is closely linked to emotional stress, sexual arousal, and hormonal fluctuations rather than cooling.

Physiology of Sweat Production and Secretion

Sweat production is a two-stage process occurring within the tubular gland. First, the secretory coil produces an initial fluid that is remarkably similar in electrolyte concentration to blood plasma. As this primary secretion travels upward through the coiled duct, sodium and chloride ions are actively reabsorbed back into the surrounding tissue. Water largely remains inside the lumen depending on hydration status, resulting in a final sweat output that is hypotonic, containing water, sodium chloride, urea, and trace metabolic wastes. Neural control is primarily mediated by the sympathetic nervous system, utilizing acetylcholine as the neurotransmitter for eccrine glands, which is a unique exception in the sympathetic nervous system.

Clinical Pathologies: Hyperhidrosis and Anhidrosis

  1. Hyperhidrosis: Excessive sweating beyond what is required for normal thermoregulation. It can be generalized or localized to the palms, soles, and armpits, causing significant social and psychological discomfort for patients.
  2. Anhidrosis: The inability to sweat normally. If widespread, this condition impairs cooling and can quickly lead to overheating, heat exhaustion, or heat stroke.
  3. Miliaria (Prickly Heat): A common condition caused by blocked sweat ducts trapping sweat beneath the skin, leading to tiny, itchy bumps and inflammation, especially in humid climates or tight clothing.

Midwifery Assessments and Patient Care Considerations

Midwives frequently evaluate skin integrity and fluid balance during antenatal, labor, and postnatal care. Pregnancy triggers increased sweat production due to higher metabolic rates and elevated hormonal levels. Midwives should monitor postpartum women for normal postpartum diaphoresis, a physiologic mechanism for shedding excess fluid accumulated during pregnancy. Ensuring adequate hydration, promoting good personal hygiene to prevent skin breakdown in skin folds, and differentiating normal postpartum sweating from infection-related fevers are essential components of holistic maternal care.
