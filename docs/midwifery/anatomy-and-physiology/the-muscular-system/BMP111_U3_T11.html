Lower Limbs Muscles

Introduction to Lower Limb Myology

The muscles of the lower limb are specialized for weight-bearing, locomotion, and maintaining upright posture. Unlike the upper limbs, which prioritize dexterity and wide ranges of motion, lower limb muscles generate strong propulsive forces and provide structural stability against gravity. These muscles span the hip, knee, ankle, and foot joints, operating through interconnected fascial compartments. Understanding their organization helps midwives and nurses anticipate complications related to mobility, nerve compression, and venous return during pregnancy and postpartum recovery.

Muscles of the Gluteal Region

The gluteal region connects the axial skeleton to the lower extremity, housing powerful muscles responsible for hip extension, abduction, and lateral rotation.

  • Gluteus Maximus: The largest and most superficial muscle in the group, acting primarily as a powerful thigh extensor and lateral rotator.
  • Gluteus Medius and Minimus: Fan-shaped muscles located deep to the maximus that function as primary abductors of the thigh and stabilize the pelvis during the stance phase of walking.
  • Deep Rotators: A group of small muscles, including the piriformis, that stabilize the hip joint and laterally rotate the thigh.

Midwives must note the exact location of the gluteus medius when choosing safe sites for intramuscular injections to avoid damaging the underlying sciatic nerve.

Anterior and Posterior Compartments of the Thigh

The thigh is divided into distinct fascial compartments that separate opposing muscle groups by function and nerve supply.

The anterior compartment contains the extensors of the knee, dominated by the quadriceps femoris (rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis, and vastus intermedius). These muscles share a common tendon inserting into the patella.

The posterior compartment contains the flexors of the knee and extensors of the hip, commonly known as the hamstrings (biceps femoris, semitendinosus, and semimembranosus).

Medial compartment muscles, primarily the adductors, pull the legs toward the midline and are supplied by the obturator nerve.

Muscles of the Leg: Compartments and Actions

The leg (between knee and ankle) is partitioned into anterior, lateral, and posterior compartments.

  1. Anterior Compartment: Contains muscles like the tibialis anterior that dorsiflex the foot and extend the toes.
  2. Lateral Compartment: Houses the fibularis longus and brevis muscles, which evert the foot and assist with plantarflexion.
  3. Posterior Compartment: Divided into superficial and deep layers. The superficial layer includes the gastrocnemius and soleus, which unite to form the Achilles tendon for powerful plantarflexion.

Swelling within these tight fascial compartments can restrict blood flow, leading to a dangerous surgical emergency known as compartment syndrome.

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Muscles of the Foot

Foot muscles are categorized into extrinsic muscles, which originate in the leg and insert into foot bones, and intrinsic muscles, which originate and insert entirely within the foot. Extrinsic muscles provide major power for walking, while intrinsic muscles fine-tune toe movements and support the longitudinal and transverse arches of the foot. Weakness in these intrinsic muscles can lead to flat feet and alter maternal gait stability during late pregnancy.

Clinical Relevance in Nursing Care

Lower limb muscle health directly impacts overall patient mobility, circulation, and safety. Prolonged bed rest can cause rapid muscle atrophy and increase the risk of deep vein thrombosis as the calf muscle pump fails to assist venous return. Midwives and nurses promote recovery by encouraging early ambulation, performing passive range-of-motion exercises, and assessing for localized pain or swelling.
