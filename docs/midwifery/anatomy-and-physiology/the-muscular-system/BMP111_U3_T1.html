Skeletal Muscle Tissue

Introduction and Gross Anatomy of Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscle tissue is primarily attached to bones and is responsible for body movements under conscious, voluntary control. Each skeletal muscle functions as an individual organ, made up of thousands of muscle cells—often referred to as muscle fibers—along with connective tissue, blood vessels, and nerve fibers.

To protect and organize these delicate fibers, connective tissue wraps around them at different structural levels:

  • Epimysium: A dense irregular connective tissue sheath that surrounds the entire skeletal muscle, holding the whole organ together.
  • Perimysium: A fibrous connective tissue layer that groups muscle fibers into bundles known as fascicles.
  • Endomysium: A fine layer of reticular connective tissue that surrounds each individual muscle fiber inside a fascicle.

These three connective tissue layers extend beyond the muscle belly to form strong tendons or aponeuroses, which anchor the muscle firmly to the periosteum of bones.

Microscopic Anatomy of Muscle Fibers

Under a microscope, a skeletal muscle fiber reveals highly specialized structures adapted for contraction. The plasma membrane of a muscle fiber is called the sarcolemma, and its cytoplasm is known as the sarcoplasm. Unlike typical cells, skeletal muscle fibers are multinucleated, containing many nuclei pushed to the periphery just beneath the sarcolemma.

The sarcoplasm is packed with long, cylinder-shaped organelles called myofibrils, which run the entire length of the cell and take up about 80% of its volume. Wrapped around each myofibril is a specialized smooth endoplasmic reticulum called the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), which stores and releases calcium ions. At regular intervals, narrow tunnels called T-tubules (transverse tubules) plunge inward from the sarcolemma, sitting closely between enlarged portions of the SR known as terminal cisternae.

The Sarcomere and Myofilaments

The repeating functional units of a myofibril are called sarcomeres, which give skeletal muscle its striated, striped appearance. A single sarcomere extends from one Z-disc to the next Z-disc. Within each sarcomere lie two types of smaller protein structures called myofilaments:

  • Thick filaments: Composed primarily of the protein myosin, featuring small globular heads that project outward.
  • Thin filaments: Composed mainly of the protein actin, along with regulatory proteins called tropomyosin and troponin.

Alternating bands create the striations: dark A-bands contain the entire length of thick filaments overlapping with thin filaments, while light I-bands contain only thin filaments anchored at the Z-disc.

The Sliding Filament Mechanism of Contraction

During contraction, skeletal muscle shortens because myofilaments slide past one another rather than changing their own length—a process known as the sliding filament model. When stimulated, myosin heads attach to binding sites on actin, forming cross-bridges.

Using energy, the myosin heads pivot, pulling the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere (the M-line). As this occurs, the Z-discs move closer together, the I-bands shorten, and the H-zone shrinks or disappears, although the length of the A-band remains constant. Once the contraction ends, cross-bridges detach, and the sarcomere relaxes back to its resting length.

Excitation-Contraction Coupling and the Neuromuscular Junction

A skeletal muscle cannot contract on its own; it requires a nerve impulse from a motor neuron. The meeting point between a motor neuron axon terminal and a muscle fiber sarcolemma is called the neuromuscular junction (NMJ).

When a nerve signal arrives, the neuron releases a chemical neurotransmitter called acetylcholine (ACh) into the synaptic cleft. ACh binds to receptor proteins on the sarcolemma, triggering an electrical action potential that sweeps across the membrane and dives down the T-tubules. This electrical wave stimulates the adjacent sarcoplasmic reticulum to release stored calcium ions into the sarcoplasm. The calcium ions bind to troponin, shifting tropomyosin away and exposing actin binding sites so that cross-bridge formation and contraction can begin.

Energy Metabolism and Muscle Fatigue

Skeletal muscle contractions require a continuous supply of ATP (adenosine triphosphate) to power cross-bridge movement and detach myosin heads. Because muscles store only a tiny amount of ATP, they rely on three main pathways to regenerate it rapidly:

  1. Direct phosphorylation: Uses creatine phosphate to quickly convert ADP back to ATP during the first few seconds of intense exertion.
  2. Anaerobic glycolysis: Breaks down glucose without oxygen to produce ATP quickly, generating lactic acid as a byproduct during strenuous activity.
  3. Aerobic respiration: Uses oxygen in the mitochondria to break down glucose, fatty acids, and amino acids, yielding large amounts of ATP for sustained endurance activity.

When muscles run low on ATP or experience ionic imbalances, muscle fatigue sets in, reducing the ability to contract effectively despite ongoing nerve stimulation.
