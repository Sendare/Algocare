Upper Limbs Muscles

Introduction to Upper Limb Musculature

The upper limb is a marvel of evolutionary engineering, designed for both powerful lifting and delicate, precise movements. Its muscles are organized into four main anatomical regions: the shoulder, the arm, the forearm, and the hand. Unlike the lower limb, which is built primarily for stability and weight-bearing, the upper limb prioritizes mobility. Midwives rely heavily on their upper limbs during clinical duties—from supporting a laboring mother to performing newborn resuscitation. Understanding how these muscles originate, insert, and act helps clinicians prevent repetitive strain injuries and properly assess neurological deficits.

Muscles Connecting the Upper Limb to the Vertebral Column

Superficial back muscles bridge the axial skeleton to the appendicular skeleton, anchoring and moving the pectoral girdle.

  • Trapezius: A large, kite-shaped muscle that elevates, depresses, and retracts the scapula. Its upper fibers help shrug the shoulders.
  • Latissimus dorsi: The broad muscle of the lower back responsible for arm extension, adduction, and medial rotation (the swimmer's muscle).
  • Levator scapulae: Situated at the back and side of the neck, it lifts the scapula.
  • Rhomboids (major and minor): Diamond-shaped muscles beneath the trapezius that retract and steady the scapula against the thoracic wall.

Muscles of the Shoulder and Pectoral Girdle

The shoulder region creates a bridge between the trunk and the arm, providing a wide range of motion. The deltoid muscle forms the rounded contour of the shoulder and is the primary abductor of the arm beyond the initial 15 degrees. Deep to the deltoid lies the rotator cuff, a group of four stabilizing muscles (Supraspinatus, Infraspinatus, Teres minor, and Subscapularis, remembered by the mnemonic SITS). These muscles hold the head of the humerus firmly within the shallow glenoid cavity during arm movements. On the anterior chest wall, the pectoralis major and pectoralis minor pull the shoulder forward and downward, assisting with deep breathing and arm adduction.

Compartments and Actions of the Arm

The arm (stretching from shoulder to elbow) is divided by deep fascial planes into an anterior (flexor) compartment and a posterior (extensor) compartment.

  • Anterior compartment: Contains the biceps brachii, brachialis, and coracobrachialis. These are innervated by the musculocutaneous nerve and primarily flex the elbow and supinate the forearm.
  • Posterior compartment: Contains the triceps brachii and anechoic small anconeus, innervated by the radial nerve. Their main job is extending the elbow joint.

Forearm Muscles: Flexors, Extensors, and Pronators

Forearm musculature is complex, controlling movements of the wrist, hand, and fingers, as well as the twisting action of pronation and supination. The anterior forearm houses flexors and pronators, mostly originating from the medial epicondyle of the humerus and controlled largely by the median and ulnar nerves. Conversely, the posterior forearm houses extensors and supinators, originating around the lateral epicondyle and driven by the radial nerve. Tendons from these forearm muscles travel through the wrist into the hand, acting like puppet strings to move the fingers without needing bulky muscles inside the hand itself.

Intrinsic Muscles of the Hand

Intrinsic muscles are entirely contained within the hand, responsible for fine motor control and intricate adjustments of grip. They are organized into distinct groups:

  • Thenar muscles: Form the fleshy base of the thumb, controlling opposition, abduction, and flexion.
  • Hypothenar muscles: Form the fleshy mound on the little finger side, controlling its movement.
  • Lumbricals and Interosseous muscles: Small worm-like and between-bone muscles that flex the metacarpophalangeal joints while extending the interphalangeal joints, enabling writing and holding instruments.

Clinical Correlates and Common Injuries

Upper limb muscles and their supplying nerves are vulnerable to trauma, compression, and repetitive strain. Midwives should be aware of conditions like Erb's palsy (brachial plexus stretch injury during difficult shoulder dystocia deliveries, leading to the classic 'waiter's tip' posture) and carpal tunnel syndrome (median nerve compression causing numbness and thenar muscle wasting). Ergonomic awareness during clinical practice helps prevent work-related musculoskeletal disorders in the shoulders and wrists.
