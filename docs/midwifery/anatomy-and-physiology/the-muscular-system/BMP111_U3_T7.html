Head Muscles

Introduction to the Musculature of the Head

Unlike most skeletal muscles in the human body that attach bones to other bones to move limbs, head muscles often have unique attachments. Many originate from bone and insert directly into the skin of the face. This structural arrangement allows for intricate movements such as smiling, frowning, chewing, and blinking. Embryologically, the muscles of the head develop primarily from the pharyngeal arches, which explains their distinct nerve supply compared to the rest of the somatic musculature. Understanding these groupings helps midwives and nurses assess neurological function, cranial nerve integrity, and general well-being in patients.

Muscles of Facial Expression: Anatomy and Innervation

The muscles of facial expression lie superficially in the subcutaneous tissue of the face and scalp. Key groups include the epicranius (comprising the occipitofrontalis) which raises the eyebrows and wrinkles the forehead, the orbicularis oculi which closes the eyelids, and the orbicularis oris which purses the lips.

Every single muscle in this group receives its motor supply from a single nerve: the facial nerve (cranial nerve VII). Damage to this nerve results in paralysis of facial expression on the affected side, a condition easily spotted during a physical assessment when a patient attempts to smile or puff out their cheeks.

Muscles of Mastication: Powering Chewing

Mastication relies on a robust group of deep muscles operating across the temporomandibular joint. Unlike facial expression muscles, these are controlled by the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (cranial nerve V3).

  • Masseter: The strongest chewing muscle, elevating the mandible to close the jaw firmly.
  • Temporalis: A fan-shaped muscle on the side of the skull that elevates and retracts the mandible.
  • Medial pterygoid: Works with the masseter to elevate the jaw and assists in side-to-side grinding.
  • Lateral pterygoid: Uniquely acts to depress the mandible to open the mouth and protrude the jaw forward.

Neck Muscles Associated with Head Movement

Head movement relies heavily on muscles anchored in the neck and upper torso. The sternocleidomastoid (SCM) originates from the sternum and clavicle and inserts on the mastoid process. When one SCM contracts, it rotates the head to the opposite side and tilts it laterally. When both contract together, they flex the neck forward.

The trapezius muscle, spanning the upper back and neck, extends the head and shrugs the shoulders. Both of these major muscle groups receive motor innervation from the accessory nerve (cranial nerve XI), making neck resistance testing a standard part of neurological evaluations.

Clinical Relevance and Assessment in Nursing Practice

Assessing head and neck musculature provides rapid insight into central and peripheral nervous system health. Midwives and nurses routinely test cranial nerve VII by asking patients to smile, frown, and raise their eyebrows. Cranial nerve V is evaluated by feeling the masseter muscles clench while palpating the jaw. For newborns, observing facial symmetry during crying helps rule out birth trauma-related facial nerve palsy, which can occur due to prolonged labor or forceps pressure. Documenting baseline muscle function ensures early detection of neurological decline or stroke symptoms.
