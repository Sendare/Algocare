Microscopic Structure of Skeletal Muscle and Sarcomere

Organization and Gross Architecture of Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscle is not just a uniform block of tissue; it is organized in layers wrapped in connective tissue membranes that protect the muscle fibers, support blood vessels, and transmit the force of contraction to the skeleton.

  • Epimysium: A dense layer of irregular connective tissue that surrounds the entire skeletal muscle, separating it from surrounding tissues and organs.
  • Perimysium: A fibrous layer that divides the muscle into a series of compartments called fascicles, holding bundles of muscle fibers together.
  • Endomysium: A delicate reticular fiber network that surrounds individual muscle fibers, insulating each cell and supporting its capillaries and nerve endings.

At the ends of the muscle, these three connective tissue layers merge to form a tendon or aponeurosis, which anchors the muscle firmly to bone. Midwife students should note that studying these layers helps explain how local infections or trauma can remain localized or spread along fascial planes in the limb.

Microscopic Anatomy of the Muscle Fiber

An individual skeletal muscle fiber is essentially a long, cylindrical cell with multiple nuclei pushed just beneath the plasma membrane. The cell membrane of a skeletal muscle fiber is called the sarcolemma, and its cytoplasm is known as the sarcoplasm.

Unlike typical cells, skeletal muscle fibers develop by the fusion of many embryonic cells, which accounts for their multinucleate condition. Packed tightly within the sarcoplasm are hundreds to thousands of long, cylindrical organelles called myofibrils, which take up most of the cell's volume. Running parallel to each other, these myofibrils give the muscle fiber its characteristic striated appearance under a light microscope, created by alternating dark and light bands along their length.

The Sarcoplasmic Reticulum and T-Tubules

For a muscle to contract uniformly, an electrical signal traveling across the surface must reach the deepest parts of the cell almost instantly. This is achieved through a specialized network of membranes.

  • Transverse Tubules (T-tubules): Narrow tunnels formed by inward projections of the sarcolemma that crisscross the muscle fiber, carrying electrical impulses deep into the cell.
  • Sarcoplasmic Reticulum (SR): A specialized smooth endoplasmic reticulum that forms a web around each myofibril, primarily responsible for storing and releasing calcium ions.
  • Terminal Cisternae: Enlarged chambers of the sarcoplasmic reticulum that lie adjacent to the T-tubules, acting as rich reservoirs for calcium storage.

A T-tubule flanked by two terminal cisternae forms a structural trio called a triad, which sits right at the junction where light and dark bands meet, ensuring rapid coupling of electrical signals to mechanical contraction.

The Sarcomere: The Functional Unit of Contraction

The myofibril is segmented into repeating functional units called sarcomeres, which are the actual generators of muscle tension. Under a microscope, a single sarcomere extends from one Z-line (or Z-disc) to the next.

The banding pattern of the sarcomere gives skeletal muscle its striated look:

  • A-band: The dark, central region that contains thick myosin filaments, overlapping with thin actin filaments at both ends.
  • I-band: The light region containing only thin actin filaments, bisected by the dark Z-line.
  • H-zone: A lighter zone in the middle of the A-band where only thick filaments are found.
  • M-line: A tiny dark line in the exact center of the H-zone formed by proteins that hold adjacent thick filaments together.

When a muscle contracts, the individual sarcomeres shorten, drawing the Z-lines closer together even though the length of the filaments themselves does not change.

Myofilaments: Actin, Myosin, and Regulatory Proteins

Within each sarcomere, two primary types of protein myofilaments interact to generate force: thin filaments made mainly of actin and thick filaments made of myosin.

Thick filaments are composed of hundreds of bundled myosin molecules, each shaped like a golf club with a globular head that projects outward, ready to bind to actin. Thin filaments consist of fibrous actin strands twisted into a helix. Attached to these actin strands are two important regulatory proteins:

  • Tropomyosin: A long, thread-like protein molecule that winds around the actin chain, blocking the binding sites in a resting muscle.
  • Troponin: A globular protein complex attached to tropomyosin that holds it in place and acts as a calcium sensor.

When calcium ions bind to troponin during excitation, the troponin-tropomyosin complex shifts its position, exposing the active binding sites on actin so contraction can proceed.

The Sliding Filament Mechanism

The process by which a muscle shortens is known as the sliding filament theory. Rather than shortening or stretching like elastic bands, the individual protein filaments simply slide past one another, driven by cyclic interactions between myosin and actin.

  1. Cross-bridge formation: The energized myosin head attaches to an exposed binding site on the actin filament.
  2. Power stroke: The myosin head pivots, pulling the actin filament toward the center of the sarcomere and releasing stored energy.
  3. Detachment: A new molecule of ATP binds to the myosin head, causing it to break its bond with actin.
  4. Resetting: The myosin head splits the ATP into ADP and phosphate, cocking the head back into its high-energy position to repeat the cycle.

This cycle repeats rapidly as long as calcium and ATP are abundant in the sarcoplasm, allowing continuous shortening of the muscle until relaxation occurs.
