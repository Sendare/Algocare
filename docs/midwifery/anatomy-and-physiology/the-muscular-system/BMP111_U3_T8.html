Neck Muscles

Introduction to Cervical Musculature

The neck muscles bridge the head and the torso, providing both mobility and stability to the cervical spine. Unlike other spinal regions, the neck houses vital structures like the trachea, esophagus, major blood vessels, and nerves. Consequently, the musculature here must protect these structures while allowing complex movements in multiple directions. Midwives and clinical practitioners must understand this foundation to assess posture, manage birth trauma affecting infant necks, or evaluate maternal discomfort during pregnancy.

Superficial Neck Muscles: The Sternocleidomastoid and Platysma

Two major superficial muscles define the external appearance and basic movements of the neck: the sternocleidomastoid and the platysma.

  • Sternocleidomastoid (SCM): A large, two-headed muscle originating from the sternum and clavicle, inserting at the mastoid process. When one SCM contracts, it tilts the head toward the same side and rotates the face to the opposite side. When both contract together, they flex the neck forward.
  • Platysma: A thin, broad, superficial sheet lying within the subcutaneous tissue of the neck. It originates from the chest fascia and inserts into the lower jaw and skin around the mouth, assisting in facial expression and drawing the corners of the mouth downward.

Deep Neck Muscles: Scalenes and Prevertebral Groups

Deep within the neck lie muscles responsible for stabilizing the cervical spine and assisting with breathing. The anterior, middle, and posterior scalenes attach the cervical vertebrae to the first two ribs. They elevate the ribs during forced inhalation and laterally flex the neck.

The prevertebral group—including the longus colli and longus capitis—lies directly anterior to the vertebral column. These muscles act primarily to flex the head and neck forward against resistance, maintaining postural balance without producing large, gross movements.

Suboccipital Muscles and Fine Head Movements

At the base of the skull, a group of four small muscles forms the suboccipital triangle. These include the rectus capitis posterior major, rectus capitis posterior minor, obliquus capitis superior, and obliquus capitis inferior.

Despite their small size, they contain a very high density of muscle spindles, making them crucial proprioceptive hubs. They do not generate heavy lifting power; instead, they fine-tune the delicate adjustments of the head on the atlas and axis vertebrae, anchoring the skull securely to the spinal column.

Biomechanics of Flexion, Extension, and Rotation

Neck movement results from the coordinated interplay of agonist and antagonist muscle groups across three anatomical planes.

  • Flexion: Bending the head forward is driven by the sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, and prevertebral muscles.
  • Extension: Pulling the head backward relies on the deep posterior extensors and the superficial trapezius.
  • Rotation and Lateral Flexion: Turning and tilting the head involve unilateral contraction of the sternocleidomastoid, scalenes, and intrinsic spinal rotators.

Smooth movement requires simultaneous relaxation of opposing muscle groups, a process regulated by the central nervous system.

Clinical Assessment and Common Injuries of the Neck Muscles

Midwives and clinical officers frequently encounter patients complaining of neck stiffness, strain, or spasm. Assessment involves inspecting for asymmetrical swelling, palpating for localized tenderness or trigger points, and testing active and passive range of motion.

Common issues include cervical strain from sudden whiplash or poor positioning during labor, and torticollis, characterized by sustained abnormal head tilting. Gentle stretching, heat therapy, and correcting ergonomic posture during nursing tasks help alleviate discomfort and promote tissue healing.
