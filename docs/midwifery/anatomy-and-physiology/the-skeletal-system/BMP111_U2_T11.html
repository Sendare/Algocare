Upper Limbs

Introduction to the Appendicular Skeleton and Upper Limbs

The human skeleton is divided into two main parts: the axial skeleton and the appendicular skeleton. While the axial skeleton forms the central core of the body, the appendicular skeleton includes the bones of the limbs and their supporting girdles. The upper limbs are specifically designed for mobility, dexterity, and reaching, allowing humans to interact with their environment in complex ways. Each upper limb attaches to the trunk via the pectoral girdle, linking the arm, forearm, wrist, and hand into a coordinated structural chain.

The Pectoral Girdle: Clavicle and Scapula

The pectoral girdle connects each upper limb to the axial skeleton. It consists of two bones: the clavicle (collarbone) and the scapula (shoulder blade).

  • Clavicle: An S-shaped bone that acts as a strut, holding the shoulder away from the chest wall to allow maximum arm movement. It is the most frequently fractured bone in the body.
  • Scapula: A flat, triangular bone positioned on the posterior ribcage. It features the glenoid cavity, a shallow socket that articulates with the head of the humerus to form the shoulder joint.

The Arm: Anatomy and Landmarks of the Humerus

In anatomical terms, the "arm" refers strictly to the region between the shoulder and the elbow. This region contains a single long bone called the humerus. The proximal end features a rounded head that fits into the scapula, flanked by the greater tubercle and lesser tubercle for muscle attachments. The shaft of the humerus runs down to the distal end, where the trochlea and capitulum articulate with the bones of the forearm. A common clinical landmark is the surgical neck of the humerus, a frequent site of fractures that can risk damage to the adjacent axillary nerve.

The Forearm: Radius and Ulna Dynamics

The forearm spans from the elbow to the wrist and contains two parallel bones: the radius and the **ulna".

  • Radius: Located on the lateral side of the forearm (thumb side). Its proximal end has a disc-shaped head that spins to allow rotation.
  • Ulna: Located on the medial side of the forearm (pinky side). Its prominent proximal tip is the olecranon process, which forms the bony point of the elbow.

Together, these bones allow for supination (turning the palm upward) and pronation (turning the palm downward) as the radius crosses over the stationary ulna.

The Hand: Carpals, Metacarpals, and Phalanges

The hand is an engineering marvel containing 27 bones, divided into three distinct groups:

  1. Carpals: Eight small bones forming the wrist, arranged in two rows of four. They allow for flexible gliding movements.
  2. Metacarpals: Five bones forming the palm of the hand, numbered one through five starting from the thumb side.
  3. Phalanges: Fourteen bones making up the digits. The thumb (pollex) has two phalanges (proximal and distal), while the other four fingers each have three (proximal, middle, and distal).

Joints and Articulations of the Upper Extremity

Movement in the upper limb depends on a series of synovial joints linking the skeletal segments. The glenohumeral (shoulder) joint offers the greatest range of motion but sacrifices stability. The elbow joint is a hinge joint allowing flexion and extension, reinforced by strong collateral ligaments. Further down, the radiocarpal (wrist) joint connects the forearm to the proximal carpal bones, permitting bending in multiple directions. Each of these junctions relies on joint capsules, synovial fluid, and surrounding muscles to maintain alignment during activity.

Clinical and Nursing Considerations for Upper Limb Injuries

Upper limb injuries—ranging from clavicle fractures during difficult deliveries to distal radius fractures from falls—are common encounters in clinical practice. Midwives and nurses must be vigilant in assessing neurovascular status following any limb trauma. The classic assessment includes checking the pulse (radial or brachial), color, temperature, capillary refill, and nerve function (sensation and movement). Prompt identification of nerve compression or restricted blood flow prevents long-term ischemic damage or permanent disability.
