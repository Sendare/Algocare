Pelvic Girdle

Anatomical Overview of the Pelvic Girdle

The pelvic girdle is a sturdy, basin-like bony structure that connects the axial skeleton to the lower limbs. It consists of the right and left hip bones, known scientifically as the coxal bones or innominate bones. During early development, each hip bone is formed by three separate bones that eventually fuse together at a cup-shaped socket called the acetabulum. At the back, the pelvic girdle securely attaches to the sacrum, forming a closed ring that provides exceptional stability. This anatomical arrangement is perfectly designed to protect delicate internal organs while withstanding intense physical forces from walking, running, and lifting.

The Three Component Bones: Ilium, Ischium, and Pubis

Each coxal bone begins as three distinct parts meeting at the acetabulum:

  • Ilium: The large, fan-shaped upper section that forms the prominent hip bones felt at the waistline. Its upper curved edge is called the iliac crest.
  • Ischium: The strong, lower-back portion that supports body weight when a person sits down, featuring a rough bony prominence known as the ischial tuberosity.
  • Pubis: The front portion that curves inward to meet its partner from the opposite side, forming the anterior junction of the pelvis.

Key Articulations and Bony Landmarks

The pelvic girdle features vital joints and landmarks essential for movement and childbirth.

  1. Sacroiliac Joints: Strong, weight-bearing synovial joints connecting the ilium to the sacrum posteriorly.
  2. Pubic Symphysis: A cartilaginous joint joining the two pubic bones at the front, which softens slightly during pregnancy due to hormonal action.
  3. Acetabulum: The deep, cup-shaped socket on the lateral surface formed by the fusion of all three hip bones, which articulates with the head of the femur to form the hip joint.

Sexual Dimorphism: Comparing Male and Female Pelves

The human pelvis shows marked differences between sexes, primarily adaptations for childbearing in females versus greater muscular leverage and narrower builds in males.

Functional Role in Weight Bearing and Locomotion

Beyond housing internal organs, the pelvic girdle acts as the central hub for human movement. When standing, body weight travels down the spinal column into the sacrum, spreads across the sacroiliac joints, and is distributed evenly through the hip joints to the lower legs. Powerful muscles attached to the outer ilium and ischium—such as the gluteus muscles and hamstrings—anchor to the pelvis to drive walking, running, and jumping. Without this rigid yet flexible bony ring, bipedal movement and upright posture would be mechanically impossible.

Clinical Relevance in Obstetrics and Midwifery

Understanding pelvic anatomy is vital for midwives during antenatal assessment and labor management. Midwives perform clinical pelvimetry to estimate the size and shape of the maternal birth canal, ensuring the fetus can pass safely through the pelvic inlet, cavity, and outlet. Variations in pelvic architecture, such as a narrow android or flat platypelloid shape, can lead to obstructed labor and increase the need for instrumental delivery or caesarean section. Additionally, hormonal shifts during late pregnancy relax the pelvic ligaments, sometimes causing symphysis pubis dysfunction and discomfort for the expectant mother.
