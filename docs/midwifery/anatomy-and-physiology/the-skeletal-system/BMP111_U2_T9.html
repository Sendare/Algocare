Thoracic Cage

Anatomy and Composition of the Thoracic Cage

The thoracic cage is a bony and cartilaginous structure that encases and protects vital organs of the chest, including the heart and lungs. It forms a conical enclosure that is narrower at the superior inlet and broader at the inferior outlet.

  • Sternum: The anterior midline flat bone providing attachment for the upper ribs and clavicles.
  • Ribs: Twelve pairs of elongated curved bones forming the lateral walls.
  • Thoracic vertebrae: Twelve posterior bones that articulate with the ribs.
  • Costal cartilages: Bars of hyaline cartilage connecting the upper ribs directly or indirectly to the sternum, adding crucial elasticity to the chest wall.

The Sternum and its Clinical Landmarks

The sternum is a dagger-shaped bone situated in the middle of the anterior chest wall. It consists of three parts: the manubrium, the body, and the xiphoid process.

The junction between the manubrium and the body forms a prominent anterior ridge known as the sternal angle (angle of Louis). Midwives and doctors rely on this angle as a vital surface landmark because it aligns with the second costal cartilage, marking the point where the trachea bifurcates and the second rib attaches. At the very bottom, the small xiphoid process serves as an anatomical marker for the upper limit of the liver and the lower boundary of the thoracic cavity.

Classification and Anatomy of the Ribs

Ribs are classified into three distinct groups based on how they connect anteriorly to the sternum.

  • True ribs (Vertebrosternal): Pairs 1 through 7 connect directly to the sternum through their own individual costal cartilages.
  • False ribs (Vertebrochondral): Pairs 8 through 10 connect to the cartilage of the rib immediately above them rather than directly to the sternum.
  • Floating ribs (Vertebroinferior): Pairs 11 and 12 have no anterior attachment at all and end freely in the abdominal musculature.

Every typical rib features a head that articulates with the vertebral bodies, a neck, a tubercle that articulates with the transverse process of a vertebra, and a long shaft that bends sharply forward at the costal angle.

Thoracic Vertebrae and Posterior Articulations

The posterior wall of the thoracic cage is formed by the twelve thoracic vertebrae. These bones possess specific features that distinguish them from cervical and lumbar vertebrae, most notably the presence of costal facets on their bodies and transverse processes. These smooth surfaces form joints with the heads and tubercles of the ribs.

The joints formed between the rib heads and the vertebral bodies are called costovertebral joints, while the joints between the rib tubercles and the transverse processes are called costotransverse joints. These gliding joints permit the subtle elevating and depressing movements of the ribs that drive normal breathing cycles.

Intercostal Spaces and Neurovascular Bundles

The spaces situated between adjacent ribs are known as intercostal spaces, and there are eleven of them on each side of the body. Each space contains three layers of intercostal muscles that help move the chest wall during respiration.

Running along the inferior margin of each upper rib inside the costal groove is the main neurovascular bundle. This bundle contains the intercostal vein, artery, and nerve, arranged in that exact order from superior to inferior (VAN: Vein, Artery, Nerve). Midwives, doctors, and nurses must remember this anatomical arrangement when performing procedures like thoracentesis or chest tube insertion, always inserting needles along the upper border of the lower rib to avoid cutting these vital vessels.

Biomechanics of Respiration and Cage Mobility

The thoracic cage is not a rigid box; it changes shape continuously to facilitate breathing. During inspiration, the contraction of the diaphragm and intercostal muscles increases the volume of the thoracic cavity, drawing air into the lungs.

Two distinct movements characterize rib motion:

  • Pump-handle movement: The upper ribs elevate, increasing the front-to-back (anteroposterior) diameter of the chest.
  • Bucket-handle movement: The lower ribs swing upward and outward, increasing the side-to-side (transverse) diameter of the chest.

During expiration, the muscles relax, and the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall decreases the thoracic volume, pushing air back out.

Clinical Assessment and Pathology of the Thorax

Pathologies affecting the thoracic cage directly impair respiratory function. A common traumatic injury is a rib fracture, which causes sharp localized pain worsened by deep breathing or coughing. If multiple adjacent ribs are fractured in multiple places, a segment of the chest wall becomes free-floating, leading to a dangerous condition called flail chest. In flail chest, the isolated segment moves paradoxically inward during inspiration and outward during expiration, severely compromising ventilation.

Clinicians inspect the chest for symmetry, abnormal deformities like pectus excavatum (sunken chest), and signs of respiratory distress such as the use of accessory muscles in the neck and shoulders.
