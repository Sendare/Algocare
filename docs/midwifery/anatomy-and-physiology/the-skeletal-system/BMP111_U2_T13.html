Lower Limbs

Introduction to the Lower Limb

The lower limb is an intricate mechanical structure designed for weight-bearing, locomotion, and maintaining upright posture. In clinical practice, understanding its anatomy helps midwives assess maternal mobility, gait changes during pregnancy, and early developmental milestones in newborns. The skeleton of the lower limb is divided into four main regions:

  • Pelvic girdle: Connects the spine to the lower limbs, formed by the hip bones.
  • Thigh: Contains the single longest bone, the femur.
  • Leg (Crus): Contains two parallel bones, the tibia and the fibula.
  • Foot (Pes): Comprises the tarsals, metatarsals, and phalanges.

Each region works in harmony with strong muscles, ligaments, and nerves to absorb the immense forces generated during walking and running.

The Pelvic Girdle and Hip Joint

The pelvic girdle anchors the lower limbs to the axial skeleton. It consists of the hip bones, which fuse from three distinct components during growth: the ilium, ischium, and pubis. These bones meet at a deep, cup-shaped socket called the acetabulum.

The hip joint is a classic ball-and-socket synovial joint formed by the articulation between the head of the femur and the acetabulum. During pregnancy, hormonal changes involving relaxin cause the ligaments surrounding the pelvis to loosen, preparing the pelvis for childbirth but occasionally causing joint instability and lower back or pelvic girdle pain.

Bones of the Thigh: The Femur

The femur is the longest, heaviest, and strongest bone in the human body. Its proximal end features a spherical head that articulates with the pelvis, a constricted neck that is a frequent site of fractures in older adults, and two bony prominences called the greater and lesser trochanters where powerful thigh and hip muscles attach.

The shaft of the femur slants slightly medially, bringing the knees closer to the body's midline for better balance during walking. At its distal end, the femur broadens into the medial and lateral condyles, which articulate with the tibia to form the major part of the knee joint.

The Knee Joint and Patella

The knee is the largest joint in the body, operating primarily as a modified hinge joint. It involves three bones: the distal femur, the proximal tibia, and the patella (kneecap).

The patella is a triangular sesamoid bone embedded within the tendon of the quadriceps femoris muscle. It protects the knee joint anteriorly and increases the leverage of the quadriceps tendon as it extends the leg.

Inside the joint capsule, stabilizing structures include:

  • Cruciate ligaments: The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) prevent front-to-back sliding.
  • Menisci: C-shaped pads of fibrocartilage that act as shock absorbers between the femur and tibia.

Bones of the Leg: Tibia and Fibula

The leg contains two bones running side by side. The tibia (shinbone) is the large, weight-bearing bone located on the medial side of the leg. Its broad proximal condyles receive the weight from the femur, and its prominent anterior border can be felt just beneath the skin. At its distal end, the tibia forms the medial malleolus, the bony bump on the inner side of the ankle.

The fibula is a slender, non-weight-bearing bone positioned parallel and lateral to the tibia. While it does not bear body weight, it provides important sites for muscle attachment and forms the lateral malleolus at the ankle, which stabilizes the ankle joint laterally.

Structure of the Ankle and Foot

The foot is an architectural marvel designed to support body weight, absorb shock, and propel the body forward during locomotion. Its 26 bones are arranged into three distinct segments:

  • Tarsals (7 bones): Form the posterior half of the foot, including the calcaneus (heel bone) and the talus, which articulates with the tibia and fibula.
  • Metatarsals (5 bones): Form the intermediate framework of the sole, numbered 1 to 5 starting from the medial side.
  • Phalanges (14 bones): The bones of the toes, with the great toe having two and the other four toes having three each.

Strong ligaments and tendons hold these bones in arched formations, known as the longitudinal and transverse arches, which act like springs during walking.

Clinical Relevance and Common Injuries

Lower limb structures are frequently affected by trauma, posture changes, and degenerative conditions. In midwifery and general healthcare, recognizing these issues is essential for holistic patient management. Common clinical presentations include:

  • Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT): Stasis of blood in the lower limb veins, a notable risk during pregnancy and the postpartum period due to hypercoagulability and pelvic vessel compression.
  • Ankle Sprains: Stretching or tearing of lateral ankle ligaments, often occurring when the foot twists inward.
  • Edema: Swelling in the lower legs and feet caused by fluid retention, common in late pregnancy due to increased venous pressure from the gravid uterus.
