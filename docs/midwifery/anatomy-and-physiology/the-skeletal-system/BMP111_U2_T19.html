Fractures

Understanding Fractures: Definition and Basic Concepts

A fracture is a break or disruption in the continuity of a bone. This structural failure happens when the physical force exerted on the bone is stronger than the bone can absorb. In midwifery and general clinical practice, recognizing how a bone breaks helps predict recovery times and potential dangers. Bones are living tissues with a hard outer shell and a spongy inner network, making them both strong and slightly flexible. When trauma exceeds this threshold, the bone yields, resulting in partial or complete breakage.

Age, nutritional status, and underlying pathologies like osteogenesis imperfecta or severe calcium deficiencies significantly weaken bone integrity. A patient with weakened bones can sustain a severe fracture from a minor fall that would normally leave a healthy bone intact.

How Fractures Are Classified: Types and Patterns

Fractures are categorized based on their pattern, the integrity of the overlying skin, and the degree of bone displacement. Midwives and nurses must accurately describe these patterns in patient records to guide medical management.

  • Closed (Simple) Fracture: The bone is broken, but the skin remains intact with no open wound leading to the fracture site.
  • Open (Compound) Fracture: The broken bone ends pierce through the skin, creating a high risk of bacterial infection.
  • Transverse Fracture: The break runs straight across the bone shaft at a right angle.
  • Oblique Fracture: The break occurs at a slanted angle across the bone.
  • Spiral Fracture: The break twists around the bone shaft, often associated with rotational trauma or non-accidental injury.
  • Greenstick Fracture: An incomplete break where the bone bends and cracks, commonly seen in young children whose bones are soft.

The Physiology of Bone Healing

Bone healing is a remarkable natural regeneration process that restores structural integrity without leaving scar tissue. The body achieves this through overlapping phases:

  1. Hematoma Formation: Immediately after the break, blood vessels bleed into the fracture gap, forming a clotting mass called a fracture hematoma within the first 24 to 72 hours.
  2. Fibrocartilaginous Callus Formation: Fibroblasts and osteoblasts migrate to the area. They create a soft bridge of fibrocartilage, connecting the broken bone ends.
  3. Bony Callus Formation: Osteoblasts replace the soft callus with spongy bone, turning it into a hard bony callus over several weeks.
  4. Bone Remodeling: Over months or years, osteoclasts resorb excess bone while osteoblasts lay down compact bone along lines of stress, restoring the original shape.

Clinical Assessment and Finding Signs of Injury

Detecting a fracture requires a systematic physical examination combined with a clear history of the trauma. Midwives should look for localized pain that worsens with movement, swelling, and bruising from internal bleeding. Loss of normal function or an unnatural, abnormal angle of the limb points strongly to a displaced break. Crepitus, a crunching sound or feeling when broken bone ends rub together, may be present but should never be intentionally tested for, as it causes needless pain and tissue damage. Palpating the distal pulses and checking capillary refill and sensation ensures that nearby nerves and blood vessels are not trapped or severed by the broken fragments.

Emergency Management and Immobilization Techniques

Immediate first-aid for a suspected fracture focuses on preventing further injury to surrounding muscles, nerves, and blood vessels. The golden rule of fracture management is to splint the bone in the position it is found unless circulation is completely compromised.

  • Stop Bleeding: Cover any open wounds with a clean dressing and apply direct pressure around the wound, avoiding pressure directly over protruding bone ends.
  • Immobilize Joints: A splint must secure the joints immediately above and below the fracture site to prevent movement.
  • Check Circulation: Always assess pulse, color, and temperature below the injury before and after applying any bandage or splint.

Surgical and Nonsurgical Treatment Options

Once emergency stabilization is complete, definitive treatment aims to align the bone fragments and keep them stable until healing is finished.

Nonsurgical management relies on closed reduction, where the clinician manipulates the bone back into alignment manually without cutting the skin, followed by casting or functional bracing.

Surgical management becomes necessary when closed reduction fails, or when dealing with unstable, open, or intra-articular fractures. Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) involves making an incision to align the bones directly, securing them with metal plates, screws, or intramedullary rods. External fixation uses pins drilled into the bone through the skin attached to an external metal frame, which is particularly useful for severe open fractures with extensive soft tissue damage.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Fractures can trigger dangerous local and systemic complications if not monitored closely.

  • Compartment Syndrome: Increased pressure within an enclosed muscle compartment restricts blood flow, causing severe, unremitting pain that is not relieved by pain medications. This is a surgical emergency requiring a fasciotomy.
  • Fat Embolism Syndrome: Fat globules from the bone marrow are released into the bloodstream, blocking small vessels in the lungs and brain, often presenting with respiratory distress and petechiae.
  • Infection (Osteomyelitis): Bacteria can enter open fractures or surgical sites, leading to chronic bone infection.

Nursing care centers on performing frequent neurovascular checks, monitoring vital signs, managing pain, and educating patients on cast care.
