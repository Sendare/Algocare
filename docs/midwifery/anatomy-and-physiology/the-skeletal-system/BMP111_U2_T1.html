Overview and Functions of Skeletal System

Introduction to the Skeletal System

The skeletal system forms the rigid internal framework of the human body, giving it shape and structural integrity. At birth, a baby has around 270 to 300 soft, pliable bones. Many of these fuse together as a person grows, leaving an adult with 206 distinct bones. Bone tissue, or osseous tissue, is a living, highly vascularized connective tissue containing active cells, blood vessels, and nerves. It continuously remodels itself in response to mechanical stress and hormonal signals, proving that bone is far from being a dry, inert structure.

Divisions of the Skeleton: Axial and Appendicular

Anatomists divide the 206 bones of the human body into two primary divisions.

  • Axial Skeleton: Consists of 80 bones that form the central vertical axis of the body. This includes the skull, vertebral column, and thoracic cage (ribs and sternum). Its main job is protecting vital organs like the brain, spinal cord, and heart.
  • Appendicular Skeleton: Comprises 126 bones forming the upper and lower limbs, plus the pectoral and pelvic girdles that attach the limbs to the axial skeleton. This division is primarily designed for movement and interacting with the environment.

Support, Protection, and Movement

Bones perform fundamental mechanical tasks that keep the body functional and safe.

  1. Support: The skeleton acts as a sturdy pillar that holds up the body, supporting soft tissues and giving posture to the body.
  2. Protection: Hard bony cases surround delicate internal organs. The cranium encases the brain, the vertebrae shield the spinal cord, and the rib cage safeguards the heart and lungs.
  3. Movement: Skeletal muscles attach to bones via tendons. When muscles contract, they pull on bones, using them as levers to create precise, coordinated body movements.

Hematopoiesis and Mineral Storage

Beyond simple mechanics, bones act as vital physiological storehouses and factories.

  • Hematopoiesis: Red bone marrow, found inside the spongy cavities of certain bones like the sternum and hip bones, produces red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. Midwives monitor maternal blood health closely, relying on efficient bone marrow function to prevent anemia during pregnancy.
  • Mineral Homeostasis: Bone matrix stores roughly 99 percent of the body's calcium and 85 percent of its phosphorus. The skeleton releases these essential minerals into the bloodstream whenever systemic levels drop, ensuring proper nerve conduction and muscle contraction throughout the body.

Endocrine Regulation and Metabolic Functions

The skeletal system also participates in endocrine and metabolic regulation. Bone cells release a hormone called osteocalcin, which helps regulate blood sugar levels by prompting the pancreas to release more insulin and signaling fat cells to release adiponectin. Furthermore, bone tissue helps maintain acid-base balance by absorbing or releasing alkaline salts when blood pH fluctuates. Adipose tissue stored within yellow bone marrow also serves as an important chemical energy reserve for the body.
