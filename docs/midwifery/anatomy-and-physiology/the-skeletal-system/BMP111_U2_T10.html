Shoulder Girdle

Introduction to the Shoulder Girdle

The shoulder girdle, scientifically known as the pectoral girdle, forms the bony bridge connecting the upper limbs to the axial skeleton. Unlike the pelvic girdle, which is deeply fused for maximum weight-bearing stability, the shoulder girdle prioritizes mobility. This wide range of movement makes daily tasks and clinical procedures involving the upper extremities possible, though it trades away some structural rigidity.

Only one small bony attachment anchors the entire upper limb to the rest of the trunk: the sternoclavicular joint. Because of this delicate connection, the girdle relies heavily on a complex web of surrounding muscles and ligaments to stay in place while executing powerful movements.

Anatomical Composition: Clavicle and Scapula

Two primary bones make up each shoulder girdle: the clavicle and the scapula.

  • Clavicle (Collarbone): An S-shaped long bone lying horizontally across the upper chest. It acts as a rigid strut, holding the shoulder away from the trunk so the arm can swing clear of the body.
  • Scapula (Shoulder blade): A flat, triangular bone positioned on the posterior thoracic wall. It features prominent landmarks like the spine of the scapula, the acromion process, and the coracoid process, providing attachment points for numerous muscles.

Joints of the Shoulder Girdle

Several key articulations allow the shoulder girdle to move smoothly against the thorax:

  1. Sternoclavicular (SC) Joint: Connects the sternal end of the clavicle to the manubrium of the sternum. It is the sole bony link between the upper appendage and the axial skeleton.
  2. Acromioclavicular (AC) Joint: Links the lateral end of the clavicle with the acromion process of the scapula. It permits subtle gliding movements during arm elevation.
  3. Glenohumeral Joint: The true shoulder joint where the head of the humerus meets the glenoid cavity of the scapula.
  4. Scapulothoracic Joint: Not a true anatomical joint with a capsule, but a physiological articulation where the scapula glides across the posterior chest wall.

Muscles Acting on the Shoulder Girdle

Movement and stabilization of the scapula and clavicle depend on a coordinated group of muscles originating from the axial skeleton and inserting onto the shoulder girdle bones.

  • Trapezius: A large, kite-shaped muscle that elevates, depresses, and retracts the scapula.
  • Serratus Anterior: Often called the 'boxer's muscle', it pulls the scapula forward against the chest wall, preventing winging.
  • Levator Scapulae and Rhomboids: Elevate and retract the scapula, pulling it toward the spine.
  • Pectoralis Minor: Draws the scapula forward and downward.

Clinical Relevance and Common Injuries

Midwives and nurses frequently encounter patients with shoulder girdle issues resulting from birth trauma, falls, or poor positioning during bed rest.

  • Clavicular Fractures: Extremely common following a fall onto an outstretched hand or direct trauma. The bone usually breaks at its middle third.
  • Shoulder Dystocia: An obstetric emergency where the anterior shoulder impacts against the mother's pubic symphysis during delivery, risking brachial plexus injury or clavicular fracture.
  • AC Joint Separation: Results from ligament tearing after a direct blow to the top of the shoulder, causing a visible upward step deformity at the outer collarbone.
