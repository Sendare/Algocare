Uterine (Endometrial) Cycle and Menstruation

Introduction to the Endometrial Cycle

The endometrial cycle describes the structural changes that occur in the inner lining of the uterus, known as the endometrium, throughout a typical female reproductive month. This cycle runs concurrently with the ovarian cycle, responding directly to circulating reproductive hormones like estrogen and progesterone. The ultimate physiological purpose of these monthly modifications is to prepare a receptive uterine bed for blastocyst implantation. If conception does not occur, the superficial layers of the endometrium shed, resulting in menstrual bleeding. Understanding this continuous biological rhythm is foundational for managing normal reproductive health and identifying deviations in clinical midwifery practice.

Layers of the Endometrium

The human endometrium is histologically divided into two distinct strata that respond very differently to hormonal stimuli:

  • Stratum functionalis: The superficial two-thirds of the endometrium that undergoes dramatic thickening, secretory activity, and complete shedding during menstruation. Its blood supply comes from long, coiled spiral arteries.
  • Stratum basalis: The deep, permanent one-third of the endometrium that remains intact during menstruation and serves as the regenerative source for rebuilding the stratum functionalis. Its blood supply comes from short, straight basal arteries.

Menstrual Phase

The menstrual phase marks day one of the clinical cycle, beginning when pregnancy does not occur. As the corpus luteum in the ovary disintegrates, levels of both estrogen and progesterone plummet sharply. This hormonal withdrawal triggers intense vasospasm in the spiral arteries, cutting off adequate blood flow and oxygen to the stratum functionalis. Deprived of nutrients, the functional tissue undergoes ischemic necrosis and detaches from the underlying basalis. The necrotic tissue, along with blood and uterine fluid, is expelled through the vagina as menstrual flow, typically lasting between three to seven days.

Proliferative Phase

Following menstruation, the proliferative phase begins under the direct influence of estrogen secreted by growing ovarian follicles. During this time, the surviving cells of the stratum basalis multiply rapidly to rebuild the entire stratum functionalis. The endometrial thickness increases significantly, glands lengthen, and the spiral arteries elongate to supply the growing tissue. Midwives understand that this phase corresponds directly with the follicular phase of the ovarian cycle, culminating in ovulation around mid-cycle.

Secretory Phase

The secretory phase begins immediately after ovulation and coincides with the luteal phase of the ovarian cycle. Driven by progesterone (and supporting estrogen) from the newly formed corpus luteum, the endometrium undergoes specialized glandular changes. The endometrial glands become highly coiled, distended, and begin secreting glycogen-rich fluids to nourish a potential blastocyst. The spiral arteries become even more tortuous, and the stroma accumulates fluid edema. If fertilization fails, the corpus luteum dies, setting the stage for the next round of menstruation.

Clinical Correlates: Abnormal Uterine Bleeding

Midwives frequently encounter women experiencing disruptions in their normal menstrual cycles, collectively referred to as abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB). Hormonal imbalances, anovulatory cycles, structural uterine fibroids, or systemic endocrine disorders can all disrupt the delicate interplay between estrogen, progesterone, and endometrial shedding. For instance, an anovulatory cycle means no corpus luteum forms, leading to continuous estrogen stimulation without progesterone opposition, resulting in erratic, heavy shedding. Clinical assessment requires a thorough menstrual history, pelvic examination, and appropriate diagnostic investigations to determine the underlying etiology.
