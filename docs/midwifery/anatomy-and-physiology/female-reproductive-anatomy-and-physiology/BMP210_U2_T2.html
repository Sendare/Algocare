Internal Genitalia: Uterus, Cervix, Fallopian Tubes, Ovaries

Introduction to the Female Internal Reproductive Organs

The internal reproductive organs of the female pelvis work in a coordinated system to support menstruation, fertilization, and gestation. Located within the true pelvis, these structures include the uterus, cervix, fallopian tubes, and ovaries. They are supported by a complex network of ligaments, fascia, and the muscles of the pelvic floor, keeping them anchored while allowing for the significant stretching required during pregnancy. Midwives must understand these anatomical relationships to accurately assess pelvic exams, interpret ultrasound findings, and support women through normal physiological processes.

Uterus: Structure, Divisions, and Layers

The uterus is a hollow, muscular, pear-shaped organ positioned in the pelvic cavity, typically anteverted and anteflexed over the bladder. Anatomically, it is divided into the fundus, the body (corpus), the isthmus, and the cervix.

Its wall consists of three distinct layers:

  • Perimetrium: The outer serous membrane covering the uterus, continuous with the broad ligament.
  • Myometrium: The thick middle layer composed of interlacing smooth muscle fibers, responsible for powerful contractions during labor.
  • Endometrium: The inner mucosal lining that undergoes cyclical changes during the menstrual cycle to prepare for embryonic implantation.

Cervix: Structure, Openings, and Mucus Changes

The cervix forms the lower cylindrical third of the uterus, protruding into the upper vagina. It features two primary openings: the internal os, which opens into the uterine cavity, and the external os, which opens into the vaginal canal. The cervical canal is lined with crypts and glands that secrete cervical mucus. Under the influence of estrogen during the follicular phase, this mucus becomes thin, watery, and stretchy to help sperm travel upward. After ovulation, progesterone makes the mucus thick and sticky to form a protective plug that bars pathogens and sperm from entering the upper reproductive tract.

Fallopian Tubes: Divisions and Function

The fallopian tubes (uterine tubes) are muscular tubes extending laterally from the uterine horns toward the ovaries. They transport the secondary oocyte from the ovary to the uterus and serve as the primary site for fertilization.

Divided into four main segments:

  1. Interstitial (Intramural) segment: The narrow portion passing through the uterine wall.
  2. Isthmus: The narrow, straight section adjacent to the uterus.
  3. Ampulla: The widest, longest section where fertilization most commonly takes place.
  4. Infundibulum: The funnel-shaped lateral end featuring finger-like projections called fimbriae that drape over the ovary to catch the released egg.

Ovaries: Follicular Development and Endocrine Roles

The ovaries are paired, almond-shaped endocrine glands situated on the lateral pelvic walls. They perform dual functions: producing female gametes (ova) and synthesizing sex hormones like estrogen and progesterone. Inside the cortex, primordial follicles develop through primary, secondary, and tertiary (Graafian) stages during each menstrual cycle under the influence of follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). Once ovulation occurs, the ruptured follicle transforms into the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone to maintain the early endometrium. If pregnancy does not occur, the corpus luteum degenerates into the corpus albicans.

Blood Supply, Lymphatic Drainage, and Nerve Supply

The internal reproductive organs receive a rich blood supply primarily from the uterine artery (a branch of the internal iliac artery) and the ovarian artery (a direct branch of the abdominal aorta). These vessels anastomose extensively within the broad ligament, ensuring robust perfusion, which increases dramatically during pregnancy. Lymphatic drainage follows the arterial pathways: the cervix and upper vagina drain into the internal and external iliac nodes, while the ovaries drain directly upward into the lumbar (aortic) nodes. Autonomic innervation comes from both sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers via the inferior hypogastric plexus, regulating involuntary smooth muscle tone and vascular resistance.

Clinical Implications for Midwifery Practice

A firm grasp of internal genital anatomy directly guides everyday midwifery assessments and interventions. When performing bimanual examinations, midwives estimate uterine size, position, and cervical effacement based on anatomical landmarks. Recognizing that the uterine artery runs close to the ureter during its pelvic journey helps midwives appreciate surgical risks during cesarean sections or postpartum hemorrhage management. Furthermore, understanding tubal anatomy clarifies why ectopic pregnancies most commonly implant within the ampulla of the fallopian tube, presenting a life-threatening surgical emergency that requires early detection.
