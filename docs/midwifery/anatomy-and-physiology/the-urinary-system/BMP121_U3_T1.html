Structure of the Kidneys, Ureters, Urinary Bladder and Urethra

Overview of the Urinary System in Pregnancy

During pregnancy, the maternal urinary system undergoes profound anatomical and physiological changes driven by hormonal shifts and mechanical pressure from the growing uterus. Progesterone causes smooth muscle relaxation throughout the body, leading to significant dilation of the renal pelvis and ureters, a condition known as physiological hydronephrosis of pregnancy. The right ureter is typically affected more severely than the left due to the dextrorotation of the uterus and the cushioning effect of the sigmoid colon on the left side.

Renal plasma flow and glomerular filtration rate increase by up to fifty percent early in the first trimester. This hyperfiltration lowers serum creatinine and urea levels. Consequently, a small amount of glucose or protein in the urine may be normal, but it always requires careful clinical evaluation to rule out gestational diabetes or pre-eclampsia. Midwives must recognize these anatomical adaptations to distinguish normal pregnancy changes from pathological urinary tract infections or obstruction.

Gross and Microscopic Structure of the Kidneys

The kidneys are reddish-brown, bean-shaped organs positioned retroperitoneally on the posterior abdominal wall. A longitudinal section reveals two distinct regions: an outer renal cortex and an inner renal medulla. The cortex contains renal corpuscles and convoluted tubules, giving it a granular appearance. The medulla is organized into conical structures called renal pyramids, which drain urine into minor and major calyces, eventually converging at the renal pelvis.

The functional unit of the kidney is the nephron, numbering over a million per kidney. Each nephron consists of a renal corpuscle—comprising a glomerulus enclosed by Bowman's capsule—and a long renal tubule divided into the proximal convoluted tubule, the loop of Henle, and the distal convoluted tubule. Blood is filtered through the glomerular membrane under high pressure, initiating urine formation through ultrafiltration.

The Ureters: Pathway and Anatomical Relations

Ureters are muscular tubes measuring roughly twenty-five to thirty centimeters in length, responsible for conveying urine from the renal pelvis to the urinary bladder via peristaltic contractions. They descend retroperitoneally along the psoas major muscle, crossing the pelvic brim near the bifurcation of the common iliac artery.

Throughout their descent, ureters feature three physiological narrowings where kidney stones or surgical trauma may cause obstruction:

  • Pelviureteric junction: Where the renal pelvis narrows into the ureter.
  • Pelvic brim crossing: Where the ureter crosses over the external iliac vessels.
  • Vesicoureteric junction: Where the ureter pierces the muscular wall of the bladder at an oblique angle.

This oblique entry acts as a one-way valve, preventing the backflow of urine toward the kidneys when the bladder fills and contracts.

Anatomy and Capacity of the Urinary Bladder

The urinary bladder is a hollow, distensible muscular organ situated in the pelvic cavity directly behind the pubic symphysis. In women, it lies anterior to the vagina and inferior to the uterus, an anatomical relationship that makes it vulnerable to trauma during obstructed labor or pelvic surgery.

The wall of the bladder consists of a transitional epithelium lining, a thick smooth muscle network known as the detrusor muscle, and an outer adventitia or peritoneum. The interior floor of the bladder features a smooth triangular region called the trigone, bounded by the two ureteric orifices superiorly and the internal urethral orifice inferiorly.

While the average adult bladder can comfortably hold between three to five hundred milliliters of urine, sensory stretch receptors in the wall trigger the micturition reflex well before maximum capacity is reached.

The Urethra: Differences Between Male and Female Structure

The urethra is the terminal tube of the urinary tract, draining urine from the bladder exteriorly. Significant structural differences exist between sexes due to reproductive anatomy.

  • Female Urethra: Measures approximately four centimeters in length. It opens into the vestibule of the vagina between the clitoris and the vaginal opening. Its short length and close proximity to the anal opening make women significantly more prone to urinary tract infections.
  • Male Urethra: Measures roughly twenty centimeters in length and is divided into prostatic, membranous, and spongy segments. It serves a dual role in both the urinary and reproductive systems, conveying semen as well as urine.

Both urethras feature an internal urethral sphincter composed of involuntary smooth muscle and an external urethral sphincter composed of voluntary skeletal muscle within the pelvic floor.

Clinical Relevance to Midwifery Practice

Understanding the structure of the urinary tract is vital for safe and effective midwifery care. During antenatal visits, routine urinalysis detects asymptomatic bacteriuria, gestational diabetes, and protein loss associated with hypertensive disorders. Midwives must perform aseptic catheterization when necessary—such as during prolonged labor or postpartum urinary retention—taking care to avoid trauma to the short female urethra.

Postpartum, the bladder has temporarily reduced tone and increased capacity due to birth trauma and regional anesthesia. This combination creates a high risk for silent overdistension and overflow incontinence. Midwives actively monitor for postpartum urine output, encouraging frequent voiding and assessing for bladder fullness to prevent uterine atony and postpartum hemorrhage.
