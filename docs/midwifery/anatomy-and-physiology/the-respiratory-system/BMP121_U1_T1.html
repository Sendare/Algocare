Anatomy of the Upper and Lower Respiratory Tracts

Introduction to the Respiratory System in Midwifery Care

Midwives must understand the respiratory system because pregnancy places enormous demands on a woman's breathing. Every cell in both the mother and the growing fetus depends on a continuous supply of oxygen and the efficient removal of carbon dioxide. Hormonal shifts and the physical growth of the uterus alter chest mechanics from the early weeks of gestation. Recognizing normal respiratory changes helps midwives quickly spot warning signs of respiratory illness, such as asthma flare-ups or pneumonia. Safe management of pregnant women during labor and the postpartum period requires a firm grasp of how air moves through the upper and lower air passages.

Division Between Upper and Lower Respiratory Tracts

Anatomists divide the respiratory tract into two main sections based on structure and function. The upper respiratory tract sits outside the chest cavity, consisting of the nose, nasal cavity, sinuses, and pharynx. The lower respiratory tract is housed within the thoracic cavity, beginning at the larynx and extending downward through the trachea, bronchi, and lungs. The larynx acts as the anatomical boundary marker separating the two regions. This division helps clinicians locate infections and foreign objects, as upper tract conditions often remain localized while lower tract disorders can severely impair gas exchange.

Structures and Functions of the Upper Respiratory Tract

The upper respiratory tract conditions incoming air before it reaches the delicate tissues of the lungs.

  • Nose and Nasal Cavity: Filter dust and large particles using coarse hairs called vibrissae, while a rich network of blood vessels warms the inspired air.
  • Paranasal Sinuses: Air-filled cavities in the skull bones that lighten head weight and add resonance to the voice.
  • Pharynx: A muscular funnel-shaped tube shared by both the respiratory and digestive systems, directing air toward the larynx and food toward the esophagus.

Mucus membranes lining these structures trap foreign debris, and tiny cilia sweep the trapped material toward the throat to be swallowed or expelled.

Structures and Functions of the Lower Respiratory Tract

The lower respiratory tract handles the crucial task of conducting air deep into the chest and executing gas exchange.

  • Larynx: Contains the vocal cords and the epiglottis, a flap that closes over the windpipe during swallowing to prevent choking.
  • Trachea: A rigid tube supported by C-shaped rings of cartilage that keeps the airway open against external pressure.
  • Bronchi and Bronchioles: The trachea branches into left and right main bronchi, which subdivide repeatedly into smaller tubes called bronchioles.
  • Alveoli: Tiny air sacs wrapped in capillary networks where oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide leaves it.

The entire inner lining features ciliated epithelium that continually moves mucus upward, creating what is known as the mucociliary escalator.

Physiological Adaptations of the Respiratory Tract During Pregnancy

Maternal hormones, especially progesterone, directly stimulate the respiratory control center in the brain. This stimulation causes pregnant women to breathe deeper and slightly faster, increasing their minute ventilation. Anatomically, the growing uterus pushes upward against the abdominal organs, which in turn elevates the diaphragm by up to four centimeters. Although the chest cavity shortens vertically, it flares outward horizontally, increasing the transverse diameter of the rib cage. These combined adjustments ensure that the mother takes in enough oxygen to satisfy both her own heightened metabolic rate and the continuous needs of the placenta.
