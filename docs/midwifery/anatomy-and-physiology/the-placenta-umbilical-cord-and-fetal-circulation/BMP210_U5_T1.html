Placental Structure, Development, and Microscopic Anatomy

Embryological Origins and Early Placental Development

Placental development begins right after fertilization and implantation. The outer layer of the blastocyst, known as the trophoblast, rapidly differentiates into two distinct layers as it burrows into the uterine decidua: the inner syncytiotrophoblast, a multinucleated protoplasmic mass without distinct cell boundaries, and the outer cytotrophoblast, a layer of active, mononucleated cuboidal cells.

As the syncytiotrophoblast erodes maternal capillaries, spaces called lacunae form within it. These spaces soon merge with maternal sinusoidal blood vessels, establishing the very first primitive uteroplacental circulation around the end of the second week. Mesenchymal tissue then grows into the primary villi, transforming them into secondary villi, and fetal blood vessels begin to differentiate within this mesenchyme, creating tertiary villi by the end of the fourth week.

Gross Anatomy and Maternal-Fetal Surface Features

At term, a normal human placenta is a discoid organ weighing roughly 500 grams, measuring about 15 to 20 centimeters across and roughly 2 to 3 centimeters thick at its center. Examination of a delivered placenta reveals two distinctly contrasting surfaces:

  • Fetal Surface: Covered by the shiny, translucent amnion, showing the insertion of the umbilical cord (usually centrally or eccentrically inserted) and radiating chorionic vessels branching out towards the margins.
  • Maternal Surface: Dark red and divided into 15 to 20 convex areas called cotyledons, covered by a thin layer of greyish decidua basalis shed from the uterine wall during delivery.

Microscopic Anatomy of the Placental Villi

The functional units of the placenta are the chorionic villi. Structurally, these branching projections evolve continuously throughout gestation to maximize surface area for exchange.

  • Stem Villi: Large anchoring branches that span from the chorionic plate to the decidua basalis, providing mechanical stability.
  • Intermediate Villi: Smaller branches providing structural scaffolding for the vascular tree.
  • Terminal Villi: Tiny, highly capillary-rich final branches where the actual physiological exchange of gases and nutrients takes place between mother and fetus.

As pregnancy progresses toward term, the cellular layers covering these villi thin out considerably. The cytotrophoblast cells become sparse, and the syncytiotrophoblast forms thin epithelial plates directly adjacent to fetal capillary endothelium, shortening the diffusion distance.

The Placental Membrane and Barrier Function

The placental membrane (often called the placental barrier) separates maternal blood in the intervillous space from fetal blood inside the capillary lumen. In early pregnancy, this membrane consists of four distinct layers:

  1. Syncytiotrophoblast
  2. Cytotrophoblast (Langhans layer)
  3. Villous connective tissue (mesenchyme)
  4. Endothelium of the fetal capillaries

By the third trimester, the cytotrophoblast cells largely disappear in many areas, and the membrane thins down to just the syncytiotrophoblast, a minimal basement membrane, and the capillary endothelium. Despite its name as a "barrier," most substances—including drugs, alcohol, and certain infectious agents—can cross it, meaning it is selectively permeable rather than an absolute block.

Cotyledons and Functional Compartmentalization

The maternal side of the placenta is divided into anatomical units called cotyledons. Each cotyledon receives maternal blood through several spiral arteries that pierce the decidual plate, pumping blood directly into the intervillous space under maternal arterial pressure.

Once in the intervillous space, maternal blood washes over the branching terminal villi, allowing gas and nutrient exchange before draining out through endometrial venules. The partitioning of the placenta into multiple semi-independent cotyledons is clinically significant: if a localized area of the placenta experiences infarction or damage, neighboring cotyledons can often maintain adequate fetal oxygenation and support normal pregnancy continuation.
