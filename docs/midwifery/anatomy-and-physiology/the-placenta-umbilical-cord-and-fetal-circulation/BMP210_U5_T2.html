Functions of the Placenta: Respiratory, Nutritive, Excretory, Endocrine

Introduction to Placental Physiology in Midwifery

The placenta is a temporary yet vital organ that acts as the physiological bridge between the mother and the fetus. In Nigerian midwifery practice, understanding how this organ operates is fundamental to interpreting antenatal findings and managing complications like intrauterine growth restriction. It performs multiple organ-like duties on behalf of the developing baby, substituting for the fetal lungs, kidneys, gut, and endocrine glands until birth.

Respiratory Function: Gas Exchange

The placenta functions as the fetal lung. Oxygen crosses the fetal-maternal barrier from maternal blood in the intervillous space into the fetal capillaries via simple diffusion. Carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction, passing from the fetus to the mother to be exhaled through her lungs. Fetal hemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen than adult hemoglobin, ensuring adequate oxygen uptake even in low-pressure gradients.

Nutritive Function: Transport of Essential Nutrients

Since the fetal gastrointestinal tract cannot process food, the placenta transfers all necessary building blocks for growth.

  • Glucose: Transferred rapidly via facilitated diffusion to serve as the primary energy source for the fetus.
  • Amino acids: Actively transported against a concentration gradient to build fetal proteins and tissues.
  • Vitamins and Minerals: Iron, calcium, and water-soluble vitamins are actively concentrated on the fetal side, sometimes depleting maternal stores if dietary intake is poor.

Excretory Function: Removal of Fetal Metabolic Waste

Metabolic waste products generated by fetal cellular activity must be cleared continuously to prevent toxicity. Urea, creatinine, and uric acid pass freely across the placental membrane from the fetal umbilical arteries into the maternal blood circulating in the intervillous space. The mother's own kidneys then excrete these waste products alongside her own. When placental perfusion drops, these toxins accumulate rapidly in the fetal circulation.

Endocrine Function: Hormones of Pregnancy

The placenta operates as a temporary endocrine gland, synthesizing crucial hormones that maintain the intrauterine environment.

  • Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG): Signals the corpus luteum to keep producing progesterone early in gestation.
  • Progesterone: Relaxes smooth muscle and maintains the uterine lining.
  • Estrogen: Stimulates uterine growth and maternal breast development.
  • Human Placental Lactogen (hPL): Modifies maternal metabolism to ensure adequate glucose availability for the fetus.

Clinical Implications of Impaired Placental Function

When placental efficiency is compromised by conditions like pre-eclampsia, chronic hypertension, or post-term gestation, the consequences for the fetus are severe. Chronic uteroplacental insufficiency restricts the transfer of oxygen and nutrients, leading directly to intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), chronic fetal hypoxia, and low Apgar scores at birth. Midwives must monitor fundal height progression and fetal movements closely during antenatal visits to detect early signs of placental failure.
