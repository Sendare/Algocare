Nervous Tissues

Introduction to Nervous Tissues in the Human Body

Nervous tissue forms the primary communication network of the human body, allowing us to sense our environment, integrate information, and trigger rapid responses. Located within the central nervous system (the brain and spinal cord) and the peripheral nervous system (all the nerves branching outward), this specialized tissue coordinates every single bodily function. Whether you are blinking from a bright light or your heart is beating faster during a contraction, nervous tissue is working behind the scenes.

Unlike other tissues that repair themselves easily, mature nervous tissue has very limited capacity to regenerate once damaged. This makes understanding its structure and protection vitally important for midwives and nurses caring for patients with neurological injuries, birth defects like spina bifida, or birth asphyxia.

The Neuron: Structure and Functional Anatomy

Neurons are the structural and functional units of the nervous system, specialized to transmit electrical signals across long distances. Each neuron consists of distinct regions designed for specific tasks:

  • Cell body (soma): The metabolic center containing the nucleus and organelles, responsible for synthesizing proteins and maintaining the cell.
  • Dendrites: Short, branching extensions that act as the receiving antennae, picking up signals from neighboring neurons.
  • Axon: A single, long fiber that carries nerve impulses away from the cell body toward other cells.
  • Myelin sheath: A fatty, insulating covering wrapped around many axons, which speeds up how fast electrical signals travel.

A common misconception among students is that all axons are myelinated; however, unmyelinated fibers do exist, though they conduct impulses much more slowly.

Glial Cells: The Supporting Cast of the Nervous System

While neurons grab most of the attention, glial cells (or neuroglia) outnumber them significantly and are essential for nervous system survival. They do not carry electrical impulses themselves, but they protect, nourish, and insulate neurons.

  1. Astrocytes: Star-shaped cells in the central nervous system that form the blood-brain barrier and mop up excess ions and neurotransmitters.
  2. Oligodendrocytes: Wrap around central nervous system axons to form the insulating myelin sheath.
  3. Schwann cells: Perform the exact same myelin-forming job as oligodendrocytes, but specifically within the peripheral nervous system.
  4. Microglia: Act as the immune system watchdogs of the brain, swallowing up debris, bacteria, and dead cells.

Generation and Conduction of Nerve Impulses

A nerve impulse, or action potential, is an electrical charge that travels down an axon. In its resting state, a neuron maintains a resting membrane potential, meaning the inside of the cell is slightly more negative compared to the outside, largely due to sodium and potassium ions.

When a strong enough stimulus hits the dendrites, voltage-gated channels open. Sodium ions rush into the cell, making the inside temporarily positive—a process called depolarization. Immediately after, potassium channels open, allowing potassium to rush out to restore the negative interior (repolarization). This wave of electrical change sweeps rapidly down the axon. In myelinated fibers, the signal jumps from one gap in the myelin (Node of Ranvier) to the next, speeding up transmission tremendously.

Synaptic Transmission: How Neurons Communicate

Neurons do not actually touch one another. The tiny gap separating an axon terminal of one neuron from the dendrite of the next is called the synapse. When an action potential reaches the end of an axon, it cannot jump the gap directly. Instead, it triggers the release of chemical messengers known as neurotransmitters.

These chemical packets cross the synaptic cleft and bind to specific receptor sites on the receiving cell, sparking a brand-new electrical impulse. Once the message passes, enzymes quickly break down the neurotransmitter or take it back up to prevent continuous, unwanted firing. Medications used in pain relief, anesthesia, and psychiatric care frequently target these exact chemical synapses.

Organization of Nervous Tissue in Central and Peripheral Systems

Nervous tissue arranges itself differently depending on whether it belongs to the central nervous system or the peripheral nervous system. In the brain and spinal cord, tissues are organized into gray matter and white matter:

  • Gray matter: Contains mostly unmyelinated nerve cell bodies, dendrites, and neuroglia, functioning as the processing and computing hub.
  • White matter: Consists mostly of myelinated axons bundled together into tracts, serving as the superhighways carrying signals between different brain regions and the spinal cord.

In the peripheral nervous system, these bundles of axons are referred to as nerves, while clusters of cell bodies outside the central nervous system are called ganglia. This clear division allows efficient routing of sensory input coming from the body and motor output going back out to muscles and glands.
