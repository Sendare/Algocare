Autonomic Nervous System (Sympathetic and Parasympathetic)

Introduction to the Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) is the part of the peripheral nervous system that regulates involuntary bodily functions without conscious effort. It keeps vital processes running smoothly, such as heart rate, digestion, respiratory rate, pupillary response, and urination. Think of it as the body's automatic pilot.

Unlike somatic motor nerves that control skeletal muscles you can move at will, the ANS targets smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands. It operates mainly through two main divisions: the sympathetic nervous system and the parasympathetic nervous system. These two divisions generally work in opposition to maintain internal balance, known as homeostasis. During pregnancy, the ANS undergoes significant shifts to accommodate the growing fetus, influencing everything from maternal blood pressure to gastrointestinal motility.

Anatomical Organization and Pathways

Autonomic pathways differ from somatic pathways because they use a two-neuron chain to reach target organs. The first neuron is the preganglionic neuron, whose cell body lies in the central nervous system. Its axon synapses with a second neuron, the postganglionic neuron, located in an autonomic ganglion outside the central nervous system. The axon of the postganglionic neuron then extends to the target effector organ.

The structural layout differs between the two divisions:

  • Sympathetic pathways: Originate in the thoracolumbar region of the spinal cord (thoracic and upper lumbar segments). They typically feature short preganglionic fibers and long postganglionic fibers, with ganglia often clustered near the spinal cord.
  • Parasympathetic pathways: Originate in the craniosacral region (brainstem and sacral spinal segments). They feature long preganglionic fibers and short postganglionic fibers, with ganglia located very close to or inside the target organs.

The Sympathetic Nervous System: Fight or Flight

The sympathetic nervous system prepares the body for stressful, threatening, or high-energy situations—famously known as the 'fight-or-flight' response. When activated, it mobilizes resources to deal with perceived danger.

Key physiological changes include:

  • Cardiovascular: Increased heart rate and blood pressure to pump more blood to active skeletal muscles.
  • Respiratory: Bronchodilation (widening of airways) to increase oxygen intake.
  • Metabolic: Glycogenolysis (breakdown of liver glycogen into glucose) to spike circulating energy levels.
  • Ocular: Mydriasis (pupillary dilation) to let in more light and improve distant vision.
  • Gastrointestinal: Inhibition of digestion and peristalsis, as blood is diverted away from the gut toward skeletal muscles.

The Parasympathetic Nervous System: Rest and Digest

The parasympathetic nervous system dominates during quiet, resting, and non-stressful conditions, promoting the 'rest-and-digest' state. It conserves energy and drives housekeeping functions that maintain the body over the long term.

When the parasympathetic system is active:

  • Heart rate and blood pressure decrease.
  • Gastrointestinal secretions and peristalsis ramp up to digest food and absorb nutrients.
  • Pupils constrict (miosis) to protect the retina from excessive light when close-up vision is needed.
  • Urination and defecation processes are facilitated.

Crucially, it does not govern mass body responses like the sympathetic system does; instead, its effects are localized and discrete, allowing individual organs to be managed independently.

Neurotransmitters and Receptor Types

Communication across autonomic pathways relies on specific chemical messengers and membrane receptors.

  1. Acetylcholine (ACh): Released by all autonomic preganglionic fibers (both sympathetic and parasympathetic), as well as all parasympathetic postganglionic fibers. Receptors that bind ACh are called cholinergic receptors, split into nicotinic receptors (found at autonomic ganglia) and muscarinic receptors (found on parasympathetic target organs).
  2. Norepinephrine (Noradrenaline): Released by most sympathetic postganglionic fibers (with a few exceptions like sweat glands). Receptors that bind norepinephrine and epinephrine are called adrenergic receptors, divided into alpha and beta subtypes, each producing different excitatory or inhibitory effects depending on the target tissue.

Dual Innervation and Organ System Effects

Most major organs receive dual innervation, meaning they are supplied by both the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions. These systems often work in opposition, creating a dynamic balance. For example, the sympathetic system increases heart rate, while the parasympathetic system slows it down.

Organ / SystemSympathetic EffectParasympathetic Effect
HeartIncreases rate and forceDecreases rate
PupilsDilates pupils (mydriasis)Constricts pupils (miosis)
GutInhibits motility and secretionStimulates digestion and motility
BladderRelaxes muscle, closes sphincterContracts muscle, opens sphincter

Some blood vessels and sweat glands receive only sympathetic input, adjusting their tone by increasing or decreasing the baseline firing rate of sympathetic nerve fibers.

Clinical Implications in Midwifery Practice

Understanding the autonomic nervous system is vital for managing pregnancy, labour, and the puerperium. During labour, pain and anxiety heavily overactivate the sympathetic nervous system. This surge can release catecholamines that reduce uterine blood flow and inhibit effective uterine contractions, potentially prolonging labour—a phenomenon sometimes called fear-tension-pain syndrome.

Midwives use non-pharmacological comfort measures, reassurance, and a calm environment to lower sympathetic tone and encourage parasympathetic dominance. Conversely, certain obstetric emergencies or medications can trigger autonomic imbalances, such as severe orthostatic hypotension in postpartum women or hypertensive disorders where vascular tone is abnormally heightened.
