Pelvic Brim, Cavity, and Outlet Dimensions and Diameters

Introduction to Pelvic Architecture in Obstetrics

The bony pelvis serves as the rigid canal through which the foetus must pass during labour. Obstetricians and midwives study its architecture not just as static bone, but as a dynamic pathway influenced by hormonal changes during pregnancy, particularly the relaxing effects of progesterone and relaxin on pelvic joints.

The true pelvis, or minor pelvis, lies below the linea terminalis and forms the actual birth canal. Understanding its internal dimensions prevents many surprises in the labour ward. A narrow pelvis can stall progress, while a roomy one facilitates a smooth descent. Midwives across Nigeria frequently evaluate pelvic shapes during antenatal booking clinics, categorising them into the classic Caldwell-Moloy classification: gynecoid, android, anthropoid, and platypelloid. Each shape presents distinct advantages or obstacles when the baby negotiates the maternal passages.

Pelvic Brim: Boundaries, Planes, and Key Obstetric Diameters

The pelvic brim, or inlet, marks the entry to the true pelvis. Its boundaries include the sacral promontory posteriorly, the ala of the sacrum and arcuate lines laterally, and the pubic crest and symphysis pubis anteriorly.

Measuring the brim helps predict whether the foetal head can enter the pelvis, a process known as engagement. The key diameters of the pelvic brim are:

  1. Anteroposterior (AP) diameters: These include the conjugate vera (true conjugate, measuring about 11 cm), the obstetric conjugate (the shortest distance from the sacral promontory to the back of the pubic symphysis, about 10.5 cm), and the diagonal conjugate (measured clinically from the lower border of the symphysis to the sacral promontory, about 12.5 cm).
  2. Transverse diameter: The widest distance across the brim, measuring approximately 13 cm.
  3. Oblique diameters: Measured from one sacroiliac joint to the opposite iliopubic eminence, each measuring about 12 cm.

The Pelvic Cavity and the Curve of Carus

Once the foetal head clears the brim, it enters the pelvic cavity. This space is bounded by the sacrum behind and the sidewalls of the ischium and pubic bones in front. Because the anterior wall is short (formed by the symphysis pubis, measuring about 4-5 cm) and the posterior wall is long (formed by the sacrum, measuring about 10-12 cm), the cavity curves forward.

This anatomical curvature is known as the Curve of Carus. The foetus does not travel in a straight vertical line; instead, it rotates and follows an arcuate path matching this pelvic curve during descent. The mid-pelvis is the most roomy part of the cavity, circular in shape, measuring 12 cm in all diameters, which encourages internal rotation of the foetal head.

Outlet Dimensions and Diameters: The Pelvic Outlet Anatomy

The pelvic outlet is not a single flat plane; instead, it consists of two triangular planes sharing a common base between the two ischial tuberosities. Its boundaries include the lower border of the symphysis pubis anteriorly, the ischial tuberosities laterally, and the tip of the coccyx posteriorly.

The key diameters of the pelvic outlet are:

  • Anteroposterior diameter: Measured from the lower margin of the symphysis pubis to the tip of the sacrococcygeal joint (about 11 cm to 13 cm when the coccyx moves backward during birth).
  • Transverse diameter (bisischial diameter): Measured between the inner borders of the ischial tuberosities, averaging about 11 cm.

Midwives pay close attention to the subpubic angle formed by the inferior pubic rami. An angle of at least 85 degrees is necessary to allow the foetal head to crown safely without causing severe perineal tears.

Clinical Implications for Labour and Fetal Engagement

Recognising pelvic dimensions helps midwives anticipate mechanical dystocia—difficult labour caused by a mismatch between the foetal head and the maternal pelvis (cephalopelvic disproportion). Engagement occurs when the widest part of the presenting part passes through the pelvic brim, usually confirmed when the lowest part of the vertex reaches the level of the ischial spines (Station 0).

If the ischial spines are prominent or the interspinous diameter is narrow (less than 10 cm), internal rotation can fail, leading to persistent occipitoposterior positions. Midwives must document pelvic assessments carefully during early antenatal visits and remain alert to progress indicators during the active phase of labour to ensure safe referrals when dimensions are unfavourable.
