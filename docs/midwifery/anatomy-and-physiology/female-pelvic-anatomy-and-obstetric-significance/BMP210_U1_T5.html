Anthropoid

Introduction to the Anthropoid Pelvis

The anthropoid pelvis resembles the pelvic shape of anthropoid apes. It is characterized by a long, oval inlet where the anteroposterior diameter exceeds the transverse diameter. In Nigeria and across West Africa, studies suggest this pelvic type occurs with varying frequency, influenced by genetic and regional factors. Midwives must recognize this shape early during antenatal assessment, as it dictates how the fetus navigates the birth canal.

Morphology and Defining Skeletal Features

The skeletal architecture of the anthropoid pelvis presents distinct anatomical landmarks.

  • Pelvic Inlet: Antero-posterior diameter is significantly longer than the transverse diameter.
  • Side Walls: Often straight or slightly convergent, reducing the available space in the midcavity.
  • Ischial Spines: Frequently prominent and set somewhat closer together compared to a gynecoid pelvis.
  • Sacrum: Long, narrow, and often posteriorly inclined, adding depth to the posterior pelvis.
  • Subpubic Arch: Typically narrow, requiring careful assessment of the bituberous diameter.

Pelvic Diameters and Inlet Characteristics

Pelvic measurements in the anthropoid pelvis favor the longitudinal axis. The anteroposterior diameter of the inlet is maximized, while the transverse diameter is narrow. This creates a deep pelvic cavity that influences engagement. Because the transverse space is restricted, the fetal head cannot easily enter in a transverse position; it must align itself with the longer front-to-back space from the very beginning of labor.

Obstetric Implications and Labor Progress

Labor progress in an anthropoid pelvis has unique characteristics. Fetal heads frequently engage in the direct occipito-anterior or occipito-posterior position. Occipitoposterior positions are notably more common here than in a gynecoid pelvis. Midwives should anticipate prolonged second stages if the occiput remains posterior, requiring patience and careful monitoring of maternal exhaustion and fetal well-being.

Management of Labor and Clinical Care

Managing labor in an anthropoid pelvis requires a watchful, conservative approach. Midwives must avoid rushing the second stage simply because it takes longer. Continuous maternal hydration, emotional encouragement, and positional changes can assist rotation if the head is posterior. Routine operative interventions should be delayed unless genuine fetal distress or prolonged labor arrest develops.
