Obstetric Assessment of Pelvic Adequacy

Why Pelvic Adequacy Matters in Nigerian Midwifery Practice

Pelvic adequacy refers to the capacity and dimensions of the maternal bony pelvis to allow the safe passage of a fetus during childbirth. In Nigerian clinical settings, obstructed labor remains a leading cause of maternal mortality, morbidity, and obstetric fistulae. Many young women in rural and semi-urban communities face nutritional deficiencies during childhood, stunting skeletal growth and resulting in contracted pelves. Midwives serve as the first line of defense in identifying these structural limitations during antenatal care. Recognizing a contracted pelvis early prevents prolonged labor, uterine rupture, and devastating birth injuries. Assessing pelvic capacity is therefore not merely an academic exercise, but a life-saving competency required at every primary health center, general hospital, and tertiary facility across the country.

Anatomical Landmarks and Divisions of the Obstetric Pelvis

The female pelvis is divided into two main parts: the false pelvis and the true pelvis. The false pelvis lies above the pelvic brim and holds abdominal organs during pregnancy, holding little obstetric significance for labor passage. The true pelvis, situated below the brim, is the true bony canal through which the baby must pass. It features three distinct planes: the inlet, the cavity, and the outlet.

  • Pelvic Inlet: The entrance to the true pelvis, bounded by the sacral promontory, ala of the sacrum, arcuate lines, pectineal lines, and the upper border of the pubic symphysis.
  • Pelvic Cavity: A curved, roomy passage extending from the inlet to the outlet, containing the widest and most accommodating dimensions.
  • Pelvic Outlet: The diamond-shaped exit bounded by the pubic arch anteriorly, ischial tuberosities laterally, and the coccyx posteriorly.

Clinical Assessment Methods for Evaluating Pelvic Capacity

Evaluating pelvic adequacy relies on a combination of clinical history, external pelvimetry, and internal digital examination. Midwives review past obstetric history for signs of previous difficult labors or pelvic trauma. Clinical pelvimetry involves estimating the internal dimensions of the pelvis using sterile gloved fingers during a vaginal examination. While imaging techniques like X-ray pelvimetry and computed tomography provide precise measurements, they expose the fetus to ionizing radiation and are rarely used in modern routine practice. Instead, careful manual assessment remains the gold standard in resource-limited primary care settings across Nigeria. The examiner assesses key landmarks such as the diagonal conjugate, ischial spines, and the subpubic angle.

Performing the Clinical Pelvic Examination

A clinical pelvic assessment requires privacy, informed consent, and a gentle touch. The procedure is usually performed around 36 to 38 weeks of gestation when tissues are soft and compliant, or early in labor before membranes rupture.

  1. Diagonal Conjugate: The examiner inserts the index and middle fingers into the vagina, aiming to reach the sacral promontory. The distance from the lower border of the pubic symphysis to the sacral promontory is measured.
  2. Ischial Spines: Palpated laterally to check if they are prominent, sharp, or blunt, and to estimate the interspinous diameter.
  3. Sacral Sidewalls: Evaluated to see if they diverge, run straight, or converge.
  4. Subpubic Angle: The examiner places two fingers beneath the pubic arch to assess if the angle is wide (greater than 85 degrees) or narrow.

Interpreting Findings and Identifying Pelvic Contraction

Interpreting pelvic dimensions allows the midwife to classify the pelvis and anticipate labor challenges. A normal diagonal conjugate measuring 11.5 cm or more usually corresponds to an adequate obstetric conjugate of at least 10 cm. When the sacral promontory is easily reached, it suggests an inlet contraction. Prominent, sharp ischial spines and converging sacral sidewalls indicate a mid-pelvic contraction, which frequently arrests fetal descent. A narrow subpubic angle pointing to an Android or Platypelloid pelvic shape suggests an outlet contraction. Midwives must document these findings clearly and recognize that a contracted pelvis is an absolute indication for close monitoring and potential referral to a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility.

Integrating Findings into Birth Preparedness and Complication Readiness

Identifying a contracted pelvis during antenaturial visits transforms routine birth planning into life-saving intervention. Midwives must not wait until labor starts to address structural inadequacy. When clinical assessment reveals a suspected or definite pelvic contraction, the birth plan must be immediately revised. The pregnant woman and her family are counseled on the necessity of delivering in a facility equipped with surgical capabilities, such as a general hospital or teaching hospital. Transportation logistics, emergency funds, and blood donor mobilization are discussed well before the estimated due date. By integrating pelvic assessment findings into the birth preparedness and complication readiness plan, midwives actively prevent obstructed labor and reduce maternal mortality across Nigerian communities.
