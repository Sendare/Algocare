Platypelloid

Defining the Platypelloid Pelvis

The platypelloid pelvis, commonly known as the flat pelvis, is the least common of the four classic Caldwell-Moloy pelvic types. Structurally, it is characterised by a flattened brim where the anteroposterior diameter is significantly reduced while the transverse diameter is wide. Picture an oval shape that has been squashed from top to bottom. In clinical practice, encountering this pelvic type requires careful planning because the narrow front-to-back space at the pelvic inlet poses distinct mechanical challenges during childbirth.

Anatomical Features and Dimensions

Examining the bony landmarks of a platypelloid pelvis reveals specific measurements that set it apart from other pelvic shapes.

  • Pelvic Inlet: Kidney-shaped or flattened transverse oval, with a short anteroposterior diameter and a wide transverse diameter.
  • Sacrum: Often short and shows less curve than normal, sometimes tilted backward, which further reduces the available space at the inlet.
  • Sidewalls: Usually straight or slightly divergent, meaning the midcavity is often roomy once the baby passes the inlet.
  • Pubic Arch: Wide and obtuse, offering good space in the anterior outlet once the baby descends.

Underlying Causes and Development

The development of a platypelloid pelvis is influenced by a combination of genetic factors, hormonal influences, and nutritional history during childhood growth. Rickets, caused by vitamin D deficiency in early life, historically played a major role in softening bones, leading to the flattening of the pelvic brim under the downward pressure of the spine. While nutritional rickets is less common today in many regions, individual skeletal variations continue to manifest naturally without an underlying disease.

Obstetric Implications for Labour and Delivery

Labour management with a platypelloid pelvis presents distinct mechanical hurdles. Because the pelvic inlet has a narrow front-to-back measurement, the baby's head cannot easily engage in the usual vertex position. Instead, the fetus must enter the pelvis via a transverse arrest or a transverse engagement, where the longest part of the baby's head aligns with the widest part of the mother's pelvic inlet (side-to-side). Midwives must monitor progress closely, as failure to engage or prolonged first stage of labour can signal disproportion, often necessitating a caesarean section if descent halts.

Clinical Assessment and Pelvimetry

Identifying a flat pelvis relies on thorough antenatal assessment. While routine external pelvimetry is rarely used today, clinical evaluation during early pregnancy or early labour provides vital clues. Midwives assess pelvic architecture by evaluating the diagonal conjugate vaginally to estimate the available anteroposterior space at the inlet. If an inlet contraction is suspected based on clinical findings or previous obstetric history, imaging modalities like ultrasound or low-dose pelvimetry may occasionally assist, though watchful clinical monitoring during a trial of labour remains the standard approach in many settings.
