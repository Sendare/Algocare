Pelvic Soft Tissues and Floor Muscles (Levator Ani, Perineal Body)

Introduction to the Pelvic Floor

The pelvic floor is a vital funnel-shaped muscular sling that stretches across the outlet of the pelvis. It closes the bony pelvic outlet and provides essential structural support to the abdominopelvic viscera, including the bladder, uterus, and rectum. Without this robust muscular hammock, intra-abdominal pressure would constantly push pelvic organs downward. Anatomically, the pelvic floor consists of the pelvic diaphragm, the urogenital diaphragm, and the associated deep fascia. In midwifery practice, understanding this supportive layer is fundamental because these muscles must stretch considerably during the second stage of labor to allow the fetal head to pass, while simultaneously maintaining enough tone to guide the mechanism of labor.

Anatomy and Function of the Levator Ani

The levator ani is the primary, most substantial muscle making up the pelvic diaphragm. It forms a dynamic muscular sheet that cradles the pelvic viscera. To visualize its structure properly, think of it as three distinct paired muscle groups:

  1. Pubococcygeus: The innermost portion running from the posterior pubis to the coccyx, wrapping tightly around the urethra, vagina, and anal canal to act as a vital sphincter.
  2. Puborectalis: A specific sling of muscle originating from the pubic bone that loops directly behind the rectum, creating the anorectal angle crucial for fecal continence.
  3. Iliococcygeus: The posterolateral section originating from the arcus tendineus levatoris ani and inserting into the coccyx and anococcygeal raphe.

Functionally, the levator ani maintains resting tone to support continence, relaxes reflexively during elimination and childbirth, and contracts actively during pelvic floor rehabilitation exercises.

The Perineal Body and Its Obstetric Role

The perineal body (often called the central tendon of the perineum) is a pyramidal, fibromuscular node situated centrally between the vagina and the anus. Several important muscles converge and interlace at this node, including the bulbospongiosus, superficial transverse perineal muscles, external anal sphincter, and fibers of the levator ani.

During childbirth, the perineal body serves as the central anchor of the perineum. Midwives closely monitor this structure during the crowning of the fetal head. As the baby descends, the perineal body thins out significantly. If it fails to stretch or if descent is too rapid, it is vulnerable to tearing. Protecting and supporting this node helps preserve the structural integrity of the entire pelvic floor, reducing the risk of long-term prolapse or incontinence.

Fascial Layers and Endopelvic Support

Pelvic support relies on more than just skeletal muscles; a complex web of endopelvic fascia and ligaments holds the pelvic viscera in their correct anatomical positions. The endopelvic fascia is continuous with the extraperitoneal tissue and surrounds the pelvic organs. Condensations of this fascia form critical suspensory ligaments, such as the cardinal ligaments and uterosacral ligaments, which anchor the cervix and upper vagina to the pelvic wall and sacrum.

When these fascial supports weaken or stretch—frequently due to the physical strain of pregnancy and vaginal delivery—the organs they hold lose their suspension. This failure of fascial support is the primary underlying cause of pelvic organ prolapse, such as cystocele or rectocele, where the bladder or rectum bulges into the vaginal canal.

Obstetric Significance During Labor and Delivery

During the first and second stages of labor, the soft tissues of the pelvic floor undergo dramatic physiological transformations. Under the influence of relaxin and estrogen, connective tissues soften. As the fetus descends through the pelvis, the levator ani muscles are forced to stretch from a thick, bowl-shaped partition into a thin, elongated tube that lines the birth canal.

This stretching guides the fetal head into internal rotation; the backward slope of the levator ani directs the occiput forward toward the pubic symphysis. Midwives must appreciate this biomechanical interaction. Pushing efforts before the cervix is fully dilated and the pelvic floor has appropriately relaxed can cause excessive edema, fatigue, and trauma to these delicate muscular tissues.

Common Injuries and Clinical Implications

Childbirth places immense mechanical stress on the perineum and pelvic floor, frequently resulting in trauma. Perineal tears are graded from first-degree (involving only skin and mucous membrane) to fourth-degree (extending completely through the anal sphincter and rectal mucosa). Additionally, avulsion of the levator ani muscle from its insertion on the pubic bone can occur during forceps delivery or rapid second-stage pushing, leading to permanent structural weakness.

Midwives play a critical role in prevention through controlled delivery of the head, warm compresses, and careful perineal guarding. Recognizing and accurately repairing lacerations immediately postpartum prevents long-term complications such as urinary incontinence, fecal incontinence, dyspareunia, and pelvic organ prolapse.
