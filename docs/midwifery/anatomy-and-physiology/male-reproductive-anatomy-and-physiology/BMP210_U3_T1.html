External Genitalia: Penis and Scrotum

Introduction to Male External Genitalia in Midwifery Practice

Midwives in Nigeria routinely examine the male newborn during the initial postnatal assessment. While much of antenatal and intrapartum care focuses on the mother and the maternal-fetal unit, the immediate postpartum period requires a thorough physical evaluation of the neonate. Understanding the normal anatomy and variations of the male external genitalia ensures timely identification of congenital abnormalities and reassures parents during postnatal counseling. The primary structures visible externally are the penis and the scrotum, both of which play crucial roles in the urinary and reproductive systems.

Penis: Gross Anatomy, Structure, and Erectile Tissue

The penis is the male copulatory organ and the distal conduit for the urinary system. It consists of three main anatomical regions: the root, the body (or shaft), and the glans penis.

Internally, the shaft is composed of three cylindrical masses of vascular, erectile tissue enclosed in a fibrous sheath called the tunica albuginea:

  • Corpora cavernosa: Two parallel columns of erectile tissue lying side by side on the dorsal and lateral aspects of the penis.
  • Corpus spongiosum: A single column of erectile tissue located ventrally, which contains the spongy urethra.

The distal end of the corpus spongiosum expands to form the glans penis, capped by the external urethral orifice. A retractable fold of skin known as the prepuce (or foreskin) covers the glans in uncircumcised males, a structure frequently addressed in cultural and religious contexts across Nigeria.

Physiology of Erection, Ejaculation, and Micturition

The functional capabilities of the penis involve complex neural and vascular interactions.

  • Erection: A parasympathetic response causes the smooth muscle in the penile arterioles to relax. Blood rushes into the sinusoidal spaces of the corpora cavernosa, compressing the draining venules and creating a rigid state.
  • Ejaculation: A sympathetic response triggers the rhythmic contraction of pelvic floor muscles, propelling semen through the urethra following emission.
  • Micturition: The passage of urine is controlled by the coordinated relaxation of the internal urethral sphincter (involuntary) and external urethral sphincter (voluntary), allowing urine to flow out via the penile urethra.

While erection and ejaculation are primarily reproductive functions, micturition is an excretory necessity shared by both sexes.

Scrotum: Thermoregulation and Protective Functions

The scrotum is a cutaneous pouch suspended from the perineal region, divided internally into two compartments by a septum. Its primary evolutionary purpose is maintaining the testes at a temperature slightly cooler than core body temperature—about 2 to 3 degrees Celsius lower—which is essential for viable spermatogenesis.

Two key muscular structures regulate this temperature automatically:

  1. Dartos muscle: A layer of smooth muscle in the scrotal skin that wrinkles the skin to reduce heat loss when ambient temperatures drop.
  2. Cremaster muscle: A continuation of the internal oblique muscle that elevates the testes closer to the pelvic body wall during cold exposure or sexual arousal.

In hot environments, both muscles relax to allow the testes to hang lower away from the body trunk, dissipating heat.

Testicular Descent and Developmental Variations

During embryonic development, the testes form high up on the posterior abdominal wall near the kidneys. As the fetus grows, they migrate downward, guided by a fibrous cord called the gubernaculum, passing through the inguinal canal to reach the scrotum by the final trimester of gestation.

Full-term male infants typically have fully descended testes in the scrotal sac. However, premature infants frequently present with cryptorchidism (undescended testes), where one or both testes have failed to complete their migration. Midwives must note that many cases of physiological undescended testes resolve spontaneously within the first few months of life as hormone levels stabilize and the infant grows, though persistent malposition requires surgical referral.

Common Clinical Findings and Congenital Abnormalities

Midwives should be familiar with several common anatomical variations and abnormalities of the male external genitalia during neonatal assessments:

  • Hypospadias: A congenital condition where the urethral meatus opens on the ventral (underside) surface of the penis rather than at the tip.
  • Epispadias: A rarer condition where the urethral opening develops on the dorsal (upper) surface of the penis.
  • Hydrocele: A collection of fluid within the tunica vaginalis of the scrotum, presenting as a smooth, painless swelling that often transilluminates brightly.
  • Inguinal Hernia: A protrusion of abdominal contents through the inguinal canal into the scrotum, which requires careful differentiation from a simple hydrocele.

Assessment and Documentation during Neonatal Examinations

Performing a systematic examination of the male external genitalia is a core competency for midwives. The procedure should be conducted in a warm room to prevent the cold reflex from retracting the testes.

  1. Inspect the penis, noting the exact position of the urethral meatus, the size of the phallus, and the presence of the prepuce.
  2. Palpate both sides of the scrotum to confirm the presence of two separate, firm testes.
  3. Check for any swelling, discoloration, or asymmetry that might indicate a hydrocele, hernia, or scrotal trauma from birth.
  4. Accurately document all findings in the child's health record, noting any deviations that require pediatric or surgical review.

Clear documentation protects the healthcare provider and ensures continuity of care as the infant grows.
