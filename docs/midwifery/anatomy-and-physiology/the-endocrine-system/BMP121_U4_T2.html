Hormones Secreted and their Physiological Functions

Introduction to the Endocrine System and Chemical Signalling

The endocrine system is a network of ductless glands that release chemical messengers called hormones directly into the bloodstream. Unlike the nervous system, which relies on rapid electrical impulses for immediate reactions, endocrine signaling takes time to travel through blood vessels to reach target organs, producing prolonged and widespread effects.

  • Endocrine signalling: Hormones travel long distances via the bloodstream to reach distant target cells.
  • Paracrine signalling: Local hormones affect neighboring cells without entering the general circulation.
  • Autocrine signalling: A cell secretes a chemical messenger that binds to receptors on its own surface, influencing its own activity.

Hypothalamic and Pituitary Hormones: Master Regulators of the Body

The hypothalamus acts as the primary control center, linking the nervous system to the endocrine system via the pituitary gland. Often called the master gland, the anterior pituitary secretes trophic hormones that stimulate other endocrine glands like the thyroid, adrenal cortex, and gonads.

  • Oxytocin: Stimulates uterine contractions during labor and milk ejection during breastfeeding.
  • Antidiuretic hormone (ADH): Promotes water reabsorption in the renal tubules to concentrate urine and prevent dehydration.
  • Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH): Promotes growth and hormone release from the thyroid gland.

Thyroid and Parathyroid Hormones in Metabolic and Calcium Homeostasis

The thyroid gland produces thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3), which regulate basal metabolic rate, oxygen consumption, and cellular growth throughout the body. Adequate dietary iodine is essential for the synthesis of these hormones. Meanwhile, the tiny parathyroid glands embed in the back of the thyroid and secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH) to raise blood calcium levels when they drop too low.

Calcitonin, secreted by the thyroid parafollicular cells, works in opposition to PTH by lowering blood calcium through the inhibition of bone resorption.

Adrenal Gland Hormones and the Stress Response

Sitting atop the kidneys, the adrenal glands are divided into an outer cortex and an inner medulla. The adrenal medulla releases adrenaline and noradrenaline for rapid 'fight or flight' responses. The adrenal cortex produces vital steroid hormones: aldosterone, which controls sodium and water balance, and cortisol, the primary glucocorticoid that manages physical stress, blood pressure, and metabolism.

Without adequate cortisol and aldosterone output, the body cannot effectively manage physical trauma, severe infection, or sudden blood loss.

Pancreatic Hormones and Blood Glucose Regulation

The pancreas serves as both an exocrine and endocrine organ. Scattered throughout the pancreatic tissue are the Islets of Langerhans, which contain alpha and beta cells. Insulin is secreted by beta cells to drive glucose uptake into body cells, lowering blood sugar after a meal. Conversely, glucagon is secreted by alpha cells when blood glucose levels fall, stimulating the liver to release stored glycogen.

Maintaining this delicate balance prevents both hyperglycemic tissue damage and dangerous hypoglycemic episodes.

Reproductive Hormones and Endocrine Control of Gestation

Reproductive physiology relies on a complex interplay of hypothalamic, pituitary, and gonadal hormones. In females, oestrogen and progesterone direct the menstrual cycle and prepare the reproductive tract for potential implantation. During pregnancy, the corpus luteum and later the placenta take over hormone production. The placenta secretes human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) to maintain the corpus luteum, along with large amounts of oestrogen and progesterone to sustain the uterine lining and suppress further ovulation.

Relaxin is another key placental hormone that softens the cervix and relaxes pelvic ligaments in preparation for childbirth.

Feedback Loops and Clinical Implications of Hormonal Imbalance

Endocrine activity is tightly controlled through feedback loops. Negative feedback is most common, where a rise in a hormone level inhibits its further release, maintaining homeostasis. Positive feedback is rare and pushes a physiological process to completion, such as oxytocin release during childbirth.

When these regulatory loops fail, clinical disorders emerge. Excess hormone production leads to hyper-states, while insufficient production causes hypo-states, both requiring careful clinical management.
