Passive Immunity

Introduction to Passive Immunity

Passive immunity occurs when an individual receives pre-formed antibodies from another source rather than producing them through their own immune system. This type of defense mechanism provides immediate protection against specific invading pathogens. Because the body's own B-cells and plasma cells are not stimulated to create antibodies, no long-lasting immunological memory is established. Once the transferred antibodies naturally degrade or are cleared from the recipient's circulation, protection fades completely. Midwives encounter this concept frequently when evaluating how newborn babies derive early disease resistance from maternal sources before their own immune systems mature.

Natural Passive Immunity in Infancy

Infants rely heavily on natural passive immunity during their vulnerable early months of life. This transfer happens primarily through two distinct physiological pathways:

  • Transplacental transfer: Maternal IgG antibodies cross the placenta during the third trimester, conferring systemic protection to the foetus.
  • Breastfeeding: Colostrum and breast milk deliver secretory IgA directly to the neonatal gut, lining the mucosal surfaces against enteric pathogens.

Preterm infants miss out on the bulk of transplacental antibody transfer, which predominantly occurs late in pregnancy, leaving them exceptionally vulnerable to infections.

Artificial Passive Immunity Through Immunoglobulins

Artificial passive immunity involves the direct administration of concentrated human or animal antibodies, commonly referred to as antisera or immunoglobulins. Clinicians utilize these preparations when an exposed individual requires immediate neutralizing antibodies because there is no time to wait for an active immune response to develop.

Standard preparations include tetanus immunoglobulin for dirty wounds, rabies immunoglobulin following animal bites, and anti-D immunoglobulin administered to rhesus-negative mothers to prevent sensitization. These products give an immediate boost of disease-fighting proteins but carry a short half-life in the recipient's bloodstream.

Onset of Protection and Duration

The defining characteristic of passive immunity is its immediate onset paired with a strictly temporary duration. Protection begins almost concurrently with the infusion, injection, or ingestion of the antibodies, making it invaluable for post-exposure prophylaxis. However, because these proteins are catabolized and cleared by the reticuloendothelial system, their presence wanes steadily. Most passive antibody titers drop significantly within weeks to a few months. Midwives must understand this timeline so they recognize that an infant protected by maternal antibodies at birth will eventually become susceptible as those levels decline, necessitating the timely start of routine immunizations.

Clinical Indications in Nigerian Nursing Practice

In Nigerian healthcare settings, midwives and nurses apply the principles of passive immunity across several urgent clinical scenarios. Preventing hemolytic disease of the newborn stands out as a primary use, where anti-D immunoglobulin is given to rhesus-negative mothers following delivery or potential sensitizing events.

Another critical application involves managing tetanus-prone injuries, particularly in rural environments where rust or soil contamination poses high risks and unvaccinated patients require immediate tetanus antitoxin alongside vaccine doses. Recognizing these indications ensures timely interventions that prevent severe neonatal and maternal morbidity.

Limitations and Lack of Immunological Memory

While passive immunity saves lives in emergencies, it possesses significant clinical limitations. The complete absence of immunological memory means the recipient remains entirely unprotected against future encounters with the same pathogen once the initial dose clears. Furthermore, animal-derived sera can occasionally trigger hypersensitivity reactions or serum sickness in humans due to foreign protein recognition. Because passive antibodies do not prompt endogenous antibody production, they cannot train the patient's immune system to mount a stronger defense during subsequent exposures.
