Structure and Functions of Lymphatic Vessels, Lymph and Lymph Nodes

Introduction to the Lymphatic Drainage System

The human body maintains fluid balance through two main circulatory pathways: the cardiovascular system and the lymphatic system. While the blood vascular system pumps blood under high pressure through arteries and capillaries, the lymphatic system operates as a low-pressure drainage network. Every day, capillary filtration forces fluid out of the blood plasma and into the interstitial spaces surrounding tissue cells. Most of this fluid returns directly to the venous capillaries, but a significant portion—about three litres daily—remains behind. If left uncollected, this excess interstitial fluid would accumulate, causing severe tissue swelling known as edema. The lymphatic system rescues this fluid, filters it, and safely returns it to the bloodstream, while also playing a vital role in defending the body against infections.

Composition and Formation of Lymph

Lymph begins as blood plasma that leaks through the walls of blood capillaries into the surrounding tissue spaces. Once this fluid enters the initial lymphatic capillaries, its name changes to lymph. Its chemical composition closely resembles interstitial fluid and blood plasma, varying depending on the specific organ it drains.

  • Water: Forms the primary bulk of lymph, carrying dissolved nutrients, gases, and hormones.
  • Proteins: Plasma proteins that escaped blood capillaries are swept into lymph vessels, as they are too large to re-enter venous capillaries.
  • White blood cells: Lymphocytes and macrophages travel within the fluid, especially after passing through lymph nodes.
  • Lipids: Lymph draining from the digestive tract, known as chyle, contains high amounts of dietary fats absorbed from the intestines.

Network and Anatomy of Lymphatic Vessels

Lymphatic vessels form a one-way drainage pathway starting as blind-ended capillaries nestled within body tissues. These initial lymphatic capillaries feature overlapping endothelial cells that act like tiny flap-like one-way valves. When tissue fluid pressure rises, the flaps separate and let fluid in; when internal pressure rises, the flaps press shut to prevent backflow.

From these tiny capillaries, lymph flows into larger collecting lymphatic vessels, which travel alongside blood vessels and feature frequent internal valves resembling those found in veins. These collecting vessels pass through various lymph nodes before converging into larger lymph trunks, and finally emptying into two massive lymphatic ducts—the right lymphatic duct and the thoracic duct—which empty lymph back into the subclavian veins near the heart.

Microscopic Architecture and Zones of Lymph Nodes

Lymph nodes are small, bean-shaped organs scattered along the path of lymphatic vessels, acting as biological filtration stations. A fibrous capsule encloses each node, sending inward partitions called trabeculae that provide internal support.

The internal tissue of a lymph node divides into distinct functional regions:

  • Cortex: The outer zone contains dense aggregations of B-lymphocytes grouped into follicles, which often develop germinal centers during active immune responses.
  • Paracortex: The inner cortex zone houses mostly T-lymphocytes continuously moving between the blood and the lymph node.
  • Medulla: The central core contains medullary cords holding plasma cells and macrophages, alongside medullary sinuses where filtered lymph collects before exiting.

Filtration and Immune Defense Functions

Lymph nodes perform two critical duties: filtering lymph and initiating immune responses. Several afferent lymphatic vessels bring unfiltered lymph into the outer convex side of a node. As the lymph slowly percolates through a labyrinth of internal sinuses, resident macrophages phagocytose and destroy foreign particles, cellular debris, and bacteria.

Simultaneously, dendritic cells present captured antigens to passing T and B lymphocytes. If an infection is present, lymphocytes multiply rapidly inside the node, which is why nodes often swell and become tender during illness. Once filtered, the lymph exits the node through one or two efferent lymphatic vessels, carrying newly minted antibodies and sensitized immune cells back into circulation.

Fluid Dynamics and Return to the Circulatory System

Because the lymphatic system lacks a central pump like the heart, moving lymph against gravity from the lower extremities back to the thorax requires specialized mechanisms. Collecting vessels rely heavily on skeletal muscle contractions that squeeze nearby lymph vessels, pushing fluid upward.

In addition, thoracic pressure changes during normal breathing create a suction effect that draws lymph toward the chest. Smooth muscle walls in larger lymph trunks rhythmically contract to propel fluid forward, assisted by numerous internal valves that prevent any backward sliding.

Ultimately, all lymph converges into the thoracic duct—which drains the lower body and upper left quadrant—and the right lymphatic duct, draining the upper right quadrant. These ducts empty their contents directly into the left and right subclavian veins, successfully returning fluid and proteins back to the blood plasma.
