History and Trends in Nursing Practice

Ancient Roots and the Religious Era of Nursing

Long before modern hospitals existed, caring for the sick was tied to family duties, religious orders, and folk medicine. In many traditional Nigerian societies, healing was deeply communal, combining herbal remedies with spiritual rites led by traditional practitioners and family elders. Globally, early organized care was heavily driven by religious groups. Christian monasteries, Islamic traditions, and temples in various ancient civilizations provided shelter and basic comfort to the sick, poor, and dying. During these eras, nursing lacked formal science or structured training. Caregivers relied on empathy, observation, and inherited cultural wisdom rather than medical theory.

Florence Nightingale and the Birth of Modern Nursing

The Crimean War changed healthcare forever. When Florence Nightingale arrived at Scutari hospital, she found soldiers dying more from filth, poor ventilation, and overcrowding than from battle wounds. By enforcing strict sanitation, handwashing, and proper nutrition, she dramatically reduced the mortality rate.

Nightingale transformed nursing from an unskilled domestic chore into a respected, disciplined profession. She established the first organized school of nursing at St. Thomas' Hospital in London in 1860. Her philosophy centered on the environment—believing that clean air, pure water, efficient drainage, cleanliness, and light helped nature heal the patient.

The Rise of Professional Nursing in Nigeria

Modern professional nursing in Nigeria was shaped significantly by colonial history and missionary efforts. In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, Christian missionary societies established hospitals and health clinics, bringing Western nursing models to the region. Early training programs were largely apprenticeship-based, preparing local women and men to assist colonial doctors.

As the country moved toward independence, local leaders recognized the urgent need to indigenize and elevate healthcare training. This paved the way for local nursing ordinances, the establishment of indigenous training schools, and the eventual creation of national regulatory bodies to set standard curricula that reflected local disease patterns, such as malaria and maternal health challenges.

Milestones in Nursing Regulation and Education

To protect the public and maintain professional standards, nursing education shifted from unstructured hospital apprenticeships to formal academic institutions.

  • Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN): The statutory body responsible for setting standards of training, conducting professional examinations, and disciplining erring practitioners.
  • Transition to Tertiary Education: Moving basic nursing education from hospital-based schools into polytechnics and universities, awarding degrees and higher diplomas.
  • Specialization: The creation of post-basic programs in areas like perioperative nursing, paediatrics, mental health, and accident and emergency care.

Trends in Modern Practice: Technology and Informatics

Technology has fundamentally altered how nurses document, monitor, and deliver care. While paper charts remain common in resource-constrained settings, global and urban shifts toward electronic health records (EHRs) are streamlining patient information management.

Nursing informatics combines nursing science with computer science and information technology. It allows nurses to track patient vitals in real-time, reduce medication errors through barcode scanning, and consult guidelines instantly. Telehealth has also expanded access, allowing nurses in urban centers to offer health education and follow-up support to remote or underserved rural communities.

Evidence-Based Practice and Advanced Roles

Modern nursing has moved away from routines based solely on tradition ("we have always done it this way") toward evidence-based practice (EBP). EBP integrates the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and patient values to make optimal treatment decisions.

Alongside this shift, the scope of nursing has expanded. Advanced practice roles, such as nurse practitioners and clinical nurse specialists, allow experienced nurses to take on expanded responsibilities, including specialized assessments and collaborating closely with multidisciplinary healthcare teams to improve patient outcomes.

Current Challenges and the Future of Nursing in Nigeria

Despite undeniable progress, nursing practice in Nigeria faces persistent hurdles. Brain drain—the migration of skilled nurses abroad in search of better pay and working conditions—has left many local facilities severely understaffed. Other challenges include heavy workloads, inadequate medical equipment, and occasional inter-professional friction within healthcare teams.

Looking forward, the future of Nigerian nursing depends on aggressive infrastructure development, improved remuneration, stronger policy advocacy, and continuous professional development. Embracing leadership roles will enable nurses to shape health policies and drive meaningful reform across the nation's healthcare system.
