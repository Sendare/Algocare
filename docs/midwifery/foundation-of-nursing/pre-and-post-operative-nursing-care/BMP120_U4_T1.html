Psychological and Physical Preparation of the Patient for Surgery

Introduction and Psychological Assessment

Surgical procedures, whether an elective caesarean section or an emergency gynaecological laparotomy, induce significant stress for patients. As midwives, our role extends beyond physical clinical tasks to encompass the holistic well-being of the woman. High levels of pre-operative anxiety are directly linked to increased pain perception, higher requirements for anaesthetic agents, and a delayed recovery period. Assessing psychological readiness involves actively listening to the patient's fears, identifying misconceptions about the upcoming surgery, and evaluating their coping mechanisms and social support systems.

Managing Anxiety and Therapeutic Communication

Effective communication remains the most powerful tool for reducing pre-operative fear. Midwives should use simple, reassuring language, avoiding complex medical jargon that can heighten confusion. Explaining what the patient will experience step-by-step—such as the cold sensation of the intravenous cannula or the sounds in the theatre—creates a sense of predictability. Encouraging the patient to ask questions and validating their emotional responses builds trust and fosters a collaborative therapeutic relationship.

Informed Consent and Legal Considerations

Informed consent is both a legal requirement and an ethical cornerstone of midwifery practice. It ensures that the patient fully understands the nature of the proposed procedure, potential risks, benefits, and alternative treatment options.

  • Voluntary agreement: The consent must be given freely without coercion, undue influence, or intimidation from family members or healthcare staff.
  • Capacity: The patient must be of sound mind and legal age to comprehend the implications of the procedure.
  • Role of the midwife: While the surgeon is responsible for explaining the medical details, the midwife often acts as a witness, verifies that the document is signed, and confirms the patient still understands and agrees.

Physical Preparation and Fasting Protocols

Physical preparation aims to minimize intra-operative and post-operative complications, particularly aspiration of gastric contents. Fasting, known as nil per os (NPO), is standard practice.

  • Clear fluids: Usually permitted up to two hours before elective surgery in uncomplicated cases.
  • Light meals: Generally restricted for at least six hours prior to induction of anaesthesia.
  • Hygiene: A thorough bath or shower helps reduce the microbial load on the skin surface, lowering surgical site infection risks.

Skin Preparation, Bowel Evacuation, and Bladder Care

Preparing the specific operative site requires careful attention to avoid trauma while reducing bacterial contamination. Hair removal, if necessary, is best performed using clippers rather than razors on the day of surgery to prevent micro-abrasions that harbor bacteria. Bowel preparation may be ordered for gynaecological or obstetric procedures involving the bowel to improve surgical access. Finally, emptying the bladder—either naturally or via catheterization immediately before theatre transfer—prevents accidental bladder injury during pelvic surgery.

Pre-Operative Medications and Final Safety Checks

Pre-operative medications such as sedatives, antiemetics, or prophylactic antibiotics are administered precisely as prescribed by the medical team. Midwives must verify allergies before administration. The final safety checklist involves confirming the patient's identity, surgical site marking, correct documentation, removal of jewelry, dentures, and nail polish, and ensuring all baseline vital signs are recorded and within safe limits.
