Special Bed Making: Admission, Operation, Cardiac Beds

Introduction to Special Bed Making in Nursing Practice

Special bed making is an essential nursing skill designed to meet specific clinical needs of patients admitted to a health facility. Unlike the standard unoccupied or occupied bed, special beds are structured to receive a newly admitted client, safely transfer a postoperative patient from the theatre, or ease severe respiratory distress in someone with a failing heart. Mastery of these techniques prevents pressure sores, reduces physical strain on the patient during transfers, and promotes quick recovery. Every midwife and nurse must understand the physiological rationale behind each fold, tuck, and placement of equipment.

Admission Beds

The admission bed, sometimes called an unoccupied or reception bed, is prepared to welcome a new client into the ward environment. Its primary objective is to make the transition into the hospital as smooth and comforting as possible while keeping the bed ready for immediate use.

  • Top covers: Folded back to the side or foot of the bed to allow easy entry for the incoming patient.
  • Accessories: A clean gown, towel, admission chart, and necessary diagnostic tools are placed neatly on the bedside locker.
  • Thermal comfort: Extra blankets are provided if the patient is arriving from a cold environment or experiencing chills.

Operation and Surgical Beds

An operation or post-operative bed is designed to receive a patient returning from the operating theater who is often unconscious, semi-conscious, or recovering from anesthesia. The main goal is to maintain a safe, warm environment, facilitate rapid transfer from a trolley, and provide quick access to emergency equipment like suction and oxygen.

  • Bed height: Kept level with the operating theater trolley to ensure a safe, smooth lateral transfer without straining nursing staff.
  • Protection: A waterproof sheet and drawsheet are placed across the middle third of the mattress to guard against bleeding, vomiting, or wound drainage.
  • Warmth: Hot water bottles or warm blankets are often used to prevent hypothermia, provided they are safely monitored to avoid skin burns.

Cardiac Beds

Cardiac beds are specially arranged for clients experiencing severe heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, or severe respiratory distress. These patients cannot lie flat due to orthopnea, a condition where breathlessness worsens when supine.

To create a cardiac bed, multiple pillows are arranged in a stepped or supportive formation at the head of the bed, allowing the client to sit upright or semi-Fowler's. A cardiac table with a soft pillow placed across the front allows the patient to lean forward, which engages accessory muscles of respiration and eases chest expansion. Footboards are sometimes utilized to prevent the patient from sliding down the inclined mattress.

Infection Control and Linen Management

Proper handling of hospital linen during bed making prevents the cross-contamination of pathogenic micro-organisms across the ward. Used linen should never be shaken in the air, as this disperses dust and infectious particles into the ward environment. Instead, linen must be rolled tightly inward toward the center to trap contaminants. Nurses must wear appropriate personal protective equipment, such as gloves and aprons, when handling soiled or wet linens. Dirty linen goes directly into designated laundry hampers or bags, keeping it off the floor at all times.

Post-Procedure Assessment and Documentation

Bed making is complete only when the nurse assesses the client's response and records the intervention. The nurse checks that the bed is free of wrinkles that could lead to pressure sores, verifies that safety side rails are secure if indicated, and ensures the call bell is within the patient's reach. Documentation should include the type of bed made, the time, any specific observations regarding the patient's skin or mobility, and how well the client tolerated the procedure. Clear records provide continuity of care across different nursing shifts.
