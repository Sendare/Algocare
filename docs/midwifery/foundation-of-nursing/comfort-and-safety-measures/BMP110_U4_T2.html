Simple Bed Making: Occupied and Unoccupied Beds

Introduction and Clinical Significance of Bed Making

A well-made bed does far more than give a ward a neat appearance. In midwifery and general nursing practice, the patient bed serves as a primary environment for recovery, rest, and skin protection. Clean, tightly pulled linen prevents friction and shearing forces that easily compromise vulnerable skin, leading to pressure ulcers. Furthermore, removing crumb debris and moisture-trapping wrinkles directly improves patient comfort and psychological well-being. Midwives must view bed making not as a mere domestic chore, but as an essential clinical intervention tied directly to infection control, patient safety, and comfort.

Principles of Infection Control and Ergonomics

Executing bed making safely requires strict adherence to infection control protocols and body mechanics.

  • Hand Hygiene: Always perform hand washing before and after handling linens.
  • Linen Handling: Hold soiled linen away from your uniform to prevent contaminating your scrubs. Never shake used sheets, as this disperses infectious dust particles and microorganisms into the air.
  • Hamper Placement: Keep the laundry hamper close to the bed to minimize walking with dirty linen.
  • Body Mechanics: Adjust the bed height to a comfortable working level to prevent lower back strain. Keep your feet apart for a wide base of support and bend at the knees, not the waist.

Gathering Essential Linens and Equipment

Organization saves time and prevents unnecessary trips around the ward. Before touching the bed, collect all necessary items in the correct order of use: bottom sheet, draw sheet (if used), waterproof pad, top sheet, blanket, spread, and pillowcase. Place collected linens on a clean bedside chair or overbed table, never on the floor or another patient's bed. Gather a laundry hamper or bag for soiled linen and ensure disposable gloves are ready if body fluid contamination is suspected.

Making an Unoccupied Bed

An unoccupied bed is made when no patient is currently assigned or when the patient is out of bed taking a walk or sitting in a chair. Strip the bed systematically, rolling each piece inward to trap debris. Wipe the mattress if necessary. Apply the bottom sheet tightly, creating neat mitered corners to secure the fabric. Add the waterproof pad and draw sheet in the middle third of the bed if required. Layer the top sheet, blanket, and bedspread, tucking them in at the foot and making a neat cuff at the head of the bed. Leave the bed in a low, safe position with the wheels locked.

Making an Occupied Bed

Making a bed while a patient remains in it requires clear communication, teamwork, and careful positioning. Explain every step to gain the patient's cooperation and reduce anxiety. Raise the bed to a comfortable working height and ensure the side rail on the opposite side is raised to prevent falls. Assist the patient to turn onto their side facing away from you. Loosen the dirty linen on your side and roll it close to the patient's back. Place the clean bottom sheet on your side, miter the corner, and roll the remaining clean fabric toward the patient's back next to the soiled roll. Help the patient roll smoothly over the ridge of linen onto the newly made side, then remove the old linen, pull the clean linen taut, and secure it.

Special Considerations for Patient Comfort and Safety

Every bed-making procedure must account for the specific clinical condition of the patient. For instance, women recovering from a difficult vaginal delivery or a Caesarean section may experience severe pain when turning, requiring gentle movements and adequate pain relief timing prior to the procedure. Patients with restricted mobility need toe pleats—created by loosening the top covers over the feet—to prevent foot drop and pressure on the toes. Always ensure call bells are within the patient's reach before leaving the unit.
