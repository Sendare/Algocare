Safety Measures in Patient Care

Introduction to Patient Safety and Clinical Risks

Patient safety is the cornerstone of professional midwifery and nursing practice in any healthcare setting across Nigeria. Clinical risks refer to any potential hazard that can cause accidental harm to a woman receiving care, her newborn, or even the healthcare worker. Understanding these risks helps midwives anticipate dangers before they result in adverse events.

Key clinical risks in maternal and child health wards include:

  • Medication errors: Giving the wrong dose of oxytocin or mistaking drug names.
  • Healthcare-associated infections: Spreading organisms due to poor hand hygiene.
  • Patient falls: Slips occurring when mothers are weak after delivery.
  • Communication breakdowns: Missing crucial vital sign changes during shift handovers.

Every clinical risk has a root cause, and recognizing these hazards allows us to build a culture of safety rather than one of blame.

Preventing Patient Falls and Mobility Hazards

Postnatal mothers are at high risk of falls due to fatigue, blood loss during delivery, and the sedative effects of medications or regional anesthesia like spinal blocks. A fall can lead to severe trauma, including postpartum hemorrhage reactivation or head injuries.

Midwives must assess every patient's fall risk upon admission and during each shift change. Simple preventive actions include keeping the bed in the lowest position, ensuring call bells or bed buzzers are within reach, keeping pathways free of clutter, and assisting mothers when they first attempt to walk after childbirth. Never assume a mother is fully steady on her feet simply because she feels fine while lying in bed.

Infection Control and Prevention of Cross-Contamination

Hospital-acquired infections threaten vulnerable neonates and postpartum mothers. Cross-contamination occurs when microorganisms transfer from one patient, surface, or healthcare worker to another.

Strict adherence to standard precautions remains our best defense. Hand hygiene must be performed before and after every patient contact using soap and water or alcohol-based hand rub. Proper waste segregation prevents needle-stick injuries and environmental contamination. Sharp instruments must never be recapped before disposal in safety boxes. Clean all shared clinical equipment, such as blood pressure cuffs and thermometers, with approved antiseptic wipes between every single patient use.

Medication Administration Safety and Error Prevention

Giving medications safely requires absolute vigilance. Midwives handle high-alert medications such as oxytocin, magnesium sulfate, and various antibiotics where a small calculation mistake can prove fatal.

Always apply the classic checks known as the rights of medication administration:

  • Right patient
  • Right drug
  • Right dose
  • Right route
  • Right time
  • Right documentation

Always verify the patient's identity using two unique identifiers before handing out any medication. If a prescription looks unclear or calculations seem confusing, pause and consult a senior colleague or the prescribing doctor rather than guessing.

Environmental Safety and Hazard Identification

A safe physical environment protects both patients and staff from everyday ward hazards. Midwives must constantly scan their working environment for potential dangers.

Common environmental hazards include frayed electrical cords, wet floors without warning signs, broken furniture, and blocked fire exits. Oxygen cylinders must be chained securely to walls or trolleys because a knocked-over cylinder can become a dangerous projectile. Report faulty infrastructure promptly to the maintenance department and place clear warning signs around temporary hazards like wet corridor floors.

Patient Identification Protocols and Communication Standards

Mistaken identity remains a leading cause of wrong-site procedures and medication errors in clinical practice. Every admitted patient must wear an identification band containing accurate, verified details.

Always ask patients to state their full name and date of birth rather than simply nodding to a name called out. During shift handovers, use structured communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). This ensures critical updates regarding a laboring woman's progress or a sick neonate are passed on accurately without missing essential clinical details.

Incident Reporting and Quality Improvement

Even with the best precautions, unexpected adverse events and near-misses can happen in the clinical area. A near-miss is an error that was caught before it reached the patient.

Incident reporting is not about finding someone to punish; it is a vital tool for learning and improving hospital systems. When a medication error or patient fall occurs, staff must complete an incident report objectively and factually. Management and senior midwives analyze these reports to identify system flaws—such as confusing drug packaging or understaffing—and implement changes that make future care safer for everyone.
