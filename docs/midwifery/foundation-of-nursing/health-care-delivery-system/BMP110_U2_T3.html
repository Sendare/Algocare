Structure of Secondary Health Facilities

Introduction to the Secondary Health Care Tier

Secondary health care in Nigeria represents the middle tier of the national health system. It acts as the first level of specialized referral, sitting directly between primary health care centers in communities and tertiary teaching hospitals. Patients who require specialized medical, surgical, or pediatric care that exceeds the capacity of a primary health clinic are managed at this level. In the Nigerian health sector, secondary facilities are primarily established and managed by state governments through their respective ministries of health or hospital management boards, though private secondary facilities also play a substantial role. Understanding this tier is essential for student midwives because the majority of complicated obstetric cases, such as prolonged labor requiring surgical intervention, are first managed when they reach secondary facilities like General Hospitals or Comprehensive Health Centres.

Secondary Level Health Care Delivery Structures

The physical and administrative structure of secondary health care in Nigeria is graded to ensure effective service distribution across urban and rural populations.

  • Comprehensive Health Centres (CHCs): Often located in rural or semi-urban local government areas, offering basic inpatient and outpatient services with minor surgical capabilities.
  • General Hospitals: Found in urban centers and major towns, serving as the backbone of secondary care with full clinical departments.
  • Specialized State Hospitals: Facilities focusing on specific areas like infectious diseases, orthopedics, or maternal and child health at the state level.

Core Units and Specialized Departments in General Hospitals

A standard General Hospital is organized into various specialized departments and functional units to handle diverse medical conditions. The Outpatient Department (OPD) receives non-emergency walk-in patients for consultation and diagnosis. The Maternity and Obstetrics Unit manages antenatal care, normal and abnormal deliveries, and postnatal services, working closely with the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) or special care baby unit. Other essential departments include the Emergency Room (Accident and Emergency), Operating Theatre, Laboratory Services, Pharmacy, and Radiology. For midwifery practice, the synergy between the labor ward, operating theatre, and the laboratory is vital for saving mothers experiencing postpartum hemorrhage or obstructed labor.

Staffing Patterns and the Multi-Disciplinary Health Team

Service delivery in secondary health facilities relies on a team of diverse healthcare professionals working in collaboration. The medical team is led by Consultants and Medical Officers across specialties such as obstetrics, pediatrics, surgery, and internal medicine. Nursing and midwifery personnel, including Registered Midwives and Registered Nurses, form the largest workforce, providing 24-hour bedside care, monitoring vital signs, and managing normal deliveries. Other vital members include pharmacists, laboratory scientists, radiographers, and medical record officers. Effective communication among this multi-disciplinary team reduces medical errors and improves maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

The Referral Pathway: Bridging Primary and Tertiary Care

The referral system is the structural mechanism that connects different tiers of the Nigerian health system. In an upward referral, a primary health clinic identifies a complication—such as pre-eclampsia or prolonged labor—and transfers the patient to a secondary General Hospital using a structured referral letter and emergency transport. Conversely, downward referral occurs when a patient stable enough for routine follow-up is sent back from a secondary facility to a primary health clinic closer to their home. A functional referral pathway prevents overcrowding in tertiary teaching hospitals and ensures patients receive care at the appropriate level without unnecessary delays.

Administrative Framework and Management Systems

Secondary health facilities in Nigeria operate under state government oversight, typically supervised by the State Ministry of Health (SMOH) or a dedicated Hospital Management Board (HMB). Day-to-day administration at the facility level is usually headed by a Medical Director or Chief Medical Director, supported by a Director of Nursing Services, a hospital secretary, and heads of various clinical departments. Financial management relies on a mix of government budgetary allocations, internally generated revenue through user fees, and state health insurance schemes. Administrative efficiency directly influences drug availability, staff morale, and the maintenance of essential medical equipment in labor wards.

Operational Challenges Facing Secondary Facilities in Nigeria

Despite their crucial role, secondary health facilities in Nigeria face persistent operational bottlenecks. Common challenges include inadequate funding, frequent shortages of essential drugs and consumable medical supplies, and unreliable public power supply which forces reliance on costly diesel generators. Additionally, the 'brain drain'—the migration of skilled doctors, nurses, and midwives abroad—has severely depleted staffing levels, leading to heavy workloads for remaining personnel. Out-of-pocket payment systems for healthcare also create financial barriers for vulnerable pregnant women, sometimes resulting in delayed care and increased maternal morbidity.
