Structure of Tertiary Health Facilities

Nigeria's Health Care Hierarchy and Tertiary Placement

Nigeria operates a three-tier health care system designed to make health services accessible across communities, local government areas, and states. The structure flows from the grassroots up to highly specialized national centers.

  • Primary Health Care (PHC): Managed mainly at the local government level, focusing on basic preventive care, immunizations, and treatment of common ailments.
  • Secondary Health Care: Managed by state governments, providing general hospital services, basic surgical procedures, and inpatient care.
  • Tertiary Health Care: Managed primarily by the federal government, serving as the apex referral center for highly complex medical and surgical conditions.

Understanding this hierarchy helps midwives recognize where a patient's clinical needs fit, ensuring appropriate movement through the health system.

Defining Tertiary Health Care in Nigeria

Tertiary health care represents the highest level of specialized medical care available in the country. Facilities at this level are often called teaching hospitals, federal medical centres, or specialized institutes (such as orthopedic or psychiatric hospitals).

Unlike primary or secondary facilities that handle common health problems, tertiary institutions are equipped with advanced diagnostic tools, intensive care units, and highly trained specialists across various fields of medicine and midwifery. In the Nigerian context, they are established by acts of parliament or state laws to provide three core mandates: specialized clinical services, training of health professionals, and medical research. Midwives working here encounter high-risk obstetric cases that require multidisciplinary teamwork.

Physical and Clinical Delivery Structures

The physical layout of a Nigerian tertiary health facility is vast, sprawling across specialized departments, wards, and laboratories. Key structural components include:

  • Outpatient Departments (OPD): Specialized clinics where consultants see referred patients by appointment.
  • Inpatient Wards: Specialized units including medical, surgical, pediatric, and obstetrics and gynecology wards.
  • Intensive Care Units (ICU) and Neonatal ICUs (NICU): High-dependency areas equipped with mechanical ventilators, monitors, and incubators for critically ill patients and newborns.
  • Diagnostics and Laboratories: Advanced pathology, radiology (CT scan, MRI, ultrasound), and blood bank services.

These structures allow for round-the-clock care and close monitoring of vulnerable patients, such as women experiencing severe postpartum hemorrhage or eclampsia.

Specialized Units and the Maternity Complex

Within the tertiary structure, maternal and child health occupies a major position. The maternity complex usually operates as a semi-autonomous unit within the hospital, housing dedicated labor suites, operating theatres for emergency cesarean sections, antenatal clinics, and postnatal wards.

Specialized units like the maternal intensive care unit (MICU) manage severe obstetric complications like severe pre-eclampsia, uterine rupture, and sepsis. Midwives working in these settings collaborate closely with obstetrician-gynecologists, anesthetists, and neonatologists. This multidisciplinary approach ensures that high-risk mothers receive prompt, comprehensive intervention, significantly reducing maternal and perinatal mortality.

Administrative Framework and Governance

Governance of federal tertiary health institutions in Nigeria follows a structured public service model. At the apex of administration is a Board of Management, appointed by the federal government, which sets policy guidelines and oversees institutional performance.

The executive leadership includes the Chief Medical Director (CMD) or Medical Director, who is a senior medical doctor responsible for day-to-day clinical and administrative affairs, working alongside the Director of Administration, Director of Nursing Services, and other principal officers.

Funding comes primarily through federal government budgetary allocations, internally generated revenue (IGR) from services, and sometimes grants or donor funds. Midwifery services are headed by senior nursing/midwifery administrators who advocate for staffing, equipment, and professional standards within the hospital.

The Referral Pathway and Linkages

The referral system is the bridge connecting primary, secondary, and tertiary health facilities. An effective pathway ensures that patients receive care at the most appropriate and cost-effective level.

  • Upward Referral: When a primary or secondary facility encounters a complication they cannot manage (e.g., obstructed labor or severe postpartum hemorrhage), they stabilize the patient and refer them upward to the tertiary center with a formal referral letter.
  • Downward Referral: Once a patient is stable and recovering at the tertiary center, they are referred back to secondary or primary facilities closer to their home for follow-up care, freeing up specialized beds for new emergencies.

Unfortunately, many Nigerian tertiary hospitals face overcrowding because patients bypass primary and secondary centers for minor ailments, highlighting a breakdown in the referral chain.

Roles in Medical Education and Health Research

Tertiary health institutions are academic hubs. Alongside patient care, they serve as training grounds for undergraduate and postgraduate medical students, student midwives, nursing students, and laboratory scientists.

Clinical instruction happens directly at the bedside, in clinics, and in operating theatres where students observe and assist experienced clinicians. Midwifery students complete clinical postings in these facilities to gain exposure to high-risk obstetric cases, neonatal resuscitation, and advanced maternal care technologies.

Additionally, these centers drive health research in Nigeria. Clinical trials, epidemiological studies on maternal mortality, and operational research conducted here generate local data that shape national health policies and improve clinical guidelines.

Current Challenges Facing Tertiary Facilities in Nigeria

Despite their critical role, tertiary health facilities in Nigeria grapple with significant systemic challenges that affect service delivery.

  • Brain Drain ("Japa" Syndrome): The mass emigration of skilled doctors, specialist nurses, and midwives seeking better working conditions abroad leaves units understaffed.
  • Inadequate Infrastructure and Power Supply: Frequent power outages and erratic water supply force hospitals to rely on expensive diesel generators, straining operational budgets.
  • Out-of-Pocket Expenditure: Many patients pay for care out of pocket because national health insurance coverage remains low, leading to delayed treatments or financial catastrophe.
  • Equipment Breakdown: Advanced diagnostic machines often break down, and bureaucratic delays hinder prompt repairs or procurement of replacement parts.

Midwives often work under immense pressure in these environments, requiring resilience and innovative resource management to save lives.
