Client/Patient in Community and Health Institution

The Concept of Client versus Patient

In healthcare practice across Nigeria, the terms client and patient are often used interchangeably, yet they represent fundamentally different perspectives on the receiver of care. A client typically denotes an individual engaging with preventive, promotional, or primary healthcare services within a community setting. This person is usually active, autonomous, and participating in decisions regarding their well-being. Conversely, a patient generally refers to an individual receiving medical treatment within a formal health institution, such as a primary health care clinic, general hospital, or tertiary medical center. The transition from client to patient often signifies a shift from wellness and self-management to acute illness and institutional dependence.

The Community Client: Empowerment and Prevention

Community healthcare places the individual at the center of their own wellness journey. As a community client, the person retains primary decision-making power over lifestyle choices, family planning, and home-based care practices. Midwives and community health extension workers act as facilitators, educators, and guides rather than authoritarian directors.

  • Autonomy: Clients direct their daily routines and decide whether to adopt health advice.
  • Partnership: Care is negotiated collaboratively based on cultural norms and available local resources.
  • Focus: Primary prevention, immunizations, antenatal health promotion, and chronic disease screening.

The Institutional Patient: Vulnerability and Medical Authority

When an individual crosses the threshold of a health institution for admission, they assume the patient role. This transition is marked by a surrender of personal autonomy to the institutional routine and professional expertise of doctors, midwives, and nurses. The hospital environment prioritizes clinical diagnosis, disease management, and safety protocols, which can sometimes alienate the individual.

Illness, pain, and unfamiliar hospital gowns diminish a person's sense of control, increasing their psychological vulnerability. Midwives working in institutional settings must recognize this power imbalance and actively protect the dignity of the women and newborns under their care, ensuring that clinical necessity does not entirely strip away personal agency.

Environmental Differences and Care Dynamics

The physical and social environment drastically alters how care is delivered and received. In a community setting, the midwife enters the client's territory—be it a mud-brick home in a rural settlement or an apartment in an urban center. This requires cultural humility and adaptability. In contrast, the hospital environment belongs entirely to the health institution. Patients must adapt to strange noises, shared wards, rigid visiting hours, and clinical smells. These stark differences affect compliance, comfort, and the overall therapeutic relationship between the healthcare provider and the care recipient.

Bridging the Gap: Referral and Transition of Care

Healthcare delivery systems rely on seamless movement between community services and institutional care. When a community client develops complications—such as obstructed labor during a home or primary health center birth—they must be transferred to a secondary or tertiary facility. This transition of care is a high-risk period. Miscommunication during handovers, delays in transport, or cultural clashes upon arrival at the hospital can lead to poor maternal and neonatal outcomes. Effective midwifery practice requires clear documentation, respectful referral notes, and preparation of the client for what to expect in the larger institution.

Advocacy and Holistic Midwifery Practice

Whether caring for a pregnant teenager in a rural primary health clinic or managing a high-risk postnatal mother in a teaching hospital, midwives serve as advocates. Advocacy involves protecting patient rights, ensuring informed consent, and bridging the communication gap between rigid medical hierarchies and human needs. Midwives must recognize that a patient's cultural beliefs, socioeconomic background, and fears directly influence their health-seeking behavior. Providing holistic care means treating the person, not just the disease or the gravid uterus, across both community and institutional landscapes.
