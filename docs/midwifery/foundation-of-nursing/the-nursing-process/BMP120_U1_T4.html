Planning

Introduction to the Planning Phase in Midwifery Care

Planning begins immediately after the midwife analyzes assessment data and identifies specific client diagnoses. During this step, the midwife maps out a clear course of action to address the pregnant woman's physical, emotional, and social needs. It bridges the gap between identifying a problem and taking action. A well-designed plan keeps the maternity team organized and ensures that nothing critical is missed during busy shifts on the ward.

Goal Setting and Outcome Criteria

Goals describe the desired changes in the mother's health status following midwifery care. Every goal must be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound, often remembered by the acronym SMART.

  • Short-term goals: Achievable within a few hours or a single shift, such as stabilizing blood pressure in pre-eclampsia.
  • Long-term goals: Require days, weeks, or the entire antenatal period, such as maintaining healthy weight gain throughout pregnancy.

Outcome criteria serve as the measuring stick to prove whether the goal was successfully met.

Prioritizing Client Needs in Obstetric Practice

In a busy Nigerian labour ward or antenatal clinic, a midwife often cares for multiple women simultaneously. Prioritization determines which client or problem receives attention first. Midwives typically use Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs or emergency response frameworks to rank problems.

Life-threatening complications, such as severe postpartum hemorrhage or eclamptic seizures, always take top priority over comfort measures like helping a stable mother walk to the bathroom. Addressing ABC (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) or severe obstetric shock overrides routine health education.

Selecting Evidence-Based Nursing Interventions

Interventions are the specific actions a midwife performs to help the client reach set goals. These actions can be independent, such as teaching a mother how to position her baby for proper breastfeeding latch, or dependent, such as administering oxytocin as ordered by the physician.

Every selected intervention must be rooted in current clinical evidence rather than outdated hospital customs. Midwives must consider facility resources, such as reliable electricity or clean water availability, to ensure the planned intervention can actually be executed in their local setting.

Developing and Documenting the Individualised Care Plan

The care plan is a written guide that communicates the mother's health status and required nursing actions to the entire healthcare team. In many clinical settings, it is structured using a multi-column format containing the nursing diagnosis, client goals, planned interventions, and space for evaluation.

Documentation must be clear, concise, and written promptly after assessment. A generic care plan copied from a textbook fails to serve an individual woman because it ignores her unique cultural background, family structure, and specific physiological responses.

Involving the Mother and Family in Care Planning

Care planning is a collaborative partnership, not a dictation from the clinician. Involving the pregnant woman and her relatives increases compliance with medical advice and reduces anxiety. Midwives must explain options in simple, local terms that the family understands, respecting their cultural beliefs regarding childbirth and newborn care.

When a husband or mother-in-law is included in discussions about nutrition or birth preparedness, compliance at home improves significantly. However, the midwife must always advocate for the pregnant woman's personal autonomy and safety if family traditions conflict with essential medical care.
