Nursing Diagnosis

Transitioning from Assessment to Diagnosis

The nursing diagnosis forms the crucial bridge between gathering patient data and planning effective care. Once a midwife completes the physical examination and history taking, a vast amount of raw information is available. Organizing this data helps identify abnormal cues, such as a postpartum blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg or a mother expressing overwhelming anxiety about caring for her newborn. Grouping related cues together reveals specific human responses to health conditions, shifting the focus from simply collecting facts to interpreting what those facts mean for the patient's well-being.

Defining the Nursing Diagnosis

A nursing diagnosis is a clinical judgment concerning a human response to health conditions or life processes, or a vulnerability to that response, by an individual, family, or community. Unlike medical conditions that stay fixed during an illness, nursing diagnoses change as the patient's response changes. In busy Nigerian wards, recognizing these responses allows midwives to take independent action, prescribing interventions that fall strictly within the legal scope of midwifery practice without needing a doctor's order.

Nursing Diagnosis Versus Medical Diagnosis

Beginners often confuse nursing diagnoses with medical diagnoses. A medical diagnosis identifies a specific disease or pathology, such as severe pre-eclampsia or malaria, and remains constant while the disease is present. A nursing diagnosis focuses on the patient's reaction to that disease, such as acute pain or risk for deficient fluid volume.

NANDA Format and Formulation

Most nursing diagnoses use the three-part PES format for actual problems: Problem (the NANDA diagnostic label), Etiology (related factors or root causes), and Signs/Symptoms (defining characteristics discovered during assessment).

  • Problem (P): The diagnostic label describing the human response (e.g., *Acute Pain*).
  • Etiology (E): Connected to the problem by the phrase "related to" (r/t), showing what is causing or contributing to the response (e.g., *related to surgical incision*).
  • Signs and Symptoms (S): Connected by the phrase "as evidenced by" (aeb), listing the objective and subjective data observed (e.g., *as evidenced by facial grimacing and a pain score of 8/10*).

For risk diagnoses, the format is shorter (two parts: Problem and Etiology) because no symptoms have occurred yet.

Types of Nursing Diagnoses

Nursing diagnoses fall into three primary categories depending on the patient's current clinical situation.

  1. Actual Nursing Diagnosis: A validated patient problem that is present right now, supported by defining characteristics like lab values or physical signs.
  2. Risk Nursing Diagnosis: A clinical judgment that a patient is vulnerable to developing a problem, such as *Risk for Infection* due to an open episiotomy wound.
  3. Health Promotion Diagnosis: A clinical judgment concerning a patient's motivation and desire to increase well-being and actualize human health potential, such as *Readiness for Enhanced Breastfeeding*.

Common Pitfalls in Formulating Diagnoses

Midwives occasionally make errors when writing nursing diagnoses that weaken the care plan. A common mistake is writing a medical diagnosis instead of a human response, such as writing "Malaria" as the nursing problem. Another error is legally implicating someone else in the etiology, like writing "Risk for injury related to doctor forgetting medication," which creates professional liability conflicts. Additionally, phrasing the diagnosis as a patient need rather than a human response—such as "Patient needs pain relief" instead of "Acute Pain"—misdirects the focus of care.

Prioritizing Diagnoses in Ward Management

On a busy postnatal or antenatal ward, midwives care for multiple patients simultaneously, each presenting with several nursing diagnoses. Prioritization is essential for patient safety. Midwives use established frameworks like Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs or the ABC priority scale to determine which issue demands immediate attention. Life-threatening issues, such as ineffective breathing pattern or risk for hemorrhage, always take precedence over comfort issues like acute pain or fatigue, ensuring that limited time and resources save lives first.
