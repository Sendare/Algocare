Overview of the Nursing Process

Introduction and Core Philosophy of the Nursing Process

The nursing process is a systematic, rational method of planning and providing individualized nursing care. It serves as the foundational framework for all clinical decision-making in midwifery and nursing practice. Far from being a rigid bureaucratic task, it provides a flexible yet structured approach to solving patient care problems. The framework consists of five continuous, interconnected phases: assessment, diagnosis, planning, implementation, and evaluation.

At its core, this process shifts care away from merely completing tasks toward holistic, patient-centered thinking. Midwives use it to address not just physical symptoms, but the psychological, cultural, and social needs of pregnant women and their families. Because clinical conditions change rapidly in maternity wards, this cyclical method allows the midwife to continuously review and adapt care plans in real-time.

Phase 1: Assessment and Data Collection

Assessment is the deliberate, systematic collection of data to determine a patient's current and past health status, functional status, and coping patterns. In midwifery, this involves taking a comprehensive obstetric history, conducting physical examinations like abdominal palpation, and monitoring vital signs.

Data falls into two distinct categories:

  • Subjective data: Information the patient tells you, such as nausea, fear, or pain levels.
  • Objective data: Measurable and observable findings, such as blood pressure readings, fetal heart rates, or laboratory results.

Gathering information accurately requires both clinical skill and active listening. If initial data collection is flawed, every subsequent phase of the nursing process will be built on a faulty foundation.

Phase 2: Nursing Diagnosis and Clinical Judgment

During the diagnosis phase, the midwife analyzes the assessment data to identify actual or potential health problems. A nursing diagnosis differs significantly from a medical diagnosis. While a medical diagnosis focuses on a disease pathology—such as pre-eclampsia—a nursing diagnosis focuses on the patient's human response to health conditions, such as deficient knowledge regarding warning signs or acute pain related to uterine contractions.

Using standardized diagnostic statements helps communicate patient needs clearly across healthcare teams. A standard problem-focused nursing diagnosis typically links the health problem to its related contributing factors and is supported by defining characteristics observed during assessment.

Phase 3: Planning and Setting Patient Goals

Planning involves setting priorities among the identified nursing diagnoses, establishing expected outcomes, and selecting evidence-based nursing interventions. Priorities are usually determined by urgency; life-threatening complications like postpartum hemorrhage always take precedence over comfort measures.

Goals and expected outcomes must be SMART: Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, and Time-bound. For example, rather than writing a vague goal like "patient will feel better," a proper nursing outcome states: "patient will demonstrate correct breastfeeding latch techniques within 24 hours of delivery." Clear goals give the healthcare team a shared target to work toward.

Phase 4: Implementation of Interventions

Implementation is the action phase where the midwife carries out the planned nursing interventions. These actions can be independent, such as teaching a mother how to massage her uterus to prevent postpartum hemorrhage, or dependent, such as administering oxytocin as ordered by a physician. Collaborative interventions involve working closely with other healthcare professionals like laboratory scientists or pediatricians.

During this phase, the midwife continues to observe the patient to detect any sudden changes or adverse reactions. Documentation is critical here; an intervention that is performed but not documented legally did not happen.

Phase 5: Evaluation and Outcome Measurement

Evaluation is the final phase of the nursing process, though it occurs continuously throughout patient care. The midwife compares the patient's current health status against the expected outcomes established during the planning phase.

Based on this comparison, one of three conclusions is reached:

  1. The goal was met, and the care plan can be concluded.
  2. The goal was partially met, requiring adjustments to the interventions.
  3. The goal was not met, necessitating a complete re-examination of the assessment data and nursing diagnoses.

This reflective step ensures that nursing care remains dynamic and responsive to patient outcomes rather than static and routine.

Clinical Benefits and Relevance in Nigerian Healthcare

Applying the nursing process within Nigerian healthcare settings addresses critical challenges such as staff shortages and heavy patient loads. By organizing care systematically, midwives can prioritize high-risk patients—such as women experiencing obstructed labor or severe malaria in pregnancy—ensuring that limited resources are directed where they are most urgently needed.

Furthermore, structured documentation improves communication during shift handovers, reducing medical errors in busy maternity wards. It empowers Nigerian midwives to practice autonomously, anchoring their clinical decisions in scientific reasoning rather than routine habit alone.
