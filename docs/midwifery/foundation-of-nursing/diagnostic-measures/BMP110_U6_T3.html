Observation and Recording: Height, Weight and Anthropometric Measures

Introduction to Anthropometry in Midwifery Practice

Anthropometry refers to the systematic measurement of the size, weight, and proportions of the human body. In antenatal and postnatal care, these physical dimensions offer immediate clues about a woman's nutritional status, baseline health, and potential pregnancy risks. Midwives use these measurements to track fetal growth indirectly, calculate medication dosages, and identify women who may require specialized nutritional or medical support. Regular tracking transforms abstract health goals into concrete numbers that guide clinical decisions.

Preparation, Equipment Calibration, and Safety Standards

Accurate measurements rely heavily on proper preparation and functioning equipment. Before taking any measurements, ensure the scale is balanced at zero and the measuring rod is securely fixed. Ask the woman to remove heavy outer garments, shoes, and bulky headgear to prevent skewed readings. Always explain the procedure clearly to gain her cooperation, especially since weight and height checks can sometimes cause body image anxiety. Maintain privacy and handle every piece of equipment with infection control standards in mind by wiping surfaces between patients.

Techniques for Accurate Height Measurement

Measuring height accurately requires strict attention to posture. Ask the woman to stand erect against the stadiometer with her heels together, arms at her sides, and shoulders relaxed. Ensure her heels, buttocks, and upper back make contact with the vertical surface. Her line of sight must be straight ahead, aligning the external auditory meatus with the inferior margin of the orbit, known as the Frankfort horizontal plane. Lower the headpiece firmly until it rests flat on the crown of her head, compressing any hair, and read the scale at eye level.

Procedures for Weight Measurement

  • Patient Positioning: Instruct the patient to stand still in the exact center of the scale platform with her weight distributed evenly across both feet.
  • Scale Adjustment: For beam-balance scales, slide the larger weight notch first until the beam drops, then slide the smaller weight until the indicator pointer floats dead center.
  • Digital Scales: Wait for the digital numbers to stabilize completely before noting the final reading.
  • Timing Consistency: Whenever possible, weigh women at the same time of day and using the same scale during follow-up visits to minimize diurnal variations.

Supplementary Anthropometric Assessments

Beyond height and weight, midwives utilize specific circumferences to gather deeper health insights. Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC) serves as a rapid screening tool for acute malnutrition, especially in resource-limited settings or emergency outreach clinics, using a color-coded tape wrapped around the left upper arm midway between the shoulder and elbow. Symphysis-Fundal Height (SFH) measurement, taken with a tape measure from the pubic symphysis to the top of the uterine fundus, helps monitor fetal growth progression during pregnancy.

Calculating Body Mass Index (BMI) and Interpreting Indices

Body Mass Index links weight and height to evaluate body fatness. The formula divides weight in kilograms by height in meters squared ($BMI = \frac{weight (kg)}{height (m)^2}$).

BMI RangeNutritional Category
Below 18.5Underweight
18.5 – 24.9Normal weight
25.0 – 29.9Overweight
30.0 and aboveObese

In pregnancy, BMI is typically calculated at the first booking visit to flag risks for conditions like gestational hypertension or macrosomia, though standard categorization shifts as pregnancy progresses.

Accurate Documentation and Reporting in the Patient Chart

Numbers collected during an assessment carry little clinical value if recorded incorrectly. Always document weight in kilograms and height in centimeters immediately after measurement, avoiding reliance on memory. Enter data directly into the patient's antenatal card and hospital register with clear, legible handwriting. If you notice a sudden, unexplained weight gain or loss between visits—such as a jump that might indicate pre-eclampsia edema—highlight the finding and report it promptly to the attending physician or senior midwife.
