Collection and Observation of Specimens: Stool

Clinical Indications for Stool Specimen Collection

Stool specimen collection is ordered by clinicians to investigate gastrointestinal symptoms that remain unexplained after a physical examination. Midwives practicing in Nigeria frequently encounter conditions such as persistent diarrhea, severe abdominal cramping, and signs of gastrointestinal bleeding in both antenatal and postnatal settings. Analyzing feces helps identify bacterial pathogens, viral agents, protozoa, and helminths causing infectious gastroenteritis. Screening for occult or hidden blood is also vital for detecting early signs of intestinal pathology, such as hemorrhoids or malignancies, which can manifest during pregnancy or the puerperium.

Preparation and Patient Education

Clear communication ensures the collection of a contamination-free sample. Explain the procedure simply to the patient, emphasizing why urine must not mix with the stool. Provide a clean, dry, wide-mouthed container with a secure lid along with a tongue depressor or collection spoon. Instruct the patient to pass urine first before passing feces, as urine introduces extraneous bacteria and alters chemical properties. For specific tests, dietary restrictions such as avoiding red meat or certain iron supplements may be necessary several days prior to testing to prevent false-positive occult blood results.

Stool Collection Methods and Macroscopic Observation

Collect feces using a clean bedpan, commode specimen catcher, or plastic wrap draped over the toilet rim. Transfer a representative portion—about walnut-sized for solid stool or 15 to 30 milliliters for liquid stool—into the specimen container using a clean applicator stick. Never collect stool directly from the toilet bowl water, as disinfectants and water contamination compromise laboratory analysis.

Perform macroscopic examination immediately after collection, noting several key physical characteristics:

  • Color: Normal brown color stems from bile pigments. Clay-colored stool indicates biliary obstruction, while black, tarry stool (melena) suggests upper gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Consistency: Hard, dry stool indicates constipation, whereas watery or loose stool points to infection or malabsorption.
  • Abnormal Constituents: Document the presence of mucus, pus, visible worms, or undigested food particles.

Special Handling for Microbiology, Parasitology, and Occult Blood

Different diagnostic tests require distinct handling protocols to yield accurate laboratory results.

  1. Microbiology (Culture and Sensitivity): Requires sterile collection containers. Transport the specimen to the laboratory immediately while fresh, or place it in appropriate transport media if a delay is unavoidable, to prevent overgrowth of normal flora.
  2. Parasitology (Ova and Parasites): Samples often require delivery to the laboratory while still warm, especially when searching for motile protozoan trophozoites. Some protocols require multiple stool samples collected over several days because parasite shedding is intermittent.
  3. Fecal Occult Blood Test (FOBT): Small smear samples are placed onto designated testing cards or guaiac paper. Chemical developers are then applied to check for color changes indicating hidden blood.

Infection Control and Safety Precautions

Feces contain numerous pathogens, including enteric bacteria, viruses, and parasite eggs, posing a transmission risk to healthcare workers and patients. Always wear clean disposable examination gloves during collection, handling, and macroscopic observation. Perform thorough hand hygiene with soap and water or alcohol-based hand rub immediately after removing gloves. Decontaminate reusable collection pans or commode chairs using standard hospital-grade disinfectants. Ensure specimen containers are tightly sealed and placed inside leak-proof biohazard bags before transport to prevent environmental contamination.

Documentation and Laboratory Transport

Accurate record-keeping completes the diagnostic process. Label the specimen container securely in the presence of the patient, including their full name, hospital number, date, time of collection, and the specific test requested. Complete the laboratory request form with relevant clinical details, such as recent antibiotic use or gastrointestinal symptoms. Document the collection in the patient's nursing notes, detailing the date, time, stool consistency, color, and any abnormal findings observed. Promptly transport the specimen to the laboratory to preserve sample integrity and ensure timely diagnosis and treatment.
