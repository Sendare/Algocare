Concept of Death and Dying

Defining Death and Dying in Midwifery Practice

In clinical practice, understanding the boundary between life and death is essential, especially when managing catastrophic obstetric complications where maternal collapse occurs. Death is traditionally defined as the irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions, or the permanent cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem. In the context of maternal or neonatal care, dying refers to the terminal phase of life where vital organs progressively lose function and medical interventions can no longer restore health. Midwives encounter this reality tragically during severe postpartum hemorrhage, amniotic fluid embolism, or unresuscitated neonatal asphyxia. Grasping these definitions helps midwives communicate accurately with grieving relatives and medical colleagues without confusion.

Cultural and Religious Beliefs Surrounding Death in Nigeria

Nigeria is culturally diverse, and death is rarely viewed merely as a biological event. Belief systems deeply influence how families perceive terminal illness, grief, and mortuary rites.

  • Traditional Beliefs: Many communities view death as a transition to the spirit world or an ancestral realm rather than a complete end. Sudden or maternal deaths may sometimes be met with specific spiritual interpretations or rituals.
  • Islamic Beliefs: Death is considered the will of Allah. Burial is expected to happen as quickly as possible, usually within twenty-four hours, accompanied by prayers and communal mourning.
  • Christian Beliefs: Death is often viewed through the lens of resurrection and eternal life. Memorial services and wake-keeping ceremonies provide structured avenues for communal support and grief expression.

Midwives must respect these diverse traditions without judgment, allowing space for prayers, family rituals, or quiet reflection within the hospital environment where feasible.

Physiological Changes Approaching the End of Life

As a patient approaches the end of life, the body undergoes predictable physical shifts as organ systems shut down. Recognizing these signs helps midwives prepare the family and provide appropriate comfort measures.

  • Cardiovascular changes: Peripheral pulses become weak and thready, blood pressure drops significantly, and extremities feel cold to the touch with potential mottling of the skin.
  • Respiratory changes: Breathing patterns alter, displaying periods of apnea followed by rapid breathing, commonly known as Cheyne-Stokes respiration. A rattling sound may occur due to the inability to swallow oral secretions.
  • Sensory decline: Vision may blur and hearing is generally considered the last sense to fade, meaning loved ones should still speak gently to the patient.
  • Level of consciousness: Drowsiness deepens into stupor and eventually unresponsiveness as cerebral perfusion decreases.

Emotional and Psychological Dimensions of Dying

Dying patients and their families experience profound psychological turbulence. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross identified five stages of grief—denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance—though patients do not always experience them in a neat linear order. A dying woman or mother may express intense anger at the unfairness of leaving her newborn or family behind. Others experience deep sadness and withdrawal. Midwives should practice active listening, allow patients to express their fears openly, and avoid offering false reassurances like 'everything will be fine' when the clinical outcome is terminal. Validating their emotional pain offers immense psychological relief.

The Role of the Midwife in End-of-Life Care

While midwifery is fundamentally dedicated to bringing new life into the world, midwives must also provide compassionate palliative care when life cannot be saved. This involves comprehensive physical, emotional, and spiritual support. Physical comfort is paramount: repositioning the patient to prevent pressure sores, keeping the lips and mouth moist, and administering prescribed pain relief promptly. Midwives coordinate care with doctors and pastoral workers to ensure dignity is maintained until the final moment. Creating a calm, private environment for the patient and allowing close relatives to stay nearby are fundamental duties of the attending midwife.

Supporting Bereaved Families Through Terminal Diagnosis

When a family receives a terminal diagnosis—such as the impending loss of a mother during childbirth or the non-survival of a severely compromised newborn—their world shatters. Midwives serve as a vital anchor during this crisis. Communication must be clear, honest, and delivered with deep empathy. Avoid medical jargon. Give family members time to absorb the news and ask questions. When death occurs, allow relatives safe space to express their grief according to their cultural and religious norms. Proper post-mortem care, handling the body with utmost respect, and guiding families on administrative and mortuary procedures help ease the heavy burden of sudden loss.
