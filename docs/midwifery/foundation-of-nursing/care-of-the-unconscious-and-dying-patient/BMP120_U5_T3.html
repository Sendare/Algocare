Stages of Grief (Kübler-Ross)

Introduction to the Kübler-Ross Model in Midwifery Practice

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross introduced a framework describing the emotional journey individuals often experience when facing terminal illness or catastrophic loss. While originally developed for dying patients, midwives encounter these exact psychological responses in obstetric and gynecological settings. This occurs during events such as sudden fetal loss, neonatal death, receiving a congenital abnormality diagnosis, or experiencing a sudden hysterectomy. Midwives must recognize that these emotional reactions are not rigid boxes. People move back and forth, skip steps, or experience them out of order. Understanding this framework helps you provide compassionate, non-judgmental care rather than rushing a grieving mother or family to 'move on.'

Denial and Isolation: Initial Shock and Defense Mechanisms

The first reaction to devastating news is often denial, serving as a temporary psychological shield. When a pregnant woman is told her baby has died in utero, she may act as though everything is normal, insisting the ultrasound machine must be broken. This numbness protects the mind from absorbing too much pain all at once.

  • Behavioral signs: Refusing to look at test results, talking about future nursery plans as if nothing happened, or absolute silence.
  • Purpose: It acts as a natural buffer, slowing down the overwhelming impact of reality until the psyche is strong enough to process it.
  • Midwife role: Maintain a calm, gentle presence, repeat medical facts honestly without force, and allow the patient time for reality to sink in.

Anger: Misdirected Frustration and Outbursts

Once the shock of denial fades, the underlying pain and helplessness often transform into intense anger. Patients may lash out, asking questions like, 'Why did this happen to my child?' or 'Why didn't you check me sooner?' This anger can be directed at spouses, relatives, God, or the attending midwifery and medical staff.

Remember that this anger is rarely about you personally. It represents masked pain, fear, and a deep sense of injustice. Taking insults or aggressive complaints personally will damage the therapeutic relationship. Instead, practice active listening, maintain clear professional boundaries, and let the patient vent without arguing back or becoming defensive.

Bargaining: Attempting to Negotiate the Inevitable

During the bargaining phase, individuals attempt to regain control or reverse the reality of their loss through secret deals or negotiations, often with a higher power. A mother facing a terminal diagnosis or mourning a sick newborn might pray, 'If only my baby survives, I will dedicate my life to charity,' or 'Take me instead, Lord.'

This stage is fueled by guilt and the illusion that we can somehow alter an unchangeable outcome through good behavior or promises. Midwives should listen respectfully without feeding false hope, gently guiding patients back to present reality while honoring their spiritual coping mechanisms.

Depression: Confronting Loss and Despair

As the reality of the situation fully sets in, bargaining fails, and anger subsides, the individual enters a profound state of sadness. In midwifery, this is where the full weight of a lost pregnancy, a dead infant, or a life-altering diagnosis hits home.

Patients may withdraw from social interaction, lose interest in daily activities, cry uncontrollably, or express feelings of emptiness and hopelessness. While this resembles clinical depression, in the context of grief, it is a natural and appropriate response to a major loss. Midwives must distinguish normal grief-related sadness from severe postpartum depression or suicidal ideation, offering gentle comfort and reassuring the patient that it is okay to mourn.

Acceptance: Finding Peace and Coming to Terms

Acceptance does not mean the person is happy about the loss or has forgotten their baby or diagnosis. Instead, it means they have learned to live with the new reality. The frantic searching, angry outbursts, and paralyzing despair give way to a quiet, steady acknowledgment of what happened.

In this final stage, mothers and families begin to reorganize their lives. They can talk about the lost child with a calm sorrow rather than raw agony. They start making plans for the future while carrying the memory of their experience. Midwives witness this when a patient returns for postnatal checks, able to engage constructively in discussions about future family planning and emotional healing.

Supporting Bereaved Families in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Providing compassionate care in Nigerian hospitals requires blending clinical competence with deep cultural and spiritual awareness. Grief is heavily influenced by societal expectations, religious beliefs (Christian, Islamic, and traditional practices), and family dynamics.

  • Cultural sensitivities: Some communities discourage open crying for infant loss, expecting stoicism, while others require immediate public mourning and prompt burial rites.
  • Spiritual support: Involving hospital chaplains, imams, or family elders provides immense comfort during the bargaining and depression phases.
  • Documentation and follow-up: Ensure respectful handling of the deceased baby, offer memory tokens like foot-prints when appropriate, and schedule community health worker follow-ups to monitor maternal mental health post-discharge.
