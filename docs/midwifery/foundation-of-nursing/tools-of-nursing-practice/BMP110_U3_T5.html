Taking and Handing Over

The Purpose and Principles of Handover in Nursing

Shift handover is the formal transfer of patient care responsibility and clinical information from one nurse or midwife to another. Its primary goal is continuity of care, ensuring no critical detail about a patient's condition, treatment plan, or pending investigation gets lost during shift changes. Effective handovers prevent clinical errors, maintain patient safety, and foster teamwork across shifts.

  • Accuracy: Information passed must be factual, objective, and based on current assessments rather than personal opinions.
  • Conciseness: Avoid unnecessary personal stories or repetitive details; stick to what impacts ongoing care.
  • Confidentiality: Discussions must occur in private spaces to protect patient privacy in compliance with professional codes of conduct.

Structuring Shift Handover Reports for Safety

An unstructured handover often leads to missed details and prolonged shift changes. Midwives must use a systematic format to organise clinical data logically. Whether using a verbal briefing or a written report sheet, the structure must prioritize immediate risks and active medical plans over historical trivia.

A safe report covers current vital signs, recent changes in clinical condition, outstanding lab results, and scheduled medications or procedures. High-risk patients, such as women with postpartum hemorrhage or pre-eclampsia, must always be discussed first to alert the incoming team to potential emergencies.

Standardized Communication Tools: The SBAR Framework

Miscommunication is a leading cause of preventable adverse events in healthcare. To bridge this gap, modern clinical practice utilizes the SBAR framework, which stands for Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation.

  1. Situation: State clearly what is happening right now (e.g., patient's blood pressure is dropping).
  2. Background: Give brief context, such as the admission diagnosis and recent interventions.
  3. Assessment: Share your professional clinical findings and current vital signs.
  4. Recommendation: Suggest what needs to happen next, such as requesting a doctor's review.

Bedside Handover: Involving the Patient and Family

Moving the handover report from the nurses' station to the patient's bedside transforms the safety culture of a ward. Bedside handover empowers patients and their relatives to participate in their care plan, catch errors, and ask questions directly.

During a bedside handover, the nurse introduces the incoming staff, visually checks critical equipment like intravenous lines or fetal monitors, and confirms the care plan with the patient. Confidentiality must still be respected by lowering voices and drawing privacy curtains when discussing sensitive personal matters.

Legal Responsibilities and Documentation Pitfalls

A handover is both a verbal communication event and a legal record of care continuity. If information is omitted or falsified during handover, legal liability can fall on the attending nurse or midwife. Documentation must be contemporaneous, meaning it is written as close to the event as possible.

  • Omitting deterioration: Failing to report a rising temperature or falling blood pressure to the incoming team is considered professional negligence.
  • Pre-signing records: Signing off on tasks or handovers before they actually occur is illegal and falsifies official hospital documents.
  • Incomplete signatures: Every handover note must include the date, time, legible signature, and professional designation of the nurse.
