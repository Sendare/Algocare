Problem Solving Techniques

Introduction to Problem Solving in Nursing Practice

Midwifery and nursing practice present daily challenges that require systematic thinking rather than guesswork. Problem solving is the deliberate process of identifying a gap between an actual clinical situation and a desired patient outcome, then taking purposeful steps to bridge that gap. In busy Nigerian health facilities—from primary health centers in rural communities to tertiary teaching hospitals—midwives constantly encounter situations like unexpected postpartum hemorrhage, delayed labor, or sudden stock-outs of essential medicines.

Good problem-solving abilities prevent minor clinical issues from escalating into life-threatening emergencies. It separates routine task execution from true professional judgment. When a midwife approaches a clinical dilemma methodically, stress levels remain manageable, patient safety is protected, and resources are used efficiently.

Critical Thinking versus Clinical Reasoning

While often used interchangeably, critical thinking and clinical reasoning represent distinct cognitive skills. Critical thinking is a broad, disciplined approach to examining information, questioning assumptions, and evaluating evidence without jumping to conclusions. Clinical reasoning, on the other hand, is the specific application of critical thinking within a healthcare context. It is the cognitive process midwives use to gather patient data, analyze cues, weigh the significance of findings, and determine the most appropriate course of action for a laboring mother or newborn.

  • Critical Thinking: Broad intellectual framework involving logic, self-reflection, and skepticism toward unverified claims.
  • Clinical Reasoning: Context-specific application where you connect patient signs and symptoms to physiological processes.

The Step-by-Step Problem Solving Framework

Effective problem solving follows a logical sequence. Memorizing a framework helps midwives avoid missing vital details when tension is high in the labor ward.

  1. Identify the problem: Recognize that something is wrong, such as a rising maternal pulse or abnormal fetal heart rate.
  2. Gather data: Collect all relevant subjective and objective findings through physical assessment and history taking.
  3. Analyze and diagnose: Interpret the findings to determine the root cause of the issue rather than just treating superficial symptoms.
  4. Generate solutions: Brainstorm possible interventions based on current evidence and available facility resources.
  5. Implement the plan: Carry out the chosen intervention decisively and safely.
  6. Evaluate outcomes: Assess the patient's response to determine if the problem has been resolved or if a new approach is needed.

Overcoming Common Barriers to Effective Resolution

Even experienced midwives encounter mental blocks and environmental obstacles that hinder problem solving. Recognizing these barriers is the first step toward overcoming them.

  • Cognitive Bias: Relying on past habits or assuming a new patient has the exact same condition as the last one without individual assessment.
  • Fatigue and Burnout: Long shifts and staff shortages impair cognitive processing speed and memory recall.
  • Communication Breakdowns: Poor handover notes or reluctance to speak up in front of senior colleagues.
  • Resource Limitations: Lack of essential supplies like gloves, oxytocin, or functional monitoring equipment.

Midwives must cultivate professional self-awareness to spot when fatigue or bias is clouding their clinical judgment.

Collaborative Approaches and Interprofessional Communication

No midwife works in complete isolation. Complex obstetric emergencies demand seamless teamwork among midwives, general practitioners, obstetricians, pharmacists, and laboratory scientists. Using structured communication tools, such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation), ensures that critical patient data is transmitted clearly and concisely during referrals or handovers.

Effective collaboration requires mutual respect and open channels of communication. When a junior midwife feels confident voicing safety concerns to a senior consultant, maternal and neonatal morbidity rates drop significantly. Problem solving in healthcare is inherently a team sport where diverse professional perspectives create safer patient care plans.
