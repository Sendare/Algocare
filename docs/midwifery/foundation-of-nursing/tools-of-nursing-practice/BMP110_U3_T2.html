Communication Skills

Introduction to Nurse-Patient Communication

Communication in midwifery and nursing is much more than simply talking and listening. It is the fundamental tool used to build trust, assess patient needs, and deliver safe, effective care. When a pregnant woman walks into a primary health clinic in Nigeria, her first impression and comfort level often depend entirely on how the midwife speaks to her. Effective communication reduces patient anxiety, improves compliance with antenatal care regimens, and directly enhances clinical outcomes. Midwives must recognize that every interaction—whether brief or prolonged—shapes the therapeutic relationship.

Verbal and Non-Verbal Communication

Clinical communication relies on two main channels working together. Verbal communication involves the spoken and written word, requiring clear language, appropriate tone, and a pace that the patient can easily follow, avoiding complex medical jargon. Non-verbal communication includes body language, facial expressions, eye contact, and physical posture. In many Nigerian cultural settings, a midwife's welcoming smile, respectful nod, or gentle touch on the arm can communicate care and reassurance far more powerfully than spoken words alone. Care must be taken to ensure verbal and non-verbal cues match; saying everything is fine while frowning creates immediate distrust.

Therapeutic Communication Techniques

Therapeutic communication is a deliberate, goal-oriented approach designed to promote patient healing and emotional well-being. Unlike casual social conversation, it focuses entirely on the patient's health concerns.

  • Broad openings: Allowing the patient to take the lead in discussing what is on their mind.
  • Restating: Repeating the core idea of what the patient said to confirm understanding.
  • Clarification: Asking the patient to explain vague statements so no dangerous assumptions are made.
  • Reflecting: Directing questions or feelings back to the patient so they can explore their own thoughts.

Active Listening Techniques

Hearing is a physiological process, but active listening is an active, conscious skill. It requires the midwife to give full attention, eliminate distractions, and process what the patient is truly saying behind the words. In busy Nigerian wards, it is easy to nod while mentally preparing the next task, but patients quickly sense this detachment. Active listening involves maintaining an open posture, nodding at appropriate times, and using encouraging minimal prompts such as "Go on" or "I understand." It prevents clinical errors by ensuring important details about pain or bleeding are never missed.

Overcoming Barriers to Effective Communication

Many obstacles can disrupt clear communication between a midwife and a patient. Environmental factors such as noisy wards, lack of privacy, and overcrowding make patients hesitant to speak freely. Physiological barriers like severe pain, exhaustion, or hearing impairment also interfere. Furthermore, psychological barriers such as fear, anxiety, or cultural intimidation can cause patients to withhold vital information. Midwives must actively address these hurdles by finding quiet spaces for private conversations, speaking calmly, and allowing adequate time for patients to express themselves.

Cultural Competence and Sensitivity in Nursing Dialogue

Nigeria is a culturally diverse nation with hundreds of ethnic groups, each holding distinct beliefs regarding health, pregnancy, childbirth, and postnatal recovery. A culturally competent midwife avoids imposing personal beliefs onto patients. Instead, she approaches every dialogue with curiosity and respect. For example, a patient may wish to use traditional herbs or insist on certain cultural postnatal practices. Rather than dismissing these practices harshly, the midwife should explore them respectfully, educate on any actual clinical risks, and find a safe compromise that honors the patient's dignity.

Documentation and Handover Communication

Communication extends far beyond talking with patients; it includes accurate written records and verbal handovers between shift teams. Poor handover communication is a leading cause of medical errors in clinical settings. The ISBAR framework (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) provides a structured, standardized way to pass critical patient information between midwives during shift changes. Written documentation must be factual, objective, and timely, following the golden rule: if it was not documented, legally it was not done.
