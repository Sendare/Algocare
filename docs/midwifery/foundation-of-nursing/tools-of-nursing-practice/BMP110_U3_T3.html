Interviewing and Counselling

Foundations of Nurse-Patient Communication

Communication in midwifery practice goes far beyond merely exchanging words. It serves as the primary tool through which trust is built, fears are alleviated, and accurate clinical data is gathered. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where patients may present with diverse cultural backgrounds, languages, and health literacy levels, effective communication bridges the gap between clinical expertise and patient understanding. Midwives must master both verbal and non-verbal channels, recognizing that a reassuring nod, an open posture, or a warm tone of voice often speaks louder than medical terminology. Active listening remains the bedrock of this interaction, requiring the midwife to set aside distractions and fully engage with what the mother is saying—and what she is leaving unsaid.

Establishing a Therapeutic Environment

A therapeutic environment creates psychological and physical safety for the pregnant woman. In busy public clinics across Nigeria, physical space is often limited, but privacy can still be preserved by drawing curtains, lowering voices, and maintaining a calm demeanor. Emotional safety is established through genuine empathy, unconditional positive regard, and non-judgmental acceptance. When a mother feels respected and safe from criticism, she is far more likely to disclose sensitive information regarding her reproductive history, domestic situation, or traditional practices that could impact her pregnancy.

Effective Interviewing Techniques

The art of clinical interviewing relies on asking the right types of questions at the right time.

  • Open-ended questions: Encourage the patient to elaborate freely, starting with words like 'how' or 'what' (e.g., 'How have you been feeling since your last antenatal visit?').
  • Closed-ended questions: Yield specific, concise data, useful for gathering quick facts (e.g., 'Did you feel any fetal movement today?').
  • Clarification and reflection: Validate the patient's statements, ensuring the midwife interprets the intended meaning rather than making assumptions.
  • Silence: Often overlooked, purposeful pauses give the patient time to gather their thoughts and emotionally process difficult topics.

Gathering Health History and Psychosocial Assessment

Comprehensive midwifery assessment blends biomedical inquiry with psychosocial evaluation. Beyond tracking parity, gestational age, and previous medical conditions, the midwife must explore the woman's home support systems, financial readiness, and emotional well-being. In many Nigerian communities, family dynamics and cultural beliefs heavily influence birth preparedness and complication readiness. Inquiring gently about domestic relationships or stressors helps identify vulnerability to postpartum depression, intimate partner violence, or neglect, ensuring holistic care from booking through the postnatal period.

Basic Counselling Skills in Midwifery

Counselling differs from routine advice-giving because it empowers the patient to make informed decisions rather than dictating actions. Midwives frequently counsel women on family planning, exclusive breastfeeding, nutrition, and HIV status disclosure. Core skills involve active exploration of options, helping the client weigh the risks and benefits of each choice, and supporting her final autonomous decision. A common pitfall is imposing personal biases or moral judgments onto the client; professional counselling requires maintaining objectivity and prioritizing the woman's well-being above personal opinions.

Overcoming Communication Barriers

Communication breakdowns can compromise patient safety and lead to misunderstandings during critical moments. Barriers include language differences, health illiteracy, sensory impairments, cultural taboos, and emotional distress such as acute anxiety or grief. Midwives working in multicultural regions often encounter patients who speak different local languages, necessitating the use of trained medical interpreters rather than relying casually on random bystanders. Simplifying technical jargon into clear, everyday language ensures that vital instructions regarding labor signs or medication dosages are fully grasped.

Ethical and Legal Considerations

Every clinical conversation is bound by strict ethical and legal duties. Confidentiality is paramount; patient information must never be shared outside the multidisciplinary care team without explicit consent, except in rare statutory exceptions like mandatory reporting of specific infectious diseases or child protection concerns. Informed consent requires that patients receive transparent, complete information about procedures, risks, and alternatives before agreeing to care. Coercion, forced treatments, or breaching a patient's trust violate professional codes of conduct and international human rights standards.

Documentation and Referral Pathways

Accurate documentation completes the communication cycle. Detailed records of patient interviews, counselling sessions, and identified psychosocial risks must be documented promptly and objectively in the case notes. Subjective opinions should be avoided in favor of clear, observable facts. When a patient's needs exceed the scope of primary midwifery care—such as severe psychiatric distress, complicated obstetric emergencies, or specialized social support—smooth referral pathways must be initiated. Clear handover notes ensure that the receiving multidisciplinary team receives accurate, continuous information for optimal patient outcomes.
