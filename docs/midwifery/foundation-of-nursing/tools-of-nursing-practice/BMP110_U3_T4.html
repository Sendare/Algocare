Principles of Reporting and Recording

Introduction to Clinical Communication in Nursing and Midwifery

Clear communication is the heartbeat of safe maternal and child health practice. Midwives and nurses do not work in isolation; every shift involves a complex exchange of information between doctors, laboratory scientists, pharmacists, and fellow nursing staff. When communication breaks down, critical signs of postpartum hemorrhage or fetal distress can be missed, placing mothers and babies at grave risk. Clinical communication occurs in two main ways: written documentation and verbal reporting. Both forms demand absolute accuracy, timeliness, and professionalism. In busy Nigerian healthcare facilities, where staff shortages are common, verbal handovers and patient case notes serve as the ultimate safety nets ensuring continuity of care from morning shift to night shift.

Core Principles of Nursing Documentation

Good documentation follows strict professional rules to be legally and clinically valid. Every entry in a patient's case note must be factual, accurate, complete, and current.

  • Factual Recording: Write what you see, hear, and do, avoiding vague terms like 'patient had a good night.' Instead, write 'patient slept for six hours, vital signs stable.'
  • Timeliness: Document care immediately after it is given. Charting hours later leads to forgotten details and compromised patient safety.
  • Legibility: If another nurse cannot read your handwriting, the entry is useless and dangerous.
  • Permanence: Always write in black or blue ink. Never use pencil or erasable ink in official health records.

Legal and Ethical Aspects of Patient Records

A patient case note is not merely a clinical guide; it is a legal document that can be subpoenaed in a court of law during malpractice suits or negligence investigations. In Nigerian courts, the golden rule of documentation applies: 'If it was not charted, it was not done.' Midwives and nurses must uphold strict confidentiality regarding patient data, sharing details only with authorized members of the healthcare team. Unauthorized disclosure of a patient's HIV status or obstetric history violates professional ethics and patient rights. Furthermore, altering records after an adverse incident or forging a colleague's signature constitutes professional misconduct and carries severe legal penalties, including the withdrawal of your practicing license by the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria.

Methods and Formats of Patient Records

Healthcare institutions use various formats to organize patient information. Traditional source-oriented records separate notes by discipline, meaning doctors write in one section, nurses in another, and physiotherapists elsewhere. While structured, this format can scatter a patient's story across many pages. Modern facilities increasingly adopt problem-oriented medical records (POMR), which center around a shared database, a problem list, initial care plans, and progress notes. Within progress notes, nurses frequently use the SOAP format:

  • S (Subjective): What the patient tells you ("My lower belly is cramping hard").
  • O (Objective): What you measure and observe (Blood pressure 130/90 mmHg, uterine contractions every 3 minutes).
  • A (Assessment): Your clinical interpretation or nursing diagnosis (Active phase of labor with mild hypertension).
  • P (Plan): What you intend to do next (Monitor vital signs hourly, inform the doctor, prepare for delivery).

Verbal Reporting and Handover Communication

Verbal reporting happens during shift change handovers, inter-departmental transfers, and urgent phone calls to attending physicians. A disorganized verbal report wastes time and obscures life-threatening changes in a patient's condition. To keep reports structured and foolproof, many Nigerian hospitals use standard communication frameworks like ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation).

When calling a doctor about an unstable postpartum mother, state your name and ward (Introduction), describe the sudden drop in blood pressure (Situation), mention her previous delivery complications (Background), share your clinical assessment that she may be entering hemorrhagic shock (Assessment), and recommend an immediate review and fluid bolus (Recommendation). Face-to-face handovers should always allow time for incoming staff to ask clarifying questions.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Errors in Charting

Even experienced nurses make documentation errors under heavy workloads. Recognizing these pitfalls is the first step toward safe nursing practice.

  • Charting Ahead: Documenting a task before you actually perform it is a major violation that can lead to falsification charges if an emergency prevents completion.
  • Omitting Signatures: Every entry must conclude with your full signature, date, time, and professional rank.
  • Making Erasing Errors: Never use correction fluid (tipp-ex) or scribble over a mistake. Instead, draw a single neat line through the incorrect entry so it remains readable, write 'Error' above it, and sign your initials.
  • Delayed Documentation: Writing notes at the end of a stressful 12-hour shift invites memory lapses and incorrect timelines.
