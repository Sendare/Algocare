Physiology of Wound Healing

Introduction to Wound Healing in Midwifery

Midwives encounter wounds daily, ranging from surgical incisions like episiotomies and lower segment caesarean sections to spontaneous perineal tears. Understanding how the human body repairs itself allows you to provide safe, evidence-based postnatal care. When tissue is injured, a complex but orderly cascade of biological events begins immediately to restore skin integrity and barrier function. Recognizing the difference between normal healing and abnormal delayed healing helps midwives detect complications early, preventing minor issues from turning into severe postpartum infections.

Overview of the Phases of Tissue Repair

The physiological process of wound healing is traditionally divided into overlapping phases. Rather than distinct boxes, these stages flow continuously into one another, each driven by specific cellular players and chemical messengers.

  1. Hemostasis: Immediate vascular constriction and clot formation to stop bleeding.
  2. Inflammation: Neutrophils and macrophages clear away debris and fight potential bacteria.
  3. Proliferation: Fibroblasts build new granulation tissue, angiogenesis occurs, and epithelial cells cover the surface.
  4. Remodeling: Collagen fibers reorganize and cross-link, slowly increasing the tensile strength of the scar over months.

The Hemostasis Phase: Stopping the Bleeding

The very moment tissue is cut or torn, the body acts to prevent hemorrhage. Blood vessels in the affected area constrict instantly to reduce blood flow. Platelets rush to the site, sticking to exposed collagen and clumping together to form a temporary plug. These activated platelets release chemical signals that trigger the clotting cascade, converting soluble fibrinogen into an insoluble fibrin mesh. This stable clot seals the damaged vessels, provides a temporary matrix for invading cells, and marks the successful completion of hemostasis.

The Inflammatory Phase: Cleaning the Wound

Often mistaken by patients as a sign of trouble, inflammation is an essential, protective stage of healing. Vasodilation occurs, allowing plasma and white blood cells to leak into the tissue, which causes the classic signs of heat, redness, swelling, and pain. Neutrophils arrive first to phagocytose bacteria and cellular debris. They are soon followed by macrophages, which take over cleanup duties and secrete vital growth factors. These growth factors act as chemical signals, summoning the cells needed for the next stage of repair. Without a robust inflammatory phase, wounds become chronic and fail to progress.

The Proliferative Phase: Building New Tissue

Once the wound bed is clean, the proliferative phase focuses on filling the structural defect and closing the surface. Fibroblasts multiply rapidly and begin synthesizing abundant amounts of collagen and extracellular matrix. Simultaneously, angiogenesis occurs as tiny new capillary buds sprout from surviving blood vessels, giving healthy granulation tissue its characteristic pink, pebbled appearance. Epithelial cells migrate across this fresh matrix from the wound edges or skin appendages to re-establish a protective covering, while myofibroblasts pull the wound margins closer together in a process known as contraction.

The Remodeling Phase: Maturation and Strengthening

The final phase of wound healing can last for several months or even up to a year. During remodeling, the cellular activity in the wound drops significantly. Type III collagen, which was rapidly laid down during proliferation, is progressively broken down and replaced by tougher Type I collagen. The individual collagen fibers cross-link and align along lines of tension, greatly increasing the tensile strength of the scar tissue. Even with this maturation, a healed wound typically only regains about 80 percent of the original tissue's pre-injury strength.

Factors That Impair or Delay Obstetric Wound Healing

Several systemic and local factors can interrupt the normal physiological steps of tissue repair in postpartum women. Poor tissue perfusion due to edema, tight suturing, or hypovolemia starves the healing cells of necessary oxygen. Systemic conditions like maternal diabetes, severe anemia, and malnutrition inhibit collagen synthesis and white blood cell function. Local complications such as wound hematoma, foreign bodies, or bacterial contamination prolong the inflammatory phase and lead to dehiscence. Midwives must identify and manage these risk factors proactively.

Clinical Implications for Postnatal Care

Applying knowledge of wound physiology directly shapes daily midwifery practice. When examining a perineal repair or caesarean section incision, midwives look for signs of progress through each healing phase. Maintaining a clean, moist environment supports epithelial migration, while gentle hygiene prevents infection without disrupting delicate new granulation tissue. Encouraging adequate protein intake and hydration provides the essential building blocks for fibroblasts and collagen synthesis. Ultimately, patient education on hygiene and recognizing danger signs empowers mothers to take part in their own recovery.
