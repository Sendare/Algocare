Suture Care and Removal

Introduction to Wound Healing and Suture Selection

Sutures bring the edges of a wound together so the body can close the gap safely and quickly. Understanding how tissue heals helps midwives choose the right material for an episiotomy or perineal tear repair.

  • Absorbable sutures: Made from materials that the body naturally breaks down over time, such as catgut or polyglactin. Used for deep tissue layers and perineal repairs where removing stitches later would cause unnecessary pain.
  • Non-absorbable sutures: Made from silk, nylon, or polypropylene. These must be taken out manually after the skin has grown back together.
  • Monofilament sutures: Consist of a single smooth strand, sliding through tissue easily with less risk of harboring bacteria.
  • Multifilament sutures: Braided strands that offer great knot security but require careful handling because tiny spaces between fibers can trap germs.

Routine Assessment and Daily Suture Care

A healing wound requires daily observation to make sure it is recovering as expected. Midwives should examine stitched areas during postnatal rounds, checking for cleanliness and stability.

Keep the wound clean and dry during the first forty-eight hours, as moisture encourages bacterial growth. Instruct mothers to wash the area gently with clean, warm water and pat dry with a soft cloth rather than rubbing. Avoid applying harsh antiseptic liquids directly onto fresh suture lines unless specifically ordered, because strong chemicals can damage delicate new skin cells and delay healing.

Identifying Signs of Infection and Complications

Midwives must spot early warning signs of wound breakdown or infection during routine checks. Catching problems early prevents localized skin issues from turning into severe systemic illnesses like sepsis.

  • Redness (Erythema): Mild pinkness is normal, but spreading redness or warmth points toward infection.
  • Swelling (Edema): Excess puffiness around the stitches can indicate inflammation or trapped fluid.
  • Pain: Pain that gets worse instead of better after the first few days is a red flag.
  • Discharge: Pus or foul-smelling fluid means bacteria are multiplying inside the wound.
  • Dehiscence: The accidental separation of wound edges before the tissue has properly healed.

Determining the Right Timing for Suture Removal

Taking out non-absorbable stitches too early can cause the wound to fly open, while leaving them in too long can lead to permanent track marks and difficult removal. Timing depends heavily on where the wound is located on the body, because different areas experience different levels of tension.

Anatomical SiteTypical Removal Timeframe
Face and eyelids3 to 5 days
Scalp and arms7 to 10 days
Chest, abdomen, and legs10 to 14 days
Over joints (knees, elbows)14 days or longer

Required Equipment and Sterile Technique

Suture removal is a minor clinical procedure that still demands strict infection prevention. Gather all necessary items before touching the patient to maintain an efficient and aseptic workflow.

Prepare a clean treatment tray containing sterile suture scissors, dissecting forceps, antiseptic solution, cotton balls or gauze swabs, and a waste container. Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water, then put on clean examination gloves. Use the forceps to gently lift the knot away from the skin, and use the scissors to snip the thread close to the skin line so that you do not pull any exposed surface bacteria down through the tissue track.

Step-by-Step Guide to Suture Removal

Perform the procedure with a calm, methodical approach, explaining each step to ease any patient anxiety.

  1. Check and confirm: Verify the patient's identity, inspect the wound to confirm it is fully healed, and ensure removal is safe.
  2. Cleanse the area: Wipe gently with an antiseptic swab to remove dried crusts and surface debris so you can see the stitches clearly.
  3. Elevate the knot: Grasp the suture knot with your forceps and lift it slightly off the skin.
  4. Snip the thread: Slip the tip of your suture scissors under the thread right next to the skin and cut once.
  5. Pull gently: Pull the cut thread smoothly out of the tissue toward the wound side so you do not pull the healing edges apart.
  6. Count and verify: Keep count of every removed stitch to ensure no small pieces are left behind.

Post-Removal Wound Care and Patient Education

Even after stitches are gone, the newly healed skin remains fragile and requires proper protection while it gains long-term strength.

Advise the patient that the fresh scar tissue can take several months to regain full tensile strength. Tell her to keep the area clean and dry for another day or two. Warn her to avoid tight clothing that rubs against the site. Instruct her to report any delayed redness, swelling, or sudden pain immediately, as infections can sometimes flare up after removal.
