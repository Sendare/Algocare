Types of Bandages and Principles of Bandaging

Introduction and Purpose of Bandaging in Midwifery

Bandaging is a fundamental clinical skill required in midwifery practice across Nigeria, used far beyond simple wound management. Midwives apply bandages to secure dressings over abdominal incisions following caesarean sections, support perineal pads, manage postpartum hemorrhage pressure dressings, or immobilize sprained joints when women sustain injuries during pregnancy. A well-applied bandage protects vulnerable tissues from environmental contamination, absorbs excess exudate, provides necessary compression to reduce edema, and offers mechanical support to weakened anatomical structures. Mastery of these techniques ensures that maternal care remains safe, hygienic, and effective in both primary health clinics and tertiary hospitals.

Classification and Common Types of Bandages

Various bandage materials exist, each selected based on the specific clinical objective required for the mother or newborn.

  • Roller bandages: Long strips of woven material, often cotton or elastic, rolled into a cylinder. They provide adjustable pressure and support.
  • Crepe bandages: Elasticized woven cotton bandages that offer elasticity, making them ideal for wrapping joints like the ankle or wrist to manage swelling.
  • Triangular bandages: Large pieces of cloth folded into a triangle, commonly used as slings to support an injured arm or hand.
  • Tubular bandages: Seamless, elasticated cylinders slipped over digits or limbs to secure dressings without adhesive tape.

Core Principles of Correct Bandaging Technique

Applying a bandage correctly requires adherence to established mechanical and hygienic standards to protect the patient's skin and ensure the dressing stays in place.

  1. Unroll outward: Always keep the roll of the bandage facing upward and unroll it against the skin rather than pulling it away.
  2. Proximal progression: Work from the distal part of the limb toward the heart (proximal) to encourage venous return and prevent fluid pooling.
  3. Even tension: Maintain consistent pressure across every turn to avoid localized constriction or loose spots that slip.
  4. Overlap margins: Each new turn should overlap the previous turn by one-third to one-half of its width to ensure complete coverage.
  5. Leave tips exposed: Whenever possible, leave fingers or toes uncovered so you can easily check circulation and color.

Patient Preparation and Positioning

Before applying any bandage, the midwife must prepare both the physical environment and the patient to ensure comfort and cooperation. Begin by explaining the procedure clearly in a language the woman understands, gaining her informed consent. Position the patient comfortably, ensuring the limb or body part to be bandaged is well-supported in a natural, functional position. Never bandage a limb while it is hanging unsupported in the air, as muscle tension can distort the true circumference of the limb, leading to improper tension once the muscles relax.

Common Bandage Application Methods and Turns

Different body contours require specific wrapping techniques to stay secure without slipping or cutting off circulation.

  • Circular turns: Wrapping the bandage directly over itself in successive rounds. Best used for anchoring the start or finish of a bandage on uniform parts like the wrist or neck.
  • Spiral turns: Moving obliquely up the limb with each turn overlapping the previous one by half. Ideal for cylindrical body parts like the upper arm or thigh.
  • Spiral reverse turns: Doubling the bandage back on itself as it goes up a tapering limb, such as the calf, so it conforms smoothly to changing widths without gaping.
  • Figure-eight turns: Crossing the bandage in alternating loops to form an eight, perfect for stabilizing joints like the knee or ankle during movement.

Assessing Circulation and Patient Comfort Post-Application

Once the bandage is secured, the midwife's responsibility does not end. Regular post-application assessment is vital to catch complications early. Check the distal extremities for color, warmth, and capillary refill time by pressing a nail bed and watching for blood to return. Ask the patient if she feels any numbness, tingling, or throbbing pain, which indicate nerve compression or restricted blood flow. If any of these signs appear, promptly loosen or reapply the bandage to prevent tissue ischemia.

Potential Complications and Prevention

Improper bandaging techniques can lead to serious clinical complications that compromise patient recovery. Applying excessive pressure restricts arterial and venous blood flow, potentially causing tissue necrosis or deep vein thrombosis in vulnerable postpartum women. Conversely, bandages wrapped too loosely fail to secure dressings, inviting secondary bacterial infections into surgical incisions or abrasions. Midwives must avoid wrinkles in the fabric, which create localized pressure points leading to skin breakdown and friction blisters.
