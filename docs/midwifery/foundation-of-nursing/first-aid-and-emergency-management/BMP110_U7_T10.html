Psychological Management of Casualties and Families in Emergency

Understanding Psychological Trauma in Emergencies

Emergency situations in Nigeria—whether road traffic crashes along the Lagos-Ibadan expressway, market fires, or community clashes—inflict deep emotional wounds alongside physical injuries. Psychological trauma occurs when an event overwhelms a person's natural ability to cope, leaving them feeling helpless and intensely terrified. Midwives and nurses responding to emergencies must recognize that the mind suffers just as severely as the body. Victims often experience a sudden rupture in their sense of safety and predictability. Understanding this helps healthcare providers look beyond physical bleeding or fractures and appreciate the invisible distress that every casualty carries.

Immediate Psychological Reactions and Acute Shock

In the immediate aftermath of a disaster, survivors display a wide variety of behavioral responses. Some people react with hyper-arousal, running around frantically, screaming, or shaking uncontrollably. Others experience a freeze response, sitting completely unresponsive, blankly staring, or unable to speak even when uninjured. Acute stress reactions are normal, temporary responses to an abnormal event. They do not automatically mean the person has a long-term mental illness. Recognizing these varied presentations prevents rescuers from misinterpreting silence as calmness or panic as disobedience.

Principles of Psychological First Aid (PFA)

Psychological First Aid (PFA) is a humane, supportive response to a fellow human being suffering who needs support. It is not professional psychotherapy or counseling; anyone can learn its core tenets. PFA focuses on human dignity, safety, and connection.

  • Look: Check for safety, check for people with obvious urgent basic needs, and check for distressed persons.
  • Listen: Approach people who may need support, ask about their needs, and listen without forcing them to talk.
  • Link: Help people address basic needs, access services, and connect with loved ones and social support.

Communicating with Distressed Families and Relatives

Families searching for loved ones at accident scenes or hospital gates experience agonizing uncertainty. Clear, honest, and compassionate communication is vital to de-escalate tension. Rescuers should introduce themselves, speak in a calm, steady tone, and avoid medical jargon. False reassurances like 'everything will be fine' should be avoided when the outcome is uncertain, as broken promises destroy trust. Instead, offer truthful updates about what is currently being done to help their relative.

Managing Grief and Bereavement at the Emergency Scene

Witnessing sudden death or delivering fatal news is one of the hardest duties in emergency care. When a casualty dies, surviving family members often display raw, intense grief—shouting, weeping, or collapsing to the ground. Rescuers must allow space for these natural expressions of mourning without judgmental interference. Provide a quiet, private space away from chaotic onlookers whenever possible. Offer a warm cup of water, sit quietly nearby, and allow trusted family members or religious leaders to support the bereaved.

Identifying Severe Psychiatric Emergencies and Referral Pathways

While most acute reactions subside with basic emotional support, some individuals develop severe psychiatric crises that require specialized intervention. Midwives and emergency nurses must quickly identify red flags such as active suicidal ideation, extreme psychosis, uncontrollable violence, or profound catatonia. These individuals cannot be safely managed with simple psychological first aid alone. Rescuers must know local referral pathways, coordinating prompt transfer to psychiatric units or mental health professionals while ensuring physical safety for both the patient and the healthcare team.

Self-Care and Resilience for Emergency Responders

Nurses, midwives, and first responders witness harrowing scenes regularly. Cumulative exposure to trauma without adequate decompression leads to compassion fatigue, burnout, and post-traumatic stress. Self-care is not a luxury; it is a professional responsibility. Rescuers must practice peer debriefing, take mandatory rest breaks, maintain proper nutrition, and seek professional counseling when distressing memories linger. Recognizing personal limits enables healthcare workers to remain effective and compassionate for future patients.
