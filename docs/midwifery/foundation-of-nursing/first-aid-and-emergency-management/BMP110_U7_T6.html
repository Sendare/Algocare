Poisoning

Understanding Poisoning in the Nigerian Context: Common Toxins and Routes of Exposure

Poisoning emergencies in Nigeria present unique clinical challenges shaped by both local industrial practices and traditional home management. Midwives and nurses must recognize the most frequent agents causing accidental and intentional toxicity in our environment.

  • Kerosene and Petroleum Products: Frequently stored in recycled beverage bottles in homes, leading to accidental ingestion by toddlers.
  • Traditional Concoctions and Herbal Remedies: Unregulated mixtures containing heavy metals, toxic plants, or high doses of local roots.
  • Agrochemicals: Organophosphate insecticides, herbicides like glyphosate, and rodenticides (such as local 'Sniper') used in agriculture and urban pest control.
  • Envenomation: Snakebites and scorpion stings common in rural farming communities.

Poisons enter the body through four primary routes: ingestion, inhalation, absorption through the skin or mucous membranes, and injection. Identifying the exact route dictates how quickly the toxin spreads and guides the initial emergency response.

Initial Assessment and Scene Safety for the Nurse First Responder

Before rendering any form of assistance, the attending midwife or nurse must evaluate the environment to prevent becoming a secondary victim. Scene safety is paramount.

Never rush blindly into a poorly ventilated room filled with chemical fumes, an enclosed space smelling of insecticides, or an area with aggressive bystanders. Always don appropriate personal protective equipment, such as gloves and masks, before touching a contaminated patient.

Once safety is established, perform a rapid primary survey using the Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure (ABCDE) approach. Check if the patient is responsive, maintain a patent airway, and assess for adequate chest rise. If the patient is unconscious but breathing, place them in the recovery position to prevent aspiration of vomitus. Gather quick history clues from relatives or bystanders regarding what was swallowed, spilled, or inhaled, keeping any suspected containers or vomitus samples for laboratory identification.

Decontamination Protocols: Skin, Eyes, and Gastrointestinal Tract

Removing the poison from the patient's body prevents further absorption. The method depends entirely on where the toxin contacted the body.

For skin contamination, immediately remove all contaminated clothing. Wash the affected skin thoroughly with copious amounts of running water and mild soap for at least fifteen to twenty minutes. Avoid scrubbing aggressively, as this can break the skin barrier and increase toxin absorption.

For eye splashes, flush the eyes continuously with clean, lukewarm water or normal saline from the inner corner outward for at least fifteen minutes. Remove contact lenses if present.

For ingested poisons, the old practice of routine vomiting induction using salt water or palm oil is dangerous and strongly discouraged, especially for corrosive substances or petroleum products, because vomiting causes secondary burns to the esophagus and aspiration pneumonia. Gastric lavage is rarely performed now and is only considered if the patient presents within one hour of ingesting a life-threatening amount of a toxin.

Recognizing Toxicdromes: Matching Clinical Signs to Specific Poisons

A toxicdrome is a constellation of signs and symptoms that suggest a specific class of poison. Recognizing these clusters helps nurses make quick decisions when the exact substance is unknown.

  • Cholinergic Toxicdrome: Caused by organophosphate insecticides. Look for SLUDGE: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Diarrhea, Gastrointestinal cramps, and Emesis, alongside pinpoint pupils and muscle twitching.
  • Anticholinergic Toxicdrome: Caused by certain antihistamines or local herbs. Patients are "blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, red as a beet, hot as a hare, and dry as a bone"—exhibiting dilated pupils, confusion, flushed skin, fever, and dry skin.
  • Opioid Toxicdrome: Caused by narcotics. Characterized by central nervous system depression, respiratory depression, and pinpoint pupils.
  • Sympathomimetic Toxicdrome: Caused by amphetamines or high doses of local stimulants. Presents with agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, and dilated pupils.

The Role of Antidotes and Supportive Care in Emergency Units

While stopping further exposure and decontaminating the patient are vital, supportive nursing care saves lives during poisoning emergencies. Most poisoned patients recover completely with meticulous monitoring of vital signs, maintaining airway patency, correcting fluid and electrolyte imbalances, and managing seizures.

Specific antidotes exist for only a small percentage of poisons, but where available, they are life-saving:

  • Atropine: Administered to reverse excessive secretions and bradycardia in organophosphate poisoning.
  • Naloxone: Used to reverse severe respiratory depression caused by opioid overdose.
  • Activated Charcoal: Administered orally or via a gastric tube within one hour of ingestion to adsorb toxins in the gut, though it is ineffective against alcohols, corrosives, and heavy metals.

Careful observation is required because patients can deteriorate suddenly, especially when long-acting toxins are involved.

Preventative Education and Community Safety Best Practices

Midwives and nurses hold a strategic position in Nigerian communities to prevent accidental poisonings before they happen. Clinical duties extend beyond hospital walls into antenatal clinics, immunisation posts, and community outreaches.

Key safety messages to share with parents and caregivers include:

  • Safe Storage: Never store chemicals, kerosene, bleach, or agricultural pesticides in empty food or drink containers. Keep all medications and household cleaners locked away in high cabinets out of children's reach.
  • Label Integrity: Ensure all containers retain their original manufacturer labels with clear usage instructions.
  • Herbal Safety: Discourage the unsupervised use of potent traditional concotions for infants and pregnant women.
  • Disposal: Safely dispose of expired drugs by returning them to healthcare facilities rather than throwing them into household dustbins where children can find them.
