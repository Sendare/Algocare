Priorities of Emergency Management

Understanding the Chaos: The Philosophy of Emergency Response

Emergency management in clinical practice begins with mental composure. When faced with a sudden crisis, the natural human reaction is panic or rushing in blindly. Midwives and healthcare providers in Nigeria often encounter chaotic scenes—whether a postpartum hemorrhage in a remote clinic or a mass casualty incident on a highway. The core philosophy of emergency response is to replace panic with a systematic, calm approach. You cannot help a patient effectively if you become a casualty yourself. Prioritization means doing the most important tasks first, leaving secondary tasks until stability is achieved. This structured mindset protects both the healthcare provider and the victim by ensuring resources and energy are spent where they matter most.

Scene Safety and Initial Size-Up

Before touching any patient, you must evaluate the environment. Scene safety is the absolute first priority in any emergency protocol. Rushing blindly toward a collapsed patient in a flood, a riot, or a room with exposed electrical wires only increases the number of victims.

  • Environmental Hazards: Look out for fire, live wires, traffic, falling debris, or aggressive crowds.
  • Mechanism of Injury: Observe how the accident or illness occurred to anticipate hidden trauma.
  • Universal Precautions: Put on gloves and personal protective equipment before making physical contact with body fluids.

Never assume a scene is safe just because others are standing around. Take a few seconds to scan the entire area.

The ABCDE Approach to Patient Assessment

Once the scene is safe, patient assessment follows a strict chronological order known as the ABCDE approach. This systematic framework prevents providers from missing life-threatening conditions while getting distracted by visible, less dangerous injuries like a broken arm.

LetterFocus AreaKey Action
AAirwayCheck if the airway is open and clear of obstructions
BBreathingAssess chest rise, breath sounds, and respiratory rate
CCirculationCheck pulse, capillary refill, and control major bleeding
DDisabilityEvaluate level of consciousness using the AVPU or GCS scale
EExposureExpose the body briefly to find hidden injuries while preventing hypothermia

If any step fails, you stop and treat it immediately before moving to the next letter.

Life-Threatening Interventions and Immediate Resuscitation

Identification of a problem during the primary survey demands immediate action. You do not finish the assessment if the patient stops breathing; you intervene right away. Resuscitation takes absolute precedence over history-taking and detailed physical examinations.

Common immediate interventions include clearing a blocked airway with suction or positioning, initiating cardiopulmonary resuscitation for cardiac arrest, applying direct pressure to massive external hemorrhage, and administering emergency drugs like oxytocin for severe postpartum bleeding or adrenaline for anaphylaxis. Every second spent delaying these interventions drastically reduces the patient's chance of survival.

Secondary Survey and Detailed Patient Assessment

Once the patient is stabilized and immediate life threats are controlled, you can proceed to the secondary survey. This is a head-to-toe clinical examination designed to catch less obvious injuries or medical problems.

Begin by taking a focused history using the SAMPLE mnemonic:

  • Symptoms and signs
  • Allergies
  • Medications currently used
  • Past medical history
  • Last oral intake
  • Events leading up to the illness or injury

Follow this with a physical check from head to toe, inspecting the head, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, limbs, and back. Document your findings clearly and monitor vital signs regularly to catch any sign of clinical deterioration early.

Preparation for Safe Transport and Handover

Stabilizing a patient in a primary clinic or emergency scene is only half the battle. They often require transfer to a higher center of care. Preparation ensures the patient does not deteriorate during transit. Secure all intravenous lines, ensure catheters and drains are flowing properly, and immobilize any unstable fractures.

Effective handover uses structured communication formats like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to brief the receiving team. Never send a patient away without a clear verbal report and written documentation outlining the care already given. Clear handover prevents dangerous gaps in ongoing management.
