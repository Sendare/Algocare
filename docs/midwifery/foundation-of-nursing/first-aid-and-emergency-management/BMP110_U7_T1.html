Introduction to Emergency Conditions

Principles of Emergency Care and First Aid

Emergency care in midwifery and general practice requires a calm, systematic approach. The primary goal of first aid is to preserve life, prevent the condition from worsening, and promote recovery until advanced medical help becomes available. Midwives often encounter unexpected crises such as postpartum haemorrhage, eclamptic fits, or severe neonatal asphyxia, making quick hazard identification vital.

  • Safety first: Always ensure the scene is safe for both the healthcare provider and the patient before initiating care.
  • Immediate action: Quickly identify life-threatening conditions using structured assessment tools.
  • Communication: Call for help early and communicate clearly with colleagues and relatives.

The Golden Hour and Rapid Response

The Golden Hour refers to the crucial first sixty minutes after a traumatic injury or sudden medical crisis, such as a severe obstetric hemorrhage. During this window, prompt intervention drastically improves the chances of survival and reduces long-term complications. Delays in recognizing shock or failing to secure rapid transport to a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility frequently lead to preventable maternal mortality in resource-constrained settings.

Triage and Patient Prioritization

When multiple patients present simultaneously, triage allows healthcare providers to sort them based on the severity of their conditions. This ensures that those with immediately life-threatening emergencies receive care first. In busy Nigerian emergency rooms and labor wards, structured systems like the South African Triage Scale (SATS) help allocate scarce resources effectively.

  • Red (Emergency): Patients requiring immediate life-saving interventions, such as severe airway obstruction or eclamptic status.
  • Yellow (Urgent): Stable for a short period, but require prompt evaluation, such as moderate pre-eclampsia.
  • Green (Routine/Non-urgent): Walking wounded or minor ailments that can safely wait.

Initial Assessment Framework: Primary Survey

The primary survey follows the systematic ABCDE approach to quickly identify and manage life-threatening conditions:

  • A - Airway: Check for patency and clear obstructions, maintaining cervical spine protection if trauma is suspected.
  • B - Breathing: Assess respiratory rate, chest movement, and oxygen saturation.
  • C - Circulation: Check pulse, capillary refill, and control any major external bleeding.
  • D - Disability: Evaluate level of consciousness using the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or Glasgow Coma Scale.
  • E - Exposure: Expose the body to check for hidden injuries while preventing hypothermia.

Common Obstetric and Medical Emergencies

Midwives working in primary health centres and tertiary hospitals must recognize common acute crises swiftly. Obstetric emergencies include postpartum haemorrhage, ruptured uterus, severe pre-eclampsia, and shoulder dystocia. Medical emergencies such as severe malaria in pregnancy, acute asthma, and anaphylaxis also demand immediate resuscitation skills. Recognizing early signs of shock—such as tachycardia, tachypnoea, and cold, clammy skin—prevents sudden maternal and neonatal collapse.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Emergency Care

Providing emergency care involves navigating complex legal and ethical boundaries. The principle of implied consent usually applies during life-threatening emergencies when an unconscious patient cannot give verbal permission. Midwives and nurses must act within their professional scope of practice while upholding the duty of care. Documenting all findings, vital signs, and interventions accurately is vital for patient safety and legal protection.
