Transportation of Casualty for Further Management

Introduction to Emergency Casualty Transport

Emergency casualty transport is the safe movement of an injured or acutely ill patient from the scene of an incident or primary clinic to a higher level of care. In the Nigerian context, this often bridges the gap between rural primary health centres and tertiary teaching hospitals. Moving a patient prematurely without adequate preparation frequently worsens their condition. The primary goal is simple: do no further harm while transferring the patient swiftly to definitive care.

Pre-Transport Stabilization and Assessment

Before any wheels turn, the midwife or first responder must stabilize life-threatening conditions. Apply the ABCDE approach: Airway clearance, Breathing support, Circulation check, Disability assessment, and Exposure with temperature control. Secure intravenous access if shock is anticipated, control active hemorrhage with direct pressure, and immobilize the spine if trauma is suspected. Never rush to transport a patient who is actively arresting or has an unprotected airway until basic stabilization is achieved.

Choosing the Right Mode of Transport

Selecting how to move a patient depends on distance, road conditions, and the severity of the illness. Options range from basic improvised stretchers and local tricycles to well-equipped ambulances.

  • Improvised Transport: Used only when no formal vehicle exists; carries high risk for spinal injuries.
  • Road Ambulance: The standard choice for urban and inter-facility transfers, offering space for oxygen and personnel.
  • Air Ambulance: Reserved for long distances, severe emergencies, or extremely difficult terrain, though rarely accessible for routine referrals in rural areas.

Methods of Safe Stretcher Handling

Lifting and carrying a stretcher requires teamwork and clear communication. Rescuers must keep their backs straight, bend at the knees, and use leg muscles to lift. When moving across rough terrain or loading into a vehicle, synchronous movement prevents tipping. Always secure the patient firmly with straps across the chest, hips, and knees to prevent falls during transit, especially when navigating bumps or steep ramps.

Patient Positioning Based on Injuries

The correct position during transit protects the airway and prevents complications:

  • Recovery Position (Lateral): Unconscious but breathing patients to prevent tongue fall and aspiration.
  • Semi-Fowler's Position: Patients with breathing difficulties or late pregnancy emergencies to ease respiratory effort.
  • Supine with Legs Elevated: Patients showing early signs of shock, unless head or spinal trauma is suspected.
  • Flat Supine with Spinal Immobilization: Anyone with suspected neck or back injuries.

Monitoring and Care During Transit

Transport is not a passive waiting period. The accompanying midwife must continuously observe vital signs, check intravenous lines for dislodgement, and monitor oxygen flow if in use. Road vibrations and engine noise can mask early signs of clinical deterioration. Keep talking to conscious patients to assess their mental status and provide reassurance, as transit anxiety is a common psychological burden.

Handover Communication at the Receiving Facility

A successful transfer ends with a structured verbal and written handover to the receiving team at the higher facility. Use standardized formats like ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to avoid omitting vital details. Hand over all referral letters, treatment charts, and personal belongings directly to the receiving nurse or doctor before leaving the premises.
