Principles of Emergency Management

Introduction to Emergency Nursing and First Aid

Emergency care in midwifery and nursing focuses on immediate action to preserve life, prevent further injury, and promote recovery during sudden health crises. Whether managing sudden postpartum hemorrhage in a remote primary health centre or responding to an accident on a Nigerian expressway, the fundamental goals remain the same. First aid is the initial assistance given to a sick or injured person before professional medical help arrives. Midwives frequently encounter emergency situations where rapid assessment and calm intervention determine whether a mother or baby survives.

General Protocols and Golden Rules of Emergency Care

When faced with an emergency, panic is the greatest enemy. Midwives must follow established protocols to ensure systematic care.

  1. Stay calm and take charge: Project confidence to reassure the patient and bystanders.
  2. Do no further harm: Never move a patient unnecessarily, especially if a spinal injury is suspected.
  3. Act swiftly but deliberately: Prioritize life-saving interventions over minor injuries.
  4. Reassure the patient: Explain what you are doing to reduce anxiety and build trust.

Scene Safety and Hazard Assessment

Before rushing to help an injured person, you must evaluate the environment. Running blindly into a dangerous scene turns the rescuer into another victim. Look out for exposed electrical wires, aggressive crowds, fire, or chemical spills. In clinical settings, look for biological hazards like spilled blood or broken glass. Always ensure the environment is secure before initiating any patient assessment or resuscitation.

The Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The primary survey is a rapid, systematic method used to identify and treat life-threatening conditions immediately. It follows the ABCDE mnemonic:

  • A - Airway: Check for patency and clear obstructions. Use chin-lift or jaw-thrust if needed.
  • B - Breathing: Look, listen, and feel for chest movements and adequate breath sounds.
  • C - Circulation: Check pulse rate, capillary refill, skin color, and control any major external bleeding.
  • D - Disability: Assess level of consciousness using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale.
  • E - Exposure: Undress the patient briefly to examine for hidden injuries, then cover them to prevent hypothermia.

The Secondary Survey and Vital Sign Monitoring

Once immediate life threats are managed during the primary survey, the secondary survey begins. This is a head-to-toe clinical evaluation to uncover less obvious injuries or illnesses. It includes taking a detailed history (using tools like SAMPLE: Signs, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last meal, Events leading up to the incident) and recording baseline vital signs: temperature, pulse, respiration rate, and blood pressure. Continuous monitoring of these signs helps detect sudden deterioration early.

Triage Principles in Mass Casualty and Clinical Settings

Triage means sorting patients based on the severity of their condition to allocate limited medical resources effectively. In a mass casualty incident—such as a major motor accident or building collapse—midwives and nurses must categorize victims quickly using color-coded systems: Red for immediate life-saving care, Yellow for urgent care that can wait a short time, Green for walking wounded, and Black for deceased or unsalvageable injuries. In labour wards, triage helps prioritize women in obstructed labour over those experiencing early normal contractions.

Communication and Handover in Emergency Situations

Clear communication during an emergency prevents fatal medical errors. When handing over a patient to doctors or incoming shift colleagues, use structured communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). Speak clearly, avoid ambiguous abbreviations, and document all interventions immediately. In high-stress obstetric emergencies, closed-loop communication—where an instruction is given, repeated back by the receiver, and confirmed—ensures that critical tasks like administering oxytocin are actually performed.
