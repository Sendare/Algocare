Principles of Drug Administration and the 'Rights' of Administration

Introduction to Safe Medication Administration

Safe medication administration is a fundamental pillar of professional midwifery and nursing practice in Nigeria. Every time a healthcare provider hands a drug to a patient or pushes a plunger on a syringe, they hold the ultimate responsibility for that patient's safety. Drugs are powerful chemical agents that heal when used correctly, but they can cause severe harm, prolonged hospital stays, or even death if mishandled.

Midwives frequently administer a wide range of medications, including oxytocics, antihypertensives, antibiotics, and routine supplements like folate and iron. Understanding the basic science of how these drugs interact with the female body, especially during pregnancy and the postnatal period, prevents avoidable tragedies. Safety begins with a questioning attitude; never administer a drug blindly simply because it was charted by someone else.

Core Principles of Drug Administration in Nursing Practice

Safe drug delivery relies on established clinical principles that guide everyday ward routines. Before touching a medication trolley, a midwife must possess adequate knowledge of the drug's action, normal dosage, side effects, and route of administration.

  • Clinical Knowledge: Knowing why the specific patient is receiving the drug and what therapeutic effect to expect.
  • Aseptic Technique: Preventing microbial contamination during preparation, especially for injectables like intravenous oxytocin or intramuscular ergometrine.
  • Patient Consent: Informing the mother about the drug, its benefits, and potential side effects before administration.
  • Timeliness: Giving medications within the prescribed time window to maintain steady therapeutic blood levels.

Mastering the Traditional and Expanded Rights of Medication

The concept of 'Rights' acts as a reliable checklist to catch errors before they reach the patient. While textbooks traditionally taught five rights, modern clinical practice expands this list to ten or more to cover complex healthcare environments.

  1. Right Patient: Confirming identity using at least two unique identifiers.
  2. Right Drug: Matching the drug label with the prescription sheet three times.
  3. Right Dose: Calculating and measuring the exact amount needed.
  4. Right Route: Delivering the medication through the correct anatomical pathway.
  5. Right Time: Administering at the precise hour ordered.
  6. Right Documentation: Recording immediately after administration, never before.
  7. Right Reason: Ensuring the drug aligns with the patient's actual diagnosis.
  8. Right Response: Monitoring the patient afterward to see if the drug worked.

Skipping even one of these checks can lead to severe adverse drug reactions.

Pre-Administration Assessments and Patient Verification

Before any medication touches a patient, a thorough pre-administration assessment must take place. This step involves more than just reading a chart; it requires active engagement with the patient.

Always ask the patient to state their full name and check their hospital identification band. Never rely solely on calling out a bed number, as patients frequently move around or swap beds. Next, assess vital signs and clinical parameters relevant to the drug. For instance, checking the blood pressure is mandatory before giving labetalol to a pre-eclamptic patient, just as listening to the fetal heart rate is vital before administering certain uterine stimulants.

Inquire specifically about known drug allergies every single time, as patient memory can be faulty or new sensitivities may have developed.

Preventing Medication Errors in Nigerian Clinical Settings

Medication errors remain a significant challenge in healthcare delivery due to factors such as heavy workload, staff shortages, poor lighting during night shifts, and interruptions. Common errors include wrong dosages, delayed administration, and incorrect routes.

To combat these issues, midwives must cultivate safe habits. Avoid taking verbal orders unless it is an absolute life-or-death emergency, as verbal instructions are easily misheard. Minimise distractions during drug preparation by avoiding casual conversations at the drug trolley. Clear communication during shift handovers ensures that ongoing intravenous infusions or scheduled doses are not forgotten.

Documentation, Reporting, and Legal Implications

The legal maxim 'if it wasn't charted, it wasn't done' holds absolute truth in midwifery and nursing. Every medication administration must be documented accurately immediately after delivery, including the drug name, dose, route, time, and the midwife's signature.

If a medication error occurs—such as a missed dose or wrong volume—the midwife must report it immediately to the attending physician and nurse manager. Hiding a mistake out of fear of punishment is dangerous and unethical; transparent reporting allows for timely medical intervention to protect the patient. Legally, poor documentation or falsifying drug charts can lead to disciplinary hearings before the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) and potential charges of professional negligence.
