Oral

Introduction to Oral Medication Administration

Oral medication administration involves giving drugs by mouth so they can enter the gastrointestinal tract and be absorbed into the bloodstream. This route is the most common, natural, and cost-effective way to treat various health conditions in clinical and community settings across Nigeria. Midwives frequently use the oral route for prenatal supplements, analgesics, and routine postpartum care. Patient cooperation is essential because the client must willingly swallow the prescribed preparation for the treatment to begin.

Advantages and Disadvantages of the Oral Route

The oral route offers distinct benefits and drawbacks that every midwife must weigh before handing medications to a client.

  • Convenience: Clients can easily swallow tablets or syrups themselves without specialized equipment.
  • Economy: Manufacturing pills and liquids costs less than producing sterile injectables.
  • Safety: Swallowed doses can sometimes be retrieved via induced vomiting or gastric lavage if an overdose occurs.
  • First-Pass Effect: Medications absorbed through the digestive tract pass through the liver first, which can deactivate a large portion of the active drug before it reaches general circulation.
  • Slow Onset: Oral drugs take longer to produce therapeutic effects compared to intravenous or intramuscular injections.

Forms of Oral Medications

Oral drugs come in various solid and liquid preparations designed to meet different pharmacological and patient needs. Tablets can be scored for splitting or enteric-coated to protect the stomach lining from irritation. Capsules enclose medication in a gelatin shell that dissolves in the digestive juices. Liquid preparations such as elixirs, syrups, and suspensions make administration easier for clients who struggle to swallow solid pills, though suspensions require thorough shaking before pouring to ensure an even dose.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Safety Checks

Before any medication reaches the client, the midwife performs rigorous safety checks to prevent medication errors. The golden rule is verifying the five rights: right client, right drug, right dose, right route, and right time. Midwives also check the client's allergy status, review current vital signs, and assess whether the client is alert enough to swallow safely without choking.

Step-by-Step Administration Procedure

Administering oral medications requires proper hygiene, accurate measurement, and attentive bedside manner. Wash hands thoroughly and prepare medications for one client at a time to avoid mix-ups. Compare the medication label against the prescription sheet three times. Position the client upright in bed or help them sit comfortably in a chair. Hand solid pills to the client in a small cup, and provide a glass of fresh water to aid swallowing. For liquid medications, place the medicine cup on a flat surface and read the fluid level at eye level against the meniscus. Stay at the bedside until the client has completely swallowed every tablet or drop.

Special Considerations for Pediatric and Geriatric Patients

Age-related physiological differences demand modified approaches when administering oral medications to the very young and the elderly. Infants and toddlers often require flavored liquid formulations administered via oral syringes rather than cups. Crushing pills for children should only be done after verifying with a pharmacist, as crushing destroys sustained-release properties. Elderly clients may experience dysphagia, reduced liver and kidney function, or polypharmacy—taking multiple drugs at once—which increases the risk of adverse drug interactions and toxicity.

Managing Complications and Preventing Aspiration

Aspiration occurs when food, liquids, or medications enter the airway and lungs, leading to choking or aspiration pneumonia. To prevent this, never give oral drugs to a client who is vomiting, unconscious, or experiencing severe swallowing difficulties. If a client coughs or chokes during administration, stop immediately, sit them upright, and suction the airway if necessary. Document any refused medications or adverse reactions in the client's chart right away.
