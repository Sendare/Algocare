Oral Feeding

Introduction to Oral Feeding in Client Care

Oral feeding is the most natural and preferred method of providing nutrition and hydration to a client. Beyond simply supplying calories, sharing a meal or being fed by another person carries deep social, emotional, and psychological comfort. In a healthcare setting, supporting a client's ability to eat by mouth helps preserve their gastrointestinal function, maintains their oral mucosa health, and supports their dignity.

However, many clients admitted to healthcare facilities experience weakness, cognitive decline, or physical impairments that compromise their ability to feed themselves independently. Midwives and nurses must recognize when a client requires assistance and step in safely without undermining the client's remaining autonomy. Preserving self-feeding ability, even if messy or slow, remains a core goal of basic client care.

Patient Assessment and Readiness Check

Before bringing a food tray to a client, a careful evaluation must confirm that it is safe for them to swallow. Check the client's level of consciousness, ensuring they are fully alert and responsive. Assess their ability to swallow voluntarily, noting any history of dysphagia, stroke, or neurological impairment.

Inspect the client's mouth for missing teeth, poorly fitting dentures, painful sores, or dryness that might make chewing uncomfortable. Review their respiratory status; a client who is severely short of breath or tachypneic should not be fed orally until their breathing stabilizes, as the coordination between breathing and swallowing is easily disrupted.

Proper Positioning for Safe Feeding

Gravity plays a major role in preventing food and liquids from entering the airway. Position the client upright in a chair whenever possible. If the client must remain in bed, elevate the head of the bed to an angle of 75 to 90 degrees, creating a high Fowler's position.

Ask the client to tilt their chin slightly downward toward their chest when swallowing, a technique often called the chin-tuck maneuver. This natural adjustment narrows the entrance to the larynx and directs food safely down into the esophagus rather than the trachea.

Assisting and Feeding Techniques

Feeding a dependent client requires patience, respect, and clear communication. Always verify the client's identity and check the meal tray against their diet sheet. Describe the food items on the plate, as sight and smell stimulate digestive juices and help orient blind or visually impaired clients.

  • Pace: Feed slowly, allowing the client time to chew and swallow completely before offering the next bite.
  • Amount: Use small amounts per spoonful, placing food on the stronger side of the mouth if the client has facial weakness.
  • Liquids: Offer fluids between bites rather than washing food down, and use thickened liquids if ordered by the clinician.
  • Engagement: Never rush the meal, and maintain eye contact while talking calmly.

Precautions Against Aspiration

Aspiration occurs when food, liquids, or saliva enter the airway below the vocal cords, potentially leading to aspiration pneumonia. Nurses must remain vigilant for subtle warning signs during every meal. Watch for coughing, choking, watery eyes, throat clearing, or a gurgling voice tone immediately after swallowing.

If any of these signs appear, stop feeding immediately. Encourage the client to cough, suction the airway if trained and necessary, and report the observation. Keep the client upright for at least 30 minutes after the meal concludes to prevent reflux and silent aspiration.

Post-Feeding Care and Documentation

Once the meal is finished, clean the client's face, hands, and mouth with a damp cloth or wipe, promoting oral hygiene and comfort. Inspect the oral cavity for any pocketed food particles that could cause irritation or decay. Ensure the client remains in a safe, comfortable upright or semi-Fowler's position as specified by their care plan.

Finally, estimate and document the amount of food and fluid consumed using standard measurements such as percentages or fractions. Note any difficulties, coughing episodes, or refusal of food in the clinical record to keep the multidisciplinary team informed.
