Care of Nails

Anatomy and Physiology of Nails

The nail is an accessory structure of the skin composed primarily of hard, keratinized epidermal cells. Understanding basic nail anatomy helps midwives spot systemic illness and maintain hygiene standards during clinical practice.

  • Nail plate: The hard, translucent portion visible to the eye, resting on the nail bed.
  • Nail bed: The vascular tissue underlying the nail plate, supplying it with blood and giving it a pinkish appearance.
  • Cuticle (eponychium): A narrow band of dead skin overlapping the proximal edge of the nail plate, acting as a watertight seal to prevent infection.
  • Matrix: The hidden area beneath the proximal nail fold where active cell division and nail growth originate.
  • Lunula: The pale, crescent-shaped area visible at the base of the nail, representing the upper edge of the matrix.

Purpose and Benefits of Nail Care in Nursing

Routine nail hygiene is a vital component of holistic nursing and midwifery care, far exceeding mere cosmetic appeal. Long, untrimmed, or dirty nails serve as a primary reservoir for pathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria and fungal spores, which can easily be transmitted to vulnerable patients during examinations or procedures.

Maintaining clean, short nails reduces the risk of accidental skin scratching, which breaks the skin barrier and invites secondary infections. Furthermore, observing the nails during routine care provides quick diagnostic clues regarding a patient's oxygenation status, nutritional state, and peripheral circulation. For hospitalized or bedridden clients, foot and nail care promotes physical comfort, prevents painful ingrown toenails, and boosts overall psychological well-being.

Assessment of Nail Health and Common Pathologies

Midwives must routinely inspect maternal and newborn nails as part of general physical assessment. Color, shape, thickness, and texture often reflect underlying systemic conditions.

  • Clubbing: Broadening and rounding of the fingertips with an increased nail angle, often indicating chronic hypoxia or cardiac disease.
  • Koilonychia: Spoon-shaped, concave nails frequently associated with severe iron deficiency anemia, a common concern in antenatal clinics.
  • Cyanosis: A bluish tint to the nail bed caused by low oxygen saturation in the circulating blood.
  • Beau's lines: Transverse depressions across the nail plate resulting from a temporary halt in nail growth due to severe illness or metabolic stress.
  • Onychomycosis: A fungal infection causing the nail to become thick, discolored, brittle, and opaque.

Manicure, Pedicure, and Nail Trimming Hygiene

Proper execution of nail trimming and hygiene prevents accidental trauma and localized infections like paronychia. Fingernails should be trimmed following the natural curve of the fingertip, whereas toenails must be cut straight across to prevent painful ingrown edges (onychocryptosis).

Never cut toenails into deep corners or use sharp, unsterilized blades. Soak hands or feet briefly in warm, soapy water to soften the keratin before trimming, making the process smoother and safer. Avoid aggressive cuticle cutting; instead, gently push back the softened cuticle using a clean wooden orange stick. Always ensure that all instruments—such as nail clippers, files, and pushers—are thoroughly cleaned, disinfected, or autoclaved between users to prevent cross-contamination in the clinical or home setting.

Special Considerations for Diabetic and Elderly Patients

Providing nail care for diabetic clients and older adults requires exceptional caution due to compromised circulation, reduced peripheral sensation, and delayed healing capacities. In diabetic patients, even a minor nick from a nail clipper can lead to a chronic foot ulcer, gangrene, and potential amputation.

Elderly individuals frequently experience dry, brittle, or thickened nails that make self-care difficult. Midwives must never use sharp blades or harsh chemical removers on high-risk patients. Instead, soak the feet gently, file nails carefully with an emery board rather than clipping if nails are extremely thick, and refer clients with severe fungal nails or neuropathy to a podiatrist. Regular foot inspection is mandatory to catch early signs of trauma or infection before they escalate.

Documentation and Infection Control in Nail Care

Accurate record-keeping and strict adherence to infection control protocols protect both the client and the healthcare provider. Midwives must document any abnormalities observed during nail assessments—such as discoloration, swelling, discharge, or structural changes—in the patient's clinical notes.

All reusable metal tools must undergo decontamination, cleaning, and sterilization between patients to prevent the transmission of blood-borne viruses and fungal infections. Disposable items, such as emery boards and orange sticks, must be discarded immediately after single-use. Hand hygiene must be strictly performed before and after providing nail care, and clean gloves should be worn whenever there is a risk of contact with non-intact skin or bodily fluids.
