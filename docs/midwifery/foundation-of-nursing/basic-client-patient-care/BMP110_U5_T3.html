Oral Hygiene

Introduction and Objectives of Oral Hygiene in Midwifery Care

Oral hygiene is a fundamental component of basic nursing and midwifery care, extending far beyond a simple cosmetic routine. Maintaining a clean mouth prevents the accumulation of dental plaque, reduces microbial proliferation, and stops local oral infections from spreading to systemic circulation. For pregnant women, good oral health carries added significance due to the well-documented link between periodontal disease and adverse obstetric outcomes such as preterm birth and low birth weight. Hormonal fluctuations during pregnancy increase susceptibility to gingivitis, often presenting as swollen, bleeding gums. Midwives must recognize that oral care promotes overall systemic health, enhances patient comfort, stimulates appetite, and preserves dignity during hospital admission.

Assessing the Oral Cavity and Identifying Risk Factors

A systematic oral assessment establishes a baseline and guides subsequent hygiene interventions. Midwives inspect the lips, tongue, mucous membranes, gingiva, hard and soft palates, and teeth using a penlight and tongue depressor while wearing clean gloves.

  • Halitosis: Foul breath often signaling poor hygiene, infection, or underlying systemic disease.
  • Xerostomia: Dry mouth resulting from dehydration, mouth breathing, or certain medications.
  • Cheilosis: Cracking or ulceration at the corners of the mouth.
  • Stomatitis: Inflammation of the oral mucosa, common in immunosuppressed clients.
  • Candidiasis: Fungal overgrowth presenting as white, curd-like patches that bleed if scraped.

Mouth Care for Conscious and Unconscious Patients

The approach to mouth care changes drastically depending on the patient's level of consciousness. For conscious clients, encourage independent brushing while providing an over-bed table, mirror, and emesis basin. Assist clients who have limited arm mobility with gentle, circular brushing motions at a 45-degree angle to the gum line.

Unconscious or semi-conscious clients present a high risk for aspiration, pneumonia, and airway obstruction. Position an unconscious patient in a side-lying lateral recovery position with the head turned toward you. Keep working suction equipment immediately available. Never use your bare fingers inside an unconscious patient's mouth, as a sudden bite reflex can cause severe injury; always use padded tongue blades or forceps. Clean the oral mucosa and tongue gently using foam swabs moistened with water or prescribed antiseptic solution, ensuring excess liquid is suctioned promptly.

Denture Care and Safe Storage

Removable dentures require careful handling because they are fragile and expensive to replace. Always line the bottom of the sink with a soft towel or fill it with water before cleaning dentures; this cushions the impact if they slip from soapy hands. Wash dentures using a soft-bristled denture brush and non-abrasive cleanser or mild soap, avoiding regular toothpaste which scratches the acrylic material.

When dentures are removed for the night, store them in a labeled container filled with clean water or a specialized soaking solution to prevent warping. Never place dentures in boiling water or wrap them in paper towels where they might be accidentally thrown away.

Special Oral Care Considerations for Compromised Patients

Patients with specific clinical vulnerabilities need modified oral hygiene protocols. Clients undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy often develop severe mucositis and painful mouth ulcers. For these individuals, avoid alcohol-based mouthwashes that burn and dry the mucosa; instead, recommend gentle saline rinses or prescribed soothing topical gels.

Clients with bleeding disorders require extra-soft toothbrushes and gentle wiping rather than aggressive flossing to prevent hemorrhage. For patients receiving long-term oxygen therapy via a nasal cannula or face mask, mucous membranes dry out rapidly, requiring frequent lip lubrication and water-based moisturizers to prevent cracking and secondary infection.

Documentation and Reporting of Oral Health Findings

Accurate documentation ensures continuity of care and creates a legal record of nursing actions. Record the time oral care was performed, the specific type of care given (e.g., routine brushing, denture care, unconscious mouth care), and the patient's tolerance of the procedure. Note any specific observations such as bleeding gums, white plaques, ulcers, foul breath, or missing teeth.

Report abnormal findings to the attending physician or senior midwife promptly so that prompt medical interventions, such as antifungal therapy or dental consultations, can be initiated before minor issues escalate into severe systemic complications.
