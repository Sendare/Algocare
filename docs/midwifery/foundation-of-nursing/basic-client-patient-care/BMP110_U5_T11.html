Standard Precautions

Introduction to Standard Precautions in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Standard precautions are the foundational infection control practices applied to the care of all patients, regardless of their diagnosed or presumed infection status. In busy Nigerian primary health clinics and tertiary hospitals alike, these measures protect both healthcare personnel and patients from the cross-transmission of bloodborne and moist-body-substance pathogens. Underpinning these guidelines is the core assumption that every patient's blood, body fluids, secretions, and excretions—excluding sweat—may contain transmissible infectious agents.

Implementing standard precautions requires constant vigilance, especially in resource-constrained environments where running water or single-use supplies might occasionally be scarce. Midwives and student nurses must integrate these habits into every clinical encounter, from routine antenatal booking to emergency intrapartum care. The primary goal is to minimize exposure risk by establishing a physical and behavioral barrier between susceptible hosts and potential reservoirs of infection.

The Chain of Infection and Why Precautions Matter

Infectious diseases do not spread by chance; they follow a predictable pathway known as the chain of infection. Understanding this chain helps midwives see why specific infection control steps matter in daily practice.

  1. Infectious Agent: Pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites.
  2. Reservoir: The natural habitat where the pathogen grows and multiplies, including human blood, contaminated surfaces, or water.
  3. Portal of Exit: The route the pathogen uses to leave the reservoir, such as respiratory droplets, open wounds, or the birth canal.
  4. Mode of Transmission: How the germ travels, via direct contact, droplet spread, or contaminated instruments.
  5. Portal of Entry: The way the pathogen enters a new host, including broken skin, mucous membranes, or the respiratory tract.
  6. Susceptible Host: A person lacking immunity or resistance to fight off the invading organism, such as a postpartum mother or a neonate.

Breaking any single link in this chain stops an infection from spreading. Standard precautions specifically target the modes of transmission and portals of entry, acting as shields that protect vulnerable patients and clinic staff.

Hand Hygiene Protocols and Techniques

Hand hygiene is widely recognized as the single most effective intervention for preventing healthcare-associated infections. Microorganisms on the hands are categorized as transient flora—acquired through direct patient contact or contaminated environment—and resident flora, which live deep in the skin layers.

  • Handwashing: Performed using soap and clean running water for at least 40 to 60 seconds whenever hands are visibly soiled, contaminated with blood or body fluids, or after caring for patients with spore-forming organisms.
  • Alcohol-based Rub: Utilized for 20 to 30 seconds when hands are not visibly dirty, offering rapid microbial reduction.

Midwives must practice the World Health Organization's Five Moments for Hand Hygiene: before touching a patient, before clean or aseptic procedures, after body fluid exposure risk, after touching a patient, and after touching patient surroundings.

Personal Protective Equipment Selection and Usage

Personal protective equipment acts as a wearable barrier shielding the clinician's skin, mucous membranes, and clothing from infectious splashes and sprays. Selecting the appropriate equipment depends on the anticipated nature of the clinical interaction and the expected risk of fluid exposure.

  • Gloves: Wear clean, non-sterile gloves for touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin. Change gloves between tasks on the same patient if moving from a contaminated body site to a clean site.
  • Aprons and Gowns: Utilize fluid-resistant gowns or plastic aprons during procedures likely to generate splashes of amniotic fluid, blood, or lochia, such as assisting a spontaneous vaginal delivery.
  • Masks and Eye Protection: Wear surgical masks combined with face shields or goggles whenever splashes to the eyes, nose, or mouth are anticipated.

Putting on PPE follows a specific sequence—usually gown, mask, eye protection, and gloves last—while removal requires careful technique to avoid self-contamination.

Infection Control Practices for Safe Patient Care

Safe patient care practices extend beyond handwashing and barriers; they involve environmental management around the patient's immediate bed space. Patient-care equipment—such as blood pressure cuffs, thermometers, and fetal monitors—must be cleaned and disinfected between users to prevent passive vector transfer of pathogens from one mother to another.

Linen soiled with blood, feces, or amniotic fluid requires handling with minimal agitation to prevent microbial aerosolization. Staff must place soiled linen directly into leak-proof bags at the point of generation without sorting or rinsing in patient care areas. Furthermore, patient placement policies dictate that individuals who contaminate the environment or exhibit uncontrolled hygienic behaviors should be prioritized for private rooms or cohorting when available.

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Management

Sharps injuries pose a severe occupational hazard for midwives and nurses, carrying the risk of transmission for hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Safe injection practices protect healthcare workers and patients alike through strict procedural discipline.

  • No Recapping: Never recap used needles using two hands. If recapping is unavoidable due to procedural constraints, utilize a safe one-handed scoop technique.
  • Immediate Disposal: Immediately deposit used needles, syringes, lancets, and scalpel blades into a puncture-resistant, leak-proof safety box located at the point of use.
  • Single-Use Policy: Never reuse disposable syringes or needles, even for the same patient across different medication administrations.
  • Container Capacity: Never allow safety boxes to be filled beyond the designated fill line—typically three-quarters full—to prevent accidental needle-stick injuries during closure.

Environmental Cleaning and Waste Disposal

Proper waste segregation and environmental sanitation form the final line of defense against community and institutional infection spread. Hospitals and clinics must sort waste at the exact point of generation into color-coded receptacles.

  • Non-Hazardous Waste (Black or Brown Bins): General domestic refuse, paper, and packaging materials that pose no infectious threat.
  • Infectious Waste (Yellow Bins with Biohazard Symbol): Items contaminated with blood, body fluids, cotton swabs, placenta, and dressing materials destined for incineration or safe autoclaving.
  • Sharps Waste (Yellow Safety Boxes): Puncture-proof containers exclusively for needles, blades, and glass ampoules.

Environmental surfaces—such as delivery couches, examination tables, and instrument trays—require regular cleaning with water and detergent, followed by appropriate chemical disinfection using chlorine-based solutions like sodium hypochlorite.
