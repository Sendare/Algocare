DNA and RNA Viruses of Public Health Importance

Introduction to Viral Pathogens in Midwifery and Public Health

Viruses are microscopic infectious agents that require a living host cell to multiply because they lack the cellular machinery for independent survival. In midwifery practice, understanding viral pathogens is crucial because several viral infections can cross the placenta, leading to severe fetal anomalies, miscarriage, or neonatal complications. Unlike bacteria, viruses do not respond to antibiotics, making prevention and supportive care the primary management tools for midwives. A clear grasp of how these pathogens interact with the human body helps clinicians identify risks early and protect vulnerable maternal and newborn populations in community and hospital settings across Nigeria.

Understanding DNA Viruses and Their Clinical Implications

DNA viruses possess deoxyribonucleic acid as their genetic material and generally replicate inside the host cell nucleus. They tend to have lower mutation rates compared to RNA viruses because their replication enzymes often possess proofreading capabilities. This genetic stability makes them prime targets for long-lasting immunity following vaccination.

  1. Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV): Causes painful vesicular lesions and poses a severe risk of neonatal infection during vaginal delivery if active lesions are present.
  2. Cytomegalovirus (CMV): A major cause of congenital hearing loss and developmental delay, often transmitted through bodily fluids.
  3. Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV): Responsible for chickenpox and shingles; maternal infection early in pregnancy can cause congenital varicella syndrome.

Examining RNA Viruses and Mechanisms of Rapid Mutation

RNA viruses use ribonucleic acid for their genetic code and typically replicate within the host cell cytoplasm. Their replication enzymes frequently lack proofreading mechanisms, resulting in high mutation rates. This rapid genetic shift allows RNA viruses to evade immune detection and develop drug resistance quickly.

  • Influenza Virus: Spreads rapidly via respiratory droplets, causing seasonal epidemics and occasional global pandemics.
  • Hepatitis E Virus: Transmitted via the fecal-oral route and associated with high mortality rates in pregnant women due to fulminant hepatic failure.
  • Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV): A retrovirus that attacks the immune system, threatening maternal health and risking vertical transmission during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

Transmission Routes and Vulnerability of Pregnant Women

Pregnancy induces a state of partial maternal immune tolerance to protect the fetus, which can inadvertently increase susceptibility to certain viral infections. Viruses spread through various routes including respiratory droplets, blood, sexual contact, and the fecal-oral pathway. Midwives must identify these transmission dynamics to educate families and implement barrier nursing or isolation protocols. Understanding how pathogens breach maternal barriers highlights why universal precautions and antenatal screening remain essential pillars of public health in resource-limited settings.

Prevention, Immunization, and Nursing Management Strategies

Preventing viral infections relies heavily on public health sanitation, vaccination programs, and early antenatal screening. Midwives play a frontline role in administering safe vaccines, such as tetanus and influenza shots, while advising patients against live vaccines during pregnancy. Supportive care remains the cornerstone of treatment for acute viral illnesses since specific antiviral drugs are limited. Effective hand hygiene, proper waste disposal, and compassionate counseling form the bedrock of reducing viral transmission in maternity wards.
