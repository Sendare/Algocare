Enterobacteriaceae

Introduction to Pathogenic Bacilli in Midwifery Practice

Pathogenic bacilli are rod-shaped bacteria responsible for a wide range of infections encountered in maternal and neonatal health. In Nigerian maternity settings, understanding these microorganisms is vital because bacteria from the gastrointestinal tract can easily contaminate the perineal region, leading to ascending infections. Midwives frequently encounter bacilli in conditions such as urinary tract infections, wound infections following caesarean sections, and puerperal sepsis. Recognizing how these pathogens behave helps in maintaining rigorous hygiene and administering timely care.

Overview of the Enterobacteriaceae Family

The Enterobacteriaceae family is a large group of gram-negative, facultative anaerobes that normally inhabit the intestines of humans and animals. While many species are harmless commensals, several act as opportunistic pathogens when they migrate to sterile body sites like the urinary tract or surgical wounds. Members of this family share common traits, such as fermenting glucose and reducing nitrates to nitrites. In obstetric practice, they are among the most frequent culprits behind maternal morbidities.

Key Characteristics and Laboratory Identification

Identifying Enterobacteriaceae in the laboratory relies on specific biochemical tests and staining properties.

  • Gram staining: They appear pink or red under the microscope due to a thin peptidoglycan layer.
  • Oxidase test: They are characteristically oxidase-negative, separating them from other gram-negative bacilli like Pseudomonas.
  • MacConkey agar: This selective and differential medium allows enteric bacteria to grow while distinguishing between lactose fermenters (pink colonies) and non-lactose fermenters (colorless colonies).

Midwives should understand these basic laboratory reports to interpret urine and wound cultures accurately.

Major Pathogenic Genera: E. coli, Klebsiella, and Proteus

Three main genera within this family dominate clinical concerns in maternity care:

  1. Escherichia coli (E. coli): The leading cause of community-acquired urinary tract infections and a major contributor to neonatal sepsis and meningitis.
  2. Klebsiella pneumoniae: Known for producing a prominent capsule, this organism causes severe hospital-acquired pneumonia, wound infections, and urinary tract infections, often exhibiting high drug resistance.
  3. Proteus species: Noted for their characteristic 'swarming' motility on agar plates, they frequently cause complicated urinary infections and wound sepsis, especially in catheterized patients.

Transmission and Common Obstetric Infections

Transmission of enteric pathogens usually occurs via the faecal-oral route, either through contaminated food and water or via poor personal hygiene. In pregnant women, anatomical and physiological changes—such as ureteral dilatation and urinary stasis—create a pathway for these bacteria to ascend from the perineum into the bladder and kidneys. Common manifestations include asymptomatic bacteriuria, acute cystitis, pyelonephritis, and postpartum endometritis resulting from ascending vaginal contamination during labor.

Clinical Manifestations in Pregnant and Postpartum Women

Infections caused by Enterobacteriaceae present with varying severity depending on the site involved. Asymptomatic bacteriuria requires screening because left untreated, it can progress to pyelonephritis, causing fever, loin pain, and vomiting, which raises the risk of premature labor. Postpartum, uterine infections present with foul-smelling lochia, lower abdominal tenderness, and pyrexia. Early recognition of these signs is vital to prevent systemic complications like septic shock.

Antimicrobial Resistance and Infection Control

Many Enterobacteriaceae strains have developed resistance to standard antibiotics through mechanisms like extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production. This resistance limits treatment choices, especially in pregnant women where several potent drugs are contraindicated. Effective infection control in maternity units relies heavily on standard precautions: consistent hand hygiene before and after patient contact, aseptic technique during catheterization and vaginal examinations, and proper sterilization of instruments to prevent hospital-acquired outbreaks.
