Structure, Morphology and Physiology of Bacteria

Introduction to Bacterial Cell Morphology and Shapes

Bacteria are single-celled microscopic organisms that lack a true membrane-bound nucleus. Despite their tiny size, they display a remarkable variety of shapes, which helps microbiologists classify them. Understanding these shapes is foundational for recognizing different pathogens encountered in clinical practice.

  • Cocci: Spherical or round bacteria. They can appear singly, in pairs (diplococci), in chains (streptococci), or in clusters (staphylococci).
  • Bacilli: Rod-shaped bacteria. Some are short and plump, while others are long and thread-like.
  • Spirilla and Spirochetes: Spiral or corkscrew-shaped bacteria capable of twisting motion.
  • Vibrios: Comma-shaped rods, famously represented by the cholera-causing organism.

Detailed Structure of the Bacterial Cell

The bacterial cell is packed with vital structures enclosed within a protective envelope. At the core lies the cytoplasm, containing the nucleoid—a single, circular strand of DNA carrying genetic instructions—alongside small circular DNA pieces called plasmids.

The cell wall, primarily made of peptidoglycan, maintains cell shape and prevents osmotic lysis. The difference in peptidoglycan thickness divides bacteria into Gram-positive and Gram-negative groups. Surrounding the wall, some species possess a slimy capsule that shields the cell from the host's immune system. Externally, flagella drive motility, while fimbriae or pili allow attachment to host tissues.

Bacterial Physiology and Nutritional Requirements

Bacteria require specific nutrients and environmental conditions to sustain life and multiply. Carbon, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and trace minerals serve as essential building blocks. Depending on how they acquire energy and carbon, bacteria are grouped into autotrophs and heterotrophs.

Environmental factors heavily dictate survival rates. Temperature plays a major role: mesophiles thrive at human body temperature and cause most clinical infections. pH preferences vary, though most pathogens prefer a neutral environment. Furthermore, oxygen requirements split bacteria into categories: obligate aerobes need oxygen, obligate anaerobes are poisoned by it, and facultative anaerobes adapt to either condition.

Metabolism and Energy Production in Bacteria

Cellular metabolism encompasses all chemical reactions that break down nutrients or build cellular components. Through catabolism, bacteria extract energy from organic molecules like glucose, storing it as adenosine triphosphate (ATP).

Energy extraction occurs through respiration or fermentation. Aerobic respiration yields high amounts of ATP using oxygen as the final electron acceptor. In contrast, fermentation processes produce far less ATP and rely on organic molecules instead of oxygen. Anabolism then uses this generated energy and smaller precursor molecules to synthesize complex cellular structures, allowing the organism to grow and repair itself.

Bacterial Growth and Replication Cycles

Bacteria multiply primarily through a process called binary fission, where one parent cell enlarges and splits into two identical daughter cells. Under optimal conditions, this reproduction happens rapidly.

When grown in a closed container, bacterial populations follow a predictable growth curve:

  1. Lag Phase: Cells adjust to the new environment and synthesize necessary enzymes without immediate cell division.
  2. Log Phase (Exponential Phase): Rapid, constant cell division occurs as resources remain abundant.
  3. Stationary Phase: Growth slows and plateaus as nutrients deplete and toxic waste products accumulate, balancing the rate of new cell division with cell death.
  4. Death Phase: Nutrient starvation and waste toxicity cause cell death to outpace division.

Clinical Relevance to Midwifery and Infection Control

Knowledge of bacterial structure and physiology directly impacts maternal and neonatal health. Pathogenic bacteria can cause serious postpartum infections, such as puerperal sepsis, or neonatal infections like meningitis. Understanding that bacteria possess protective structures like capsules or resilient cell walls helps midwives appreciate why strict aseptic techniques are non-negotiable during labor and delivery.

Disinfectants, antiseptics, and sterilisation methods work by disrupting bacterial cell walls, denaturing proteins, or halting metabolic enzymes. Recognizing how bacteria grow and reproduce underscores the necessity of hand hygiene, proper instrument sterilization, and timely antibiotic administration to prevent the spread of hospital-acquired pathogens.
