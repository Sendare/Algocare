Bacterial Growth, Cultivation and Culture Media

Introduction to Bacterial Growth and Reproduction

Bacteria primarily reproduce through a process known as binary fission. During binary fission, a single bacterial cell grows in size, replicates its single circular DNA molecule, and then divides into two identical daughter cells. This form of reproduction is asexual.

Under optimal conditions, the time required for a bacterial population to double in number is called the generation time or doubling time. This rate varies significantly among species. For instance, *Escherichia coli* can divide every 20 minutes under ideal laboratory conditions, whereas *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* has a remarkably slow generation time of 15 to 20 hours. Understanding these differences helps midwives and laboratory scientists anticipate how quickly an infection might develop or how long a specimen might take to grow in the laboratory.

The Phases of the Bacterial Growth Curve

When bacteria are introduced into a fresh liquid culture medium, their population growth follows a predictable pattern known as the bacterial growth curve. This curve is divided into four distinct phases:

  1. Lag phase: Cells are metabolically active but not dividing. They are synthesizing enzymes and adapting to the new environment.
  2. Logarithmic (Exponential) phase: Cells divide at a maximal rate, doubling regularly. This is when bacteria are most vulnerable to antibiotics that target cell wall synthesis.
  3. Stationary phase: Nutrient depletion and toxic waste accumulation cause the growth rate to equal the death rate, stabilizing the total population.
  4. Death (Decline) phase: The number of viable bacteria decreases exponentially as resources are entirely exhausted.

Environmental Factors Influencing Bacterial Growth

Bacteria require specific environmental conditions to thrive. Key factors include temperature, oxygen availability, pH, and osmotic pressure.

  • Temperature: Pathogenic bacteria are typically mesophiles, growing best at human body temperature around 37°C.
  • Oxygen: Obligate aerobes require oxygen to survive, obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, and facultative anaerobes can grow with or without oxygen.
  • pH: Most clinical bacteria prefer a neutral pH close to 7.2.
  • Moisture: Water activity is crucial for nutrient transport across the cell membrane.

Classification and Types of Culture Media

Culture media provide the nutrients necessary for growing microorganisms in the laboratory. They are classified based on their physical state, chemical composition, and functional use.

Preparation, Sterilization, and Quality Control

Preparing culture media requires precise measuring, dissolving, and sterilizing to ensure accurate diagnostic results. Most solid media use agar, a complex polysaccharide derived from red algae, as a solidifying agent because it melts at 100°C and remains solid at human body temperatures.

Sterilization of media is routinely achieved using an autoclave, which applies moist heat under high pressure (121°C at 15 pounds per square inch for 15 minutes). Quality control checks are vital before clinical use. Technicians test media batches for sterility by incubating uninoculated plates to ensure no contamination exists, and they test growth promotion using known reference bacterial strains.

Isolation Techniques for Obtaining Pure Cultures

Clinical specimens often contain a mixture of different bacteria. To identify the specific pathogen causing an infection, a pure culture containing only a single species must be isolated.

The most common method is the streak plate technique. Using a sterile inoculating loop, a clinical sample is spread across the surface of an agar plate in a pattern that progressively dilutes the number of organisms. By the final section of the streak, individual bacterial cells are deposited far enough apart that they grow into distinct, isolated colonies. Each colony theoretically originates from a single parental cell, ensuring purity for subsequent identification tests and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.
