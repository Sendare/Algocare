Hospital-Acquired Infections (Nosocomial Infections) and Surveillance

Understanding Hospital-Acquired Infections

Hospital-acquired infections, also known as nosocomial infections, are infections that patients contract while receiving medical care in a hospital or health facility. Crucially, the patient was neither infected nor incubating the infection at the time of admission; symptoms typically manifest at least 48 hours after entering the facility. These infections present a major challenge in Nigerian healthcare settings, stretching scarce resources, prolonging hospital stays, and increasing the risk of maternal and neonatal mortality.

In maternity wards, newly delivered mothers and newborns are uniquely exposed due to open wounds from episiotomies, caesarean sections, and immature immune systems. When basic hygiene protocols fail, pathogens easily spread from patient to patient, or via healthcare workers' hands and equipment. Recognizing how and why these infections develop is the first step toward effective prevention.

Key Clinical Definitions and Common Types

To manage nosocomial infections effectively, midwives must be familiar with the specific anatomical sites where they occur. The most frequent types encountered in clinical practice include:

  • Catheter-Associated Urinary Tract Infection (CAUTI): Infection of the urinary tract linked directly to the presence of an indwelling urethral catheter.
  • Surgical Site Infection (SSI): Infection occurring at or near the surgical incision up to 30 days post-operative, affecting skin, tissue, or deeper organs.
  • Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP): Lung infection developing in a patient admitted for at least 48 hours, often associated with prolonged bed rest or assisted ventilation.
  • Bloodstream Infection (BSI): Systemic infection often introduced via contaminated intravascular lines or poor venipuncture technique.

Common Pathogens and Modes of Transmission

Microorganisms responsible for hospital-acquired infections are often hardy strains thriving in clinical environments. Bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa are frequent culprits. Many of these organisms have developed resistance to common antibiotics, making treatment difficult.

Transmission occurs through distinct pathways:

  • Contact transmission: The most common route, happening either directly through skin-to-skin touch or indirectly via contaminated instruments, door handles, and unwashed hands.
  • Droplet transmission: Spread when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks, propelling large respiratory particles short distances.
  • Airborne transmission: Involves tiny droplet nuclei remaining suspended in the air over longer distances.
  • Vehicle transmission: Occurs through contaminated items like water, medications, or food.

Vulnerable Populations in the Maternity and Ward Environment

Not all patients carry the same risk level for nosocomial infections. In maternity units, pregnant women, postpartum mothers, and neonates face heightened vulnerability.

Postpartum mothers have fresh uterine wounds (the placental site) and perineal lacerations or surgical incisions from caesarean sections. These raw surfaces provide direct entry points for bacteria. Newborns, particularly preterm infants, have immature immune systems and fragile skin barriers that fail to keep out opportunistic invaders. Furthermore, frequent invasive procedures—such as intravenous cannulation, catheterization, and vaginal examinations—compound this risk. Midwives must remain vigilant, treating every vulnerable patient with strict adherence to aseptic techniques.

Surveillance Systems for Detecting and Tracking Outbreaks

Infection surveillance involves the systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data essential for planning and implementing infection control practices. Without active surveillance, hospitals operate blindly, noticing outbreaks only after multiple patients are severely affected.

Effective surveillance programs track infection rates, identify unusual clusters, and evaluate whether control measures are working. Midwives and ward charges contribute by accurately documenting clinical signs of infection, such as fever, foul-smelling lochia, or wound discharge, and reporting them promptly to the hospital's infection control team. Early detection stops a localized ward issue from becoming a devastating facility-wide epidemic.

Infection Control Strategies and Prevention Protocols for Midwives

Prevention remains far more effective than treatment when managing hospital-acquired infections. Midwives play a frontline role in breaking the chain of infection across every shift.

  • Hand Hygiene: The single most effective measure. Perform handwashing with soap and water or use alcohol-based hand rub before and after every patient contact.
  • Aseptic Technique: Maintain strict sterility during procedures like catheter insertion, wound dressing, and pelvic examinations.
  • Proper Waste Management: Segregate infectious items, sharps, and placenta waste using correct color-coded bins to prevent needle-stick injuries.
  • Environmental Cleaning: Ensure delivery couches, instruments, and surrounding surfaces are thoroughly decontaminated between patients.
