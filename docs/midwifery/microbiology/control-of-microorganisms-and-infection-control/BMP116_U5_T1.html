Sterilization and Disinfection Principles

Introduction to Infection Control in Midwifery Practice

Midwives handle vulnerable patients during childbirth and the postnatal period, making infection prevention a core duty. Microorganisms like bacteria, viruses, and fungi live everywhere in the hospital and community environment. When skin integrity is broken during labour, episiotomies, or caesarean sections, these germs can easily enter the body and cause severe sepsis or death. Standard precautions dictate that every patient must be treated as potentially infectious. Hand hygiene, proper use of gloves, and safe waste disposal form the first line of defense. Understanding the difference between cleaning, disinfection, and sterilization ensures that equipment used for internal examinations or surgical procedures does not transmit pathogens from one mother to another.

Sterilization Principles

Sterilization is the absolute destruction or removal of all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial endospores. Unlike disinfection, which only reduces germ numbers, a process is either completely sterile or it is not. In midwifery, surgical instruments that enter sterile body tissues, such as scalpel blades, suture needles, and uterine curettes, must undergo sterilization. Spores produced by bacteria like *Clostridium tetani* can survive harsh conditions, so standard boiling or wiping with antiseptic is completely ineffective against them. Achieving true sterility requires validated methods like high-pressure steam, dry heat, or approved chemical sterilants operating under strict time and temperature controls.

Disinfection Principles

Disinfection eliminates most pathogenic microorganisms on inanimate objects, but it does not reliably kill all bacterial spores. It is divided into three levels: high, intermediate, and low. High-level disinfection destroys all viruses, fungi, and vegetative bacteria, along with some bacterial spores, and is used for semi-critical items like speculums or respiratory equipment. Intermediate-level disinfection kills mycobacteria, most viruses, and bacteria, suitable for non-critical surfaces with visible blood contamination. Low-level disinfection destroys common bacteria and some fungi and viruses, typically used for routine environmental cleaning of furniture and floors. Disinfection is distinct from antisepsis, which is applied to living human tissue.

Physical Methods of Sterilization and Disinfection

Physical methods rely on heat, radiation, or filtration to remove or kill microbes. Heat is the most reliable and widely used physical agent in clinical practice.

  • Autoclaving (Steam under pressure): Uses saturated steam at high temperatures (typically 121°C or 134°C) under pressure. It is the gold standard for surgical instruments and linens because moisture helps coagulate cellular proteins quickly.
  • Dry Heat: Uses hot air ovens (160°C to 170°C) for items that resist moisture, such as glassware and powders, though it requires longer exposure times.
  • Boiling water: A traditional method that achieves high-level disinfection rather than true sterilization because it rarely kills tough bacterial spores at standard atmospheric pressure.
  • Radiation: Gamma rays are used industrially for pre-packaged single-use plastic items like syringes and gloves.

Chemical Methods and Solutions

Chemical agents are vital when heat would damage delicate equipment, or for disinfecting surfaces and large furniture. The effectiveness of any chemical disinfectant depends on concentration, contact time, water hardness, and organic matter presence.

  • Chlorine releasing agents (e.g., Sodium hypochlorite): Excellent for blood spills and surface decontamination, though corrosive to metals if left too long.
  • Glutaraldehyde: Used for high-level disinfection of heat-sensitive scopes and speculums, requiring careful ventilation due to toxic fumes.
  • Alcohol (70% Isopropyl or Ethyl): Rapidly acts against vegetative bacteria and enveloped viruses on clean surfaces, but ineffective against spores.
  • Chlorhexidine: Commonly combined with cetrimide for skin antisepsis and wound cleaning.

Decontamination Workflow for Instruments and Equipment

Processing used instruments requires a strict sequential workflow to protect staff and ensure patient safety. Skipping steps or reversing the order leads to equipment failure and cross-infection.

  1. Decontamination (Soaking): Immediate post-use immersion in a disinfectant solution (such as 0.5% chlorine) to kill blood-borne viruses and protect personnel during handling.
  2. Cleaning (Washing): Scrubbing with brush, water, and detergent to remove all visible blood, tissue, and organic debris.
  3. Rinsing and Drying: Thorough washing with clean water to remove soap residue, followed by complete drying to prevent corrosion or dilution of subsequent sterilizing agents.
  4. Packaging and Inspection: Checking for mechanical defects and wrapping instruments securely before placing them in the sterilizer.
  5. Sterilization/High-Level Disinfection: Running the appropriate cycle based on the classification of the item.
  6. Storage: Storing sterile packs in clean, dry, closed cupboards protected from dust and moisture.

Monitoring and Quality Assurance in Clinical Settings

Quality assurance ensures that sterilization equipment actually performs correctly every single time it runs. Relying solely on the machine's timer or pressure gauge is insufficient. Midwives and clinical staff utilize three main monitoring categories:

  • Mechanical monitoring: Checking cycle gauges, temperature readings, and digital printouts during the run.
  • Chemical indicators: Using indicator tape, strips, or special pouches that change color when exposed to specific temperatures or steam, confirming exposure conditions were met.
  • Biological indicators: The gold standard for verification, containing heat-resistant bacterial spores (such as *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* for autoclaves). If these spores are killed during the cycle, it proves the sterilizer is truly destroying microbial life.
