Pathogenic Fungi and Superficial, Systemic Mycoses

Introduction to Pathogenic Fungi in Clinical Practice

Fungi are eukaryotic organisms that differ significantly from bacteria. They possess a rigid cell wall containing chitin and glucans, rather than the peptidoglycan found in bacteria. Their cell membranes contain ergosterol instead of cholesterol. In clinical practice, medically important fungi generally exist in two main morphological forms: yeasts and molds.

  • Yeasts: Single-celled organisms that reproduce by budding, forming creamy or pasty colonies on culture media, such as *Candida albicans*.
  • Molds: Multicellular organisms composed of branching filaments called hyphae, forming fuzzy or powdery colonies, such as *Aspergillus* species.
  • Dimorphic Fungi: Organisms that can switch forms depending on temperature, growing as molds in the environment and as yeasts inside the human body.

Pathogenic fungi cause infections known as mycoses. While many fungi are harmless saprophytes living in the soil or on human skin, certain species become pathogenic when host immunity drops or skin integrity is compromised.

Superficial and Cutaneous Mycoses

Superficial and cutaneous mycoses are infections limited to the outermost layers of the skin, hair, and nails. Because these tissues contain dead, keratinized protein, the fungi responsible for cutaneous infections are called dermatophytes.

  • Tinea corporis: Ringworm of the body, presenting as annular, itchy lesions with raised, scaly borders.
  • Tinea capitis: Ringworm of the scalp, commonly seen in school-aged children, causing patchy hair loss and scaling.
  • Tinea pedis: Athlete's foot, characterized by cracking, maceration, and itching between the toes.
  • Pityriasis versicolor: A superficial condition caused by *Malassezia* yeast, leading to hypopigmented or hyperpigmented skin patches, especially in humid climates.

Transmission occurs through direct contact with infected humans, animals, or contaminated fomites like combs and shared clothing. Warmth and moisture greatly encourage their growth.

Systemic and Opportunistic Mycoses

Systemic mycoses are deep-seated infections that can affect internal organs and spread throughout the body. They are divided into endemic systemic infections caused by true primary pathogens and opportunistic infections that exploit weakened immune systems.

Endemic dimorphic fungi like *Histoplasma capsulatum* live in soil and are inhaled into the lungs, mimicking tuberculosis. In contrast, opportunistic fungi do not usually cause severe disease in healthy individuals. *Candida albicans* can overgrow on mucosal surfaces during antibiotic therapy or in pregnancy. *Cryptococcus neoformans*, found in pigeon droppings, can cause severe meningitis in patients with advanced HIV/AIDS. *Aspergillus* species produce spores that can colonize lung cavities in immunocompromised hosts, leading to severe respiratory compromise.

Diagnostic Techniques for Fungal Infections

Accurate diagnosis of fungal infections relies on a combination of clinical evaluation and laboratory investigation. Because fungal cultures can take days or weeks to grow, rapid microscopic examination is often the first step.

  • KOH preparation: Scrapings of skin or nails are mixed with potassium hydroxide to dissolve human cells, leaving fungal hyphae or spores visible under a light microscope.
  • Fungal culture: Samples are inoculated onto Sabouraud dextrose agar to encourage fungal growth and allow identification by colony morphology.
  • Wood's lamp: An ultraviolet light used in clinical settings to detect specific fungal fluorescence, such as the coral-red glow in certain erythrasma or green glow in some tinea capitis cases.
  • Serology and Biopsy: Blood tests checking for fungal antigens or antibodies help diagnose deep systemic infections when samples are hard to obtain.

Antifungal Pharmacotherapy and Nursing Care

Treating mycoses requires targeted pharmacotherapy based on the infection site and severity. Because fungi are eukaryotic, medications must exploit biochemical differences between human and fungal cells, such as fungal ergosterol.

  • Polyenes: Such as Amphotericin B, bind to ergosterol and form pores in the fungal cell membrane, used for severe systemic infections.
  • Azoles: Such as fluconazole and clotrimazole, inhibit the enzyme needed to synthesize ergosterol, treating both superficial and systemic infections.
  • Echinocandins: Such as caspofungin, disrupt the fungal cell wall by inhibiting glucan synthesis.

Midwives and nurses play a vital role in patient management. This includes educating patients on completing the full course of topical creams to prevent recurrence, monitoring liver function tests during long-term oral azole therapy, and observing patients receiving intravenous Amphotericin B for infusion-related fevers and chills.
