Rectovaginal Fistula (RVF)

Introduction to Rectovaginal Fistula in the Nigerian Midwifery Context

A rectovaginal fistula (RVF) is an abnormal epithelialized tract running between the rectum and the posterior wall of the vagina. In Nigeria, this condition remains a devastating public health challenge deeply intertwined with maternal healthcare access, poverty, and prolonged obstructed labor. Unlike high-income countries where RVFs often stem from Crohn's disease or surgical complications, the vast majority of cases in our local setting are direct results of neglected obstetric trauma. Midwives working in primary and secondary health facilities frequently encounter women who suffer in silence due to the intense social stigma associated with continuous fecal incontinence through the vagina. Understanding this context helps healthcare providers approach affected women with the empathy, clinical urgency, and cultural sensitivity required to facilitate healing and restore dignity.

Etiology and Causes of Obstetric Trauma

  • Prolonged Obstructed Labor: The leading cause in Nigeria, where the fetal head impacts the pelvis, crushing the intervening rectovaginal septum against the pubic bone and causing ischemic necrosis.
  • Iatrogenic Causes: Accidental lacerations during episiotomy repair, difficult forceps delivery, or low rectal injuries during gynecological surgeries.
  • Pelvic Inflammatory Conditions: Severe pelvic infections, Crohn's disease, or diverticular abscesses breaching the tissue planes.
  • Malignancy and Radiation: Advanced cervical or rectal cancers and pelvic radiotherapy leading to tissue breakdown.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Distress

The hallmark symptom of an RVF is the passage of flatus or feces through the vagina, which presents an immense psychological and physical burden. Patients frequently report persistent vaginal discharge, recurrent vaginitis, and foul-smelling odors that make normal social and marital life nearly impossible. Many women isolate themselves from their communities due to shame, believing they are cursed or permanently ruined. Midwives must listen carefully during history-taking, as patients may use euphemisms or hesitate to describe fecal leakage out of extreme embarrassment. Physical examination often reveals soiled perineal pads, excoriated vulvar skin, and visible defects along the posterior vaginal wall upon speculum or digital examination.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Clinical Assessment

Confirming an RVF requires a thorough physical examination in a well-lit pelvic examination area. The midwife or clinician inspects the posterior vaginal wall and performs a careful digital rectal examination combined with a vaginal examination to assess sphincter tone and locate the opening. Sometimes, small or high fistulas are difficult to visualize directly; in these cases, simple clinical tests like the methylene blue dye test or bulb-syringe air insufflation through the rectum can help identify bubbling or fluid leakage into the vagina. Endosonography, fistulography, or magnetic resonance imaging are utilized in advanced centers to map complex tracts, though basic inspection remains the cornerstone in resource-limited Nigerian settings.

Surgical and Nursing Management

Definitive treatment for RVF is almost always surgical, aiming to close the defect and restore normal anatomical function without tension. Preoperative care focuses on optimizing nutritional status, treating localized infections, and thoroughly clearing the bowel with enemas or oral purgatives. Intraoperatively, nurses assist with positioning and maintaining strict sterile fields. Postoperative nursing care is critical for success: maintaining a low-residue or liquid diet to minimize bowel movements, administering stool softeners, ensuring tension-free wound healing, and monitoring for signs of infection or suture breakdown. Catheter care is also maintained to prevent bladder distension from pressing against the delicate surgical repair site.

Psychosocial Support, Rehabilitation, and Prevention

Healing an RVF involves more than closing a physical hole; it requires complete psychosocial rehabilitation to help the woman reintegrate into her family and community. Midwives play a vital role by providing compassionate counseling, peer support groups, and education on hygiene and future reproductive health. Prevention efforts remain paramount and must focus on the community level: discouraging early childbearing, promoting skilled birth attendance at primary health centers, ensuring active management of the third stage of labor, and recognizing prolonged labor early to facilitate timely referral for Caesarean section before tissue necrosis occurs.
