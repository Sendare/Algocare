Vesicovaginal Fistula (VVF)

Introduction to Vesicovaginal Fistula and Obstetric Trauma in Nigeria

A Vesicovaginal Fistula (VVF) is an abnormal fibrous tract that extends between the bladder and the vagina, resulting in continuous, involuntary leakage of urine. In Nigeria and across sub-Saharan Africa, this condition remains a major public health challenge due to the high burden of obstructed labor. When a woman experiences prolonged, obstructed labor, the baby's head presses the soft tissues of the base of the bladder against the pubic bone. This sustained pressure cuts off the blood supply, causing ischemic necrosis of the tissues between the vagina and the bladder. Days later, the dead tissue sloughs off, leaving an abnormal opening.

While iatrogenic causes from pelvic surgeries do occur, obstructed labor accounts for the vast majority of cases in our local setting. Early marriage, poverty, lack of access to emergency obstetric care, and harmful traditional practices further compound this crisis. For a Nigerian midwife, understanding VVF requires recognizing not just the physical tear, but the tragic socioeconomic circumstances surrounding its genesis.

Etiology and Predisposing Factors

The development of a VVF is heavily tied to biological and social vulnerabilities. Recognizing these factors helps midwives identify patients at risk and advocate for timely interventions.

  • Obstetric Causes: Prolonged and obstructed labor without timely access to a Caesarean section is the leading cause.
  • Surgical Causes: Accidental injury to the bladder or ureters during gynecological surgeries like total abdominal hysterectomy.
  • Biological Vulnerability: Young maternal age, specifically adolescent mothers whose pelvises are not fully developed.
  • Socioeconomic Factors: Extreme poverty, rural isolation, and reliance on unskilled traditional birth attendants who delay referrals.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Workup

The hallmark symptom of a vesicovaginal fistula is the continuous, uncontrollable leakage of urine from the vagina. Patients often report that they cannot hold urine at all following a difficult delivery or pelvic surgery. This constant dampness leads to severe secondary complications, including excoriation of the vulva, perineal dermatitis, and a strong, persistent ammoniacal odor.

Diagnosing a VVF involves a thorough clinical evaluation:

  1. Detailed History: Asking about the duration of labor, mode of delivery, and onset of leakage.
  2. Physical Examination: Inspecting the vulva and vagina using a speculum in the Sims' position to visualize the fistula opening.
  3. Blue Dye Test: Instilling sterile methylene blue into the bladder via a catheter while placing a clean white gauze in the vagina to confirm the tract if the opening is tiny.
  4. Imaging and Lab Tests: Performing urinalysis to rule out infection, and sometimes a cystoscopy or retrograde pyelogram to check ureter involvement.

Psychosocial Impact and Patient Assessment

Living with a VVF is profoundly distressing. The constant leakage of urine creates a powerful, inescapable odor that often leads to severe social stigmatization. Many women are ostracized by their husbands, families, and communities. They may be forced to live in isolation, abandoned in outhouses, or divorced, which triggers deep depression, anxiety, and profound loss of self-worth.

When conducting a nursing assessment, the midwife must look beyond the physical symptoms. Assess the patient's emotional state, support systems, and coping mechanisms. Establishing a trusting, empathetic relationship is vital. Midwives should listen without judgment, offer gentle reassurance, and validate the patient's pain. Holistic assessment ensures that psychological healing begins alongside medical preparation.

Nursing and Surgical Care

Surgical closure is the definitive treatment for vesicovaginal fistula. Preoperative care focuses on improving the patient's nutritional status, treating any underlying urinary tract infections, and encouraging high fluid intake. If the tissues are inflamed, the surgeon may delay the operation for a few weeks to allow the tissues to heal and soften.

Postoperative nursing care centers on keeping the bladder empty and relaxed to protect the fresh surgical repair. A continuous indwelling urinary catheter must remain patent and free of kinks at all times. Midwives must monitor urine output closely, watch for hematuria, and ensure the patient drinks plenty of water. Any blockage in the catheter can cause the bladder to fill, put pressure on the suture line, and cause the repair to fail.

Community Reintegration and Prevention Strategies

Healing a VVF extends far beyond the operating theatre. True recovery includes restoring the woman's place in her community and equipping her with skills for an independent future. Many specialized centers offer vocational training, literacy classes, and microloans to help survivors rebuild their lives. Midwives play a key role in counseling survivors and preparing them for a confident return home.

Prevention remains the ultimate weapon against this condition. Midwives lead the charge by promoting:

  • Universal access to quality antenatal care.
  • Prompt identification and referral of prolonged labor using the partograph.
  • Disincentivizing early marriage and adolescent pregnancy.
  • Community education on safe delivery practices and hospital-based births.
