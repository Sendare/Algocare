Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)

Understanding Premenstrual Syndrome in the Nigerian Context

Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) refers to a recurring cluster of physical, psychological, and behavioral changes that happen during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle and resolve once menstruation begins. In Nigerian clinical practice, young women and mothers frequently report these cyclical episodes during antenatal or general outpatient visits, often dismissing them as normal stress or spiritual attacks. Midwives play a vital public health role by normalizing these conversations in local clinics and community outreaches.

The socio-cultural environment shapes how Nigerian women experience and report PMS. Many women balance demanding family responsibilities, trading, and professional work, meaning that severe mood swings or debilitating cramps can significantly disrupt their daily productivity. Recognizing PMS as a legitimate medical condition helps reduce stigma and encourages women to seek appropriate care rather than suffering in silence.

Pathophysiology and Hormonal Triggers

The exact biological trigger for PMS remains complex, but it is fundamentally linked to the normal hormonal fluctuations of the ovarian cycle. As estrogen and progesterone levels rise and fall sharply after ovulation, they interact directly with neurotransmitters in the central nervous system.

  • Serotonin Interaction: Declining estrogen levels lead to lower brain serotonin activity, which directly triggers mood swings, anxiety, and intense food cravings.
  • Progesterone Sensitivity: The breakdown products of progesterone affect gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) receptors in the brain, influencing mood and stress resilience.
  • Fluid Retention: Hormonal shifts cause temporary aldosterone and renin-angiotensin system activation, leading to bloating, breast tenderness, and peripheral edema.

Physical and Emotional Symptoms

Women experience a wide array of symptoms that can change from one cycle to the next. Clinicians categorize these manifestations into physical signs and emotional disturbances to aid accurate diagnosis.

CategoryCommon Signs and Symptoms
PhysicalAbdominal bloating, mastalgia, headaches, fatigue, and joint aches
EmotionalIrritability, sudden sadness, anxiety, anger outbursts, and poor concentration
BehavioralInsomnia, social withdrawal, and intense cravings for sugary or salty foods

These symptoms must follow a strict timeline: appearing consistently during the days leading up to menses and easing significantly within a few days of flow starting.

Differential Diagnosis and Clinical Assessment

Before confirming a diagnosis of PMS, midwives and other healthcare providers must rule out other medical conditions that mimic its symptoms. Thyroid disorders, severe anemia, chronic fatigue syndrome, and major depressive disorders can present with identical physical exhaustion and mood disturbances.

Effective assessment relies heavily on prospective charting. Asking a patient to complete a daily symptom diary for at least two consecutive menstrual cycles provides reliable proof of the cyclical pattern. Retrospective recall is often unreliable because patients tend to exaggerate recent discomfort. If mood symptoms are overwhelmingly severe and debilitating, the clinician should consider Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD), a more intense variant requiring specialized mental health care.

Management Strategies and Nursing Care

Care for women dealing with PMS begins with empathetic listening and lifestyle adjustments. Midwives should educate patients on dietary modifications, such as reducing caffeine, salt, and refined sugar intake, which helps minimize bloating and mood irritability. Regular aerobic exercise releases natural endorphins, easing both cramps and stress.

For moderate to severe cases, medical support may involve mild analgesics like ibuprofen for pain, or targeted supplements such as calcium and magnesium. In refractory instances involving severe emotional distress, referral to a physician for selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) may be necessary. Supportive nursing care empowers women to take charge of their reproductive health through simple, practical self-care routines.
