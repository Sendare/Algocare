STIs

Sexually Transmitted Infections in Adolescents

Sexually transmitted infections represent a major public health concern for young people globally, with adolescents bearing a disproportionate share of new acquisitions. In Nigerian clinical practice, youth frequently present with lower genital tract infections that, if missed, progress silently. Midwives working in youth-friendly health spaces must recognize that sexual debut often occurs during early adolescence, making this group prime targets for targeted education, barrier contraception promotion, and routine screening programs.

Why Adolescents are Biologically and Socially Vulnerable

Adolescent vulnerability to infections stems from unique physiological and social realities. Cervical ectopy, where columnar epithelium extends onto the ectocervix, is common in young females, providing a larger surface area susceptible to pathogens like Chlamydia trachomatis. Economically, young people may lack bargaining power in relationships, leading to coerced sex or unprotected encounters. Peer pressure and experimentation further compound these risks, creating an environment where infections spread rapidly.

Common Bacterial and Viral STIs in Youth

Youth frequently encounter both bacterial and viral pathogens. Bacterial infections are generally curable with appropriate antibiotics, whereas viral infections require management of symptoms or long-term suppression.

  • Chlamydia: The most common bacterial infection, often symptomless, caused by Chlamydia trachomatis.
  • Gonorrhoea: A bacterial infection causing purulent discharge and dysuria, caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae.
  • Trichomoniasis: A protozoan infection resulting in frothy, yellow-green vaginal discharge.
  • Human Papillomavirus (HPV): A viral infection linked to genital warts and cervical cancer.
  • Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV): A viral infection attacking the immune system, transmitted via bodily fluids.

Recognizing Signs, Symptoms, and Asymptomatic Cases

Many youth with sexually transmitted infections exhibit no outward signs, complicating clinical detection. When symptoms do appear, they vary based on the specific organism and the site of exposure. Common clinical indicators include abnormal vaginal or urethral discharge, lower abdominal pain, burning during urination, and unexplained genital sores or rashes. Because chlamydia and gonorrhoea frequently run an asymptomatic course in young women, midwives must rely on risk assessment protocols rather than waiting for physical complaints to appear.

Screening, Confidential Testing, and Diagnosis

Confidentiality remains the cornerstone of adolescent reproductive healthcare. Fear of parental discovery or community judgment stops many youth from seeking clinical help. Midwives must provide a non-judgmental environment, assuring clients that their medical records are protected by professional ethics. Diagnostic approaches range from rapid point-of-care tests for HIV and syphilis to laboratory-based nucleic acid amplification tests for chlamydia and gonorrhoea using urine or swab samples.

Treatment Protocols and Partner Notification

Treating an adolescent requires clearing the infection and breaking the cycle of reinfection through effective partner management. Treatment protocols depend on the confirmed pathogen, utilizing single-dose or multi-day regimens of specific antimicrobials. Partner notification, or contact tracing, is essential to treat exposed sexual partners simultaneously. Midwives must handle partner notification delicately, often using provider-referred methods or patient-delivered partner medication where legal and safe, ensuring the adolescent's safety is never compromised.

Long-Term Reproductive Health Consequences

Untreated infections in young people frequently lead to severe, irreversible complications later in life. Ascending bacterial infections cause pelvic inflammatory disease, which damages fallopian tubes through scarring and fibrosis. This structural damage significantly increases the lifetime risk of ectopic pregnancy and tubal factor infertility. Furthermore, chronic inflammation from untreated viral and bacterial agents can facilitate the transmission and acquisition of HIV, compounding future health burdens.

Midwifery-Led Counseling and Prevention Strategies

Effective prevention requires proactive counseling and education delivered in youth-friendly spaces. Midwives should promote consistent and correct condom use, advocate for human papillomavirus vaccination before sexual debut, and provide risk reduction counselling tailored to adolescent realities. Creating an open, non-judgmental dialogue empowers youth to make informed choices about their sexual health, seek early care when symptoms arise, and utilize available clinical services without fear.
