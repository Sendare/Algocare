Sexually Transmitted Reproductive Tract Infections

Understanding Sexually Transmitted RTIs in Midwifery Practice

Reproductive tract infections remain a major public health concern for women across Nigeria. Midwives encounter these conditions daily in antenatal clinics, family planning units, and postnatal wards. A sexually transmitted RTI refers to an infection acquired primarily through sexual contact that affects the reproductive tract, including the vagina, cervix, uterus, and fallopian tubes. These infections may be caused by bacteria, viruses, parasites, or fungi. Left untreated, they ascend from the lower genital tract and cause severe upper genital tract complications. Recognizing these conditions early allows midwives to intervene before irreversible damage occurs to a woman's reproductive health.

Transmission Routes and Epidemiological Factors

Epidemiological patterns of sexually transmitted RTIs are driven by a combination of biological, social, and economic factors. In many Nigerian communities, gender inequality, poverty, and limited access to healthcare hinder effective prevention. Pathogens cross mucosal barriers during unprotected sexual acts. Vertical transmission remains a devastating route, directly impacting neonatal health. Midwives must understand these epidemiological realities to identify women at higher risk during routine booking visits.

Common Pathogens and Their Clinical Presentations

Different microorganisms produce distinct clinical pictures in the female reproductive tract.

  1. Neisseria gonorrhoeae: A bacterium causing purulent vaginal discharge, dysuria, and intermenstrual bleeding.
  2. Chlamydia trachomatis: Often asymptomatic, but can cause mild discharge, lower abdominal discomfort, and cervical inflammation.
  3. Trichomonas vaginalis: A protozoan responsible for frothy, yellow-green, foul-smelling discharge accompanied by intense vulvar itching.
  4. Herpes Simplex Virus: Causes painful vesicular lesions and ulcers on the vulva, perineum, or cervix.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Protocols

Accurate diagnosis guides targeted antimicrobial therapy. Midwives must be skilled in performing speculum examinations to inspect the cervix and collect high vaginal or endocervical swabs. Syndromic management—treating based on a constellation of visible symptoms—is widely utilized in resource-limited settings where laboratory tests take days or are unavailable. However, gold-standard laboratory diagnostics include nucleic acid amplification tests, culture, and microscopy. Routine antenatal screening for infections like syphilis and chlamydia forms the bedrock of preventive maternity care.

Antenatal and Postpartum Implications for Mother and Fetus

The presence of an untreated sexually transmitted RTI during pregnancy introduces grave risks. Ascending infections can trigger preterm pre-labor rupture of membranes, preterm labor, and chorioamnionitis. Intrauterine growth restriction and low birth weight are frequently documented. During childbirth, neonates passing through an infected birth canal may contract conjunctivitis, such as ophthalmia neonatorum, or systemic infections like neonatal pneumonia. In the postpartum period, damaged endometrial tissue increases the mother's susceptibility to postpartum endometritis and pelvic sepsis.

Medical Management and Partner Notification Strategies

Treating the index patient solves only half the problem. Effective medical management requires specific antimicrobial regimens tailored to the diagnosed or suspected pathogen, paired with mandatory partner notification. Partner management breaks the chain of reinfection. Midwives must provide partner notification slips or facilitate contact tracing with utmost confidentiality. If sexual partners are left untreated, the woman will inevitably become reinfected upon resuming sexual activity, rendering initial therapy ineffective.

Prevention, Counseling, and Patient Education

Prevention remains the cornerstone of reducing the burden of reproductive tract infections. Midwives occupy a trusted position to deliver non-judgmental counseling on safer sex practices, correct condom use, and the importance of limiting sexual partners. Health education sessions in antenatal waiting rooms demystify these infections and dispel cultural stigmas that prevent women from seeking care early. Empowering women with accurate knowledge transforms them into active participants in safeguarding their sexual and reproductive health.
