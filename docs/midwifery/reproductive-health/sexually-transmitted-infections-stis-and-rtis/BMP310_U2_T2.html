Iatrogenic Reproductive Tract Infections

Understanding Iatrogenic Reproductive Tract Infections

An iatrogenic reproductive tract infection is an infection of the female upper or lower genital tract that occurs as a direct result of a medical, surgical, or diagnostic procedure. Unlike community-acquired sexually transmitted infections, these conditions are introduced into the body during healthcare interactions. In Nigerian maternity settings, where a high volume of obstetric and gynecological procedures take place daily, understanding how these infections develop is vital for every midwife. They often stem from a breakdown in sterile protocol, contamination during instrumentation, or the upward spread of normal vaginal flora during a pelvic intervention. Recognizing that the healthcare environment itself can be the source of harm shifts our clinical focus toward rigorous prevention.

Common Medical and Surgical Procedures Linked to Infection

Multiple procedures performed routinely in midwifery and gynecological practice carry a risk of introducing pathogens into the sterile parts of the reproductive tract.

  • Manual vacuum aspiration (MVA): Used for incomplete miscarriage or post-abortion care, introducing instruments through the cervix.
  • Cesarean section: A major abdominal surgery where the uterine cavity is opened, exposing internal tissues to environmental contaminants.
  • Endometrial biopsy and curettage: Scraping or sampling the uterine lining, which can push vaginal bacteria past the internal cervical os.
  • Insertion of intrauterine devices (IUDs): Placing a foreign body into the uterus can carry lower genital tract bacteria upward during insertion.
  • Catheterization: Indwelling urinary catheters can act as a bridge for bacteria ascending near the vaginal introitus.

Pathophysiology and Microbial Pathways

The female reproductive tract possesses natural defense mechanisms, including the acidic pH of the vagina, the thick cervical mucus plug, and the protective normal flora dominated by lactobacilli. When a clinician introduces an instrument, these barriers are temporarily breached. Pathogens from the patient's own lower genital tract—such as *Escherichia coli*, *Streptococcus* species, or anaerobic bacteria—can hitchhike on instruments and ascend into the sterile endometrial cavity. In other instances, microbes are introduced from external sources, including contaminated equipment or unwashed hands. Once inside the upper tract, these organisms multiply, triggering an inflammatory response that can lead to endometritis, salpingitis, or generalized pelvic inflammatory disease.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Challenges

Spotting an iatrogenic infection early requires keen clinical observation, as symptoms can mimic other postpartum or post-surgical complications. Patients commonly present with lower abdominal pain, abnormal or foul-smelling vaginal discharge, and persistent fever appearing days after a procedure.

Diagnostic challenges arise because mild postoperative discomfort is common, leading patients and clinicians to dismiss early signs of infection. Furthermore, localized tenderness can be mistaken for normal surgical healing. Midwives must rely on a thorough history noting recent instrumentation, alongside physical findings like uterine tenderness and cervical motion tenderness. Laboratory investigations, including complete blood counts and endocervical swabs, help confirm the diagnosis, though treatment is frequently initiated based on strong clinical suspicion.

Aseptic Techniques and Prevention in Midwifery Practice

Prevention remains the most powerful tool in reducing the burden of iatrogenic infections. Strict adherence to aseptic technique during every pelvic examination, catheterization, and surgical assist creates a barrier against microbial invasion. Midwives must ensure proper hand hygiene, wear sterile gloves when touching mucosal boundaries, and use autoclaved or properly sterilized instruments. Pre-procedure cleansing of the vulva and vagina with appropriate antiseptic solutions reduces the microbial load before instrumentation. Additionally, minimizing the number of unnecessary internal examinations during labor significantly lowers the risk of ascending infection.

Clinical Management and Antibiotic Stewardship

Managing established iatrogenic infections promptly prevents severe long-term complications such as chronic pelvic pain, tubal factor infertility, and pelvic abscesses. Treatment usually involves broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy chosen to cover both aerobic and anaerobic organisms commonly found in the genital tract. Midwives working in collaborative care settings must ensure timely administration of prescribed regimens. At the same time, antibiotic stewardship is crucial; clinicians must avoid indiscriminate prescribing for mild, non-infectious inflammation to prevent the rise of drug-resistant strains. Supportive care, adequate hydration, and patient education on completing the full course of medication round out effective management.
