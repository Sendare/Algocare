Endogenous Reproductive Tract Infections

Understanding Endogenous RTIs

Endogenous reproductive tract infections arise from the overgrowth of organisms that already naturally live inside the vagina, rather than being acquired through sexual transmission. This is a critical distinction for student midwives to grasp, because patients often experience deep emotional distress, wrongfully suspecting infidelity when symptoms appear. The most common condition in this category is bacterial vaginosis, followed by vulvovaginal candidiasis and aerobic vaginitis. Unlike sexually transmitted infections such as gonorrhea, endogenous infections do not require partner treatment unless symptoms recur frequently or the woman is experiencing concurrent issues. Midwives in Nigeria frequently encounter women presenting with heavy vaginal discharge and itching who have been incorrectly treated with multiple rounds of antibiotics for presumed STIs, making a clear understanding of endogenous causes essential for effective clinical care.

The Vaginal Microenvironment and Normal Flora

A healthy vaginal ecosystem is dominated by Lactobacilli, often called Döderlein's bacilli. These friendly bacteria play a protective role by fermenting glycogen produced by vaginal epithelial cells into lactic acid. This process maintains an acidic vaginal pH typically ranging from 3.8 to 4.5, creating an environment that inhibits the multiplication of harmful opportunistic pathogens. When this delicate acidic balance is disrupted, the population of Lactobacilli drops, allowing anaerobic bacteria or fungi to multiply rapidly and produce clinical symptoms.

Mechanisms Behind the Overgrowth of Normal Flora

Several everyday factors can alter the vaginal ecosystem and trigger endogenous infections. Common catalysts include:

  • Antibiotic use: Destroys protective Lactobacilli while leaving resistant anaerobic bacteria unharmed.
  • Hormonal fluctuations: High estrogen levels during pregnancy or from hormonal contraceptives alter glycogen content in vaginal tissues.
  • Frequent douching: Washes away beneficial bacteria and neutralizes natural vaginal acidity.
  • Uncontrolled diabetes: Increases sugar levels in bodily secretions, feeding yeast organisms.
  • Poor menstrual hygiene: Prolonged use of damp pads creates a warm, alkaline breeding ground for bacteria.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Specimen Collection

Accurate diagnosis prevents the wasteful use of inappropriate medications. When speculum examination is performed, the midwife collects high vaginal swabs for wet mount preparation and pH testing. Using standard pH indicator strips directly on the vaginal sidewall is a fast clinical step. Under a microscope, saline wet mounts can reveal clue cells—epithelial cells coated with bacteria hiding distinct cell borders—which confirm bacterial vaginosis. Adding a drop of 10% potassium hydroxide to the discharge sample can release a strong fishy amine odor, known as the whiff test, and highlight yeast hyphae or spores.

Medical Management and Treatment Protocols

Treatment depends entirely on identifying the correct causative organism. Bacterial vaginosis is managed with oral or topical antimicrobial agents such as metronidazole or clindamycin. Vulvovaginal candidiasis is treated using topical or oral antifungals like clotrimazole pessaries or fluconazole tablets. Midwives must counsel patients to complete their full medication course even if itching or discharge stops early, to prevent immediate relapse. Routine treatment of male sexual partners for uncomplicated bacterial vaginosis is generally discouraged by clinical guidelines, as studies show it does not reduce recurrence rates.

Implications for Pregnant Women and Neonates

Endogenous infections during pregnancy carry real clinical risks that every midwife must monitor. Bacterial vaginosis is linked to premature rupture of membranes, preterm labor, chorioamnionitis, and postpartum endometritis due to ascending pathogens weakening the amniotic sac. Candidiasis is less threatening to the pregnancy itself, but babies passing through an infected birth canal can contract oral thrush or cutaneous candidiasis (diaper dermatitis). Midwives should screen and treat symptomatic pregnant women promptly to protect both maternal and fetal well-being.

Patient Counseling and Hygiene Education

Effective patient education focuses on practical hygiene habits that support the natural balance of the vagina. Midwives should advise women to wash the vulva with plain water or mild, unfragranced soap, explicitly warning against douching or inserting medicated harsh substances into the vagina. Wearing breathable cotton underwear, avoiding tight synthetic trousers, and changing damp clothes or menstrual pads promptly help reduce excess moisture and heat. Emphasizing these simple lifestyle adjustments empowers women to prevent recurrent bouts of endogenous discomfort.
