Bacterial Vaginosis

Introduction and Vaginal Microbiome Balance

A healthy vagina relies on a delicate balance of microorganisms. The dominant resident bacteria are Lactobacilli, specifically species that produce lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide. This acidic environment, typically maintaining a pH between 3.8 and 4.2, naturally prevents the overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens. When this protective ecosystem is disrupted, the population of Lactobacilli plummets, allowing anaerobic bacteria to multiply rapidly. This shift marks the onset of Bacterial Vaginosis (BV), which is the most common cause of abnormal vaginal discharge among women of reproductive age. Unlike candidiasis or trichomoniasis, BV is not strictly a classic sexually transmitted infection, but rather a dysbiosis, meaning it represents an imbalance in the normal flora.

Etiology and Microbiology

Bacterial Vaginosis involves a complex shift in vaginal flora rather than a single infectious agent. The hallmark of this condition is the dramatic reduction of hydrogen peroxide-producing Lactobacilli alongside a massive overgrowth of anaerobic and facultative bacteria. Key organisms heavily implicated in this polymicrobial shift include:

  • Gardnerella vaginalis: A facultative anaerobe that forms a protective biofilm on the vaginal epithelium.
  • Atopobium vaginae: An obligate anaerobe frequently found co-existing with Gardnerella in persistent cases.
  • Prevotella species: Gram-negative anaerobic rods that contribute to the characteristic amine production.
  • Mobiluncus species: Curved anaerobic bacilli whose presence strongly correlates with clinical BV.

These organisms work synergistically to produce volatile amines, which cause the characteristic unpleasant odor associated with the condition.

Risk Factors and Triggers in Nigerian Practice

Several everyday practices and physiological states increase a woman's susceptibility to developing bacterial vaginosis. In local clinical settings, midwives frequently observe triggers that upset the vaginal ecosystem. Frequent vaginal douching remains a leading culprit, as it washes away protective Lactobacilli and alters the natural pH. Multiple sexual partners or starting a new sexual partner can also introduce new microflora or alter the vaginal buffering capacity through the alkalinity of semen. Using scented soaps, bubble baths, or antiseptic washes inside the perineal area strips away mucosal defenses. Additionally, the use of intrauterine devices (IUDs) for contraception can sometimes correlate with higher rates of recurrence due to biofilm formation on the device strings.

Clinical Presentation

Many women with bacterial vaginosis are entirely asymptomatic, with estimates suggesting up to half of all cases present with no outward signs. When symptoms do manifest, the classic presentation includes a thin, homogeneous, grayish-white discharge that coats the vaginal walls uniformly. Unlike the thick, curd-like discharge of candidiasis, BV discharge is typically smooth, watery, and profuse. The most distinct clinical feature is a strong, unpleasant fishy odor. This odor often becomes much more noticeable following sexual intercourse or during menstruation, because semen and menstrual blood are alkaline and react with amines to release the foul-smelling gas. It is worth noting that external vulvar itching and severe dysuria are less common in pure BV; their presence usually points toward a co-infection like candidiasis or trichomoniasis.

Diagnostic Criteria in Clinical Settings

In busy clinical environments, healthcare providers rely on standardized bedside tools to diagnose bacterial vaginosis accurately. The most widely accepted clinical standard is Amsel's Diagnostic Criteria. To confirm BV, a clinician must identify at least three of the following four signs:

  1. Homogeneous discharge: A thin, white, non-inflammatory discharge coating the vaginal walls.
  2. Vaginal pH above 4.5: Tested using narrow-range indicator paper.
  3. Positive amine test (Whiff test): A fishy odor is released immediately upon adding a drop of 10% potassium hydroxide (KOH) to the discharge.
  4. Clue cells on wet mount: Epithelial cells studded with adherent bacteria, giving their edges a speckled, blurred appearance under a microscope.

Alternatively, Gram staining of vaginal secretions with Nugent scoring is considered the gold standard in laboratory settings, though it is less practical for routine outpatient screening.

Management and Treatment

Treatment for bacterial vaginosis aims to relieve symptoms and restore the normal balance of vaginal flora. Antibiotic therapy is the mainstay of management. The standard pharmacological treatments include:

  • Metronidazole: Administered either orally (500 mg twice daily for seven days) or as a vaginal gel.
  • Clindamycin: Available as an oral tablet or a vaginal cream.

Alcohol must be strictly avoided during oral metronidazole treatment and for 24 hours afterward to prevent a severe disulfiram-like reaction (nausea, vomiting, and flushing). Routine treatment of male sexual partners is generally not recommended, as clinical trials show it does not alter the recurrence rate of BV in women. However, addressing high recurrence rates remains a clinical challenge because the biofilm formed by Gardnerella can shield bacteria from complete eradication.

Maternal and Neonatal Complications

While bacterial vaginosis is sometimes dismissed as a minor nuisance due to its lack of severe systemic inflammation, it poses significant risks during pregnancy. Ascending infection and the local production of inflammatory cytokines can weaken the fetal membranes and trigger early uterine contractions. Consequently, untreated BV is strongly linked to adverse obstetric outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, and premature rupture of membranes (PROM). Furthermore, women with BV have an increased susceptibility to acquiring other sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, Chlamydia, and gonorrhea, because the normal mucosal barrier and immune defenses are compromised.

Patient Counseling and Prevention

Effective midwifery care extends beyond prescribing medication; it requires empowering patients with knowledge to prevent reinfection. Midwives should counsel women on avoiding behaviors that disrupt vaginal ecology, such as douching, using harsh antiseptic soaps inside the vagina, or applying scented feminine hygiene products. Patients should be informed that recurrent episodes are common due to the persistence of bacterial biofilms. Emphasizing adherence to the full course of prescribed antibiotics is critical, even if symptoms disappear within a couple of days. Finally, offering a supportive, non-judgmental environment encourages women to seek prompt care for abnormal discharge rather than relying on self-medication with unproven herbal concoctions.
