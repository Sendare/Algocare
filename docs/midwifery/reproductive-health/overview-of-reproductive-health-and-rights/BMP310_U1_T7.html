Midwifery Interventions and Legal Protections for GBV Survivors

Understanding Gender-Based Violence in the Nigerian Context

Gender-based violence (GBV) remains a pervasive public health and human rights crisis across Nigeria. It encompasses any harmful act directed against an individual based on their gender, rooted in unequal power dynamics, cultural norms, and systemic inequalities. In many Nigerian communities, traditional practices, economic dependence, and deeply ingrained patriarchal structures compound the vulnerability of women and girls.

Forms of GBV commonly encountered in clinical and community settings include:

  • Intimate Partner Violence (IPV): Physical, sexual, or psychological harm inflicted by a current or former partner.
  • Sexual Violence: Non-consensual sexual contact, attempted assault, or rape, which can occur within or outside marriage.
  • Harmful Traditional Practices: Child marriage, female genital mutilation (FGM), and widowhood rites that violate bodily autonomy.
  • Socio-economic Violence: Denial of resources, educational opportunities, or inheritance rights.

Midwives are often the first healthcare professionals to encounter survivors. Recognizing that GBV transcends social, economic, and religious boundaries is vital for delivering unbiased, compassionate care.

Identifying Survivors: Clinical Presentation and Assessment

Survivors of gender-based violence frequently present to maternity units, antenatal clinics, or general outpatients with vague physical complaints rather than disclosing the abuse immediately. Midwives must maintain a high index of suspicion, especially when clinical findings do not match the history provided.

Common physical and psychological indicators include:

  • Unexplained injuries during pregnancy, such as bruises on the abdomen, breasts, or genitalia.
  • Recurrent somatic complaints like chronic pelvic pain, headaches, or gastrointestinal distress without clear organic cause.
  • Symptoms of anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, or active suicidal ideation.
  • Delayed seeking of antenatal care or appearing overly submissive in the presence of a partner.

Assessment requires privacy, active listening, and direct, gentle questioning. Creating a safe physical environment encourages disclosure without forcing the patient to speak before they feel ready.

Trauma-Informed Care and Communication

Trauma-informed care shifts the clinical question from "What is wrong with this patient?" to "What happened to this patient?" This approach minimizes re-traumatization and restores a sense of safety and control to the survivor.

The core principles guiding trauma-informed midwifery practice are:

  • Safety: Ensuring physical and emotional security during every interaction.
  • Trustworthiness: Maintaining clear communication, transparency, and confidentiality.
  • Choice: Giving the survivor control over their examination and treatment plan.
  • Collaboration: Working alongside the patient rather than exercising authoritarian control.
  • Empowerment: Validating their strengths and supporting their decision-making.

Communication must be free of judgment. Avoid asking "Why did you stay?" or making statements that imply fault. Instead, use validating phrases like, "I believe you," "It is not your fault," and "You are safe here."

Legal Protections and Frameworks in Nigeria

Midwives working in Nigeria must be familiar with the legislative frameworks designed to protect citizens from gender-based violence. While laws vary across states, key national legislation provides vital avenues for justice and protection.

The Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) (VAPP) Act, signed into law federally in 2015 and subsequently domesticated by numerous states, represents a milestone. It criminalizes various forms of abuse previously unaddressed by the penal and criminal codes, including emotional abuse, harmful widowhood practices, spousal battery, and forceful ejection from a home.

Key legal rights for survivors include:

  • Right to free medical treatment for assault victims in designated public facilities under specific state policies.
  • Right to legal aid and representation through agencies like the Legal Aid Council or state-level human rights commissions.
  • Protection orders issued by courts to restrain perpetrators from contacting or approaching the survivor.

Midwives facilitate access to these protections by connecting survivors with social workers, legal aid providers, and law enforcement agencies while maintaining strict medical confidentiality.

Support Systems and Multi-Sectoral Referral Pathways

Effective care for GBV survivors extends far beyond what a single midwife or clinic can provide. A robust multi-sectoral referral pathway links health facilities with legal, social, police, and shelter services to ensure comprehensive recovery.

Key components of the referral network include:

  • Shelters and Safe Houses: Provide secure temporary accommodation for survivors fleeing immediate danger.
  • Psychosocial Support Services: Professional counseling and support groups to aid emotional healing.
  • Law Enforcement and Justice Sector: Trained police gender desks and legal aid organizations to handle investigations and prosecution.
  • Economic Empowerment Programs: Skills acquisition initiatives that help survivors achieve financial independence from abusive partners.

When making a referral, the midwife must obtain informed consent from the survivor, explain where they are being referred, and provide contact details securely. Avoid exposing the survivor to further risk by prematurely contacting third parties without their explicit agreement.

Ethical Responsibilities and Mandatory Reporting

Balancing patient confidentiality with ethical and legal duties presents a delicate challenge in midwifery practice. Confidentiality is a cornerstone of professional ethics, fostering trust between the midwife and the survivor. Breaching confidentiality without legal backing can endanger the survivor and violate their autonomy.

However, exceptions exist in cases involving:

  • Child Abuse and Minors: Cases involving individuals below the age of consent (typically 18 years under Nigerian law) generally mandate reporting to child protection agencies or law enforcement.
  • Court Subpoena: A legally binding directive from a court of law requiring medical testimony or records.

Midwives must understand local statutory requirements regarding mandatory reporting. Where adult competent survivors are concerned, respecting autonomy means the decision to report to police rests with the survivor, unless an immediate, life-threatening danger to others exists. Midwives must document all ethical decision-making processes clearly in the clinical notes.
