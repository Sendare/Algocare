Human Rights Framework for Sexual and Reproductive Health

Introduction to Human Rights in Midwifery Practice

Midwifery is fundamentally tied to human rights because every pregnant woman and girl deserves dignity, respect, and safe care. As a midwife in Nigeria, you do not just deliver babies; you protect the fundamental freedoms of your clients during one of the most vulnerable moments of their lives. Human rights in maternity care mean that healthcare is provided without discrimination based on tribe, religion, age, or financial status. When midwives understand these principles, maternity wards become safer, more welcoming spaces where patients are treated as active participants in their own healthcare rather than passive recipients of orders.

Understanding the Human Rights Framework

The human rights framework is a set of international and domestic standards designed to protect human dignity. It rests on several core principles that directly apply to sexual and reproductive health:

  • Universality: Rights belong to every single person simply by virtue of being human.
  • Inalienability: Rights cannot be taken away or surrendered voluntarily.
  • Indivisibility: Civil and political rights cannot be fully enjoyed without economic, social, and cultural rights, such as the right to health.
  • Non-discrimination: Services must be accessible to all without bias.

In clinical practice, these principles mean a rural peasant woman has the exact same claim to emergency obstetric care as a wealthy urban resident.

Sexual and Reproductive Health Rights as Fundamental Freedoms

Sexual and reproductive health rights (SRHR) are not separate privileges; they are established human rights. They guarantee that individuals can make free and informed decisions about their own bodies, sexuality, and reproduction without coercion, violence, or discrimination. For a midwife, this encompasses access to safe contraception, comprehensive maternal health services, prevention of harmful practices, and the right to information. When a woman is denied family planning counseling or forced to continue a pregnancy against her will, her fundamental freedoms are violated.

Core International Treaties and Declarations

Global consensus on reproductive rights is anchored in several key international agreements that hold signatory governments accountable. Nigeria is a signatory to many of these instruments:

  1. CEDAW: The Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women mandates state action to end discrimination against women in healthcare.
  2. ICPD Programme of Action: Resulting from the 1994 Cairo conference, it shifted population policies toward individual reproductive rights and women's empowerment.
  3. Maputo Protocol: The Protocol to the African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights on the Rights of Women in Africa explicitly addresses African realities, protecting women's health, calling for the reduction of maternal mortality, and advocating for safe abortion under specific legal conditions.

Obligations of Healthcare Providers and the State

Human rights impose strict legal and ethical duties divided into state obligations and provider responsibilities. The government must respect, protect, and fulfill rights by funding functional clinics, training skilled health workers, and passing protective laws. Healthcare providers act as agents of the state in this context. You have a legal duty to provide timely emergency care, maintain medical confidentiality, and respect patient autonomy. Neglecting a patient in active labor or demanding illegal bribes before rendering emergency obstetric care is not just unprofessional; it is a direct violation of human rights.

Addressing Bodily Autonomy and Consent in Labor

Bodily autonomy means every person has the right to make decisions about their own body without external interference. In labor and delivery, this translates directly to the principle of informed consent and the right to refuse procedures. A midwife must explain every intervention—such as episiotomies, vaginal examinations, or inductions—in clear, local language and obtain permission before proceeding. Routinely performing procedures without explanation or permission, often called obstetric violence, strips women of their dignity and violates their bodily integrity. Emergency situations require rapid action, but clear communication remains essential wherever possible.

Overcoming Barriers to Rights-Based Care in Nigeria

Implementing rights-based midwifery care in Nigeria faces significant hurdles, including severe infrastructural deficits, cultural norms that silence women, poverty, and overburdened health facilities. Midwives often work long shifts with poor equipment, leading to burnout and frustration that can inadvertently manifest as harsh treatment of clients. Overcoming these barriers requires individual commitment and systemic change. Midwives can lead by example by practicing respectful maternity care, advocating for better facility resources, and educating communities to understand that safe maternity care is a fundamental entitlement, not a privilege.
