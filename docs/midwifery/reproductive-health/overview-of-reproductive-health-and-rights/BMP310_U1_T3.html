Gender Dynamics

Understanding Gender Dynamics in Healthcare

Gender dynamics refer to the social and cultural ways in which men and women relate, share power, and access resources within a community. In Nigerian healthcare spaces, these dynamics heavily influence who seeks medical attention, who controls household funds for treatment, and how health messages are received. Midwives must recognize that sex is biological, while gender is socially constructed—meaning society dictates the roles, behaviors, and expectations attached to being male or female.

When a pregnant woman visits a clinic, her ability to follow medical advice or return for follow-up care is rarely just an individual choice. It is often shaped by the expectations of her partner, mother-in-law, or broader community structure. Understanding these underlying patterns prevents midwives from blaming patients for missed appointments and helps trace barriers back to household and societal power imbalances.

Social Roles and Expectations in Nigerian Communities

Traditional and modern Nigerian communities often assign distinct, rigid spheres of influence to men and women. Men are traditionally viewed as the primary breadwinners and public protectors, placing them in positions of authority regarding family finances and external affairs. Women are frequently associated with domestic duties, child-rearing, and subsistence farming or small-scale trading.

  • Economic Dependence: Many women lack independent sources of income, making them reliant on male partners to pay for transport to health facilities, drugs, and laboratory tests.
  • Time Burden: Women shoulder heavy domestic workloads, which can delay them from leaving home for antenatal visits.
  • Stoicism Expectations: Cultural norms sometimes pressure women to endure pregnancy discomforts silently, viewing pain as a normal test of endurance rather than a symptom needing medical review.

Power Relations and Decision-Making in Reproductive Health

Decision-making power within the family directly impacts reproductive health outcomes. In many patriarchal settings, decisions regarding family size, contraceptive use, and the choice of birthplace are reserved for men or senior family members. A woman may desire birth spacing or hospital delivery but lack the autonomy to execute her choice without male consent.

This dynamic creates friction when healthcare messaging targets only the pregnant woman. If a midwife advises a patient to sleep under an insecticide-treated net or eat nutritious foods, but the husband controls the household budget and food distribution, the advice cannot be easily implemented. Midwives frequently encounter situations where patients whisper requests for family planning services, fearing backlash from partners who equate fertility control with infidelity or loss of control.

Impact of Gender Inequities on Maternal and Child Health Outcomes

Gender inequities translate directly into preventable maternal morbidity and mortality statistics. When women have delayed access to care due to lack of transport money, or when they arrive late at health facilities because permission was withheld, the risk of severe complications skyrockets. The three delays model—delay in deciding to seek care, delay in reaching a facility, and delay in receiving appropriate care—is heavily driven by underlying gender disparities.

Furthermore, chronic maternal exhaustion from overwork combined with poor nutrition, often caused by cultural rules where men eat first and get the best portions, contributes to high rates of iron-deficiency anemia and low birth weight infants. Addressing clinical complications without addressing these foundational social inequalities leaves the root causes of poor health untouched.

Strategies for Midwives to Navigate Gender-Sensitive Care

Providing gender-sensitive care requires midwives to look beyond clinical protocols and actively engage communities. Midwives must create welcoming environments that encourage male partner participation without stripping pregnant women of their autonomy. Inviting men to special antenatal education sessions helps bridge the knowledge gap and transforms partners from gatekeepers into active supporters of maternal health.

  • Male Involvement: Design clinic schedules or outreach programs that welcome male partners so they understand danger signs in pregnancy.
  • Community Dialogue: Work with traditional and religious leaders to reshape harmful gender norms that restrict healthcare access.
  • Empowering Communication: Counsel women privately while building their confidence to discuss health needs with their households.
