Child Marriage

Defining Child Marriage and its Cultural Context in Nigeria

Child marriage refers to any formal marriage or informal union where one or both parties are under the age of eighteen. In Nigeria, this practice is deeply rooted in a complex interplay of patriarchy, poverty, religion, and customary laws, varying significantly across different geopolitical zones. While the southern regions have largely enacted and enforced laws protecting girl children, parts of the north maintain high prevalence rates driven by historical traditions and misinterpretations of religious texts. Midwives working across Nigeria must understand these local nuances, recognizing that attacking cultural practices directly often causes community resistance. Instead, engagement through trusted community leaders yields better dialogue regarding the protection of young adolescents.

Drivers and Root Causes of Early Marriage

Multiple intersecting factors perpetuate the practice of child marriage across vulnerable communities. Poverty remains a primary driver, as some families view marrying off a young daughter as a way to ease household financial burdens or secure dowries. Gender inequality also plays a massive role; families often prioritize the education of boys while treating girls as economic assets to be traded. Furthermore, insecurity and humanitarian crises in certain regions lead parents to marry off young girls prematurely under the mistaken belief that a husband will offer physical protection. Traditional gender norms that equate a young girl's value with chastity and early reproduction further cement these harmful patterns.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Consequences

The physiological toll of early pregnancy on an immature body is profound and often life-threatening. Young adolescent girls have pelvises that are not fully developed, leading to a drastically increased risk of obstructed labor.

  • Obstetric Fistula: Prolonged, obstructed labor without timely medical intervention causes tissue necrosis, leading to continuous, uncontrollable leakage of urine or feces.
  • Hemorrhage and Sepsis: Immature uteruses contract poorly after delivery, predisposing young mothers to severe postpartum bleeding and fatal infections.
  • High Maternal and Neonatal Mortality: Pregnancy-related complications remain a leading cause of death for girls aged fifteen to nineteen globally, with even higher risks for younger adolescents.

Social, Psychological, and Economic Impacts

Beyond physical injuries, child marriage abruptly halts a girl's personal development and traps her in a cycle of disadvantage. Victims are typically forced to drop out of school, permanently destroying their chances of financial independence and locking them into lifelong poverty. Psychologically, these young brides experience high rates of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress due to isolation, forced sexual initiation, and power imbalances within their marital homes. They are stripped of their agency, often unable to negotiate safe sex or make basic decisions regarding their own health and the well-being of their children.

Legal Frameworks and Human Rights Violations

Child marriage is internationally recognized as a severe violation of fundamental human rights, robbing girls of their right to education, health, and a safe childhood. In Nigeria, the Child Rights Act sets the minimum age of marriage at eighteen, yet its adoption and enforcement vary across states, particularly in the northern region where Sharia or customary laws sometimes take precedence. Midwives must understand that international conventions like CEDAW and the African Charter on the Rights and Welfare of the Child explicitly condemn this practice, providing a solid legal basis for advocacy and community protection.

The Role of the Midwife in Prevention and Care

Midwives occupy a frontline position in both preventing child marriage and managing its devastating health outcomes. In clinical settings, they provide compassionate, non-judgmental antenatal care tailored to the unique physical needs of young adolescent mothers. Beyond the clinic, midwives act as educators and advocates by engaging communities, schools, and religious leaders to raise awareness about the dangers of early unions. When encountering an underage pregnant girl or a planned child marriage, midwives must work within multidisciplinary teams involving social workers and legal aid services to protect the child's rights and safety.
