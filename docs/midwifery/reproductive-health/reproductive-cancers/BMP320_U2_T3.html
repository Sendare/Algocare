Breast Cancer: Screening Methods, Breast Self-Examination (BSE), Mammography, and Treatment Modalities

Introduction to Breast Cancer and Early Detection

Breast cancer remains one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting women globally, including in Nigeria. It originates from the epithelial cells lining the ducts or lobules of the breast tissue. Early detection is the cornerstone of reducing mortality and improving treatment outcomes. When diagnosed at an early local stage, the five-year survival rate is significantly higher compared to when the disease is discovered after distant metastasis. Midwives play a vital frontline role in educating communities, demystifying cultural myths surrounding breast lumps, and promoting timely clinical evaluations.

Overview of Screening Methods

A comprehensive breast cancer screening strategy relies on multiple modalities tailored to resource availability and patient risk profiles.

  • Breast Self-Examination (BSE): A monthly self-assessment empowering women to know their normal breast contours and detect changes early.
  • Clinical Breast Examination (CBE): A physical examination performed by a trained healthcare provider during routine clinic visits.
  • Mammography: Low-dose X-ray imaging considered the gold standard for early detection in asymptomatic women, though availability varies in low-resource settings.
  • Ultrasound: Often used as an adjunct imaging tool to differentiate between solid masses and fluid-filled cysts, especially in dense breast tissue.

Breast Self-Examination (BSE) Technique and Education

BSE remains an accessible, cost-free awareness tool, though it does not replace mammography. Midwives must teach women to perform BSE monthly, ideally three to five days after the onset of menstruation when breasts are least tender and swollen. For postmenopausal women, selecting a fixed calendar date each month is ideal.

  1. Inspection: Stand before a mirror with shoulders straight and hands on hips to observe symmetry, skin dimpling, or nipple retraction.
  2. Palpation: Use the pads of the middle three fingers, applying varying levels of light, medium, and deep pressure across the entire breast tissue.
  3. Pattern: Utilize a systematic search pattern, such as the vertical strip method, circular motion, or wedge pattern, ensuring the entire breast and axilla are covered.

Mammography: Procedures, Indications, and Interpretation

Mammography captures high-resolution radiographic images of compressed breast tissue. Proper compression is necessary to spread out overlapping tissue structures, minimize radiation exposure, and enhance image clarity. Standard views include the craniocaudal (CC) and mediolateral oblique (MLO) projections. Midwives should counsel women that temporary discomfort during breast compression is normal. Interpreting radiologists look for clustered microcalcifications, architectural distortions, and suspicious soft tissue masses that signal potential malignancy.

Complementary Imaging and Diagnostic Workup

When a screening test reveals an abnormality, diagnostic workup moves beyond basic imaging. Diagnostic mammography includes magnified or spot-compression views to take a closer look at a specific area. Breast ultrasound is invaluable for characterizing masses discovered on mammograms or physical exams. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) offers high sensitivity and is reserved for high-risk screening or evaluating local disease extent. Ultimately, definitive diagnosis requires a tissue biopsy—either core needle biopsy or surgical excision—evaluated by a pathologist.

Overview of Treatment Modalities

Management of breast cancer is multidisciplinary, depending on tumor stage, receptor status, and patient health status.

  • Surgery: Options range from breast-conserving surgery (lumpectomy) to total mastectomy, often accompanied by sentinel lymph node biopsy.
  • Radiation Therapy: Uses high-energy rays to eradicate microscopic residual cancer cells in the breast after conservative surgery.
  • Chemotherapy: Systemic cytotoxic medications used to destroy rapidly dividing cancer cells, given either before surgery (neoadjuvant) or after (adjuvant).
  • Hormone Therapy: Medications like tamoxifen block estrogen receptors in hormone-receptor-positive tumors.
  • Targeted Therapy: Specialized biological drugs designed against specific molecular targets, such as HER2-positive malignancies.

The Midwife's Role in Patient Support and Follow-up

Midwives encounter women at various reproductive life stages and occupy a trusted position in the community. Beyond screening education, midwives provide crucial emotional support following a cancer diagnosis, addressing fears regarding body image, fertility, and mortality. They facilitate timely referrals to oncology specialists and help navigate complex healthcare pathways. Long-term follow-up care includes monitoring for treatment side effects, promoting lymphedema prevention strategies, and reinforcing the importance of regular surveillance visits to catch any recurrence early.
