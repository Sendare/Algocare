Marasmus

Understanding Marasmus: Overview and Core Pathophysiology

Marasmus is a severe form of protein-energy malnutrition resulting from a profound, chronic deficiency of both total calories and protein. It typically affects infants under the age of one year living in environments with chronic food insecurity, recurrent infections, or inadequate breastfeeding practices.

At the core of the pathophysiology is starvation. When dietary energy intake falls catastrophically short of metabolic demands, the body mobilizes its own tissues to survive.

  1. Glycogen Depletion: The liver quickly exhausts its stored glucose within the first 24 hours of fasting.
  2. Lipolysis: Adipose tissue breaks down, releasing free fatty acids to fuel vital organs, stripping the body of its subcutaneous fat layer.
  3. Muscle Wasting: Cortisol levels rise, breaking down skeletal muscle to amino acids for gluconeogenesis in the liver, preserving brain and heart function at the expense of somatic mass.

This adaptation explains the striking physical appearance of severe muscle and fat loss, reflecting the body's desperate attempt to maintain vital organ perfusion amidst total energy bankruptcy.

Etiology and Precipitating Factors in the Nigerian Context

In Nigeria, marasmus is frequently driven by socioeconomic challenges, poverty, and cultural infant-feeding dynamics. While exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months is strongly advocated, early cessation of breastfeeding often introduces contaminated, highly diluted cereal gruels (such as 'ogi' or 'eko') that lack adequate caloric density and essential micronutrients.

Recurrent infections act as a major precipitating factor. Conditions like gastroenteritis, malaria, and measles trigger a catabolic state while simultaneously reducing appetite. A sick infant eats less, absorbs fewer nutrients, and burns through energy reserves faster—a vicious cycle known as the infection-malnutrition complex. Poor environmental sanitation and lack of potable water amplify this cycle through repeated diarrheal episodes.

Clinical Presentation and Physical Assessment Findings

The clinical presentation of a child with marasmus is distinct and immediately recognizable upon physical examination. The child appears drastically underweight with a wizened, 'old man' face, known as facies senilis, caused by the complete loss of buccal fat pads.

Key physical assessment findings include:

  • Severe Wasting: Pronounced loss of subcutaneous fat and skeletal muscle, making ribs and joints appear prominent.
  • Skin Changes: Loose, wrinkled skin folds hanging loosely over the buttocks and thighs due to the disappearance of underlying fat.
  • Alertness: Unlike children with kwashiorkor who are often apathetic and lethargic, marasmic infants tend to remain intensely alert, fretful, and irritable.
  • Vital Signs: Often exhibits bradycardia, hypothermia, and hypotension secondary to reduced basal metabolic rate and poor nutritional reserves.

Differentiating Marasmus from Kwashiorkor

While both marasmus and kwashiorkor belong to the spectrum of protein-energy malnutrition, their clinical features and biochemical profiles differ significantly.

Systemic Complications and Associated Deficiencies

Severe marasmus impacts every organ system in the growing body. Immunodeficiency is a major concern, as thymic atrophy and suppressed cell-mediated immunity leave the child vulnerable to overwhelming sepsis, pneumonia, and persistent diarrhea.

Gastrointestinal mucosal atrophy leads to reduced digestive enzyme production and impaired barrier function, making refeeding challenging due to malabsorption. Electrolyte imbalances are common, particularly hypokalemia and hyponatremia. Concurrent micronutrient deficiencies frequently coexist, manifesting as vitamin A deficiency (increasing the risk of corneal ulceration and blindness), iron-deficiency anemia, and zinc deficiency, which further impairs growth and wound healing.

Midwifery Management and Nutritional Rehabilitation Protocols

Managing marasmus requires a structured, multi-phase approach guided by established international protocols to prevent refeeding syndrome and metabolic collapse.

  1. Stabilization Phase: Treat hypoglycemia, hypothermia, and dehydration with specialized oral rehydration solutions (such as ReSoMal) rather than standard solutions high in sodium. Treat infections with broad-spectrum antibiotics even if overt signs are absent.
  2. Rehabilitation Phase: Gradually increase caloric and protein intake using therapeutic milk formulas (such as F-75 transitioning to F-100) or Ready-to-Use Therapeutic Food (RUTF). Encourage frequent, small feeds around the clock.
  3. Catch-up Growth: Provide stimulating care, emotional support, and micronutrient supplementation once the child's appetite returns and medical stabilization is achieved.

Community Education and Prevention Strategies

Sustainable reduction of marasmus relies heavily on community-based health education and primary healthcare outreach led by midwives. Key prevention pillars include promoting exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months, followed by the introduction of nutrient-dense, locally available complementary foods rich in proteins and micronutrients.

Midwives must educate caregivers on proper hygiene practices, safe water handling, and complete childhood immunization schedules to prevent infectious disease outbreaks like measles and diarrhea. Growth monitoring and promotion (GMP) sessions at local clinics allow for early detection of faltering growth before a child descends into full-blown marasmus.
