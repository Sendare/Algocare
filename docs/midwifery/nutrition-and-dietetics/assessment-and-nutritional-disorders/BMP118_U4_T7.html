Overnutrition, Obesity and Lifestyle-Related Non-Communicable Diseases

Understanding Overnutrition and Anthropometric Assessment in Midwifery

Overnutrition occurs when energy intake regularly exceeds energy expenditure, leading to abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that impairs health. In maternal health across Nigeria, changing dietary patterns and urban lifestyles have driven up the prevalence of this condition. Midwives evaluate nutritional status primarily through anthropometric measurements during antenatal booking and routine visits.

  • Body Mass Index (BMI): Calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²), serving as a standard population screening tool.
  • Mid-Upper Arm Circumference (MUAC): Often used for rapid nutritional screening, though specialized cut-offs are needed to assess overweight states.
  • Waist Circumference: Measures central adiposity, correlating strongly with visceral fat accumulation and metabolic risk.
  • Weight Gain Tracking: Monitoring the rate of gestational weight gain against pre-pregnancy BMI helps prevent excessive accumulation.

Pathophysiology of Excess Adipose Tissue

Adipose tissue is not merely a passive energy storage depot; it functions as an active endocrine organ. When fat cells expand beyond their normal capacity, they undergo cellular stress and hypoxia. This triggers the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and free fatty acids into the bloodstream.

Systemic inflammation caused by excess fat interferes with normal insulin signaling pathways. This disruption leads to insulin resistance, forcing the pancreas to produce higher amounts of insulin to maintain blood glucose balance. Over time, pancreatic beta-cell exhaustion can occur, transitioning the patient toward impaired glucose tolerance and overt type 2 diabetes. Furthermore, altered lipid profiles driven by visceral adiposity promote endothelial dysfunction, laying the structural groundwork for hypertension and cardiovascular complications.

Lifestyle-Related Non-Communicable Diseases Linked to Obesity

Obesity serves as a major common denominator for several chronic, non-communicable diseases (NCDs) that severely impact long-term health and maternal wellbeing. Chronic high blood pressure, or hypertension, frequently accompanies excess weight due to increased blood volume and heightened vascular resistance. Type 2 diabetes mellitus often follows prolonged insulin resistance. Additionally, dyslipidemia—characterized by elevated triglycerides and low HDL cholesterol—accelerates atherosclerosis. Midwives frequently encounter these intersecting conditions during routine screening of older gravidae or women with chronic metabolic disorders.

Specific Risks for Pregnant Women and the Developing Fetus

Maternal obesity significantly alters the intrauterine environment, creating unique clinical challenges for both mother and baby. Pregnant women with high BMI face markedly elevated risks of gestational diabetes mellitus, pre-eclampsia, thromboembolic events, and prolonged labor. Surgical interventions, including cesarean sections, are more frequent and carry higher rates of wound infection.

For the developing fetus, maternal hyperglycemia leads to excessive fetal growth, resulting in macrosomia. Macrosomia increases the likelihood of shoulder dystocia and birth trauma. Furthermore, babies exposed to a high-nutrient, hyperglycemic intrauterine environment face a higher programmed risk of developing obesity and metabolic syndrome themselves in later life.

Midwifery Interventions: Counseling, Diet, and Physical Activity

Midwives play a pivotal role in promoting healthy lifestyles and preventing excessive weight gain during the childbearing years. Antenatal visits provide an ideal window for non-judgmental nutritional counseling. Guidance should focus on whole foods, adequate protein, fiber intake, and the reduction of sugary beverages and refined carbohydrates.

Physical activity recommendations must be tailored safely to pregnancy status. Moderate exercises such as brisk walking, swimming, or stationary cycling help improve insulin sensitivity and manage weight gain without endangering the pregnancy. Emphasizing small, sustainable behavioral changes yields better long-term health outcomes than restrictive, short-term dieting.
