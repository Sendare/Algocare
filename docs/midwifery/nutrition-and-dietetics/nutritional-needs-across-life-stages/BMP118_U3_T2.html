Exclusive Breastfeeding

Defining Exclusive Breastfeeding and Its Global and Local Targets

Exclusive breastfeeding means giving an infant only breast milk from birth until the end of the sixth month of life. The baby receives no other food, water, other liquids, or traditional herbal mixtures, with the exception of prescribed vitamin drops, mineral supplements, or medicines. In Nigeria, despite widespread awareness, the exclusive breastfeeding rate remains low due to cultural pressures, early introduction of water, and early return to work. Midwives play a key role in explaining that breast milk alone contains enough water and nutrients for the first six months, even in hot weather. Meeting the World Health Organization and national targets helps reduce infant mortality and improves childhood development across communities.

Physiology of Lactation: Hormones and Milk Production

Breast milk production relies on a delicate balance of hormones working throughout pregnancy and after delivery. Prolactin stimulates the secretory cells in the alveoli to produce milk, driven largely by the frequency of suckling. Oxytocin triggers the let-down reflex, causing the myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli to contract and push milk down the ducts. Emotional stress, pain, or anxiety can inhibit oxytocin release, making milk flow difficult. Conversely, hearing the baby cry or feeling confident can trigger the reflex effortlessly.

Nutritional and Immunological Superiority of Breast Milk

Breast milk is uniquely tailored to meet an infant's needs, changing in composition from colostrum to mature milk. Colostrum, the thick yellow milk produced in the first few days, acts as the baby's first immunization because it is rich in secretory IgA, white blood cells, and protective factors.

  • Colostrum: High in protein and antibodies, low in fat, perfectly suited for the newborn gut.
  • Foremilk: Quenches thirst at the start of a feed, containing high levels of water, vitamins, and protein.
  • Hindmilk: Appears later in the feed, rich in fats and calories necessary for steady weight gain.

Initiation, Positioning, and Attachment Techniques

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of birth establishes a strong bond and stimulates milk supply. Good attachment prevents sore nipples and ensures the baby removes milk efficiently.

  1. Body alignment: The baby's head and body must be in a straight line.
  2. Close contact: The baby is held close facing the mother's breast, with the nose opposite the nipple.
  3. Wide gape: The mother waits for the baby to open the mouth wide before moving the baby onto the breast.
  4. Areola coverage: More of the lower areola should be in the baby's mouth than the upper areola.

Maternal Nutritional Requirements and Health Support

Producing milk demands extra energy, fluids, and nutrients from the mother. A nursing mother needs roughly 500 extra calories per day, alongside increased protein, calcium, and iron intake. Hydration is vital; mothers should drink water whenever they feel thirsty during feeds. In many Nigerian settings, traditional soups and porridges provide nourishing calories and fluid support. Midwives must encourage family members to support the mother with household chores so she can rest and nourish herself properly.

Common Challenges, Myths, and Midwifery Interventions

Breastfeeding is natural, but it is also a learned skill that can encounter hurdles. Common challenges include engorgement, mastitis, inverted nipples, and the pervasive myth that breast milk alone is insufficient for a baby.

  • Engorgement: Caused by milk stasis and swelling; managed by frequent feeding and gentle warm compresses before expressing milk.
  • Mastitis: An infection of breast tissue marked by pain, redness, and fever; managed with continued milk removal and appropriate antibiotics.
  • Myths: Addressing beliefs that colostrum is 'dirty' or that crying always indicates hunger rather than wind or discomfort.

Monitoring Infant Growth and Development Milestones

Assessing an infant's progress reassures both the mother and the midwife that exclusive breastfeeding is succeeding. Indicators of adequate intake include weight gain along the growth chart, frequent wet nappies (at least six pale yellow diapers daily), and content behavior after feeds. Midwives use infant weighing scales and developmental observation during immunization visits to track health. Babies who lose more than 10% of their birth weight or fail to regain it by two weeks need careful assessment.

Transitioning to Complementary Feeding

As the infant reaches six months, nutritional demands exceed what breast milk alone can supply, particularly for iron and zinc. Complementary foods must be introduced gradually while continuing frequent breastfeeding. Foods should be nutrient-dense, clean, and prepared safely without excessive salt or spice. Midwives guide mothers to introduce one single-ingredient food at a time, observing for allergies before mixing foods or moving to family meals.
