Screening for Eligibility Using WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use

Introduction to Contraceptive Eligibility Assessment

Providing family planning services safely requires more than simply handing out a method of choice. Screening for contraceptive eligibility protects women from health risks associated with specific hormonal or barrier products. Midwives in Nigeria often encounter clients who have pre-existing medical conditions like hypertension, diabetes, or a history of thromboembolism. Without careful assessment, certain contraceptives can worsen these conditions or create severe complications.

The World Health Organization developed the Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use, universally known as the WHO MEC, to guide these clinical decisions. This tool classifies medical conditions and personal characteristics into safety categories, helping midwives match clients with methods that are both effective and safe. Proper screening builds trust, prevents unwanted pregnancies, and ensures that clinical care respects the holistic health of every woman walking into the clinic.

Understanding the WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria Categories

The WHO MEC system is built around four distinct numerical categories. These categories indicate whether a woman with a specific medical condition can safely use a particular contraceptive method. Midwives must memorize or readily reference these groupings during client consultations.

  • Category 1: A condition for which there is no restriction for the use of the contraceptive method. You can use the method under any circumstances.
  • Category 2: A condition where the advantages of using the method generally outweigh the theoretical or proven risks. Use of the method is generally allowed.
  • Category 3: A condition where the theoretical or proven risks usually outweigh the advantages of using the method. The method is not usually recommended unless other more appropriate methods are not available or acceptable.
  • Category 4: A condition that represents an unacceptable health risk if the contraceptive method is used. The method must not be used.

Taking a Focused Medical and Reproductive History

A thorough client history forms the bedrock of safe contraceptive screening. Midwives cannot rely on guesswork or brief superficial questions when assessing eligibility. The history-taking process must investigate past and present illnesses, reproductive goals, and specific risk factors.

Key areas to cover during the assessment include:

  1. Cardiovascular health: Screen for a history of high blood pressure, deep vein thrombosis, stroke, or severe headaches like migraines with aura.
  2. Liver and reproductive history: Inquire about unexplained vaginal bleeding, current or past liver disease, and breast cancer history.
  3. Infection risks: Ask about sexually transmitted infections or pelvic inflammatory disease risk.
  4. Lifestyle factors: Note tobacco use, especially for women over thirty-five years of age, as this drastically alters combined hormonal contraceptive safety.

Navigating the WHO MEC Wheel and Tables in Clinical Practice

Clinical environments are busy, and memorizing every single WHO MEC table entry is nearly impossible. That is why the WHO provides practical job aids, most notably the MEC Wheel and quick-reference summary tables. Midwives should keep these tools on the clinic desk or inside their consultation room.

The MEC wheel is a rotating dial device that allows a clinician to quickly align a client's medical condition with various contraceptive classes. It provides instant color-coded or numbered category readouts. Using these tools reduces clinical error and reassures clients that professional guidelines support their care plan. Always cross-check complex conditions with the full printed tables if the wheel provides ambiguous results.

Applying Categories to Hormonal and Intrauterine Methods

Different contraceptive methods interact with the body through different mechanisms, meaning a condition rated Category 4 for one method might be a Category 1 for another. For instance, combined hormonal contraceptives contain estrogen, which increases clotting risks. Consequently, a postpartum woman within twenty-one days of delivery cannot use combined oral contraceptives (Category 4), yet she can safely use progestin-only pills or copper intrauterine devices.

Intrauterine devices carry their own distinct screening rules. A woman with current purulent cervicitis or pelvic inflammatory disease must not have an intrauterine device inserted (Category 4), because the procedure can push bacteria deeper into the upper reproductive tract. Midwives must evaluate each method separately rather than assuming all hormonal or long-acting methods share the same safety profile.

Managing Complex Cases and Medical Consultation

Not every client presents with a straightforward medical background. Some women have multiple co-existing morbidities, such as systemic lupus erythematosus with positive antiphospholipid antibodies, or severe cirrhosis. In these complex scenarios, the WHO MEC guidelines often suggest consulting a specialist physician before initiating a method.

When managing complex cases, the midwife acts as a vital coordinator of care. Document the client's exact clinical findings, write a clear referral note detailing the contraceptive options under consideration, and collaborate with the attending medical officer. Never guess when a condition falls into ambiguous Category 3 or 4 territories. Client safety always supersedes the desire to provide a quick prescription.
