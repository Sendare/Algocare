Medical History Taking and Physical Assessment for Family Planning Clients

Introduction to Client Assessment in Family Planning

Client assessment in family planning is the systematic process of gathering health information to help a client choose a safe and effective contraceptive method. Midwives conduct this assessment during the initial visit and periodically during follow-up care. The primary goals are identifying any underlying health conditions, preventing complications related to specific contraceptive hormones or devices, and tailoring choices to the client's reproductive goals.

A thorough assessment moves beyond simply asking what method the client wants. It uncovers hidden risks, such as undiagnosed hypertension or heavy menstrual bleeding, which completely change which methods are safe to use. By combining medical history, physical checks, and empathetic communication, the midwife protects the client's health while supporting their bodily autonomy.

Establishing Rapport and Confidentiality

Family planning deals with deeply personal aspects of life, sexuality, and reproduction. Building trust immediately is non-negotiable. Greet the client warmly, use local language preferences where appropriate, and maintain a non-judgmental tone.

Confidentiality forms the bedrock of reproductive health care, especially for adolescents or unmarried clients in restrictive community settings. Explicitly explain to the client that their health records and conversation stay private within the clinical team. Reassuring clients that their disclosures will not be shared with family members or community leaders encourages complete honesty about sexual history, previous abortions, or STI symptoms.

Comprehensive Medical History Taking

A structured medical history captures past and present illnesses that influence contraceptive safety. Midwives use a systematic inquiry covering several key areas:

  • Obstetric and Menstrual History: Record parity, date of last normal menstrual period, cycle length, and presence of dysmenorrhea or menorrhagia.
  • Medical History: Screen for chronic conditions such as diabetes, severe headaches, liver disease, cardiovascular disorders, and thromboembolic events.
  • Surgical History: Note previous pelvic surgeries, cesarean sections, or tubal ligations.
  • Social and Lifestyle History: Inquire about tobacco smoking, alcohol use, and intimate partner violence risks.

Screening for Medical Eligibility Criteria (MEC)

The World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria (WHO MEC) categorizes contraceptive methods into four distinct classes based on safety for clients with specific conditions:

  1. Category 1: A condition for which there is no restriction for the use of the contraceptive method.
  2. Category 2: A condition where the advantages of using the method generally outweigh the theoretical or proven risks.
  3. Category 3: A condition where the theoretical or proven risks usually outweigh the advantages of using the method.
  4. Category 4: A condition that represents an unacceptable health risk if the contraceptive method is used.

Midwives cross-reference client findings against these categories during every initial evaluation to rule out dangerous method combinations.

Standard Physical Assessment Procedures

Physical assessment in family planning provides baseline clinical measurements to ensure safety. Every client requires a baseline blood pressure measurement, as rising pressures contraindicate estrogen-containing methods. Weight and body mass index (BMI) checks help monitor metabolic changes over time, particularly with progestin-only injectables or implants.

A general inspection also helps identify outward signs of anemia, jaundice, or thyroid disorders that could influence method selection or signal underlying illness. While extensive physical exams are not always required for every method, vital signs and general observations remain foundational.

Targeted Pelvic and Reproductive System Examination

A pelvic examination is not an absolute prerequisite for initiating many modern contraceptive methods, but it remains crucial when checking for specific conditions or when fitting intrauterine devices (IUDs).

When performed, the pelvic exam includes visual inspection of the external genitalia, speculum examination of the cervix and vagina to rule out active infections or abnormal discharge, and bimanual palpation to assess uterine size, position, and detect adnexal tenderness or masses. Midwives must explain each step of the pelvic examination beforehand to reduce client anxiety and secure informed consent.

Laboratory Investigations and Baseline Tests

Laboratory tests support clinical decision-making but should never become an unnecessary barrier to accessing family planning. The most common investigations include:

  • Pregnancy Testing: Essential when there is any doubt about whether the client is currently pregnant before starting a method.
  • Hemoglobin or Packed Cell Volume (PCV): Useful baseline for clients with heavy menses, particularly when considering intrauterine devices.
  • Cervical Screening: Pap smear or visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA) where available, aligned with national screening guidelines.
  • STI Screening: Targeted tests for infections when history or clinical signs suggest exposure.

Synthesizing Findings to Guide Method Choice

Assessment concludes by bringing all gathered data together. The midwife reviews the medical history, physical checks, lab results, and the client's personal preferences in a cohesive summary.

During this synthesis, the midwife presents safe contraceptive options that match the client's eligibility category and lifestyle needs. If a preferred method is contraindicated, the midwife explains why in clear, supportive language and guides the client toward an equally effective alternative. Shared decision-making ensures the client leaves the clinic with a method they understand, trust, and can use successfully.
