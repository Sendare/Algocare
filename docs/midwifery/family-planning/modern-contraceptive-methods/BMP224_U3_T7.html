Implants (Jadelle, Implanon)

Understanding Contraceptive Implants and Mechanism of Action

Contraceptive implants are small, flexible rods or capsules containing synthetic progestin hormones that are placed just beneath the skin of a woman's upper arm. Once inserted, they continuously release a low, steady dose of hormone into the bloodstream, providing long-acting reversible contraception.

The primary way implants prevent pregnancy involves three distinct physiological actions:

  1. Inhibition of ovulation: The constant progestin level suppresses the mid-cycle surge of luteinizing hormone, preventing the release of an egg from the ovary.
  2. Thickening of cervical mucus: This creates a physical barrier that stops sperm from entering the upper reproductive tract to fertilize an egg.
  3. Thinning of the endometrium: The lining of the uterus becomes unfavourable for the implantation of a fertilized egg.

Students often wonder if the hormone causes weight gain. While individual experiences vary, the primary action remains strictly local and hormonal on the reproductive organs rather than systemic metabolic disruption.

Overview of Available Implant Types: Jadelle and Implanon

In Nigerian clinical practice, two main brands of subdermal implants are widely distributed through public health facilities and private clinics: Jadelle and Implanon NXT. Understanding their structural differences and duration of action ensures safe and effective client management.

Both devices offer extremely high efficacy rates, comparable to female sterilization, but with the distinct advantage of being fully reversible once removed by a trained healthcare provider.

Client Assessment and Pre-Insertion Counselling

Before any implant procedure, a thorough health assessment is mandatory to rule out pregnancy and screen for medical eligibility using the World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use.

Take a detailed medical history focusing on:

  • Unexplained vaginal bleeding that requires investigation
  • Current or past history of breast cancer
  • Severe liver disease or liver tumours
  • Current use of liver enzyme-inducing drugs like rifampicin or certain anticonvulsants

Counselling must cover expected changes in menstrual bleeding patterns, as spotting or amenorrhea is normal and harmless. Emphasize that implants do not protect against sexually transmitted infections, making dual protection with condoms vital.

Step-by-Step Insertion and Removal Techniques

Proper insertion and removal demand strict aseptic technique and precise anatomical placement on the inner side of the non-dominant upper arm, about 8 to 10 cm above the medial epicondyle of the humerus, avoiding the sulcus where major blood vessels and nerves lie.

  1. Preparation: Position the client comfortably with her arm flexed at the elbow and hand tucked under her head. Clean the site with an antiseptic solution and drape sterilely.
  2. Anesthesia: Inject a local anesthetic like 1% lidocaine intradermally to create a small skin wheal along the planned insertion track.
  3. Insertion: For Jadelle, insert the trocar through a tiny skin incision and place the two rods in a V-pattern just beneath the dermis. For Implanon NXT, use the pre-loaded applicator to slide the single rod subdermally.
  4. Verification: Palpate both ends of the rod(s) to confirm correct placement, then apply a pressure bandage to minimize bruising.

Removal requires a small incision near the tip of the implant, gentle pushing toward the incision, and extraction using mosquito forceps.

Clinical Efficacy and Potential Side Effects

Subdermal implants rank among the most effective reversible contraceptives available, with failure rates of less than one percent in typical use. Because the method does not rely on daily user compliance, its real-world effectiveness matches clinical trial data closely.

Clients frequently experience benign side effects that require reassurance rather than medical intervention:

  • Changes in menstrual bleeding, ranging from frequent spotting to complete absence of menses (amenorrhea)
  • Headaches, mild mood swings, or breast tenderness
  • Localized skin reactions, bruising, or mild pain at the insertion site

Reassure clients that these side effects are generally harmless and tend to decrease over the first few months of use.

Managing Complications and Follow-Up Care

While implants are safe, occasional complications require skilled midwifery management. Localized infection at the insertion site can present with increasing pain, warmth, and purulent discharge; treat this promptly with warm compresses, local wound care, and appropriate antibiotics. If an infection fails to resolve or the implant begins to extrude spontaneously, removal may be necessary.

Deep insertion—where the rod cannot be felt under the skin—is a rare complication usually caused by faulty technique. Do not attempt blind dissection in the clinic; refer the client to a specialized center where ultrasound or X-ray guidance is available.

Encourage clients to return whenever they have concerns, wish to remove the device, or reach its expiration date.
