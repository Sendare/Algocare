Progestin-Only Pills

Introduction to Progestin-Only Pills (POPs) in Nigerian Healthcare

Progestin-Only Pills, commonly known as POPs or the mini-pill, contain a low dose of synthetic progestin and no estrogen. In Nigerian maternal and child health clinics, POPs serve as a vital contraceptive choice, particularly for nursing mothers. Because they contain no estrogen, they do not interfere with breast milk production or infant weight gain, making them a cornerstone of postpartum family planning.

Unlike combined oral contraceptives, POPs require strict adherence to a daily routine. This characteristic demands thorough client education during antenatal and postnatal visits. Midwives across primary health care centres promote POPs to expand contraceptive prevalence and reduce closely spaced pregnancies, which contribute significantly to maternal morbidity in the region.

How Progestin-Only Pills Work: Mechanism of Action

The contraceptive efficacy of progestin-only pills relies on three main physiological actions:

  1. Cervical mucus thickening: Low-dose progestin makes the cervical mucus remarkably viscous and thick, which forms a biological barrier preventing sperm from entering the upper reproductive tract.
  2. Endometrial thinning: The hormone alters the lining of the uterus, making it hostile and unsuitable for the implantation of a fertilized ovum.
  3. Ovulation suppression: While newer desogestrel-containing POPs consistently stop ovulation, traditional levonorgestrel POPs only inhibit ovulation in about half of cycles. The contraceptive effect therefore relies heavily on the first two mechanisms.

Midwives must understand these pathways to reassure clients who worry when they still experience regular or irregular cycles while using the method.

Identifying Ideal Candidates and Medical Eligibility

Progestin-Only Pills offer a safe contraceptive option for many women who cannot tolerate estrogen. According to World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria, POPs are generally safe for clients with a history of deep vein thrombosis, hypertension, or heavy migraines.

  • Breastfeeding mothers: Safe to start as early as six weeks postpartum.
  • Smokers over 35 years: Can use POPs safely since estrogen is absent.
  • Clients with cardiovascular risks: Suitable for those where combined pills are strictly contraindicated.

However, women with current breast cancer, severe liver disease, or unexplained vaginal bleeding must avoid POPs until further medical evaluation is completed.

Initiation and Daily Counseling Protocols

Starting a client on POPs requires clear, practical guidance. Midwives must explain that every single pill in the pack contains hormones and must be taken at the exact same time every day. A delay of just three hours for traditional pills can significantly reduce contraceptive protection.

When initiating treatment, the midwife confirms the client is not pregnant. If starting within the first five days of menstrual bleeding, immediate protection is achieved. If started at any other time in the cycle, the client must use a backup barrier method, such as condoms, for the first forty-eight hours. Emphasizing the importance of continuous daily intake without pill-free intervals helps prevent accidental pregnancies.

Managing Side Effects and Ensuring Compliance

Changes in menstrual bleeding patterns are the most common reason clients discontinue progestin-only pills. Midwives should counsel clients beforehand about potential spotting, irregular bleeding, or complete absence of menses, reassuring them that these changes are harmless.

Building rapport and validating these experiences encourages clients to return for support rather than abandoning contraception secretly.

Addressing Missed Pills and Backup Contraception

Clear action steps must be provided for missed pills. If a client misses a pill by more than three hours, she should take the forgotten pill as soon as she remembers, and then take the next pill at the usual time.

Additional backup contraception, such as male condoms, must be used for the next forty-eight hours. If unprotected sexual intercourse occurred within the past five days during the missed pill window, emergency contraception should be considered. Midwives should write these simple instructions on a clinic reminder card for the client to take home.
