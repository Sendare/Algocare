Copper IUCDs

Mechanism of Action of Copper IUCDs in Emergency Contraception

The copper intrauterine contraceptive device works primarily as a spermicide and prevents fertilization before it even happens. When copper ions are released into the uterine cavity, they create a hostile, inflammatory environment that is toxic to both sperm and ova. This chemical reaction significantly impairs sperm motility and viability, stopping the sperm from reaching and fertilizing an egg in the fallopian tube.

Beyond stopping fertilization, a copper IUCD also changes the lining of the uterus, known as the endometrium. If fertilization somehow manages to occur despite the spermicidal action, this altered endometrial environment prevents implantation of a fertilized egg. Note that a copper IUCD does not disrupt an established pregnancy; its entire action takes place before implantation is complete.

Critical Timing and Window for Emergency Insertion

A copper IUCD is the most effective form of emergency contraception available to midwives, but timing is strict. It must be inserted within 120 hours (5 days) of unprotected sexual intercourse.

Unlike emergency contraceptive pills, which lose effectiveness rapidly over time, the copper IUCD maintains its high efficacy rate right up until the end of the five-day window. In fact, if the exact time of ovulation can be estimated, a copper IUCD can sometimes be inserted even beyond five days, as long as insertion occurs before ovulation takes place. Midwives must calculate the exact hours elapsed since the act of intercourse to ensure the client stays within this safe and effective timeframe.

Client Selection, Screening, and Contraindications

Thorough screening is mandatory before any copper IUCD insertion to protect the client's reproductive health. Midwives must take a careful history to rule out existing pregnancy, active pelvic inflammatory disease, and sexually transmitted infections.

  • Current Pregnancy: Must be ruled out using a reliable pregnancy test or careful history.
  • Pelvic Infections: Active cervicitis, chlamydia, or gonorrhea prevent immediate insertion until treated.
  • Distorted Uterine Cavity: Fibroids or uterine anomalies that distort the cavity make proper placement impossible.
  • Wilson's Disease: A rare metabolic disorder involving copper accumulation makes copper devices unsafe.

Step-by-Step Insertion Procedure for Midwives

  1. Pre-procedure counselling: Explain the steps, check consent, and ensure pain relief options are discussed.
  2. Bimanual examination: Assess the size, position, and mobility of the uterus to avoid perforation.
  3. Speculum insertion: Visualize the cervix clearly and clean the area with an antiseptic solution.
  4. Uterine sounding: Gently insert a sterile sound to measure the depth and direction of the uterine cavity.
  5. Loading and placement: Load the copper IUCD inside its sterile package, pass it through the cervical os, and release it at the fundus.
  6. Trimming threads: Cut the threads so they protrude about two to three centimeters from the external os.

Key Advantages Over Hormonal Emergency Options

Choosing a copper IUCD over emergency contraceptive pills offers distinct clinical and practical benefits for the client. Unlike pills that must be taken as soon as possible and decline in effectiveness over time, the copper IUCD remains equally potent right up to the 120-hour limit.

Another major benefit is ongoing protection. Once inserted for emergency contraception, the client can choose to leave the device in place to serve as her ongoing regular birth control method for years. Furthermore, copper IUCDs contain zero hormones, making them ideal for women who experience adverse side effects from systemic estrogen or progestin. They do not interfere with breastfeeding, liver function, or regular metabolic processes.

Post-Insertion Counselling, Follow-Up Care, and Managing Side Effects

After insertion, midwives must prepare clients for what to expect in the coming weeks. Mild cramping and irregular spotting are common during the first few days as the uterus adjusts to the foreign body. Midwives should reassure clients that mild analgesics like paracetamol or ibuprofen usually control this discomfort.

Clients must also be taught how to feel for the IUCD threads to confirm the device is still in place, especially after their first heavy menstrual period. Warning signs requiring prompt clinic return include severe lower abdominal pain, abnormal vaginal discharge, fever, or unexplained heavy bleeding. A routine follow-up visit is typically scheduled after the client's first menses to check thread length and ensure proper placement.
