Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM): Criteria and Clinical Application

Introduction and Physiological Basis of LAM

The Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM) is a temporary, highly effective natural family planning method based on the natural postpartum infertility that occurs when a woman is fully or nearly fully breastfeeding. When specific physiological conditions are met, the act of suckling suppresses the release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus. This suppression subsequently blocks the pituitary gland from releasing luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), preventing follicular development and ovulation.

For a nursing mother, this hormonal block means her ovaries take a temporary break, making spontaneous pregnancy highly unlikely during the first few months postpartum, provided she meets strict infant feeding routines. This mechanism offers a natural biological delay in the return of fertility without requiring pharmaceutical hormones or barrier devices.

Strict LAM Criteria for Efficacy

LAM is not simply breastfeeding; it is a structured behavioral method. For LAM to achieve greater than 98% contraceptive efficacy during the first six months postpartum, a mother must satisfy three strict criteria simultaneously:

  1. Amenorrhea: The mother must not have experienced any vaginal bleeding after the 56th day (eight weeks) postpartum. Any true menstrual bleeding after this period indicates the return of potential fertility.
  2. Exclusive or Near-Exclusive Breastfeeding: The infant must be fed solely on breast milk. Occasional tastes of water, herbal teas, or complementary foods significantly lower the hormonal suppression and reduce efficacy.
  3. On-Demand Feeding: Feeding must occur frequently day and night, with intervals between feeds generally no longer than four hours during the day and six hours at night.

Clinical Assessment and Patient Counseling

During postnatal clinics in Nigeria, midwives must systematically evaluate whether a nursing mother truly qualifies for LAM. Counseling should never assume a woman understands the strict rules simply because she is breastfeeding. Midwives must ask targeted screening questions regarding the baby's exact feeding patterns, the use of pacifiers or water supplements, and the exact date of any postpartum bleeding.

Counseling also involves preparing the mother for the inevitable end of LAM. Midwives should explore lifestyle factors, such as returning to work or school, which often introduce pumping, expressed milk, or scheduled gaps in feeding that violate the on-demand criterion. Empowering the mother with clear warning signs ensures she recognizes when her biological window of protection is closing.

Advantages and Limitations in Nigerian Clinical Practice

LAM offers unique cultural and economic benefits within Nigerian communities. It is completely free, requires no medical devices or prescription drugs, promotes optimal infant nutrition, and fosters close maternal-infant bonding. In settings where family planning commodities may occasionally experience supply chain disruptions or where cultural stigma surrounds early postpartum contraception, LAM provides an immediately accessible option.

However, limitations do exist. LAM has a strict expiration date: it is only reliable for the first six months postpartum. Furthermore, societal pressures to introduce water, herbal concoctions, or thin porridges early in an infant's life can make maintaining exclusive breastfeeding difficult. Misconceptions that breastfeeding women cannot conceive often lead clients to ignore the return of menses, resulting in unplanned pregnancies.

Seamless Transition to Other Contraceptive Methods

Because LAM expires at six months—or earlier if menses returns or feeding patterns change—midwives must proactively plan for transition. Contraceptive counseling should occur well before the infant turns six months old so the client is never left unprotected.

Many modern contraceptive methods integrate smoothly with breastfeeding. Progestin-only pills (POPs), depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) injectables, and hormonal or copper intrauterine devices (IUDs) do not interfere with established milk production and can be started safely. Barrier methods such as condoms also provide dual protection against sexually transmitted infections while bridging the gap to a longer-term method.
