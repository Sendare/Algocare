Management of Subfertility and Infertility: Primary vs Secondary Infertility Evaluation and Care

Definitions of Primary and Secondary Infertility

In clinical midwifery practice, understanding the precise classification of reproductive difficulties guides the entire diagnostic journey. Primary infertility refers to a couple who has never achieved a clinical pregnancy after at least twelve months of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse. Secondary infertility, conversely, describes couples who have previously achieved at least one recognized pregnancy—regardless of whether it resulted in a live birth, miscarriage, or ectopic gestation—but are now unable to conceive again after twelve months of unprotected intercourse.

In many Nigerian communities, these definitions carry distinct social implications. A woman presenting with secondary infertility often faces unique pressures because she already has a child, leading family members to dismiss her struggles. Midwives must recognize that both conditions cause profound emotional distress. The twelve-month threshold is a standard clinical guideline, though evaluation may begin earlier if the female partner is over thirty-five years of age or if obvious pelvic pathology exists.

Initial Evaluation Procedures for Couples

Evaluating fertility requires a thorough joint investigation of both partners rather than focusing exclusively on the woman. The initial clinical assessment involves a detailed medical, surgical, and reproductive history. Midwives and primary care clinicians look for markers of ovulatory dysfunction, previous pelvic infections, and lifestyle factors that impact gamete quality.

  • History taking: Evaluates menstrual cycle regularity, coital frequency, past sexually transmitted infections, and previous contraceptive use.
  • Ovulation assessment: Utilizes basal body temperature charting, mid-luteal serum progesterone levels, or urinary ovulation predictor kits.
  • Sperm analysis: A foundational test for the male partner assessing concentration, motility, and morphology after a brief period of sexual abstinence.
  • Tubal patency tests: Procedures such as hysterosalpingography to check for blockages in the fallopian tubes.

Care and Referral Pathways

Midwives often serve as the first point of contact for couples experiencing fertility challenges. Recognizing the limits of primary care scope is essential for ensuring timely specialist intervention. When initial basic evaluations reveal complex abnormalities—such as severe oligospermia, complete tubal occlusion, or suspected stage three endometriosis—the midwife must initiate a structured referral to a gynaecologist or a specialized fertility clinic.

Effective referral pathways prevent patients from wasting precious time and financial resources on unproven remedies. The midwife prepares the couple by compiling all preliminary test results, explaining what to expect at the tertiary center, and maintaining supportive continuity of care once the couple returns from the specialist.

Psychosocial Support and Coping Strategies

Fertility challenges evoke a heavy psychological toll, characterized by grief, anxiety, depression, and social isolation. In many African settings, childlessness places immense strain on marital stability, with women frequently bearing the brunt of societal blame. Midwives occupy a trusted position to offer empathetic, non-judgmental counseling.

Creating a safe space for couples to express their fears without immediate medicalizing helps reduce psychological distress. Midwives should actively screen for depressive symptoms and connect couples with peer support groups or professional mental health services when emotional burdens become overwhelming. Validating their emotional experience is just as vital as interpreting diagnostic laboratory results.

Community Education and Stigma Reduction

Myths surrounding infertility persist widely in local communities, often attributing childlessness solely to female failure or supernatural forces. Community-level health education led by midwives plays a transformative role in correcting these misconceptions. Midwives conduct outreach programs to educate the public that fertility is a shared biological responsibility involving both male and female reproductive health.

Promoting open conversations about pelvic infections, post-abortion complications, and sexually transmitted diseases helps prevent avoidable secondary infertility. By normalizing discussions around reproductive health, midwives dismantle harmful stigmas, encourage early health-seeking behavior, and foster supportive community environments for affected couples.
