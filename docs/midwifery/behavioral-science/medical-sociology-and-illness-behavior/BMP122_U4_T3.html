Impact of Hospitalization on Patients and Families

Introduction to the Hospital Environment and Illness Behavior

When a pregnant woman or a new mother leaves her familiar home environment to enter a hospital, she experiences a sudden shift in her social role. Illness behavior refers to how an individual monitors their body, defines and interprets symptoms, takes remedial action, and uses the health care system. Entering a clinical facility means surrendering personal autonomy to institutional routines. Bright fluorescent lights, constant noise from monitors, strange medical equipment, and the presence of many unfamiliar healthcare workers can cause profound sensory overload.

For many patients in Nigerian healthcare facilities, this institutional setting induces immediate anxiety. The loss of personal clothing, rigid schedules for sleeping and eating, and separation from established support networks disrupt normal coping mechanisms. Midwives must recognize that how a patient reacts to admission is shaped by her cultural background, previous medical experiences, and community perceptions of institutional care. A hospital is not merely a healing space; it is a total institution that temporarily strips away the patient's familiar social identity.

Psychological and Social Impact on Patients

Hospitalization frequently triggers distinct psychological distress. Loss of control is a primary stressor; patients must wait for medications, meals, and permission to move about. This dependency fosters feelings of helplessness and vulnerability.

Socially, the patient is removed from her community, workplace, and family roles. In a maternity setting, a mother admitted for prolonged antenatal bed rest or postpartum complications may worry about her other children at home. This physical isolation can lead to clinical anxiety or depressive symptoms. Furthermore, the loss of privacy in shared ward bays exposes intimate bodily processes to strangers, causing embarrassment and psychological withdrawal. Midwives notice that some patients become overly compliant while others become irritable or uncooperative—both are defensive reactions to the stress of institutional confinement.

Socioeconomic and Emotional Impact on Families

The admission of a family member sends ripples through the entire household. In Nigeria, illness is rarely an individual affair; it is a family event involving extended networks.

  • Financial Strain: Out-of-pocket expenses for medications, diagnostics, and supplies can drain household savings rapidly.
  • Logistical Disruption: Family members must juggle work responsibilities to maintain a constant presence at the bedside, which is often expected in many public hospitals.
  • Emotional Burden: Relatives experience high anxiety regarding the patient's prognosis, compounded by the stress of navigating complex hospital bureaucracies.

When a nursing mother is hospitalized, the extended family must reorganize childcare and domestic duties, often causing significant domestic friction and economic hardship.

Role of the Midwife in Mitigating Hospitalization Stress

Midwives occupy a frontline position to humanize the hospital experience. Effective care goes beyond monitoring vital signs and administering oxytocin; it requires active psychological support.

Communication is the most powerful tool available. Introducing oneself, explaining every procedure before it is performed, and using simple, non-jargon language helps restore a sense of dignity and control to the patient. Midwives should encourage patients to voice their fears and cultural preferences. Simple acts—such as allowing a birth companion in the labor ward, facilitating early mother-infant contact, and orienting the newly admitted woman to the ward layout—drastically lower anxiety levels and improve clinical outcomes.

Promoting Family-Centered Care in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Family-centered care is an approach to healthcare planning, delivery, and evaluation that is grounded in mutually beneficial partnerships among healthcare providers, patients, and families. In the context of Nigerian maternity units, this model respects cultural traditions where relatives play an active role in supporting the new mother.

Implementing this model requires shifting hospital policies. Instead of treating family members as disruptive visitors, facilities should view them as essential members of the care team who assist with feeding, comfort measures, and emotional reassurance. Midwives can advocate for flexible visiting hours, accommodation for accompanying relatives, and inclusive health education sessions. Embracing this philosophy bridges the gap between clinical efficiency and compassionate, culturally safe care.
