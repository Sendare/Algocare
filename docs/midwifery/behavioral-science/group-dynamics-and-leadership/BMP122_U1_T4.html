Conflict Resolution and Team Building in Healthcare Settings

Understanding Group Dynamics in Midwifery Practice

Group dynamics refers to the attitudes, behaviors, and interactions that occur within a group of people working together. In a maternity unit, midwives, doctors, nurses, and attendants constantly form clinical teams. How these team members relate directly influences maternal and neonatal outcomes. When group dynamics are positive, members trust one another, share critical patient updates promptly, and support each other through demanding night shifts. Conversely, toxic dynamics lead to hesitation, poor information sharing, and dangerous delays in obstetric emergencies. Recognizing these underlying interpersonal currents helps midwives navigate daily ward pressures and build stronger professional bonds.

Characteristics of High-Performing Maternity Teams

High-performing maternity teams do not happen by accident. They share distinct traits that separate them from dysfunctional units.

  • Clear roles: Every worker knows their specific duties during a resuscitation or normal delivery, preventing confusion.
  • Psychological safety: Junior staff feel safe speaking up if they notice a dropping fetal heart rate without fear of being shouted down by senior colleagues.
  • Shared goals: The primary focus remains firmly on safe mother and baby outcomes rather than individual status.
  • Mutual accountability: Team members respectfully hold each other responsible for maintaining high clinical standards.

Common Sources of Conflict in Nigerian Health Facilities

Healthcare settings are high-pressure environments where friction is almost inevitable. In many Nigerian hospitals, resources are constrained, which breeds intense competition for basic supplies like gloves, oxytocin, or functional suction machines. Another major source of conflict stems from role ambiguity and hierarchy clashes between midwives, resident doctors, and nursing management. Shift handover periods often trigger tension when outgoing staff leave incomplete documentation or messy treatment charts. Additionally, sheer fatigue from chronic staff shortages and delayed salary payments lowers patience levels, turning minor disagreements into bitter verbal altercations.

Conflict Resolution Frameworks for Midwives

Resolving conflict constructively requires a structured approach rather than emotional reactions. Midwives often utilize recognized conflict handling styles depending on the situation.

  1. Collaboration: Working together to find a win-win solution that satisfies all parties, ideal for complex clinical protocol disagreements.
  2. Compromise: Both sides give up something to reach a middle ground, useful when time or resources are limited.
  3. Accommodation: One party yields to the other, which is helpful when preserving peace is more important than the specific point of argument.
  4. Avoidance: Temporarily withdrawing from the issue, though poor for long-term resolution if used constantly.
  5. Competition: Asserting one's view forcefully, appropriate only during emergency resuscitations where instant compliance is required.

Effective Communication Strategies

Miscommunication sits at the heart of most clinical errors in maternity care. Midwives must master active listening, which means paying full attention without planning a rebuttal while the other person speaks. Structured communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) provide a reliable framework for handing over patients or calling doctors during emergencies. SBAR cuts out unnecessary narrative and delivers crisp, professional facts. Furthermore, closed-loop communication—where the receiver repeats an instruction back to the sender—ensures that medication dosages or emergency steps are clearly understood and executed without error.

Fostering Interprofessional Collaboration

Midwifery does not exist in isolation. Safe childbirth requires seamless cooperation between midwives, obstetricians, pediatricians, laboratory scientists, and pharmacists. Interprofessional collaboration means moving away from traditional professional silos where doctors and nurses work in strict separation. Joint clinical audits, multidisciplinary ward rounds, and shared simulation training help break down these barriers. When healthcare professionals train together on simulation manikins for postpartum hemorrhage management, they learn to appreciate each other's professional strengths and limitations, ultimately creating a safer and more harmonious birth environment for Nigerian mothers.
