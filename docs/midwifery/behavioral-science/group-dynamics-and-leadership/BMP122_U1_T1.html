Concept of Groups, Types and Stages of Group Formation

Defining the Concept of Groups in Healthcare Settings

A group consists of two or more individuals who interact with one another, share common goals, and perceive themselves as a distinct social unit. In midwifery and healthcare, groups are not merely collections of people standing in the same room. They are dynamic systems where members influence each other's behaviors, attitudes, and clinical practices. Whether it is a ward shift handover team, an antenatal education class, or a multi-disciplinary emergency response unit, effective groups rely on shared communication and mutual dependence to deliver safe client care.

Primary and Secondary Groups in Midwifery Practice

Groups in healthcare settings generally fall into two main categories based on the depth of relationships:

  • Primary Groups: Characterized by small size, face-to-face interaction, and deep emotional ties. Examples include a close-knit community midwifery unit or an immediate family supporting a pregnant woman. Members share a strong sense of belonging.
  • Secondary Groups: Larger, more formal, and task-oriented. Relationships are often professional rather than personal. Examples include the National Association of Nigerian Nurses and Midwives (NANNM) or a hospital-wide maternal mortality committee. Interaction is structured around specific goals.

Formal and Informal Group Structures

Every clinical environment operates using a mix of formal and informal structures.

Formal groups are deliberately created by an organization to fulfill specific institutional objectives. A labor ward management committee or a quality improvement task force are classic examples, complete with assigned roles, designated leaders, and official mandates.

Informal groups emerge naturally from personal friendships, shared backgrounds, or common interests among staff. Midwives who consistently eat lunch together or junior staff who gather privately to discuss coping strategies form informal groups. These unofficial networks profoundly influence workplace morale, communication speed, and compliance with institutional changes.

The Five Stages of Group Development

Bruce Tuckman's model outlines the predictable stages groups pass through as they mature:

  1. Forming: Members get acquainted, test waters, and determine acceptable behaviors while remaining polite and cautious.
  2. Storming: Conflict emerges as members push against boundaries, disagree on methods, and challenge leadership or authority.
  3. Norming: Relationships settle, unity develops, and the group establishes shared rules, roles, and mutual trust.
  4. Performing: The group operates at peak efficiency, focusing directly on task completion and collaborative problem-solving.
  5. Adjourning: The group wraps up its work, evaluates outcomes, and disbands as members transition to new projects.

Navigating Conflict and Achieving High Performance

Moving a group from turbulent disagreement to high-performance synergy requires active facilitation. During the storming phase, suppressing dissent damages long-term trust; instead, leaders must encourage open, respectful airing of differences.

Once the group reaches the performing stage, synergy takes over. Synergy occurs when the collaborative output of the team far exceeds what individual members could achieve working alone. In a busy labor ward, a synergistic midwifery team anticipates complications, supports one another seamlessly during emergencies, and maintains high standards of maternal care without constant supervision.
