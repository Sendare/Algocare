Stigma, Discrimination and Sick Role Behavior Concepts

Understanding Social Change and Illness in Nigeria

Health and illness are never purely biological events. They happen within a cultural, economic, and social framework that is constantly shifting. In Nigeria, rapid urbanization, changing traditional beliefs, and the introduction of Western medicine create a unique landscape where people interpret and respond to sickness differently. Social change alters family structures, community support systems, and access to resources. When a pregnant woman or a sick individual seeks help, their experience is shaped by what their community believes about the cause of the illness. Midwives must recognize that societal attitudes directly influence health-seeking behaviors. A condition that carries traditional shame in one community might be openly discussed in another. Understanding these societal dynamics helps health workers see beyond the clinical symptoms to the person experiencing the illness within their community.

The Concept of Social Stigma in Healthcare

Stigma is a powerful social label that changes how a person is viewed by their community, reducing them from a whole person to a tainted, discounted one. In healthcare, conditions like HIV, mental health disorders, and infertility often carry heavy social baggage. Society frequently attaches moral judgment to these conditions, implying the sufferer brought the illness upon themselves through bad behavior or spiritual failure. This fear of judgment drives patients to hide their symptoms, delay seeking antenatal care, or patronize unqualified practitioners in secret. Stigmatized individuals internalize these negative views, leading to profound shame, anxiety, and social isolation. Midwives encounter stigma regularly, particularly when caring for young unmarried mothers or women living with sexually transmitted infections. Recognizing the roots of stigma is the first step toward breaking its grip on patient care.

Manifestations of Discrimination in Healthcare Settings

While stigma is an attitude or belief, discrimination is the unfair action that results from it. In Nigerian healthcare facilities, discrimination takes many subtle and overt forms. Midwives and other staff may unintentionally display biased behaviors.

  • Verbal disparagement: Using harsh words, scolding, or mocking patients because of their health status, age, or financial standing.
  • Substandard care: Providing delayed, rushed, or rough treatment to patients perceived as undesirable or troublesome.
  • Breach of confidentiality: Gossiping about a patient's diagnosis, such as an HIV-positive status, to other staff members or community members.
  • Outright refusal of service: Denying care or demanding unreasonable informal fees before attending to an emergency.

Such actions violate the fundamental human right to health and destroy trust between the community and the health system.

Parsons' Sick Role Theory and Patient Behaviors

Sociologist Talcott Parsons introduced the concept of the "sick role" to explain how society expects sick people to behave. According to this theory, illness is a form of deviant behavior that disrupts social functioning, so society grants certain temporary rights and duties to the sick person.

  • Exemption from normal duties: The sick person is temporarily excused from regular work, school, and family responsibilities.
  • Non-responsibility for condition: The individual is not blamed for their sickness and cannot simply snap out of it by willpower.
  • Duty to want to get well: Sickness is viewed as undesirable, so the patient must want to recover.
  • Duty to seek technically competent help: The patient must cooperate with healthcare professionals like doctors and midwives.

In Nigerian maternity practice, women adopt variations of the sick role when pregnant or experiencing labor complications, expecting care, exemption from heavy farm or market work, and guidance from health workers.

Psychosocial Impacts on Midwifery Practice and Patient Outcomes

When stigma and discrimination permeate healthcare facilities, the consequences for maternal and child health are devastating. Pregnant women carrying stigmatized conditions experience severe anxiety, depression, and high levels of stress hormones that can negatively affect pregnancy outcomes, including preterm labor and low birth weight. Furthermore, toxic interactions with judgmental midwives create a climate of fear. Patients stop asking questions, hide vital medical history, and leave the hospital against medical advice. For the midwife, working in an environment tainted by discrimination leads to moral distress, compassion fatigue, and burnout. Midwives begin to view patients as chores rather than human beings in need of compassionate care. Restoring humanity to maternity care requires dismantling these psychological barriers and creating a welcoming, empathetic environment.

Strategies for Midwives to Mitigate Stigma and Promote Inclusive Care

Mitigating stigma in midwifery practice requires deliberate, active intervention at personal and institutional levels. Midwives must first examine their own internal biases and cultural stereotypes regarding gender, poverty, and specific illnesses. Cultivating active listening and empathy transforms the clinical encounter from a top-down lecture into a healing dialogue. Facilities must enforce strict confidentiality policies, ensuring patient records are kept safe from prying eyes and loose lips. Community engagement is equally vital; midwives should collaborate with local leaders and traditional birth attendants to dispel myths and encourage open health discussions. Respectful maternity care training must be integrated into every nursing and midwifery curriculum, ensuring that the next generation of practitioners views every woman with dignity, regardless of her background or diagnosis.
