Memory Processes, Retention and Forgetting Factors

Introduction to Memory in Midwifery Practice

Memory is the foundation of safe clinical decision-making. When a midwife assesses a laboring woman, recalls the warning signs of postpartum hemorrhage, or remembers the correct dosage for oxytocin, they are relying on complex cognitive systems. In the fast-paced maternity wards across Nigeria, from bustling urban teaching hospitals to quiet primary health centres, mental readiness saves lives. Midwives must constantly absorb new clinical guidelines, patient histories, and emergency protocols. Understanding how the human brain processes and retains this vital information helps prevent medical errors and improves maternal and neonatal outcomes. Learning is not just about passing nursing and midwifery council examinations; it is about building a reliable internal library of skills that can be accessed instantly during a crisis.

Stages of Memory: Encoding, Storage, and Retrieval

Memory operates as a continuous three-step process: encoding, storage, and retrieval. If any one of these stages fails, the memory cannot be successfully used.

  • Encoding: The initial perception and translation of incoming sensory data into a neural code that the brain can accept. When a clinical instructor describes a breech delivery, your brain records the sights and sounds.
  • Storage: The physiological maintenance of encoded information over time. The brain consolidates these neural traces, strengthening connections so they persist.
  • Retrieval: The conscious or unconscious act of locating and pulling stored information back into active awareness when needed on the labour ward.

Structural Stores: Sensory, Short-Term, and Long-Term Memory

Psychologists divide human memory into three distinct structural stores based on capacity and duration.

Memory StoreDurationCapacityFunction
Sensory MemoryFraction of a secondVery largeHolds raw environmental inputs briefly
Short-Term Memory15 to 30 secondsLimited (about 7 items)Holds active thoughts and working data
Long-Term MemoryUnlimited timeVast, limitlessStores permanent knowledge and life experiences

Short-term memory acts as a temporary workbench, sometimes called working memory. Without deliberate rehearsal, information in short-term memory quickly fades or gets pushed out by new incoming data.

Theories of Forgetting

Forgetting is the inability to retrieve previously stored information. Several scientific theories explain why this happens.

  • Trace Decay Theory: Memory traces in the brain simply fade away naturally over time if they are not reactivated or used.
  • Interference Theory: Other memories get in the way. Proactive interference happens when old learning blocks new memories. Retroactive interference happens when new learning wipes out old memories.
  • Retrieval Failure: The information is safely stored in long-term memory, but the proper cue or trigger is missing to help find it.

Enhancing Memory Retention in Midwifery Practice and Patient Education

Midwives spend a large portion of their day educating pregnant women on nutrition, warning signs of complications, and postpartum self-care. Patients often forget much of what is told to them during stressful antenatal visits. To improve retention, midwives should use clear, simple language without heavy medical jargon. Breaking instructions down into small, digestible chunks prevents cognitive overload. Teaching back—asking the mother to repeat the instructions in her own words—confirms true understanding and strengthens her memory trace. For student midwives studying complex subjects, techniques like spaced repetition, flashcards, and connecting abstract concepts to real clinical cases yield much better results than last-minute cramming before exams.
