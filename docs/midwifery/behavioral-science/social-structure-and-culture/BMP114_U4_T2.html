Influence of Culture on Health, Illness and Health-Seeking Behaviors

Introduction to Culture and Health in Nigeria

Nigeria is a culturally diverse nation with over two hundred and fifty ethnic groups, each possessing unique traditions, worldviews, and social structures. These cultural frameworks dictate how communities interpret bodily changes, assign meanings to suffering, and decide when and where to seek relief. Midwives practicing within this environment must recognize that health is never viewed purely through a biological lens. Instead, health is deeply intertwined with social harmony, spiritual balance, and ancestral relationships. When a pregnant woman walks into a clinic in Kano, Enugu, or Ibadan, she carries the collective beliefs of her family and community regarding what is safe, what is dangerous, and who is qualified to heal. Ignoring these cultural foundations often leads to poor communication, mistrust, and patient attrition from formal health facilities.

Cultural Definitions of Health and Disease

Across many Nigerian communities, health is perceived as a state of complete physical, social, and spiritual equilibrium. Disease is rarely understood as a mere malfunction of organs or the result of a pathogen. Instead, illness may be interpreted as a punishment for moral transgression, the result of spiritual attack, or an imbalance caused by breaking cultural taboos. For instance, severe jaundice in a newborn might be attributed to hot foods eaten by the mother during pregnancy rather than physiological breakdown of red blood cells. Similarly, mental health issues are frequently blamed on supernatural interference rather than neurochemical imbalances. These definitions dictate the urgency and type of intervention sought, often leading families to consult spiritualists or traditional bonesetters before visiting a primary health center.

Traditional Healing Practices and Practitioners

Traditional healthcare systems are deeply rooted in Nigerian society and remain accessible, affordable, and culturally resonant. Practitioners include traditional birth attendants (TBAs), herbalists, bone-setters, and spiritualists.

  • Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs): Highly respected community women who manage labor and delivery using local herbs, massage, and spiritual prayers.
  • Herbalists (Onisegun/Dibia): Specialists who use roots, barks, and leaves to treat various ailments, ranging from malaria to infertility.
  • Bone-setters: Practitioners who manipulate fractures using splints and local concoctions, enjoying high patronage despite the risk of compartment syndrome.
  • Spiritualists/Faith Healers: Leaders of syncretic churches, mosques, or shrines who use prayers, holy water, and rituals to drive away malevolent spirits.

While some traditional practices offer valuable social support, others can lead to severe complications, such as prolonged obstructed labor managed by TBAs without referral capabilities.

Factors Shaping Health-Seeking Behaviors

A person's journey toward seeking healthcare is influenced by an interplay of economic, social, geographical, and cultural determinants. In many Nigerian communities, financial constraints force families to prioritize daily survival over preventive health checks. When illness strikes, the first step is usually self-medication using patent medicine vendors (chemists) because they offer credit, speak the local language, and demand no consultation fees. Gender dynamics also play a massive role; married women in patriarchal settings may need permission and financial backing from their husbands before visiting a health facility. Geographic barriers, such as deplorable roads and lack of functional transport during rainy seasons, further push rural dwellers to rely entirely on nearby traditional options.

Cultural Beliefs Affecting Maternal and Child Health

Cultural practices surrounding pregnancy, childbirth, and infancy carry both protective and harmful elements. Dietary taboos are widespread; pregnant women in certain regions are forbidden from eating eggs, snails, or meat out of fear that the child will develop abnormal traits or become a thief. Paradoxically, these foods are essential sources of protein and micronutrients, contributing directly to maternal anemia and low birth weight. Postpartum practices often involve confinement indoors and the application of hot herbal baths or steaming to the perineum. While intended to promote healing, some thermal practices cause severe skin burns or exacerbate dehydration. Additionally, colostrum—the first yellowish milk—is sometimes discarded as 'dirty' milk, depriving the newborn of vital early antibodies.

Cultural Competence in Nursing and Midwifery Care

Cultural competence requires midwives to acknowledge their own cultural biases, respect patient diversity, and adapt clinical care to fit the patient's cultural framework. A culturally competent midwife does not ridicule a patient who arrives at the clinic wearing waist beads for spiritual protection or bearing tribal scarifications. Instead, the midwife engages in respectful dialogue, asking open-ended questions to understand the patient's perspective without judgment. Effective communication bridges the gap between scientific obstetrics and traditional beliefs. When midwives listen with empathy, patients feel valued and are more likely to disclose their use of herbal concoctions, allowing the clinician to screen for potential drug-herb interactions.

Bridging Traditional Practices and Modern Healthcare

Achieving sustainable improvements in maternal and child health in Nigeria requires collaboration rather than confrontation between modern health systems and traditional healers. Traditional birth attendants and herbalists remain influential because they are trusted community fixtures. Progressive public health initiatives now focus on training TBAs to recognize danger signs like severe bleeding, prolonged labor, and high fever, ensuring they refer patients promptly to formal hospitals rather than attempting risky home deliveries. Integrating traditional healers into referral networks helps reduce delays in seeking care, builds mutual respect, and ultimately saves the lives of mothers and babies across diverse Nigerian communities.
