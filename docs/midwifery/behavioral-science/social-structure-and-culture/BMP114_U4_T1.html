Concept of Culture, Norms, Values and Beliefs

Introduction to Social Structure and Culture in Healthcare

Social structure refers to the organized pattern of social relationships and institutions that together compose society. In healthcare, this structure dictates how patients and providers interact, who holds decision-making power within a household, and how resources are distributed. Culture acts as the invisible glue holding these social frameworks together, shaping how individuals perceive wellness and illness.

When a pregnant woman walks into a primary health centre in Nigeria, she does not arrive as a biological unit alone. She carries the expectations of her family, the traditions of her ethnic group, and the economic reality of her community. Midwives must understand that social structure and culture directly influence health-seeking behaviors. Recognizing these patterns prevents healthcare workers from viewing patient choices as mere stubbornness, reframing them instead as logical responses to a lived social environment.

Definition and Characteristics of Culture

Culture is a complex whole that includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society. It is not something a person is born with biologically; rather, it is learned through socialization from infancy.

  • Learned: Culture is acquired through observation, interaction, and instruction within a community, not inherited genetically.
  • Shared: Members of the same cultural group hold common understandings, symbols, and practices that bond them together.
  • Dynamic: Culture is never static; it constantly evolves, adapts to new environments, and absorbs outside influences over time.
  • Symbolic: Language, gestures, and objects carry special, agreed-upon meanings unique to a specific cultural group.

A common misconception is that culture only belongs to rural or traditional populations. In reality, every human being operates within multiple cultures, including professional cultures like midwifery itself.

Social Norms and Cultural Values

Values are deeply held criteria, standards, or guiding principles that a cultural group considers good, right, desirable, and important. They provide the moral compass for a community. Norms are the specific rules and expectations that guide behavior in accordance with these values.

Norms manifest in different ways:

  • Folkways: Everyday customs and manners of polite behavior, such as how younger people greet elders in many Nigerian communities.
  • Mores: Strict moral rules that carry heavy ethical significance, where violation threatens the well-being of the group.
  • Taboos: Behaviors so strongly forbidden that their occurrence causes deep revulsion and severe social sanction.

Midwives encounter norms daily, particularly regarding modesty, childbirth positions, and dietary restrictions during the puerperium. Respecting these boundaries while safeguarding clinical safety requires a delicate balance of cultural intelligence.

Belief Systems and Their Role in Society

Belief systems comprise the convictions and ideas that people hold to be true, regardless of whether they can be scientifically proven. These systems give meaning to existence, explain the origins of suffering, and offer frameworks for coping with life crises such as birth, illness, and death.

In many Nigerian societies, belief systems blend traditional spiritual worldviews, religious doctrines, and empirical observations. When a woman believes that a difficult labor is the result of spiritual interference or broken taboos, her distress is entirely real to her. Addressing only her physical contractions with oxytocin while ignoring her psychological and spiritual terror leaves her holistic care incomplete. Belief systems dictate hope, fear, and the search for healing remedies outside formal hospital walls.

Implications of Culture and Beliefs on Midwifery Practice in Nigeria

Midwifery in Nigeria operates within a rich tapestry of diverse ethnic groups, each with distinct cultural practices surrounding pregnancy and childbirth. Effective maternity care requires bridging the gap between professional biomedical knowledge and local cultural realities.

Midwives frequently navigate complex clinical scenarios shaped by culture:

  • Dietary Restrictions: Pregnant women may be forbidden from eating eggs or snails due to local fears about child development or character traits.
  • Placenta Disposal: Families often demand custody of the placenta for burial rituals, reflecting deep spiritual connections to the land and the newborn.
  • Birth Attendants: Preference for traditional birth attendants (TBAs) is often driven by cultural familiarity, trust, and the desire for emotional companionship during labor.

Forcing compliance through harsh reprimands alienates patients and drives them away from formal care facilities. Midwives who practice cultural safety listen without judgment, negotiate safe compromises, and build trusting bridges that save mothers' and babies' lives.
