Piaget

Introduction to Jean Piaget and Constructivism

Jean Piaget was a Swiss psychologist whose work fundamentally changed how we understand children's thinking. Before his research, many adults viewed children simply as miniature adults who lacked knowledge. Piaget argued instead that children actively construct their own understanding of the world through their everyday experiences and interactions with their environment.

This core concept is known as constructivism. Rather than just passively absorbing facts taught by parents or teachers, a child builds mental frameworks, or schemata, to make sense of what happens around them. In clinical midwifery and pediatric practice across Nigeria, recognizing constructivism helps healthcare providers understand why a toddler or young child interprets medical procedures, pain, or hospital environments completely differently from an adult.

Core Mechanisms: Assimilation and Accommodation

Piaget explained that cognitive growth happens through a continuous effort to achieve equilibrium, a state of mental balance between what we experience and what we understand. When children encounter new information, they use two main processes to adapt: assimilation and accommodation.

  • Assimilation: Fitting new information into an existing schema. For example, a young child who only knows dogs sees a goat for the first time and calls it a dog because it has four legs and fur.
  • Accommodation: Changing an existing schema or creating a completely new one because the old information does not fit. When someone explains that goats have horns and bleat differently from dogs, the child adjusts their understanding and forms a new schema for goats.

The Four Stages of Cognitive Development

Piaget divided cognitive development into four distinct, sequential stages. Every child moves through these stages in the exact same order, though the exact age at which they transition can vary slightly based on individual differences and cultural environments.

  1. Sensorimotor Stage (Birth to 2 years): Learning through sensory experiences and physical actions.
  2. Preoperational Stage (2 to 7 years): Development of language, symbolic thinking, and egocentric viewpoints.
  3. Concrete Operational Stage (7 to 11 years): Logical thinking about physical, concrete objects and events.
  4. Formal Operational Stage (11 years through adulthood): Abstract reasoning, systematic problem-solving, and hypothetical thinking.

Sensorimotor and Preoperational Stages in Early Childhood

The first two stages lay the groundwork for basic survival, communication, and social interaction.

During the sensorimotor stage, infants master object permanence—the understanding that objects and people still exist even when they cannot be seen, heard, or touched. A baby who lacks object permanence will not search for a toy hidden under a blanket.

As children enter the preoperational stage, they engage in make-believe play and use language rapidly. However, their thinking is heavily marked by egocentrism, meaning they struggle to see things from another person's perspective. A four-year-old standing right in front of the television blocking everyone's view genuinely believes everyone else can see the screen just fine because they can see it.

Concrete and Formal Operational Stages in Older Children

As children grow older, their cognitive abilities mature to handle complex logical operations.

In the concrete operational stage, children grasp the concept of conservation—knowing that changing the shape or appearance of a substance does not change its basic amount or mass. If you pour water from a wide short glass into a tall thin glass, a concrete operational child knows the amount of water stays the same, whereas a younger preoperational child will argue the taller glass has more water simply because it looks higher.

During the final formal operational stage, adolescents develop the capacity for abstract thought. They can ponder moral dilemmas, think about the future, and test scientific hypotheses systematically without needing physical props in front of them.

Clinical Relevance for Midwifery and Child Health

Understanding Jean Piaget's stages helps midwives and pediatric nurses communicate effectively with mothers, children, and families in clinics and community health centers. When counseling a mother about her toddler's behavior, a midwife can explain that tantrums and egocentric demands are a normal part of preoperational development rather than willful disobedience.

Furthermore, when preparing children for medical procedures or immunizations, healthcare providers should tailor their explanations to the child's developmental stage. A school-age child in the concrete operational stage benefits from seeing and touching clean medical equipment like a syringe before an injection, whereas a toddler needs simple, reassuring language and immediate comfort rather than complex anatomical explanations they cannot yet process.
