Freud

Introduction to Sigmund Freud and Psychoanalytic Theory

Sigmund Freud revolutionized how humanity understands the human mind. Working in Vienna during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, he developed psychoanalysis, both a method of treating mental disorders and a theory describing human personality. Freud argued that much of our mental life occurs outside our immediate awareness, buried deep within the unconscious mind.

His clinical work with patients suffering from conversion hysteria—physical symptoms without an obvious organic cause—led him to believe that repressed childhood experiences significantly shape adult behavior. By listening closely to his patients' free associations and dreams, Freud concluded that hidden psychological conflicts drive many everyday actions. While modern psychology has moved past some of his original proposals, his core premise that early life experiences and unconscious drives influence adult personality remains foundational to psychiatric and psychological thought.

Structure of the Mind: The Id, Ego, and Superego

Freud proposed that personality is made up of three interacting structures: the id, the ego, and the superego. These three agencies constantly pull the individual in different directions.

  • The Id: Present from birth, the id operates on the pleasure principle. It demands immediate satisfaction of all basic urges, needs, and desires without any regard for rules, morality, or reality.
  • The Ego: Developing soon after, the ego operates on the reality principle. It acts as a realistic mediator, trying to satisfy the id's demands in ways that are safe and socially acceptable in the real world.
  • The Superego: Developing last through internalizing parental and societal values, the superego acts as our moral conscience. It creates feelings of guilt when we do wrong and strives for perfection.

When these three forces clash, psychological tension occurs.

The Psychosexual Stages of Development

Freud believed that personality develops through a series of predetermined stages during childhood, centered on different erogenous zones of the body. He argued that how parents manage these developmental milestones shapes adult character.

  1. Oral Stage (0-1 year): Focus is on the mouth. Pleasure comes from sucking, biting, and breastfeeding. Fixation here can lead to thumb-sucking, smoking, or nail-biting in adults.
  2. Anal Stage (1-3 years): Focus is on bowel and bladder control during toilet training. Success brings a sense of competence; harsh training can lead to an anal-retentive or anal-expulsive personality.
  3. Phallic Stage (3-6 years): Focus shifts to the genitals. Children experience the Oedipus or Electra complex, eventually resolving this by identifying with the same-sex parent.
  4. Latency Stage (6 years to puberty): Sexual feelings are dormant. Energy is channeled into schoolwork, hobbies, and developing social friendships with peers.
  5. Genital Stage (Puberty onwards): Sexual interests mature, and the individual seeks out satisfying, adult romantic relationships.

Coping with Anxiety Through Defense Mechanisms

When the ego cannot successfully balance the demands of the id, the superego, and harsh reality, it experiences anxiety. To protect the conscious mind from this overwhelming stress, the ego unconsciously deploys defense mechanisms.

These strategies distort reality to make emotional pain bearable. For example, repression pushes painful memories out of awareness, while denial refuses to acknowledge an external threat altogether. In displacement, a person redirects unacceptable impulses from a dangerous target onto a safe one, such as an angry mother shouting at a table instead of her boss. Rationalization invents logical, acceptable excuses for unacceptable behaviors. While these mechanisms offer temporary emotional relief, overusing them prevents individuals from confronting and resolving their actual underlying problems.

Relevance of Freudian Concepts in Midwifery and Maternal Mental Health

Midwives often wonder how a historical theory about childhood drives applies to caring for pregnant and postpartum women. Understanding psychoanalytic principles helps midwives appreciate the deep emotional vulnerability mothers experience during the perinatal period.

Pregnancy and childbirth can trigger unresolved childhood conflicts, reactivating early attachment issues with the woman's own mother. When a new mother exhibits intense anxiety, sudden mood swings, or regression—such as becoming extremely dependent on hospital staff—she may be using unconscious defense mechanisms to cope with the immense stress of parenthood. Furthermore, recognizing the power of the unconscious helps midwives validate maternal mental health concerns that lack a simple physical explanation. By offering empathetic, non-judgmental listening, midwives create a safe space for women to process fears that might otherwise manifest as physical tension or postnatal distress.
