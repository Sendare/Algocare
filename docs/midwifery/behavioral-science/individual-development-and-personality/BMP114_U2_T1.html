Factors Influencing Personality Development: Heredity and Environment

Introduction to Personality Development

Personality development refers to the long-term process in which an individual's unique patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving emerge and change over time. It is not something fixed at birth; rather, it is a dynamic journey shaped by both biological roots and daily life experiences. Midwives and nurses often observe how differently newborns react to their environment—some are calm and easy to soothe, while others startle easily and cry intensely. These early behavioral styles, often called temperament, form the foundation upon which adult personality is built.

Genetic and Biological Influences (Nature)

Genetics provides the biological blueprint for human development. Through DNA inherited from parents, individuals receive predispositions that influence physical traits, neurological wiring, and baseline emotional reactivity. Twin and adoption studies have consistently shown that genes account for a significant portion of the variance in major personality traits, such as extraversion, neuroticism, and openness to experience.

  • Chromosomal Foundation: The baseline genetic code that directs basic physical and physiological development.
  • Neurotransmitter Systems: Brain chemicals like serotonin and dopamine that modulate mood, energy, and stress response.
  • Constitutional Factors: Physical health, energy levels, and innate biological rhythms that affect how a person interacts with the world.

Environmental and Sociocultural Influences (Nurture)

While genes set potential boundaries, the environment shapes how those potentials are expressed. From the moment of birth, cultural norms, family dynamics, peer groups, and socioeconomic conditions mold a person's outlook on life. In Nigerian communities, communal living and extended family structures heavily influence early socialization, emphasizing respect, communal responsibility, and strong social bonds.

  • Family Environment: Parenting styles, sibling relationships, and home stability during formative years.
  • Culture and Tradition: Shared values, religious beliefs, and societal expectations that dictate acceptable behavior.
  • Peer and School Influences: Friendships and educational settings that build social confidence and personal identity.

The Epigenetic Interaction of Nature and Nurture

Modern science views personality not as a battle between nature versus nurture, but as a continuous collaboration between them. Epigenetics demonstrates how environmental factors—such as chronic stress, nutrition, or trauma—can actually alter how genes are expressed without changing the underlying DNA sequence. A child may inherit a genetic vulnerability to anxiety, but growing up in a stable, nurturing environment can prevent that trait from ever becoming dominant. Conversely, severe early adversity can trigger dormant genetic vulnerabilities.

Implications for Midwifery and Patient Care

Understanding personality factors helps midwives and nurses deliver holistic, compassionate care. Patients come from diverse cultural backgrounds with varying emotional responses to pain, illness, and hospitalization. A parturient woman who appears unusually withdrawn or anxious may be reflecting her cultural norms, past trauma, or innate temperament rather than uncooperativeness. Recognizing these influences allows healthcare providers to individualize communication, build trust, and reduce anxiety during clinical encounters.
