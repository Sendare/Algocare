Human Motivation, Needs and Maslow Hierarchy

Understanding the Concept of Motivation in Healthcare

Motivation is the internal drive or external stimulus that moves a person to take action. In healthcare settings, understanding motivation helps midwives decipher why a pregnant woman might consistently skip her antenatal visits or why a nursing mother might struggle with exclusive breastfeeding.

Motivation generally falls into two distinct categories:

  • Intrinsic motivation: Driven by internal rewards, personal satisfaction, or a desire for personal health. A mother who takes her iron supplements diligently because she wants her baby to be strong and healthy is intrinsically motivated.
  • Extrinsic motivation: Driven by external rewards, praise, or the avoidance of negative consequences. A patient who attends a clinic solely to receive a free mosquito net or to avoid being scolded by the community health extension worker demonstrates extrinsic motivation.

Recognizing these drives allows healthcare providers to tailor health education effectively, moving beyond mere instruction to genuine engagement.

Exploring the Spectrum of Human Needs

Human needs form the foundation upon which all motivation is built. Every individual requires certain physical and psychological elements to survive, grow, and maintain well-being. When these basic requirements are unmet, a person experiences a state of deficit, which naturally triggers a motivational drive to correct the imbalance.

In clinical practice, a midwife observes this spectrum daily. A woman in labor requires physiological stability, pain management, and hydration. However, she also requires reassurance, dignity, and a sense of safety from the maternity staff. If her physiological needs are met but she feels terrified and unsupported, her overall well-being remains compromised. Human needs are therefore interconnected, spanning the physical, emotional, and social dimensions of life.

Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs Framework Explained

Abraham Maslow proposed that human actions are directed toward the goal of meeting various needs, arranged in a hierarchical pyramid. Traditionally depicted with five levels, the model suggests that lower-level, fundamental needs must be at least partially satisfied before an individual can focus on higher-level psychological and self-fulfillment needs.

LevelCategoryClinical Example in Midwifery
BasePhysiologicalAir, water, food, warmth, and rest for the postpartum mother
SecondSafety and SecurityProtection from physical harm, financial stability, and shelter
ThirdLove and BelongingAcceptance by family, community, and support groups
FourthEsteemSelf-respect, status, recognition, and personal confidence
ApexSelf-ActualizationAchieving one's full potential and personal growth

A common misconception is that a lower level must be 100 percent satisfied before someone cares about the next level. In reality, human beings pursue multiple needs simultaneously, though urgency usually dictates that survival needs take precedence during acute crises.

Application of Maslow's Hierarchy to Nursing and Midwifery Care

Applying Maslow's framework in midwifery allows clinicians to prioritize care efficiently during busy shifts. When a client arrives in the clinic presenting with multiple complaints, the midwife addresses immediate life threats first.

For instance, if a postpartum woman is hemorrhaging, her physiological need for circulation and oxygen takes immediate precedence over teaching her how to bath her newborn. Once the hemorrhage is controlled and her vital signs stabilize, the midwife shifts attention upward to safety, comfort, emotional reassurance, and teaching infant care. Neglecting basic physical survival to focus on health education is ineffective because a terrified, bleeding, or exhausted patient cannot process complex information.

Assessing Patient Motivation and Priorities in Clinical Practice

Effective midwifery care requires active listening and structured assessment to uncover what a client values most. A care plan imposed by a midwife without regard for the client's personal priorities often fails once the patient returns home.

During booking clinics or postnatal visits, midwives should ask open-ended questions about what concerns the mother most. A teenager attending antenatal care for the first time may be paralyzed by fear of social rejection (belonging and esteem needs) rather than worries about normal physiological changes. By identifying where the patient stands on the motivational spectrum, the midwife can offer targeted support, build trust, and foster compliance with recommended health practices.
