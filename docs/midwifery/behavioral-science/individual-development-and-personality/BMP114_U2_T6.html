Emotions, Stress, Frustration and Coping Mechanisms

Understanding Emotions, Stress, and Frustration in Midwifery

Midwifery practice is emotionally demanding. Midwives witness intense human experiences, from the joy of childbirth to the tragedy of sudden obstetric emergencies. Emotions are brief, conscious feelings accompanied by physical and behavioral changes that help us react to our environment. Stress arises when the demands placed upon a midwife exceed their perceived ability to cope. Meanwhile, frustration occurs when a goal is blocked or delayed, such as when lack of equipment or hospital delays prevent timely interventions. Recognizing these psychological states in yourself and your colleagues is the first step toward maintaining professional well-being and providing safe, compassionate care to mothers and newborns.

Physiological and Psychological Aspects of Emotion

When a midwife faces a crisis in the labor ward, the body responds instantly. The autonomic nervous system triggers the sympathetic pathway, releasing adrenaline and cortisol. This causes physical changes: the heart rate speeds up, breathing becomes rapid, and blood flow is diverted to large muscles. Psychologically, emotions involve subjective cognitive labeling—how your mind interprets the physical arousal. For instance, a racing heart during an emergency might be labeled as fear or intense excitement, depending on the context. Understanding this mind-body connection helps midwives manage panic during high-stakes deliveries.

The General Adaptation Syndrome and Stressors

Hans Selye described how the human body responds to long-term stress using the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS), which unfolds in three distinct stages:

  1. Alarm reaction: The immediate fight-or-flight response where shock and counter-shock mobilize body resources.
  2. Stage of resistance: The body tries to adapt and cope with the ongoing stressor, maintaining high physiological arousal.
  3. Stage of exhaustion: If the stress continues without relief, the body's energy stores are depleted, leading to burnout, illness, or chronic fatigue.

In Nigerian healthcare settings, chronic stressors often include staff shortages, long shifts, and inadequate infrastructural support, pushing many health workers toward the exhaustion phase.

Sources of Frustration and Behavioral Reactions

Frustration can stem from internal sources, such as a midwife's unrealistic personal expectations of perfection, or external sources, such as bureaucratic delays, lack of medications, or uncooperative relatives. When midwives experience prolonged frustration, their behavioral reactions vary widely:

  • Aggression: Direct or displaced verbal outbursts toward colleagues or patients.
  • Withdrawal: Emotional or physical detachment, becoming silent and disengaged.
  • Fixation: Repeatedly trying the same failed method without adapting.
  • Regression: Displaying childish or overly dependent behaviors under pressure.

Healthy and Unhealthy Coping Mechanisms

Coping mechanisms are the cognitive and behavioral strategies people use to manage stress and frustration. Healthy coping mechanisms (constructive strategies) include physical exercise, talking through problems with a trusted mentor, deep-breathing exercises, and time management. In contrast, unhealthy coping mechanisms (defense mechanisms or maladaptive habits) offer temporary relief but cause long-term harm. These include denial, excessive alcohol use, projection of blame onto junior staff, and emotional eating. Recognizing maladaptive habits early allows midwives to replace them with supportive, life-giving routines.

Implications for Patient Care and Nurse Burnout Prevention

Unmanaged stress and emotional exhaustion directly compromise patient safety. A burned-out midwife is more prone to clinical errors, reduced empathy, and poor communication with women in labor. Preventing burnout requires both individual resilience and institutional changes. Midwives must practice regular self-care, set firm professional boundaries, and seek peer support. At the same time, healthcare institutions must foster supportive work environments, ensure adequate staffing levels, and provide access to mental health resources. Protecting the mental health of midwives ensures better health outcomes for mothers and babies alike.
