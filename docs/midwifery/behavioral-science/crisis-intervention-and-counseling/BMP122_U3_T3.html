Basic Counseling Skills in Nursing Practice

Introduction to Counseling in Healthcare

Counseling is a fundamental part of holistic nursing and midwifery care. While medical treatment addresses the physical body, counseling helps patients process the emotional and psychological impact of illness, diagnosis, or life changes. In busy Nigerian healthcare settings, midwives often provide brief, informal counseling during antenatal visits, post-natal care, or labor ward admissions.

Effective counseling does not mean giving advice or telling a patient what to do. Instead, it creates a safe space for patients to explore their own feelings and find workable solutions to their health challenges.

Core Basic Counseling Skills

Several foundational skills enable healthcare providers to guide patients through difficult moments. Mastering these techniques transforms routine clinical interactions into therapeutic encounters.

  • Active Listening: Fully concentrating on what the patient says rather than just waiting for your turn to speak.
  • Empathy: Demonstrating a genuine understanding of the patient's emotional experience from their perspective.
  • Open-ended Questioning: Asking questions that require more than a simple 'yes' or 'no' answer to encourage deeper sharing.
  • Reflection: Repeating or rephrasing the patient's words to confirm understanding and show validation.

Establishing Trust and Rapport

Patients rarely share their deepest fears or true health concerns with a clinician they do not trust. Rapport is the harmonious connection built between the nurse or midwife and the patient during early interactions.

Creating this bond requires simple actions: greeting the patient warmly, using their preferred name, maintaining an approachable posture, and respecting cultural norms. Confidentiality is the bedrock of trust. When a patient believes their private information stays within the professional team, they feel secure enough to disclose critical health details that might otherwise remain hidden.

Overcoming Communication Barriers

Many obstacles can disrupt clear communication in a clinical setting. Recognizing these barriers is the first step toward correcting them.

Barrier TypeDescriptionExample
LanguageDifference in spoken language between clinician and patientSpeaking English to a patient who only understands Hausa or Igbo
EnvironmentalPhysical distractions in the clinic spaceA noisy, crowded antenatal waiting room
EmotionalHigh anxiety or fear clouding perceptionA woman terrified of surgical delivery
CulturalMisaligned beliefs about health and diseaseAttributing postpartum hemorrhage solely to spiritual attacks

Overcoming these barriers requires patience, utilizing local language interpreters when necessary, and finding quiet spaces for private conversations.

Ethical Considerations and Professional Boundaries

Counseling creates a close interpersonal bond, which makes maintaining clear professional boundaries essential. Nurses and midwives must remember that counseling is professional, not social.

Dual relationships—such as trying to counsel a close personal friend or relative—can impair professional objectivity. Additionally, midwives must guard against imposing their personal religious, moral, or cultural values onto patients making difficult decisions about their reproductive health. Respecting patient autonomy means supporting informed choices, even when those choices differ from the clinician's personal beliefs.
