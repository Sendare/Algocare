Bereavement Counseling and Psychological Support

Understanding Grief and Loss in the Nigerian Midwifery Context

Perinatal loss carries a heavy emotional and social weight in Nigeria. When a woman loses a pregnancy, a baby, or experiences a stillbirth, the grief is rarely confined to the parents alone; it ripples through the extended family and community. Midwives must recognize that grief here is deeply shaped by cultural expectations around childbearing, family lineage, and spiritual beliefs. Unlike in some Western settings where pregnancy loss might be spoken of in private, Nigerian families often face immediate social scrutiny, pressure to explain the cause, or well-meaning but harmful spiritual interpretations.

  • Stillbirth and Neonatal Death: The sudden transition from expectation to mourning leaves parents in intense shock, compounded by physical postpartum changes like lactation without a baby.
  • Spontaneous Miscarriage: Often minimized by society as "just a missed period," yet it can trigger profound personal grief and a deep sense of bodily failure for the mother.
  • Infertility-related Loss: The loss of a pregnancy after years of trying or expensive fertility treatments carries an extra layer of financial and emotional devastation.

Communication Strategies for Delivering Difficult News

Breaking bad news about a fetal abnormality, intrauterine fetal death, or neonatal demise is one of the hardest duties a midwife faces. How you deliver this information sets the foundation for how the mother and family will process their grief. Avoid blunt, rushed statements or medical jargon that leaves parents confused.

  1. Prepare the environment: Find a quiet, private room away from the busy labor ward noise. Sit down rather than standing over the patient to show presence and empathy.
  2. Use clear language: Drop euphemisms like "we lost the baby" which can confuse young children or shock parents later. Say gently that the baby's heart has stopped beating.
  3. Allow silence: After speaking the news, stop talking. Parents need time for the words to sink in before they can ask questions or express emotion.
  4. Check for understanding: Gently ask the parents to repeat back what you told them to ensure shock has not blocked their comprehension.

Providing Immediate Psychological Support to Grieving Parents

Immediate post-loss care requires a delicate balance of clinical safety and compassionate presence. Physical care must continue—monitoring lochia, blood pressure, and uterine involution—but it must be done with acute sensitivity to the emotional storm the mother is experiencing.

Encourage parents to see, hold, and touch their baby if they wish to do so, while respecting their boundaries if they refuse. For many mothers, seeing the baby helps make the reality concrete, transforming an abstract tragedy into a tangible person they can say goodbye to. Wrap the baby cleanly, support the head, and present the infant with dignity. Validate all emotional expressions—whether she is crying loudly, screaming, or sitting in total silence. Do not use platitudes like "it is God's will" or "you can always have another child," as these dismiss the unique identity of the baby they have just lost.

Navigating Cultural and Religious Beliefs Surrounding Perinatal Loss

Nigeria's rich tapestry of cultural and religious traditions greatly influences how families interpret and handle death. In many Islamic communities, burial happens quickly, sometimes within hours, which leaves very little time for prolonged hospital mourning or keepsake creation. In Christian settings, memorial prayers or naming ceremonies might be adapted to acknowledge the child's brief existence.

Midwives must remain neutral, respectful, and open-minded. Some families attribute losses to spiritual attacks, generational curses, or reincarnation beliefs (such as the *ogbanje* phenomenon). Never mock or argue against these beliefs. Instead, provide a safe space where spiritual rituals can be accommodated safely within the hospital guidelines, such as allowing a pastor or Imam to visit the bedside or letting family members perform permitted last rites.

Recognizing Complicated Grief and When to Refer

While grief is a normal, healthy response to loss, it can sometimes freeze or intensify into complicated or prolonged grief disorder. Normal grief fluctuates over time, allowing moments of acceptance or neutral functioning interspersed with sadness. Complicated grief, however, traps the individual in unrelenting despair, severe functional impairment, or denial lasting beyond six months post-loss.

  • Warning Signs: Expressing persistent wishes to join the baby in death, complete inability to care for surviving children or oneself, total social withdrawal, or persistent hallucinations of the baby.
  • Referral Pathway: Midwives are frontline care providers, not psychiatric specialists. When red flags appear, coordinate a referral to a clinical psychologist, psychiatric nurse, or medical social worker attached to the facility.

Self-Care and Coping Strategies for the Midwife

Midwives working in busy Nigerian maternity units witness loss with alarming frequency. Absorbing the trauma of multiple patients without adequate decompression leads directly to compassion fatigue, burnout, and secondary traumatic stress.

Developing personal and professional coping mechanisms is a matter of professional survival. Debriefing sessions with trusted colleagues after a traumatic shift help process unexpressed emotions. Maintaining clear professional boundaries prevents taking total emotional ownership of a patient's tragedy while still delivering warm, human care. Rest, adequate nutrition, spiritual grounding, and knowing when to step back from high-stress assignments safeguard your own mental health.
