Qualitative

Understanding the Qualitative Paradigm in Midwifery Research

Qualitative research focuses on understanding human experience, beliefs, behaviors, and interactions from the perspective of the people living them. While quantitative research counts things using numbers and statistics, qualitative research explores the 'why' and 'how' behind human actions. In Nigerian midwifery practice, this approach helps us understand complex clinical realities that numbers cannot fully capture. For instance, statistical data might show a high rate of low birth weight babies in a community, but a qualitative study uncovers the cultural beliefs around maternal diet during pregnancy that actually drive those numbers.

The qualitative paradigm assumes that reality is socially constructed and subjective. Different women experience pregnancy, labor, and postnatal care differently based on their culture, socioeconomic status, and personal history. Rather than trying to control all variables like a laboratory experiment, qualitative researchers immerse themselves in the natural settings of their participants—such as antenatal clinics, traditional birth homes, or rural communities—to gather rich, descriptive data.

Core Characteristics of Qualitative Studies

Qualitative studies share several defining features that set them apart from experimental or survey research:

  • Naturalistic inquiry: Research happens where participants are, rather than in a sterile laboratory or via rigid questionnaire forms.
  • Inductive analysis: Instead of starting with a rigid hypothesis to test, researchers build concepts and themes from the bottom up, starting with the detailed data collected.
  • The researcher as the key instrument: Data is mediated through human interaction, meaning the interviewer's listening skills, cultural competence, and self-awareness directly shape the depth of findings.
  • Emergent design: The study design can adapt and evolve as fieldwork progresses and new insights come to light.

Common Qualitative Data Collection Methods

To capture rich human experiences, qualitative researchers use flexible data collection methods that allow participants to speak in their own words.

  1. In-depth interviews: One-on-one conversations guided by open-ended questions. They allow participants to share sensitive personal stories, such as coping with a stillbirth or navigating postpartum depression.
  2. Focus group discussions: Group conversations led by a facilitator. They work well for exploring shared community norms, such as traditional postpartum confinement practices, because participants stimulate thoughts and discussions in each other.
  3. Participant observation: The researcher spends time in the setting—such as a busy labor ward—watching daily routines, non-verbal communication, and interactions between patients and midwives.
  4. Document analysis: Reviewing existing written materials, such as hospital policy guidelines, birth registers, or personal diary entries, to understand historical or institutional contexts.

Approaches to Data Analysis: Narrative and Thematic Analysis

Once hours of recorded interviews and field notes are collected, they must be transcribed into text and analyzed. Two of the most common approaches in midwifery research are thematic analysis and narrative analysis.

Thematic analysis involves reading through textual data repeatedly to find repeating patterns, codes, and broader themes. For example, after interviewing women about their labor experiences, a researcher might code various statements and group them into major themes like 'fear of disrespect,' 'need for a companion,' and 'trust in traditional herbs.'

Narrative analysis focuses instead on whole stories. Rather than breaking text down into fragments and codes, the researcher looks at how an individual structures their personal story of illness or childbirth from beginning to end, paying attention to plot, turning points, and emotional arcs.

Ensuring Rigor and Trustworthiness in Qualitative Findings

Because qualitative research does not use statistical tests of validity and reliability, researchers use alternative criteria to establish the trustworthiness of their studies. Lincoln and Guba's framework outlines four key pillars:

  • Credibility: Confidence in the truth of the findings. Midwives achieve this through prolonged engagement in the field, peer debriefing, and member checking—taking findings back to participants to confirm if their words were interpreted correctly.
  • Transferability: Showing that findings can apply to other settings. While qualitative studies are context-specific, rich and thick descriptions allow readers in other clinics or hospitals to judge whether the findings fit their own environment.
  • Dependability: Ensuring the research process is logical, traceable, and well-documented so another researcher can follow the audit trail.
  • Confirmability: Ensuring the findings are shaped by the experiences and ideas of the informants rather than the personal biases or theoretical preferences of the researcher.
