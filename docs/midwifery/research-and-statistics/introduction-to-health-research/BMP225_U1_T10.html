Identifying Research Problems and Formulating Hypotheses and Research Questions

Introduction to Health Research in Midwifery

Midwifery research forms the bedrock of safe, evidence-based clinical practice across Nigeria. When midwives investigate everyday challenges on the labour ward or in community health centres, they generate local solutions tailored to Nigerian mothers and newborns. Research bridges the gap between traditional practices and modern scientific evidence, directly improving maternal mortality indices.

  • Clinical Improvement: Finding better ways to manage postpartum haemorrhage using active management of the third stage of labour.
  • Policy Development: Providing local data that influences state and federal health policies regarding free maternal care.
  • Professional Growth: Elevating midwifery from a purely task-oriented occupation to an autonomous, evidence-informed profession.

Identifying Research Problems in Clinical Practice

A research problem is an unanswered question or a troublesome condition in clinical practice that requires a systematic investigation. In busy Nigerian health facilities, student midwives often notice clinical gaps during their postings. These gaps might involve delayed referrals for obstructed labour, poor uptake of routine immunisation, or high rates of postpartum sepsis.

Good research problems usually stem from three main sources:

  1. Clinical Observations: Noticing that mothers using a specific birth position experience fewer perineal tears.
  2. Professional Literature: Reading about a new guideline on cord clamping and wondering if it works in a rural setting.
  3. Theoretical Puzzles: Questioning why health education methods fail to change cultural beliefs about exclusive breastfeeding.

Refining Broad Issues into Feasible Research Topics

Novice researchers often pick topics that are far too broad, such as "Causes of maternal mortality in Nigeria." While important, this topic is too vast for a single undergraduate project. Refining a topic means narrowing it down by population, location, time, and specific variables.

To test if a research topic is feasible, apply the FINER criteria:

  • Feasible: Do you have enough time, money, and access to patients?
  • Interesting: Does the topic genuinely spark your curiosity?
  • Novel: Does it confirm, refute, or add new findings to existing knowledge?
  • Ethical: Can it be studied without harming human participants?
  • Relevant: Will the findings actually help clinical practice or health policy?

Developing Clear and Focused Research Questions

Once a topic is narrowed down, it must be translated into a precise research question. A well-crafted question guides the entire study design and tells the reader exactly what the researcher intends to discover. Poorly phrased questions lead to ambiguous data collection and uninterpretable results.

Using the FINER and SPIDER or PICO frameworks helps structure these questions. For quantitative studies, a PICO format is common:

  • Population: e.g., Primigravid mothers in antenatal clinics
  • Intervention: e.g., Birth preparedness education
  • Comparison: e.g., Standard routine health talk
  • Outcome: e.g., Knowledge of danger signs in pregnancy

Formulating Testable Hypotheses

A hypothesis is a tentative, educated prediction about the relationship between two or more variables. It translates the research question into a statement that can be tested statistically. Not all studies require hypotheses—exploratory or qualitative studies often use research questions instead.

When hypotheses are used, they typically come in two forms:

  • Null Hypothesis ($H_0$): States that there is no significant relationship or difference between variables (e.g., "There is no difference in birth weight between babies born to mothers who attended antenatal care early and those who did not").
  • Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$ or $Ha$): States that a relationship or difference does exist (e.g., "Babies born to mothers who attended antenatal care early have higher birth weights").

Linking Questions, Hypotheses, and Study Objectives

Alignment is critical in research design. The title, research problem, objectives, research questions, and hypotheses must tell a consistent, logical story. If your objective is to determine the effect of a drug, your research question must ask about that effect, and your hypothesis must predict the direction of that effect.
