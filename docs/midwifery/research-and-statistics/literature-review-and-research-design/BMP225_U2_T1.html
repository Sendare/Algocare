Sources of Literature and Search Strategies for Health Research

Why Literature Reviews Matter in Midwifery and Health Research

A literature review is not merely a summary of past writings; it is the critical foundation of any sound research project in midwifery. When a midwife embarks on a study about postpartum hemorrhage or traditional birth attendant practices, reviewing existing literature prevents the duplication of effort and highlights gaps in current maternal healthcare delivery. It situates the new study within a broader scientific conversation, showing readers what is already known and what remains unanswered. Furthermore, a thorough review helps refine the research methodology by revealing which data collection tools or sampling techniques succeeded or failed in similar West African contexts. Grounding clinical inquiries in existing evidence ultimately protects patients by ensuring that healthcare practices evolve from validated scientific knowledge rather than mere tradition or guesswork.

Primary versus Secondary Literature Sources

Health literature is broadly divided into primary and secondary sources, each serving a distinct function in evidence-based practice.

  • Primary Sources: These contain original research data reported by the people who actually conducted the study. Examples include randomized controlled trials published in peer-reviewed journals, direct observational studies on labor progression, and clinical case reports.
  • Secondary Sources: These analyze, synthesize, or interpret primary research data. Examples include systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and textbook chapters summarizing decades of obstetric care guidelines.

Beginners sometimes confuse secondary sources with primary data because both appear in academic journals. Always check if the authors collected original measurements or merely summarized other people's findings.

Grey Literature and Local Nigerian Health Contexts

Relying solely on international commercial databases often blinds researchers to vital local data. Grey literature consists of reports, policy guidelines, conference abstracts, and institutional documents produced by government bodies, non-governmental organizations, and local university departments outside of traditional commercial publishing.

In Nigeria, crucial data regarding maternal mortality audits, community health interventions, and traditional birth attendant utilization often reside in unpublished ministry documents or institutional archives rather than high-impact international journals. Locating these documents requires manual searches of institutional websites, hospital libraries, and direct contact with public health agencies. Ignoring grey literature introduces severe publication bias, as international journals rarely publish negative local trial results or local quality improvement reports that hold immense value for Nigerian midwifery practice.

Formulating Clinical Research Questions Using PICO

A well-structured research question is the compass of any literature search. Without it, searching databases yields an overwhelming avalanche of irrelevant articles. The PICO framework breaks clinical inquiries down into four manageable components:

  1. P (Population/Patient): The specific group of people you are studying, such as primigravid teenagers or women with gestational diabetes.
  2. I (Intervention): The specific treatment, diagnostic test, or midwifery practice being introduced, such as active management of the third stage of labor.
  3. C (Comparison): The alternative practice, placebo, or standard care against which the intervention is measured, such as physiological third-stage management.
  4. O (Outcome): The measurable clinical result you care about, such as the volume of postpartum blood loss or maternal hemoglobin levels.

Translating a vague clinical curiosity into a PICO format immediately clarifies which keywords to type into search engines.

Search Strategies and Academic Databases

Accessing the right databases determines whether a literature search succeeds. Major biomedical repositories index thousands of peer-reviewed journals, each with unique indexing rules.

Effective searching requires moving beyond single keywords to use controlled vocabulary, such as Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) in PubMed. Using controlled terms ensures that articles written with different regional spellings or synonyms are still captured under one standardized subject heading.

Boolean Operators and Advanced Search Techniques

Mastering Boolean operators transforms a clumsy internet search into a precise scientific query. These three connecting words dictate how search engines combine or exclude keywords:

  • AND: Narrows your search by telling the database that every retrieved article must contain all connected terms. Example: *maternal mortality AND Nigeria* retrieves only articles mentioning both concepts.
  • OR: Broadens your search by finding articles containing any of the connected synonyms. Example: *labor OR childbirth* ensures you capture papers using either word.
  • NOT: Excludes specific terms from your search results. Use this with caution so you do not accidentally throw away relevant papers. Example: *cesarean NOT emergency* removes elective or emergency section studies.

Combining these with truncation (using symbols like an asterisk * to catch word variations like *midwife*, *midwives*, or *midwifery*) builds highly efficient search strings.

Appraising and Managing Retrieved Literature

Gathering hundreds of journal articles creates an organizational challenge if not handled systematically. Reference management software such as Zotero, Mendeley, or EndNote helps store citations and automatically format bibliographies in required styles like APA or Vancouver.

However, having many references is useless if their quality is poor. Critical appraisal involves evaluating retrieved papers for methodological validity, sample size adequacy, and potential bias before citing them as evidence. Tools like the CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme) checklists provide structured questions to determine if a study's conclusions genuinely justify its data. Students must learn to separate robust scientific findings from poorly designed studies with flawed conclusions.
