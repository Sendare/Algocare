Writing a Literature Review and Conceptual/Theoretical Framework

Purpose of the Literature Review in Midwifery Research

A literature review is a comprehensive summary of previous research related to your specific midwifery topic. When investigating maternal mortality reduction or postnatal care practices in Nigerian primary health centres, you must first discover what has already been studied. This process prevents researchers from unintentionally duplicating existing work. It establishes the theoretical roots of your study, demonstrates your familiarity with current clinical guidelines, and highlights gaps in existing maternal health knowledge that your own research aims to fill.

Synthesis of Literature

Writing a strong review requires more than stringing together isolated summaries of individual articles. Synthesis means bringing multiple sources together to show how they agree, disagree, or build upon one another. Instead of writing separate paragraphs about ten different authors, group your findings by themes or debates. For instance, if you are studying traditional birth attendant integration, group articles discussing referral delays together, separate from those discussing community trust.

Structuring and Organizing the Review

A well-organized literature review guides the reader smoothly from broad concepts down to your specific research question.

  • Introduction: Define the core concepts and outline the scope of the review.
  • Thematic Body: Group studies by methodology, chronology, or clinical themes to build a logical argument.
  • Conclusion: Summarise the main insights and explicitly state the research gap your study addresses.

Theoretical Frameworks

A theoretical framework provides an established lens or blueprint borrowed from a recognized academic theory to explain why things happen. In midwifery, you might use theories from nursing, sociology, or psychology—such as Orem's Self-Care Deficit Theory—to explain patient behaviors during antenatal care. This framework sits in the background of your study, guiding how you interpret your findings and connect them to broader scientific principles.

Conceptual Frameworks

While a theoretical framework uses broad, established theories, a conceptual framework is built specifically for your current study. It maps out the exact variables you are investigating and how they relate to one another. You often present this as a diagram showing independent variables (like maternal age or parity) pointing toward dependent variables (like exclusive breastfeeding rates), connected by mediating or confounding factors.

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them

Many student researchers make predictable mistakes when compiling their literature reviews. A common error is writing an annotated bibliography—summarizing one paper after another without showing connections. Another trap is relying too heavily on secondary sources or outdated textbooks instead of current peer-reviewed journal articles. Ensure your search strategy uses recent publications from reputable maternal health databases.
