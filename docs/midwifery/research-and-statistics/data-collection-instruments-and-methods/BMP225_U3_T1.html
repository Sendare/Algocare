Design and Validation of Questionnaires and Checklists

Introduction to Midwifery Research Tools

Data collection instruments are the specific tools a midwife researcher uses to gather information from pregnant women, nursing mothers, or colleagues. In clinical research and maternal health audits in Nigeria, the quality of your findings depends entirely on the tools you use. If your questions are unclear or biased, the data collected will be useless, no matter how advanced your data analysis is.

Two primary instruments dominate clinical and community midwifery studies: questionnaires and checklists. Questionnaires are best for capturing opinions, knowledge, attitudes, and reported behaviours from respondents. Checklists, on the other hand, are observational tools used to systematically record physical actions, environmental conditions, or clinical procedures as they happen. Selecting the correct tool early saves time and ensures your research objectives match your data collection method.

Questionnaire Design Principles

Designing a strong questionnaire requires clear, simple language that respondents can easily understand without needing constant clarification. Avoid medical jargon when interviewing community women; use terms they use daily. Every question must tie directly back to your research objectives.

  • Closed-ended questions: Provide fixed options like 'Yes', 'No', or specific categories, making analysis fast and straightforward.
  • Open-ended questions: Allow respondents to write or speak freely in their own words, capturing rich qualitative details.
  • Leading questions: Must be strictly avoided because they unfairly push the respondent toward a specific answer, introducing bias.

Keep your instrument short. Tired respondents give rushed answers, which destroys data reliability.

Checklist Construction

Building an effective observational checklist means breaking a complex clinical procedure into clear, sequential steps. When observing active management of the third stage of labour, for instance, your checklist must list every critical action in the exact chronological order it should occur.

Items on a checklist should be mutually exclusive and exhaustive of the expected behaviours. Avoid vague descriptors like 'good technique' or 'patient handled well'. Instead, use objective, observable markers like 'administered oxytocin within one minute of delivery'. Clear criteria ensure that two different midwife observers watching the same labour ward scenario will record the exact same score.

Validation Procedures

Before launching your data collection in Nigerian hospitals or communities, your tool must be validated to prove it actually measures what it claims to measure.

  1. Face validity: Involves giving the draft tool to colleagues or peers to look at quickly and confirm that it looks sensible and relevant at first glance.
  2. Content validity: Requires expert judges, such as senior obstetricians and experienced midwifery educators, to review every single item to ensure the tool covers all important aspects of the topic.
  3. Construct validity: Assumes statistical testing to prove that the tool accurately measures theoretical concepts like 'maternal satisfaction' or 'pain perception'.

Skipping validation means you risk publishing findings based on a faulty instrument.

Pilot Testing and Refining Data Tools

A pilot test is a small-scale rehearsal of your research study conducted before the main project begins. You administer your questionnaire or checklist to a small group of people who share similar characteristics with your target population, but who will not take part in the final study.

Pilot testing helps you uncover hidden flaws: ambiguous questions that confuse respondents, items that take too long to answer, or logistical gaps in how the data is recorded. Once the pilot test is finished, analyze the feedback, rewrite confusing sentences, drop dead-weight questions, and finalize your instrument for full field deployment.
