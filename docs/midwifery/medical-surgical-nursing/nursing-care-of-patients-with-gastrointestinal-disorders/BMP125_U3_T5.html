Peptic Ulcer Disease

Understanding Peptic Ulcer Disease and Local Risk Factors

Peptic Ulcer Disease, commonly called PUD, refers to open sores that develop on the inner lining of the stomach, known as a gastric ulcer, or the upper part of the small intestine, known as a duodenal ulcer. In clinical practice across Nigeria, midwives frequently encounter patients presenting with chronic epigastric pain attributed to this condition. While global medical literature heavily highlights bacterial infection and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs as primary drivers, local realities heavily compound the disease burden.

Economic pressures, widespread self-medication with over-the-counter analgesics, and dietary habits rich in highly spiced foods all worsen mucosal injury. Many patients cope with early abdominal discomfort by taking potent analgesics from local chemist shops without a prescription, inadvertently accelerating gastric mucosal damage. Recognizing these local socioeconomic and cultural factors helps midwives provide contextually relevant care and education.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Gastric and Duodenal Mucosa

The stomach and duodenum are naturally designed to withstand a harsh chemical environment because they produce powerful protective mechanisms. Parietal cells secrete hydrochloric acid to break down food, while chief cells release pepsinogen, an enzyme activated by acid into pepsin for protein digestion. To prevent this acidic juice from digesting the stomach itself, specialized surface cells secrete a thick, alkaline mucus layer laced with bicarbonate.

A delicate balance exists between aggressive factors like acid and pepsin, and defensive factors like mucus, bicarbonate, and rich local blood flow. When mucosal blood flow drops or protective factors fail, acid breaches the barrier, causing cellular destruction and ulcer formation.

Major Causes: Helicobacter pylori and NSAIDs

Two major culprits account for the vast majority of all peptic ulcer cases seen globally and locally:

  • Helicobacter pylori Infection: A spiral-shaped bacterium that burrows into the protective mucus layer of the stomach. It produces an enzyme called urease, which neutralizes surrounding acid, allowing the bacteria to survive and trigger chronic inflammation.
  • NSAID Use: Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs like ibuprofen, diclofenac, and aspirin block an enzyme called cyclooxygenase (COX). Blocking the COX-1 pathway stops the production of prostaglandins, which are vital chemicals that maintain the stomach's protective mucous lining.

Pathophysiology and Mucosal Breakdown

Ulcer formation is fundamentally a mismatch between aggressive forces and mucosal defense. When *H. pylori* infects the mucosa, bacterial toxins and the body's immune response cause localized tissue inflammation, known as gastritis. The bacteria weaken the tight junctions between epithelial cells, making the tissue vulnerable to acid penetration.

Similarly, when NSAIDs suppress mucosal prostaglandins, local blood flow diminishes. Deprived of oxygen and nutrients, the superficial epithelial cells die off rapidly. Once the mucosal barrier is breached, hydrochloric acid and pepsin rush into the exposed submucosa, digesting local tissue, causing bleeding, and producing a crater-like wound known as a peptic ulcer.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Workup

Patients with peptic ulcer disease typically present with a burning, gnawing, or aching pain located in the epigastric region. A helpful clinical distinction exists between the two main types:

  • Duodenal Ulcer Pain: Often worsens 2 to 3 hours after a meal when the stomach is empty, and is classically relieved by eating food or taking antacids.
  • Gastric Ulcer Pain: Frequently worsens almost immediately during or right after eating, as food stimulates acid secretion against the damaged gastric mucosa.

Associated symptoms include bloating, excessive belching, nausea, and vomiting. To confirm diagnosis, clinicians rely heavily on upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, which allows direct visualization of the ulcer crater and tissue biopsy for *H. pylori*. Non-invasive tests include the urea breath test and stool antigen tests.

Medical Management and Pharmacology

Treatment goals for peptic ulcer disease focus on relieving pain, healing the crater, and preventing recurrence by targeting the underlying cause.

  • Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Drugs like omeprazole or esomeprazole block the final hydrogen-potassium pump in parietal cells, effectively shutting down acid secretion.
  • H2 Receptor Blockers: Medications such as cimetidine or famotidine reduce acid production by blocking histamine receptors.
  • Eradication Therapy: If *H. pylori* is detected, patients receive triple therapy, combining a PPI with two different antibiotics (such as amoxicillin and clarithromycin) for 7 to 14 days.
  • Antacids and Mucosal Protectants: Agents like sucralfate coat the ulcer base to shield it from acid, while simple antacids neutralize existing gastric acid for immediate relief.

Recognizing and Managing Acute Complications

Untreated or severe peptic ulcer disease can lead to life-threatening emergencies that require prompt surgical and medical intervention:

  • Hemorrhage: Occurs when an eroding ulcer damages a nearby blood vessel. Patients may vomit bright red blood or dark digested blood, known as "coffee-ground" emesis, and pass black, tarry stools called melena.
  • Perforation: Happens when the ulcer burns completely through the wall of the stomach or duodenum, spilling gastric contents into the sterile peritoneal cavity. This causes sudden, agonizing, knife-like abdominal pain with a rigid, board-like abdomen.
  • Gastric Outlet Obstruction: Caused by chronic inflammation and scar tissue swelling around the pylorus, preventing the stomach from emptying its contents.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Midwives play a vital role in managing patients with peptic ulcer disease through holistic care and targeted health education. The nursing care plan prioritizes managing acute pain, monitoring for signs of internal bleeding, and assessing nutritional status.

Midwives must educate patients on completing their full course of prescribed antibiotics if *H. pylori* is present, rather than stopping when pain subsides. Patients should be strictly advised to avoid routine use of non-prescription anti-inflammatory drugs and to seek safe alternatives like paracetamol under medical supervision. Dietary counseling focuses on eating balanced, regular meals while avoiding known personal irritants such as excessive caffeine, alcohol, and very hot, spicy foods.
