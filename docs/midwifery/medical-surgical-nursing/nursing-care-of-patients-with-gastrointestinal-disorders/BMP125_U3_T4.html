Gastritis

Understanding Gastritis: Pathophysiology and Mucosal Barrier Breakdown

Gastritis is simply the inflammation of the gastric mucosa, the protective inner lining of the stomach. Under normal conditions, this lining produces a thick layer of mucus containing bicarbonate, which shields the stomach wall from its own powerful hydrochloric acid and digestive enzymes like pepsin. When this defensive mucosal barrier breaks down, gastric acid diffuses back into the tissue layers. This back-diffusion causes cellular injury, local vasodilation, edema, and bleeding, leading to the clinical picture of gastritis. The two main culprits behind this barrier breakdown are *Helicobacter pylori* infection and the regular use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). *H. pylori* bacteria burrow into the mucous layer and produce urease, an enzyme that neutralizes stomach acid around them while destroying mucosal cells. NSAIDs, frequently taken for aches and pains in Nigerian communities, block prostaglandins—substances that normally stimulate the production of the protective mucus layer. Once this defense fails, even normal amounts of stomach acid begin to eat away at the stomach tissue.

Acute versus Chronic Gastritis: Etiology and Risk Factors

Gastritis presents in two main forms based on how quickly it develops and how long it lasts: acute and chronic.

  • Acute Gastritis: Appears suddenly and is usually caused by dietary indiscretions, excessive alcohol intake, heavy smoking, or the acute stress of severe illness, burns, or major surgery. It is typically short-lived and heals completely once the offending trigger is removed.
  • Chronic Gastritis: Develops slowly over a long period. It is most commonly driven by a persistent *H. pylori* infection, but can also stem from autoimmune disorders where the body's immune cells mistakenly attack the parietal cells of the stomach.

In many Nigerian hospitals, patients often present with acute flare-ups triggered by prolonged fasting, heavy use of local herbal mixtures containing harsh ingredients, or continuous self-medication with strong analgesics for malaria-related joint pains.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

The clinical presentation of gastritis varies depending on whether the inflammation is sudden and sharp or long-standing. Many patients with chronic forms may actually have very few symptoms, while acute cases can cause sharp discomfort.

Common signs and symptoms include:

  • Epigastric pain or burning discomfort in the upper abdomen
  • Loss of appetite, medically known as anorexia
  • Nausea and persistent episodes of vomiting
  • A feeling of early fullness or bloating after eating small amounts
  • Belching or sour taste in the mouth

During assessment, the midwife must ask detailed questions about the patient's diet, medication history, alcohol use, and stress levels. Palpation of the abdomen may reveal mild tenderness in the epigastric region. If bleeding occurs due to mucosal erosion, the patient might vomit blood, known as hematemesis, or pass dark, tarry stools called melena, signaling upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Endoscopic Findings

While a thorough clinical history helps suspect gastritis, definitive diagnosis often requires specific investigations. The gold standard for diagnosing gastritis is an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy or EGD). During this procedure, a flexible lighted tube with a camera is passed through the mouth down into the stomach, allowing the doctor to visually inspect the mucosal lining. The lining often appears red, swollen, and sometimes shows erosions or small bleeding spots. During the endoscopy, small tissue samples or biopsies can be taken.

These biopsy samples are crucial because they can be tested directly for the presence of *H. pylori* using a rapid urease test or sent to the laboratory for microscopic examination. Other supportive tests include stool antigen tests, urea breath tests to check for bacterial presence without an endoscopy, and a complete blood count to check for anemia if chronic blood loss has occurred.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Medical treatment for gastritis focuses on removing the underlying cause, reducing gastric acidity, and protecting the damaged mucosal lining. When *H. pylori* is detected, doctors prescribe a combination regimen known as triple therapy, which usually includes a proton pump inhibitor and two different antibiotics taken for 10 to 14 days.

Several classes of medications are used to manage symptoms and promote healing:

  • Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Drugs like omeprazole that strongly block acid production.
  • H2-Receptor Antagonists: Medications like famotidine that reduce the volume of acid secreted.
  • Antacids: Over-the-counter preparations that quickly neutralize existing stomach acid for temporary relief.
  • Cytoprotective Agents: Drugs like sucralfate that form a protective coating over the ulcerated or inflamed mucosa.

Patients are also advised to avoid irritating substances such as alcohol, spicy foods, caffeine, and unnecessary NSAIDs during treatment.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Midwives play a vital role in caring for hospitalized patients with severe gastritis and educating those being discharged. The nursing care plan begins with assessing pain levels and administering prescribed anti-emetics and antacids promptly. During acute episodes, the patient may need temporary fasting to let the stomach rest, followed by a gradual introduction of bland, easily digestible foods like plain porridge, yam, or rice.

Patient education is essential for long-term recovery and preventing recurrence. Midwives should counsel patients to:

  • Eat small, frequent meals rather than large, heavy portions to reduce acid secretion.
  • Avoid foods and drinks that trigger symptoms, such as very spicy stews, citrus fruits, caffeine, and alcohol.
  • Stop smoking, as nicotine increases gastric acid production and delays mucosal healing.
  • Avoid self-medication with strong analgesics; if pain relief is necessary, consult a healthcare provider for safer alternatives or take medications with food.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

If left untreated or improperly managed, chronic or severe acute gastritis can lead to serious complications. The continuous erosion of the mucosal barrier can deepen into peptic ulcers, which may bleed profusely or even perforate the stomach wall, spilling gastric contents into the abdominal cavity and causing peritonitis. Another long-term complication of chronic atrophic gastritis is pernicious anemia, which occurs because damaged parietal cells can no longer produce intrinsic factor, a substance vital for absorbing vitamin B12 in the small intestine.

Midwives must remain vigilant for emergency warning signs that require immediate medical intervention. These include sudden, severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting of blood, or signs of hypovolemic shock such as a rapid, weak pulse, cold clammy skin, and dizziness, which indicate significant internal bleeding.
