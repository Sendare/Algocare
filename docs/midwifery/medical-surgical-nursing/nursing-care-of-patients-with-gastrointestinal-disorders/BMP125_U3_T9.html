Intestinal Obstruction

Introduction to Intestinal Obstruction

Intestinal obstruction occurs when there is a significant block that stops the normal movement of digested food, fluids, and gas through the small or large intestine. This condition can develop suddenly or progress slowly over time. In Nigerian clinical practice, midwives and nurses frequently encounter this emergency in surgical and gynecological wards, often arising from post-operative adhesions or complicated hernias. Recognizing the seriousness of a blocked bowel is vital because trapped contents lead to rapid fluid shifts, bacterial growth, and potentially life-threatening tissue death if intervention is delayed.

Pathophysiology and Dynamic Changes in the Bowel

When the intestine becomes blocked, fluid, gas, and swallowed air accumulate above the point of obstruction. The bowel wall initially tries to push past the block with strong, painful peristaltic waves, leading to hyperactive bowel sounds early on. As the pressure inside the lumen rises, blood flow to the intestinal wall decreases. Fluid leaks from the capillaries into the peritoneal cavity and the bowel lumen, causing severe hypovolemia and electrolyte imbalances. If the pressure is unrelieved, the bowel wall becomes ischemic, necrosis sets in, and perforation can release stool into the sterile abdominal cavity, triggering peritonitis.

Etiology: Mechanical Obstruction Versus Paralytic Ileus

Intestinal obstructions are broadly divided into two major categories based on their underlying cause.

  • Mechanical Obstruction: A physical barrier blocks the lumen. Common causes include adhesions from previous surgeries, strangulated hernias, volvulus (twisting of the bowel), and tumors.
  • Paralytic Ileus: Also called a functional obstruction, this occurs when peristalsis stops without any physical block. It frequently follows abdominal surgery, severe electrolyte disturbances like hypokalemia, or intra-abdominal infections.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Evaluation

Patients with an intestinal obstruction present with a recognizable pattern of symptoms. The classic cardinal signs include colicky abdominal pain, nausea, persistent vomiting, abdominal distention, and absolute constipation where neither stool nor gas is passed. Vomiting may become feculent if the obstruction is low in the gastrointestinal tract. Diagnostic confirmation relies on upright and supine abdominal X-rays, which typically show dilated loops of bowel with multiple fluid-level patterns. Electrolyte panels often reveal dehydration, hyponatremia, and hypokalemia due to fluid loss and vomiting.

Emergency Medical Management and Resuscitation

Immediate stabilization takes priority before any surgical correction. Healthcare teams initiate fluid resuscitation using isotonic intravenous solutions like normal saline or Ringer's lactate to correct extracellular volume depletion. A nasogastric (NG) tube is inserted and connected to low intermittent suction to decompress the stomach, relieve nausea, and minimize the risk of aspiration. Accurate intake and output monitoring, including insertion of an indwelling urinary catheter, ensures kidney perfusion is maintained. Analgesics and antiemetics are administered cautiously so they do not mask worsening surgical pain or signs of peritonitis.

Surgical Interventions and Post-Operative Care

While a paralytic ileus often resolves with rest, hydration, and medical management, a mechanical obstruction typically requires prompt surgery. The surgeon performs an exploratory laparotomy to release adhesions, reduce a volvulus, repair a hernia, or resect necrotic bowel tissue. If a section of the intestine is removed, an anastomosis or temporary stoma may be created. Post-operative care centers on pain control, early ambulation to prevent further adhesions, monitoring surgical wound healing, and watching for the return of normal bowel sounds and flatulence.

Nursing Care Plan and Complication Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care involves continuous vigilance for deteriorating clinical signs. Midwives and nurses track vital signs closely to catch early indicators of septic shock, such as tachycardia, tachypnea, and falling blood pressure. The abdomen is regularly inspected and palpated for increasing tenderness, guarding, or rigidity, which suggest perforation or peritonitis. Nurses manage fluid balance charts meticulously, keeping track of NG tube drainage, urine output, and intravenous infusions. Emotional support for the patient and their family is maintained throughout, explaining every procedure clearly to reduce anxiety.
