Nursing Care for Patients with Colostomy and Ileostomy

Understanding Colostomies and Ileostomies

An ostomy is a surgically created opening on the abdominal wall that allows waste to leave the body when a part of the bowel is diseased, injured, or removed. In gastrointestinal nursing, distinguishing between a colostomy and an ileostomy is fundamental for planning safe and effective patient care.

  • Colostomy: Created from a portion of the large intestine (colon). The output is typically formed or semi-formed because much of the water absorption has already occurred in the proximal bowel.
  • Ileostomy: Created from the terminal part of the small intestine (ileum). The output is continuous, watery, and contains high amounts of digestive enzymes that can easily irritate the skin.

Midwives and nurses must recognize that the anatomical placement dictates both the consistency of the stool and the specific management challenges the patient will face.

Preoperative Preparation and Psychological Support

Preparing a patient for stoma surgery involves both physical readiness and deep emotional reassurance. Fear of body image alteration is very common in Nigerian wards, where cultural perceptions around body wholeness can heighten anxiety.

  1. Stoma Site Marking: The enterostomal therapist or surgeon marks a flat spot on the abdomen, away from skin folds, scars, and the beltline, while the patient is sitting, standing, and bending.
  2. Psychological Counseling: Allowing the patient to express fears and introducing them to a recovered ostomate helps demobilize anxiety.
  3. Baseline Education: Showing a clean, unapplied pouch helps the patient visualize the device before surgery.

Immediate Postoperative Management and Stoma Assessment

The first forty-eight hours after surgery require rigorous monitoring of the newly created stoma. A healthy stoma should appear beefy red, moist, and slightly raised above the skin level.

Notifying the surgical team immediately if the stoma turns dark or dusky is critical, as this indicates compromised blood supply leading to tissue necrosis.

Routine Stoma Care and Pouching Techniques

Changing the pouching system is a routine procedure that protects the peristomal skin and maintains hygiene. The skin barrier opening must be measured accurately—usually no more than 1 to 2 millimeters larger than the stoma base—to prevent raw skin exposure.

  • Wash the peristomal skin gently using warm water and pat dry completely. Avoid oils or greasy soaps that prevent the skin barrier from sticking.
  • Apply a skin sealant or protective powder if the skin is raw or weeping.
  • Center the pouch opening carefully over the stoma and press the adhesive flange down firmly for 30 to 60 seconds to activate the heat-sensitive glue.

Fluid, Electrolyte, and Nutritional Considerations

Patients with ostomies face unique dietary and fluid challenges, especially those with high-output ileostomies who lose significant water and sodium.

  • Hydration: Patients must drink adequate fluids, but should avoid relying solely on plain water during high output episodes, as this can dilute blood electrolytes. Oral rehydration solutions containing salt and sugar are safer.
  • Dietary Progression: Foods are reintroduced slowly after surgery, beginning with low-residue items.
  • Gas and Odor Management: Foods like beans, onions, cabbage, and eggs produce gas or strong odors, while yogurt and buttermilk can help reduce natural odors.

Identifying and Managing Stomal Complications

Early recognition of complications prevents severe morbidity in ostomy patients. Nurses must watch for local skin problems and deeper structural issues.

  • Peristomal Skin Breakdown: Caused by leakage of effluent under the barrier. Managed by cleaning, drying, and using protective powders or sealants.
  • Stomal Prolapse: The bowel protrudes excessively outward through the stoma site. Often reduced by resting, but requires surgical review if blood flow is threatened.
  • Parastomal Hernia: A bulge around the stoma caused by muscle weakness allowing loops of bowel to push through the abdominal wall.
  • Stomal Retraction: The stoma sinks below the skin level, making pouch sealing extremely difficult and causing frequent leaks.

Patient Education and Long-Term Adaptation

Discharge planning focuses on empowering the patient and their family to manage stoma care independently before leaving the hospital. Teaching should be broken into small, manageable steps, starting with simply looking at the stoma and progressing to emptying and changing the pouch.

Patients also need practical guidance on clothing choices—avoiding tight belts directly over the stoma—and how to resume normal daily activities, social events, and work. Connecting patients with local ostomy support networks provides immense encouragement and practical advice for navigating daily life.
