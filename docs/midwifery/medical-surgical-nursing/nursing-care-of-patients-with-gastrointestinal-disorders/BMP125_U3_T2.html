Stomatitis

Understanding Stomatitis in Clinical Practice

Stomatitis is a broad term used to describe any inflammatory condition affecting the mucous membranes of the mouth, which may involve the cheeks, gums, tongue, lips, and floor of the mouth. In Nigerian midwifery and general nursing practice, you will frequently encounter this condition among vulnerable patient groups, including young children with viral infections, pregnant women experiencing severe nutritional deficiencies, and oncology patients undergoing chemotherapy or radiotherapy.

The oral mucosa serves as the first line of defense in the digestive tract. When this delicate lining becomes inflamed, routine activities like eating, drinking, swallowing, and even speaking cause intense discomfort. Midwives must recognize that oral lesions are not merely localized nuisances; they can rapidly escalate into systemic issues if pain prevents adequate fluid and nutritional intake, leading quickly to dehydration, especially in pediatrics.

Pathophysiology and Common Causes

The development of stomatitis typically begins with cellular injury to the oral epithelium. This damage triggers an acute inflammatory response, characterized by localized vasodilation, leukocyte infiltration, and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines that break down mucosal integrity, resulting in painful ulceration.

Causes are generally categorized into infectious and non-infectious origins:

  • Infectious agents: Viruses such as Herpes Simplex Virus, bacteria, and fungi like *Candida albicans* (oral thrush).
  • Trauma: Accidental biting of the cheek, ill-fitting dentures, sharp edges of decayed teeth, or thermal burns from hot pap or tea.
  • Systemic conditions: Vitamin deficiencies (especially B-complex and iron), immunosuppression, and autoimmune disorders.
  • Chemical agents: Harsh mouthwashes, tobacco use, and certain cytotoxic drugs used in cancer treatment.

Clinical Presentation: Aphthous vs. Herpetic Lesions

Differentiating between the two most common types of oral lesions—aphthous ulcers and herpetic stomatitis—is essential for guiding appropriate treatment. Midwives should observe the location, appearance, and systemic signs accompanying the lesions.

Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough clinical assessment begins with a detailed history regarding the onset of pain, dietary changes, recent medication use, and history of similar outbreaks. During the physical examination, use a penlight and tongue depressor to carefully inspect the lips, buccal mucosa, tongue, gingiva, and oropharynx. Note the size, depth, color, and exact location of any lesions, as well as the status of the surrounding tissue.

Laboratory investigations are usually reserved for recurrent, severe, or non-healing cases. A healthcare provider may order a swab for viral culture or PCR if herpes is suspected, or a fungal scraping (KOH prep) if candidiasis is likely. Blood tests, including complete blood counts and serum iron or vitamin levels, may be requested to rule out underlying systemic illnesses like anemia or nutritional deficiencies.

Pharmacological and Supportive Management

Management of stomatitis focuses on relieving pain, controlling underlying infection, and promoting rapid mucosal healing.

  1. Analgesks and Topical Agents: Viscous lidocaine is often prescribed as a mouth rinse to numb painful ulcers before meals, allowing the patient to eat. Protective barrier pastes can cover lesions to shield them from chemical irritation.
  2. Antimicrobial Therapy: Antifungal suspensions like nystatin treat oral candidiasis. Antiviral medications such as acyclovir shorten the duration of herpetic outbreaks if administered early.
  3. Anti-inflammatory Agents: Mild topical corticosteroids may be used for severe aphthous ulcers to reduce local inflammation.
  4. Hydration and Nutrition: Intravenous fluids may be necessary if oral intake is completely compromised due to pain.

Nursing Care Plan and Oral Hygiene Interventions

Implementing a structured nursing care plan for a patient with stomatitis requires balancing effective hygiene with extreme gentleness to avoid aggravating fragile tissue.

Encourage the patient to perform gentle mouth rinses every few hours using a mild saline solution or plain water, avoiding commercial mouthwashes containing alcohol, which burn and dry out the mucosa. Instruct patients to use an ultra-soft toothbrush or a foam swab to clean teeth gently. If bleeding or severe pain occurs, advise temporary cessation of mechanical brushing in favor of frequent medicated rinses. Maintain accurate fluid balance charts and monitor daily weight, intervening early if pain limits nutritional intake.

Patient Education on Prevention and Dietary Modifications

Empowering patients with practical knowledge prevents recurrent episodes and speeds up recovery from existing lesions. Dietary modification is the cornerstone of daily comfort during an active outbreak. Advise patients to avoid citrus fruits, spicy peppers, highly salted dishes, rough or crunchy foods like dry toast or chips, and very hot beverages that can irritate raw mucosal surfaces. Instead, recommend cool, bland, soft foods such as pap, custard, mashed potatoes, yoghurt, and lukewarm soups.

Teach patients to manage stress, maintain adequate hydration, and report chronic dental issues like sharp tooth edges to a dentist promptly. For individuals with nutritional deficiencies, emphasize the inclusion of locally available foods rich in B-vitamins and iron, such as beans, dark green leafy vegetables, and lean meats.
