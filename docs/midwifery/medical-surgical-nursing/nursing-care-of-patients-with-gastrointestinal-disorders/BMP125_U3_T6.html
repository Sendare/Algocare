Appendicitis

Anatomy and Pathophysiology of the Appendix

The vermiform appendix is a narrow, tube-like pouch attached to the cecum, which is the beginning of the large intestine. It sits in the lower right quadrant of the abdomen, near the ileocecal valve. Although it contains lymphatic tissue that helps immune function in young people, the body functions well without it.

Pathophysiology begins when the lumen of the appendix gets blocked. This blockage creates a closed-loop system where mucus continues to secrete, increasing pressure inside the organ.

  • Obstruction: Fecaliths, foreign bodies, or swollen lymphoid tissue block the opening.
  • Pressure build-up: Secretions accumulate, stretching the walls of the appendix and stimulating visceral nerve fibers.
  • Ischemia: High pressure cuts off blood flow to the walls, causing tissue death.
  • Bacterial invasion: Deprived of oxygen, the tissue breaks down, allowing bacteria to multiply rapidly and invade the surrounding layers.

Clinical Manifestations and Pain Progression

The classic presentation of appendicitis is a textbook sequence that helps midwives and nurses suspect the diagnosis before tests return. Pain usually starts as a vague, dull discomfort around the umbilicus. This happens because the appendix is supplied by the autonomic nervous system, which localizes pain poorly at first.

As the inflammation worsens and reaches the parietal peritoneum, the pain shifts to a sharp, steady sensation in the right lower quadrant at McBurney's point. Along with this migration of pain, patients often experience low-grade fever, nausea, vomiting, and a loss of appetite. Constipation or mild diarrhea can also occur, though anorexia is one of the most reliable early signs in pregnant and non-pregnant patients alike.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Nursing Assessment

Diagnosing appendicitis relies heavily on clinical assessment, supported by blood tests and imaging. During physical examination, midwives look for specific signs of peritoneal irritation. Rovsing's sign is positive if pressing on the left lower quadrant causes pain in the right lower quadrant. Rebound tenderness—pain that hurts more when pressure is released than when it is pressed—indicates inflammation has spread to the peritoneum.

Laboratory tests generally show an elevated white blood cell count with a shift to the left, indicating infection. Abdominal ultrasound or computed tomography scans are often used to visualize an enlarged, inflamed appendix, especially in ambiguous cases. A pregnancy test is mandatory for women of childbearing age to rule out ectopic pregnancy.

Preoperative Nursing Care and Preparation

Once appendicitis is diagnosed, preparation for emergency surgery begins swiftly. The primary goal of preoperative care is to prevent perforation and stabilize the patient for theater. Midwives and nurses must ensure the patient remains strictly nil per os (nothing by mouth) to prepare for general anesthesia and reduce bowel activity.

Pain management requires careful consideration. While patients need comfort, heavy analgesics should be used judiciously because they can mask worsening symptoms of a developing perforation. Intravenous fluid therapy is started immediately to correct fluid deficits from vomiting and poor oral intake. Baseline vital signs, consent forms, and pre-op medications like prophylactic antibiotics are completed according to hospital protocol.

Postoperative Care and Recovery

Following an appendectomy, nursing care shifts toward promoting recovery and preventing complications. Patients usually return from theater with intravenous fluids, an incision dressing, and sometimes a urinary catheter if surgery was complicated.

  • Vital signs monitoring: Check blood pressure, pulse, and temperature regularly to catch early signs of bleeding or infection.
  • Pain control: Administer prescribed analgesics so the patient can breathe deeply and move comfortably.
  • Early ambulation: Encourage short walks as soon as anesthesia wears off to prevent deep vein thrombosis and speed up bowel motility.
  • Dietary progression: Advance from ice chips to sips of water, and then to normal meals as bowel sounds return and the patient passes flatus.

Recognizing and Managing Complications Like Perforation

Delay in seeking care or rapid disease progression can lead to perforation, where the wall of the appendix bursts, spilling infected fecal matter and bacteria into the peritoneal cavity. This complication can cause generalized peritonitis, localized abscess formation, or life-threatening sepsis.

A sudden relief of pain followed by a sharp worsening of generalized abdominal pain often signals a rupture. On examination, the abdomen becomes rigid, board-like, and extremely tender, accompanied by high fever, tachycardia, and a rapid drop in blood pressure.

Management requires emergency surgery, aggressive intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics, and continuous monitoring of fluid balance. Midwives must remain vigilant because a ruptured appendix requires prolonged hospital care and extensive wound drainage.
