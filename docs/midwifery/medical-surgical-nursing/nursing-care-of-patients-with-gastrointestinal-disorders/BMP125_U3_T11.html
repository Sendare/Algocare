Crohn's Disease

Understanding Crohn's Disease in Clinical Practice

Crohn's disease is a chronic inflammatory condition that can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract, from the mouth to the anus. Unlike ulcerative colitis, which is limited to the mucosal layer of the large intestine, Crohn's disease features deep inflammation that spans the entire thickness of the bowel wall. In Nigerian clinical practice, managing this condition requires patience and a strong understanding of its unpredictable alternating phases of active flares and quiet remissions. Midwives and nurses often encounter young adults presenting with chronic abdominal pain and weight loss, making early recognition vital for preventing long-term complications like bowel obstruction and malnutrition.

Pathophysiology and Transmural Intestinal Inflammation

The hallmark pathological feature of Crohn's disease is transmural inflammation, meaning the inflammatory process penetrates through all layers of the bowel wall—from the mucosa down to the serosa. As immune cells gather in the intestinal wall, they form microscopic lumps called non-caseating granulomas, which are a definitive diagnostic marker.

Over time, this continuous deep inflammation leads to several structural changes:

  • Fissures and Ulcers: Deep breaks in the tissue lining that can tunnel through the bowel wall.
  • Fistulas: Abnormal passages connecting the bowel to other organs, such as the bladder, vagina, or skin surface.
  • Strictures: Narrowing of the intestinal lumen caused by scar tissue accumulation, leading to partial or complete blockages.
  • Cobblestoning: A gross appearance of the mucosal surface created by alternating deep ulcerations and normal or oedematous tissue ridges.

Clinical Presentation and Extraintestinal Manifestations

Patients with Crohn's disease typically present with persistent non-bloody diarrhea, cramping abdominal pain (often in the right lower quadrant), and unexplained weight loss driven by malabsorption and reduced food intake. Fever and fatigue are also common systemic complaints during active disease flares.

Beyond the digestive tract, the systemic nature of the inflammatory response triggers extraintestinal manifestations:

  • Musculoskeletal: Peripheral arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis causing joint pain and stiffness.
  • Dermatological: Erythema nodosum (painful red nodules on the shins) and pyoderma gangrenosum.
  • Ocular: Uveitis and episcleritis presenting with eye redness and pain.
  • Hepatic: Primary sclerosing cholangitis and gallstones due to impaired bile salt reabsorption in the terminal ileum.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Endoscopic Findings

Diagnosing Crohn's disease requires a combination of clinical assessment, laboratory tests, imaging, and endoscopy. Blood work often reveals elevated inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) and the erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), alongside anemia from chronic blood loss or poor nutrient absorption. Stool sample tests help rule out infectious causes of gastroenteritis.

Colonoscopy with ileocolonoscopy is the gold standard investigation, allowing direct visualization of the bowel mucosa. Typical endoscopic findings include skip lesions (patches of severe inflammation alternating with completely normal mucosa), deep linear ulcers, and a cobblestone appearance. Biopsies taken during the procedure confirm the diagnosis by revealing chronic inflammatory changes and granulomas. Radiological imaging such as CT enterography or MRI helps assess deeper structural damage and identify fistulas or strictures outside the reach of a standard scope.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Medical treatment focuses on inducing remission during acute flares and maintaining that calm state over the long term. Because there is no medical cure, therapy is tailored to disease severity and location.

  1. Aminosalicylates (5-ASAs): Mild anti-inflammatory agents useful for mild colonic disease, though less effective for isolated small bowel involvement.
  2. Corticosteroids: Powerful anti-inflammatory drugs like prednisolone used for rapid control of acute flares, but avoided for long-term maintenance due to severe side effects.
  3. Immunomodulators: Medications like azathioprine and methotrexate that quiet down an overactive immune system to maintain remission.
  4. Biologic Therapies: Monoclonal antibodies that target specific inflammatory proteins (such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha) to heal deep mucosal tissue and close fistulas.

Surgical Interventions and Indications

Surgery is not a cure for Crohn's disease because inflammation frequently recurs near the surgical reconnection site. However, up to three-quarters of patients eventually require a surgical procedure due to complications that fail to respond to medical therapy.

Indications for surgery include bowel obstruction caused by fixed strictures, recurrent fistulas, internal abscesses, massive bleeding, or suspected bowel perforation. Surgeons typically perform strictureplasty to widen narrowed bowel segments without removing tissue, or bowel resection to cut out severely damaged sections and rejoin the healthy ends. Careful surgical planning minimizes the risk of short bowel syndrome, ensuring the patient retains enough absorbing surface area to maintain adequate nutrition.

Nutritional Support and Dietary Management

Malnutrition is a major challenge in Crohn's disease due to decreased oral intake, intestinal malabsorption, and nutrient loss through chronic diarrhea and fistulas. During active flares, low-residue, low-fiber diets help reduce bowel stimulation and cramping. When the small intestine is heavily damaged, patients may require enteral nutrition (tube feeding) or parenteral nutrition (intravenous feeding) to rest the bowel and restore normal nutritional status.

Midwives and nurses should advise patients to identify personal food triggers rather than following overly restrictive diets that worsen weight loss. Supplementation of specific micronutrients—such as iron, vitamin B12, vitamin D, and calcium—is frequently necessary, especially for patients who have undergone terminal ileum resection where vitamin B12 absorption is impaired.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for a patient with Crohn's disease centres on monitoring clinical status, managing symptoms, and providing emotional support. Nurses must regularly assess abdominal pain levels, monitor stool frequency and consistency, track fluid balance, and record daily weights to catch malnutrition early. Perianal skin care is critical for patients experiencing chronic diarrhea or draining fistulas, requiring gentle cleansing and barrier creams to prevent painful skin breakdown.

Education empowers patients to manage their condition long-term. Nurses should teach patients how to recognize early warning signs of a flare or bowel obstruction, emphasize the importance of taking prescribed medications consistently even when feeling well, and connect them with local support networks to cope with the psychosocial impact of a chronic illness.
