Ulcerative Colitis

Understanding Ulcerative Colitis and Affected Layers

Ulcerative colitis is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease that causes long-lasting inflammation and ulcers in the innermost lining of the large intestine, which includes the colon and the rectum. Unlike Crohn disease, which can affect any part of the digestive tract and penetrate all layers of the bowel wall, ulcerative colitis is strictly confined to the mucosal and submucosal layers. The disease almost always begins in the rectum and spreads continuously upward toward the cecum without skipping areas. Midwives and nurses caring for women of childbearing age should note that chronic gastrointestinal inflammation can flare during pregnancy, requiring careful coordination between obstetric and medical teams to maintain bowel remission while protecting the developing fetus.

Clinical Presentation and Common Symptoms

The clinical picture of ulcerative colitis is dominated by recurring flare-ups of gastrointestinal distress. Patients frequently report abdominal cramping, rectal urgency, and bloody diarrhea mixed with mucus. The severity of symptoms depends on how much of the colon is inflamed.

  • Proctitis: Inflammation limited to the rectum, often causing rectal bleeding and urgency with minimal diarrhea.
  • Left-sided colitis: Inflammation extending from the rectum up to the splenic flexure, causing bloody stools, abdominal pain on the left side, and weight loss.
  • Pancolitis: Inflammation affecting the entire colon, leading to severe frequent diarrhea, profound weight loss, fever, and generalized fatigue.

Systemic complications such as joint pain, skin lesions, and eye inflammation can also occur outside the gut.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Endoscopic Findings

Diagnosing ulcerative colitis requires a combination of clinical history, laboratory investigations, and direct visualization of the bowel mucosa. Stool cultures are essential first steps to rule out infectious causes of bloody diarrhea. Colonoscopy with mucosal biopsy remains the gold standard diagnostic tool. During an endoscopic procedure, clinicians look for loss of the normal vascular pattern, mucosal erythema, granular texture, friability, and active ulcerations covered by exudate. Blood tests often reveal elevated inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) and an elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), alongside iron-deficiency anemia resulting from chronic blood loss through the stool.

Medical Management and Pharmacotherapy

Medical treatment aims to induce and maintain remission, heal the bowel mucosa, and improve quality of life. Drug therapy is tailored based on the extent and severity of the disease.

  • Aminosalicylates (5-ASAs): Medications like mesalamine act locally on the bowel mucosa to reduce inflammation and are used for mild-to-moderate disease or maintenance.
  • Corticosteroids: Agents such as prednisone or budesonide provide rapid anti-inflammatory action for acute flare-ups but are avoided for long-term maintenance due to adverse side effects.
  • Immunomodulators: Drugs like azathioprine help maintain remission in patients who depend heavily on steroids.
  • Biologics: Monoclonal antibodies target specific inflammatory proteins in the immune system for moderate-to-severe refractory cases.

Surgical Interventions for Severe Disease

Surgery becomes necessary when medical management fails, or when life-threatening complications arise such as toxic megacolon, severe uncontrollable hemorrhage, bowel perforation, or dysplasia discovered during routine surveillance. Unlike Crohn disease, where surgery is conservative due to high recurrence rates, ulcerative colitis can be completely cured by removing the entire colon and rectum, a procedure known as total proctocolectomy. Following removal, surgeons often perform an ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (IPAA), also called a J-pouch, which allows the patient to pass stool via the natural route without requiring a permanent external ostomy bag. When a J-pouch cannot be safely created immediately, a temporary diverting ileostomy is constructed while the internal pouch heals.

Nursing Care and Patient Support

Comprehensive nursing care for a patient with ulcerative colitis centers on managing acute bowel symptoms, promoting adequate nutrition, and supporting emotional well-being. Nurses must monitor stool frequency, consistency, and the presence of blood, while keeping strict intake and output records during severe flares. Electrolyte imbalances and dehydration must be promptly corrected. Dietary modifications often involve avoiding high-fiber foods, dairy products, caffeine, and spicy items during acute exacerbations to minimize bowel stimulation. Because chronic illness significantly impacts mental health, nurses should provide a supportive environment for patients to discuss body image concerns, fatigue, and the unpredictability of flare-ups.
