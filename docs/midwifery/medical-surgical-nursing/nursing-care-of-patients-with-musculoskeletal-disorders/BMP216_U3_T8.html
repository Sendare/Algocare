Amputation Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Understanding Amputation: Indications and Surgical Levels

An amputation is the surgical removal of all or part of a limb or extremity. In Nigerian clinical practice, the procedure is most frequently performed due to severe complications of diabetes mellitus, peripheral vascular disease, malignant bone tumors, unmanageable osteomyelitis, or high-velocity traumatic injuries from road traffic crashes. Selecting the appropriate surgical level is a critical decision that dictates future mobility and functional independence. Surgeons aim to preserve as much length and joint function as possible while ensuring adequate tissue perfusion and a healthy, durable stump (residual limb).

  • Below-Knee Amputation (BKA): Preserves the knee joint, which significantly improves energy efficiency during walking compared to higher-level resections.
  • Above-Knee Amputation (AKA): Involves removal of the leg above the knee joint, requiring greater energy expenditure to master prosthetic use.
  • Upper Extremity Amputations: Resections of the arm, forearm, or hand, where preserving length and tactile sensation in the residual limb is prioritized for fine motor tasks.

Preoperative Preparation and Psychological Support

Preparing a patient for an amputation involves addressing both physical health and deep emotional trauma. Midwives and nurses must assess baseline nutritional status, manage underlying chronic conditions like hypertension or diabetes, and ensure informed consent is thoroughly understood. Preoperative counseling plays a vital role in easing anxiety. Introducing the patient to a peer visitor who has successfully adapted to life with an amputation often provides unmatched reassurance. Marking the correct surgical site with the patient awake and participating is a non-negotiable safety step to prevent wrong-site surgery.

Immediate Postoperative Amputation Nursing Care

The hours immediately following surgery focus on hemodynamic stability and monitoring for acute complications. The most dangerous immediate risk is hemorrhage due to the transection of major blood vessels. A tourniquet should always be kept visibly secured at the bedside for emergency use if severe bleeding breaks through the dressing.

  • Vital Signs: Monitor blood pressure and pulse frequently to detect early signs of hypovolemic shock.
  • Stump Dressing: Inspect the dressing regularly for excessive bright red drainage. Circle any blood-stain expansion with a pen, noting the exact time.
  • Drain Management: Ensure surgical drains function properly to prevent fluid accumulation and subsequent wound infection.

Managing Phantom Limb Pain and Other Complications

Patients frequently report distressing sensations originating from the missing limb. It is essential to distinguish between phantom limb sensation—which is a normal, usually painless feeling that the limb is still attached—and phantom limb pain (PLP), which involves burning, shooting, or cramping pain in the missing body part. PLP is real and originates from disrupted neural pathways, not psychological issues.

Treatment often involves a multimodal approach including gentle stump massage, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), mirror therapy, and prescribed neuropathic medications like gabapentin. Beyond pain, nurses must watch for surgical site infection and deep vein thrombosis due to immobility.

Stump Care and Prevention of Contractures

Proper care of the residual limb ensures it eventually fits comfortably into an artificial prosthesis. The skin must be washed daily with mild soap, dried thoroughly by patting rather than vigorous rubbing, and inspected daily for redness, blisters, or skin breakdown.

Preventing joint contractures—especially flexion deformities of the hip or knee—is vital for future walking. Nurses must teach patients proper positioning techniques:

  • Avoid keeping the residual limb elevated on pillows for extended periods, as this promotes hip or knee flexion contractures.
  • Encourage lying in a prone position (on the stomach) for short periods several times a day to stretch hip flexor muscles.
  • Discourage prolonged sitting in a wheelchair with the stump dangling or supported in a flexed posture.

Comprehensive Rehabilitation and Prosthetic Training

Rehabilitation transforms an amputation survivor from dependent care to active daily living. Once the surgical wound heals and swelling subsides, the process of stump shaping begins, typically using elastic bandages or a shrinker sock to create a tapered conical shape suited for a socket.

Physical and occupational therapists guide patients through strengthening exercises for the upper body and remaining limb segments. Balance training, transfer practice, and gradual prosthetic fitting follow. Midwives and nurses collaborate with the rehabilitation team to encourage persistence, as mastering a prosthesis demands significant physical effort and mental endurance.

Psychosocial Adaptation and Community Reintegration

The loss of a limb alters body image, self-esteem, and social roles. Patients frequently experience stages of grief similar to bereavement: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and eventual acceptance. In many Nigerian communities, physical disability can carry social stigmas or economic hardships, making holistic support even more crucial.

Nurses facilitate psychosocial adaptation by encouraging open communication about body image concerns, involving family members in education, and connecting patients with vocational rehabilitation services. Successful reintegration means the individual returns to meaningful family roles, work, and community life with dignity and independence.
