Rheumatoid Arthritis

Understanding Rheumatoid Arthritis Pathophysiology

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic, systemic inflammatory disease that primarily targets the synovial membranes of joints. Unlike osteoarthritis which stems from simple wear and tear, this condition is driven by a misguided immune response. The body's white blood cells mistakenly attack the synovium, causing it to become thick, inflamed, and swollen.

Over time, this persistent inflammation produces an abnormal layer of granulation tissue called pannus. The pannus releases destructive enzymes that slowly erode the adjacent cartilage and underlying bone. As the joint structures break down, fibrous scar tissue forms, which can eventually bridge the joint space and cause permanent bony fusion, known as ankylosis.

Clinical Presentation and Joint Symptoms

Patients with rheumatoid arthritis typically seek care due to symmetrical, polyarticular joint pain and swelling. The small joints of the hands, wrists, and feet are almost always involved first.

  • Morning stiffness: Lasting longer than one hour, which helps distinguish it from other joint disorders.
  • Symmetrical involvement: Both hands or both knees affected at the same time.
  • Deformities: Late signs include characteristic changes like ulnar deviation of the fingers, swan-neck deformities, and boutonnière deformities.

Systemic features are also common. Patients frequently report persistent fatigue, low-grade fever, general malaise, and unintended weight loss during active flare-ups.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Diagnosing rheumatoid arthritis relies on a combination of clinical findings, imaging studies, and specific blood tests. No single test provides a definitive answer, so doctors look at the complete picture.

Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Management

Management focuses on reducing pain, halting joint destruction, and preserving functional ability. Treatment plans are tailored to the severity of the disease and are typically led by rheumatologists.

Disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs, commonly known as DMARDs like methotrexate, form the cornerstone of medical therapy because they slow down the underlying disease progression rather than just masking symptoms. Biologic response modifiers are added if conventional DMARDs fail to control the inflammation. Corticosteroids provide rapid relief during acute flare-ups, while nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs help manage pain and stiffness.

Physical and occupational therapy play a vital role alongside medications. Therapists teach joint protection techniques, prescribe custom splints to prevent deformity, and design balanced exercise programs to maintain muscle strength without overloading vulnerable joints.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Midwives and nurses caring for patients with rheumatoid arthritis must address both physical limitations and psychological well-being. Chronic pain and changing joint appearance often lead to frustration and depression.

Nurses should assist patients in balancing periods of rest with light activity to prevent fatigue and joint stiffness. Applying moist heat packs helps soothe aching joints before exercise, while cold packs can reduce swelling during acute inflammation. Educating the patient on proper body mechanics, assistive devices, and medication adherence is essential for promoting independence and preventing unnecessary joint stress during daily activities.

Potential Complications and Long-Term Outlook

Without prompt and effective treatment, rheumatoid arthritis can lead to severe local and systemic complications. Joint destruction often results in permanent deformities, loss of mobility, and chronic disability.

Because the disease involves widespread systemic inflammation, extra-articular manifestations can develop in various organs. Rheumatoid nodules can form under the skin, especially over pressure points like the elbows. Patients also face an increased risk of cardiovascular disease due to chronic systemic vascular inflammation. Dry eyes and dry mouth can occur secondary to Sjögren's syndrome, and chronic anemia of chronic disease is frequently observed.
