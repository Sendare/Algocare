Nursing Care of Patients in Traction and Plaster Casts (POP)

Introduction to Musculoskeletal Immobilization

Musculoskeletal immobilization is a cornerstone of orthopedic care. When bones fracture, joints dislocate, or soft tissues undergo major repair, keeping the affected limb or spine still is vital for proper healing. Immobilization reduces pain, prevents further tissue damage, and maintains proper anatomical alignment.

Two primary methods achieve this stabilization in clinical practice:

  • Traction: The application of a pulling force to a body part to stretch and align muscle and bone.
  • Plaster of Paris (POP) casts: Rigid external devices molded directly to the contours of a limb to hold bones in place.

Midwives and nurses caring for patients with these devices must understand the underlying principles of alignment and counter-traction. Every pulling force requires an opposing force to be effective. For traction, the patient's body weight often acts as that counter-traction. For casts, the rigid material itself encases the limb, preventing movement at the joint above and below the fracture site. Regular monitoring ensures these devices support healing without causing unintended harm to the skin or underlying nerves.

Principles and Types of Traction

Traction uses weights, ropes, and pulleys to apply a continuous pulling force to a fractured or deformed limb. The goals are to reduce muscle spasms, realign bone fragments, and correct deformities.

Traction is broadly categorized into two types:

  • Skin traction: Applies pulling force directly to the skin using adhesive tape, foam boots, or straps. The weight used is relatively light, usually under 5 kilograms, to prevent skin breakdown. Buck's traction is a common example used for hip fractures before surgery.
  • Skeletal traction: Applies pulling force directly to the bone using metal pins, wires, or tongs surgically inserted through the bone. This allows much heavier weights to be used for extended periods, commonly for femur or cervical spine fractures.

Maintaining effective traction requires strict adherence to mechanical rules. Weights must hang freely at all times and never rest on the floor or the bed frame. Ropes must remain securely in the pulley grooves, and the patient's body alignment must match the line of pull. Interference with these weights instantly reduces the therapeutic pull.

Application and Management of Plaster Casts (POP)

Plaster of Paris (POP) casts remain widely used in resource-limited settings due to their low cost and excellent moldability. When dry bandages are soaked in water and applied over stockinette and padding, an exothermic crystallization reaction occurs, generating mild warmth as the cast hardens.

During the drying phase, nurses must handle a wet cast with extreme care. Always use the palms of your hands—never your fingertips—to lift a drying cast. Fingertips create local indentations in the soft plaster, forming pressure points that can severely damage the underlying skin and tissue.

Elevate the newly casted limb on pillows to encourage venous return and minimize swelling. Keep the cast uncovered while it dries to allow air circulation; do not cover it with heavy blankets or plastic wrap, which trap heat and moisture. Once dry, inspect the edges. If rough edges rub against the patient's skin, petalling the cast with adhesive tape prevents friction sores.

Essential Neurovascular Assessments

Patients with casts or traction face a high risk of impaired blood flow and nerve damage due to swelling or tight dressings. Routine neurovascular checks are mandatory, typically performed every hour for the first 24 hours, then every four hours thereafter.

Pin Site Care and Infection Prevention

Skeletal traction involves metal pins passing directly through the skin and soft tissue into bone. These pin sites serve as direct pathways for bacteria to enter the body, creating a continuous risk of osteomyelitis and local soft tissue infection.

Standard pin site care protocol involves:

  1. Performing meticulous hand hygiene and wearing sterile gloves.
  2. Cleaning around each pin site using sterile normal saline or prescribed antiseptic solutions, working in a circular motion outward from the pin.
  3. Removing any crusting gently, as trapped crusts can harbor bacteria beneath them.
  4. Leaving the site uncovered or applying sterile, non-adherent dressings according to hospital policy.

Monitor the sites closely for signs of infection, which include spreading redness, local warmth, purulent drainage, and increasing pain. Never adjust or loosen traction pins yourself; any loose hardware must be reported immediately to the orthopedic surgeon.

Recognizing and Managing Complications

Immobilization carries serious systemic and local risks that require vigilant nursing oversight. Prolonged bed rest and pressure on blood vessels promote venous stasis, predisposing patients to deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). Encourage deep breathing exercises, leg exercises, and administer prescribed anticoagulants promptly.

Compartment syndrome is another surgical emergency. It occurs when swelling within a closed fascial compartment—such as a tight cast—increases pressure, cutting off blood flow and nerve function. The classic clinical signs are remembered as the Ps: Pain out of proportion to the injury, Pallor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia (numbness), and Paralysis. If compartment syndrome is suspected, elevate the limb only to heart level (not above) and notify the medical team immediately; bivalving or splitting the cast is often required.

Skin breakdown and pressure ulcers frequently develop over bony prominences like the heel, sacrum, or elbow. Regular repositioning, skin hygiene, and padding vulnerable spots protect integumentary integrity.

Patient Mobility and Rehabilitation Support

Recovery from musculoskeletal trauma extends far beyond bone healing. Prolonged immobilization leads to rapid muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, and deconditioning. Midwives and nurses play a vital role in supporting early mobilization and rehabilitation within the limits prescribed by the medical team.

Teach isometric exercises—contracting muscles without moving the underlying joints—to maintain muscle tone while in a cast or traction. Assist patients with passive and active range-of-motion exercises for unaffected joints. When the patient is cleared to get out of bed, supervise transfers carefully, ensuring they understand how to use assistive devices like crutches, walkers, or walking frames safely.

Nutritional support is equally important for tissue repair. Encourage a balanced diet rich in protein, calcium, Vitamin D, and Vitamin C to promote bone matrix formation and wound healing. Psychological encouragement helps patients cope with temporary dependence and long recovery timelines.
