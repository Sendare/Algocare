Fractures: Types, Healing Process, Reduction, and Immobilization Care

Introduction to Fractures and Classification of Types

A fracture is a break or disruption in the structural continuity of a bone. When a bone breaks, adjacent soft tissues, blood vessels, and nerves often sustain damage as well. Fractures are broadly classified based on their communication with the external environment, the extent of the break, and the configuration of the fracture line.

  • Closed (Simple) Fracture: The overlying skin remains intact, meaning there is no direct opening from the fracture site to the outside world.
  • Open (Compound) Fracture: The broken bone ends pierce the skin or a deep wound exposes the bone to the external environment, carrying a high risk of bacterial contamination and osteomyelitis.
  • Complete Fracture: The break goes completely across the entire width of the bone, dividing it into two or more distinct pieces.
  • Incomplete Fracture: The bone is only partially cracked or buckled, commonly seen in infants and children whose bones are more flexible (greenstick fracture).
  • Displaced Fracture: The bone ends are out of alignment at the fracture site.
  • Non-displaced Fracture: The bone cracks either part or all of the way through, but maintains its normal alignment.

Fracture patterns also provide clues about the mechanism of injury. A transverse fracture runs straight across the bone shaft, usually caused by a direct, sharp blow. A spiral fracture twists around the bone shaft, often resulting from a twisting injury and raising suspicion of non-accidental trauma in vulnerable populations. A comminuted fracture shatters the bone into three or more pieces, typically from high-energy trauma like a motor vehicle crash on Nigerian highways.

The Bone Healing Process: Stages of Repair

Bone healing is a remarkable biological process of regeneration rather than simple scar formation. When a bone breaks, the body initiates a predictable sequence of physiological events to restore strength and function.

  1. Hematoma Formation: Immediately following the fracture, blood vessels rupture, bleeding into the injury site and forming a blood clot called a fracture hematoma within the first 24 to 72 hours.
  2. Fibrocartilaginous Callus Formation: Granulation tissue invades the hematoma. Fibroblasts, chondroblasts, and osteoblasts migrate to the area, creating a soft bridge of fibrocartilage that connects the bone ends.
  3. Bony Callus Formation: Osteoblasts deposit spongy bone trabeculae, gradually replacing the soft cartilage callus with a hard bony callus over three to four weeks.
  4. Remodeling: Osteoclasts resorb dead or excess bone fragments while osteoblasts lay down compact bone along lines of stress, eventually restoring the bone to its original shape and strength over months or years.

Methods of Fracture Reduction

Reduction is the realignment of broken bone fragments into their normal anatomical position to ensure proper healing. If a fracture is left unreduced or poorly aligned, the limb may suffer from permanent deformity, chronic pain, or loss of function.

Closed reduction involves manually manipulating and pulling the bone fragments back into place while the patient is under local, regional, or general anesthesia. Once aligned, the extremity is secured with a plaster cast, splint, or traction device.

Open reduction requires surgical intervention to directly view and align the fracture site. Surgeons use internal fixation devices such as metal plates, screws, intramedullary nails, or pins to hold the bones securely together. This approach, known as Open Reduction Internal Fixation (ORIF), is chosen when closed reduction fails, when joint surfaces are displaced, or when soft tissue is trapped between the bone ends.

Immobilization Care and Nursing Management

Once reduced, the fracture must remain completely still to let new bone cells bridge the gap. External immobilization is commonly achieved using plaster of Paris or synthetic fiberglass casts, traction setups, or rigid splints.

Fresh plaster casts require careful handling. Midwives and nurses must support wet casts using the palms of their hands rather than fingertips to prevent indentations that can press against the skin and cause pressure sores. The casted limb should be elevated above the level of the heart during the first 24 to 48 hours to minimize dependent edema.

Regular neurovascular checks form the cornerstone of safe cast care. Assess the five Ps: Pain that worsens despite medication, Pallor or cyanosis in the toes or fingers, Pulselessness distal to the site, Paresthesia (tingling or numbness), and Paralysis (inability to move digits). Report any foul odor coming from beneath the cast or unexplained hot spots immediately, as these warn of localized infection or pressure necrosis.

Monitoring for Complications in Immobilized Patients

Prolonged immobility and bone trauma expose patients to serious, life-threatening systemic complications that demand vigilant nursing surveillance.

  • Compartment Syndrome: Occurs when increased pressure inside a closed muscle fascia compartment chokes off blood flow and nerve transmission. Severe, unremitting pain that is unresponsive to heavy analgesics and pain aggravated by passive stretching of the fingers or toes is an early sign. Late signs include loss of pulses and paralysis, which can lead to permanent muscle and nerve death within hours.
  • Fat Embolism Syndrome: Yellow bone marrow fat globules escape from the fractured medullary cavity into the bloodstream, traveling to the lungs and brain. It typically strikes young adults with long bone fractures within 24 to 72 hours, presenting with respiratory distress, tachycardia, and a characteristic petechial rash across the chest and neck.
  • Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT): Stagnant venous blood flow in immobilized lower limbs can form clots, which may dislodge and travel to the lungs as a pulmonary embolism. Prophylaxis includes early mobilization, leg exercises, hydration, and anticoagulant therapy.
