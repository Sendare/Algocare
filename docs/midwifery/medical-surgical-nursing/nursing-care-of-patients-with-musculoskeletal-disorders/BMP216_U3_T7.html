Osteoarthritis

Understanding Osteoarthritis and Joint Degeneration

Osteoarthritis is the most common form of arthritis, characterized by the gradual breakdown of the cartilage that cushions the ends of bones within a joint. Unlike inflammatory joint diseases, this condition is primarily a mechanical and degenerative disorder. Over time, the smooth, protective cartilage thins, frays, and eventually wears away completely. This leaves bone rubbing directly against bone, causing severe friction, pain, swelling, and a significant loss of joint mobility.

While any joint can be affected, load-bearing joints like the knees and hips bear the brunt of the damage. In many Nigerian clinics, middle-aged and elderly patients frequently present with advanced knee osteoarthritis. As the cartilage deteriorates, the body attempts to repair the damage by growing new bone at the joint margins, leading to bony projections known as osteophytes or bone spurs. These spurs alter the normal shape of the joint and further restrict movement.

Key Risk Factors and Pathophysiology

Several interrelated factors accelerate joint degeneration, and understanding them helps midwives guide patients effectively in community and hospital settings.

  • Age: Cumulative mechanical stress over decades makes joint wear almost inevitable in older adults.
  • Obesity: Excess body weight places constant, high-impact pressure on weight-bearing joints, particularly the knees.
  • Joint Trauma: Previous fractures, sports injuries, or occupational injuries damage joint structures permanently, predisposing them to early degeneration.
  • Genetics: Inherited flaws in cartilage proteins can weaken joint resilience.
  • Gender: Post-menopausal women experience a higher incidence of osteoarthritis, partly due to hormonal shifts affecting tissue integrity.

Clinical Presentation and Common Symptoms

Patients with osteoarthritis typically seek medical attention due to persistent joint discomfort that worsens with physical activity and improves with rest. In the early stages, pain subsides quickly after stopping the aggravating task. As the disease advances, however, pain becomes constant and even disrupts sleep.

A classic clinical sign is morning stiffness, though unlike rheumatoid arthritis, this stiffness in osteoarthritis usually lasts less than thirty minutes and eases once the patient starts moving around. Midwives should listen for a distinct grinding sensation or sound, known as crepitus, when the affected joint moves. Joint swelling is often hard and bony rather than soft and spongy, reflecting the presence of osteophytes.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Clinical Assessment

Diagnosing osteoarthritis relies heavily on a thorough clinical history and physical examination. Midwives and doctors look for characteristic joint tenderness, bony enlargement, restricted range of motion, and crepitus.

While blood tests are generally normal in primary osteoarthritis—helping rule out systemic inflammatory conditions like rheumatoid arthritis—imaging studies confirm the structural damage. Plain radiography (X-rays) is the gold standard diagnostic tool. Standard X-ray findings characteristic of osteoarthritis include:

  • Joint space narrowing caused by the loss of cartilage.
  • Osteophyte formation at the joint margins.
  • Subchondral sclerosis, which is a hardening of the bone just beneath the damaged cartilage.
  • Subchondral cysts in advanced cases.

Comprehensive Management Strategies

Because joint cartilage cannot regenerate on its own, management focuses on pain relief, preserving function, and slowing disease progression. Treatment combines non-pharmacological, pharmacological, and surgical modalities tailored to the patient's functional status.

  1. Lifestyle Modification: Weight reduction for overweight individuals significantly decreases mechanical stress on the knees and hips.
  2. Physical Therapy: Targeted exercises strengthen surrounding muscles to support the joint and maintain flexibility.
  3. Pharmacotherapy: Analgesics like paracetamol are often tried first for mild pain. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) help manage moderate to severe pain and inflammation, though they must be used cautiously to avoid gastric ulcers and kidney strain.
  4. Surgical Intervention: In advanced, disabling cases where conservative measures fail, joint replacement surgery (arthroplasty) provides significant relief.

Nursing Care and Patient Education for Mobility

Midwives and nurses play a vital role in helping patients manage daily activities while living with chronic joint pain. Education centers on energy conservation, joint protection techniques, and the safe use of assistive devices such as walking sticks or crutches.

Patients should be taught to use larger, stronger joints whenever possible—for example, carrying heavy bags close to the body or using the forearm instead of smaller finger joints. Alternating periods of activity with scheduled rest prevents excessive joint fatigue. Midwives also counsel patients on maintaining a balanced diet rich in nutrients to support overall health and weight control. Encouraging adherence to prescribed exercise routines ensures that surrounding muscle support does not waste away from disuse.
