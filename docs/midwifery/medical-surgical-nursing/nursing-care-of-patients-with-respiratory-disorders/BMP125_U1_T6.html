Pneumonia

Understanding Pneumonia: Pathophysiology and Classification

Pneumonia is an acute inflammatory condition of the lung parenchyma affecting the alveoli and bronchioles. When pathogens invade the lower respiratory tract, they trigger a local immune response. Neutrophils and macrophages flood the alveolar spaces to fight the infection, leading to an exudative accumulation of fluid, cellular debris, and fibrinous exudate. This process impairs gas exchange, resulting in ventilation-perfusion mismatch and arterial hypoxemia.

Clinicians classify pneumonia based on the setting of acquisition, which guides empirical treatment choices:

  • Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP): Infections contracted in the community setting outside of healthcare facilities.
  • Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP): Infections developing 48 hours or more after hospital admission, not incubating at the time of entry.
  • Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP): A specific subset of HAP occurring within 48 to 72 hours following endotracheal intubation.

Etiology and Common Pathogens in Nigeria

In the Nigerian healthcare setting, pneumonia etiology spans both bacterial and viral agents, heavily influenced by environmental and socioeconomic factors.

  • Streptococcus pneumoniae: The leading bacterial cause of community-acquired pneumonia across all age groups.
  • Klebsiella pneumoniae: Frequently isolated in hospital-acquired cases and among patients with malnutrition or chronic alcoholism.
  • Staphylococcus aureus: Often seen as a secondary infection following viral illnesses like measles or influenza.
  • Atypical pathogens: Organisms such as *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* contribute to milder, walking pneumonia variants.
  • Viral agents: Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV) and influenza viruses are major triggers, particularly in pediatric populations.

Environmental exposures, such as indoor air pollution from biomass fuels used in cooking, significantly increase vulnerability to these pathogens among rural and semi-urban populations in Nigeria.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Findings

Patients with pneumonia present with a classic triad of respiratory and systemic symptoms, though presentations vary widely in elderly or immunocompromised individuals. Common clinical features include a productive cough with rust-colored or purulent sputum, pleuritic chest pain that worsens with deep breathing, and high-grade fever with rigors.

On physical examination, inspection may reveal tachypnea and use of accessory muscles. Auscultation typically demonstrates bronchial breath sounds, crackles over the affected lung zone, and egophony. Percussion yields a dull note over areas of consolidation.

Diagnostic confirmation relies on a chest radiograph showing lobar or patchy infiltrates. Sputum Gram stain and culture help isolate the specific pathogen, while pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas analysis assess the degree of hypoxemia and acid-base balance.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Medical management centers on eradicating the offending pathogen, supporting ventilation, and preventing complications. Empirical antibiotic therapy must be initiated promptly after obtaining sputum and blood cultures.

Pathogen CategoryFirst-Line Therapy Example
Community-Acquired (Adults)Amoxicillin or benzylpenicillin plus gentamycin
Atypical PathogensMacrolides (e.g., azithromycin or erythromycin)
Hospital-AcquiredBroad-spectrum beta-lactams or fluoroquinolones

In addition to antimicrobials, supportive care includes intravenous fluid hydration to loosen secretions, antipyretics for fever control, and analgesics for pleuritic chest pain. Oxygen therapy is titrated carefully to maintain target oxygen saturations above 92% in non-hypercapnic patients.

Nursing Care Plan and Respiratory Support

The nursing care plan for a patient with pneumonia focuses on maintaining an effective airway, optimizing gas exchange, and promoting energy conservation. Midwives must monitor vital signs frequently, paying close attention to respiratory rate, depth, and oxygen saturation levels.

Interventions include positioning the patient in a high-Fowler's position to maximize chest expansion and lung capacity. Guiding the patient through deep breathing and controlled coughing exercises helps mobilize retained secretions. Chest physiotherapy, including percussion and postural drainage, may be implemented for patients with copious sputum production who cannot clear them independently. Adequate rest periods must be scheduled between nursing tasks to prevent oxygen desaturation from physical exertion.

Potential Complications in Vulnerable Populations

Pneumonia can progress rapidly to severe systemic complications, particularly among vulnerable groups such as infants, malnourished children, pregnant women, and the elderly.

  • Pleural Effusion and Empyema: Accumulation of serous fluid or pus in the pleural space, requiring thoracentesis or chest tube insertion.
  • Bacteremia and Sepsis: Spread of infection from the lungs into the bloodstream, leading to systemic inflammatory response syndrome and multi-organ failure.
  • Lung Abscess: Localized necrosis of lung tissue forming a cavity filled with purulent material.
  • Respiratory Failure: Inability of the lungs to maintain adequate oxygenation or ventilation, necessitating mechanical ventilatory support.

Prevention Strategies and Public Health Education

Preventing pneumonia requires a combination of community-level public health interventions and individual behavioral changes. Midwives play a central role in educating families during antenatal visits, immunisation clinics, and postnatal home visits.

Key public health strategies include promoting exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life to enhance infant immunity, expanding access to routine childhood vaccines such as the Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine (PCV) and measles vaccines, and advocating for clean cooking technologies to reduce indoor air pollution. Health education should also emphasize hand hygiene, smoking cessation, and prompt treatment of upper respiratory tract infections before they progress to lower respiratory involvement.
