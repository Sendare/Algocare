Bronchitis

Introduction to Bronchitis

Bronchitis is an inflammation of the large airways, known as the bronchi, which carry air to and from your lungs. When these bronchial tubes become irritated and inflamed, they swell and produce thick mucus, leading to persistent coughing and breathing discomfort. In clinical practice, this condition is broadly categorized into two main forms based on its duration and underlying cause. Understanding bronchitis requires looking closely at how the respiratory tract reacts to infections, environmental irritants, and lifestyle factors. Midwives in Nigeria frequently encounter patients presenting with respiratory complaints worsened by local environmental conditions like dust and indoor smoke.

Acute versus Chronic Bronchitis: Key Differences

Differentiating between acute and chronic bronchitis is essential for proper treatment planning. Acute bronchitis is a short-term illness that typically develops after a viral upper respiratory infection, resolving completely within a few weeks without permanent lung damage. Chronic bronchitis, conversely, is a long-term, progressive condition defined clinically as a productive cough that lasts for at least three months a year over two consecutive years. It falls under the umbrella of Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (COPD). While acute cases are common during the rainy or harmattan seasons in Nigeria, chronic bronchitis is strongly linked to long-term exposure to tobacco smoke or heavy biomass fuel smoke from indoor cooking stoves.

Pathophysiology and Airway Changes

The pathological process of bronchitis involves significant changes to the lining of the airways. When an irritant or infectious agent attacks the bronchial mucosa, it triggers an immune response. This response causes vasodilation, edema, and infiltration of inflammatory cells. Furthermore, the goblet cells and mucous glands hypertrophy and hypersecrete thick mucus. Cilia, the tiny hair-like structures responsible for sweeping mucus upward, become paralyzed or damaged. This impairs the mucociliary escalator, causing mucus to pool in the lower airways, which narrows the lumen and creates an ideal breeding ground for secondary bacterial infections.

Etiologies and Risk Factors in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the triggers and risk factors for bronchitis vary across different settings and socioeconomic groups.

  • Viral infections: Influenza and rhinovirus are the most frequent causes of acute bronchitis.
  • Environmental pollution: Dust from the dry harmattan season and heavy traffic fumes in urban centers like Lagos or Ibadan irritate the airways.
  • Biomass fuel smoke: Many households in rural and peri-urban areas rely on firewood and kerosene stoves inside poorly ventilated kitchens, exposing women and children to chronic smoke.
  • Tobacco smoking: Active and passive cigarette smoke remains a major contributor to chronic airway inflammation.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment Findings

Patients with bronchitis typically present with a prominent, hacking cough that may yield clear, yellow, or green sputum. The change in sputum color does not automatically indicate a bacterial infection; it often simply reflects white blood cells fighting inflammation. During physical assessment, a midwife may observe tachypnea and hear wheezes or coarse crackles upon auscultation of the chest. In more severe cases, the patient might use accessory muscles to breathe. Chronic bronchitis patients often present with a chronic productive cough and may develop cyanosis and peripheral edema in advanced stages due to chronic low oxygen levels.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosing bronchitis is primarily based on clinical history and physical examination, but additional tests help rule out other conditions like pneumonia or tuberculosis, which are prevalent in Nigeria. A chest X-ray is often ordered to check for lung infiltrates or consolidation. Sputum analysis may be performed if a bacterial infection is suspected, though routine cultures are rarely needed for simple acute bronchitis. Pulse oximetry measures blood oxygen saturation levels. In chronic cases, spirometry is crucial to measure lung function and assess airflow limitation.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Management of acute bronchitis focuses largely on supportive care because most cases are viral. Antibiotics are generally unnecessary unless there is clear evidence of a secondary bacterial infection. Hydration is vital to thin out bronchial secretions, making them easier to cough up. Bronchodilators, such as salbutamol inhalers, may be prescribed if wheezing and bronchospasm are present. For chronic bronchitis, long-acting bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, and pulmonary rehabilitation form the cornerstone of ongoing therapy to reduce flare-ups and improve quality of life.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Midwives and nurses play a crucial role in supporting recovery and preventing recurrence. Care planning includes monitoring vital signs, assessing respiratory status, and ensuring effective breathing exercises like pursed-lip breathing. Patient education should focus on smoking cessation, avoiding biomass smoke by improving kitchen ventilation, and recognizing early signs of respiratory distress that require prompt medical attention. Emphasizing proper hand hygiene helps prevent the spread of viral infections within families and communities.
