Myocardial Infarction

Understanding Myocardial Infarction and Ischaemic Heart Disease

Myocardial infarction, commonly known as a heart attack, occurs when blood flow to a section of the heart muscle abruptly stops, causing tissue damage or death. This event sits at the severe end of ischaemic heart disease, a spectrum of conditions characterized by reduced blood supply to the myocardium due to narrowed coronary arteries. The underlying process usually begins with atherosclerosis, where fatty plaques build up inside the vessel walls over many years.

When a plaque ruptures, a blood clot forms on top of it, completely blocking the artery. Without oxygen and vital nutrients, heart muscle cells begin to die within minutes. Midwives working in Nigerian clinical settings must recognize that cardiovascular emergencies, once considered rare in younger populations, are increasingly seen due to changing lifestyles, urban stress, and rising rates of hypertension.

Pathophysiology and Coronary Artery Occlusion

The heart muscle relies entirely on the coronary circulation for its oxygen supply. When an atherosclerotic plaque develops a fissure or tear, platelets rush to the site, triggering the coagulation cascade and forming an occlusive thrombus.

  • Ischaemia: The immediate reduction in blood flow forces heart cells to switch from aerobic to anaerobic metabolism, producing lactic acid.
  • Injury: If the blockage persists, the metabolic waste buildup causes cellular membrane damage and electrical instability.
  • Infarction: After 20 to 40 minutes of complete occlusion, irreversible necrosis of the myocardial tissue sets in, usually starting in the subendocardium and spreading outward.

The extent of the damage depends on which coronary artery is blocked, how long the blockage lasts, and whether small collateral blood vessels have had time to form around the obstruction.

Risk Factors and Precipitating Triggers

Identifying risk factors helps healthcare providers anticipate cardiac events and educate patients on prevention. Risk factors are generally divided into two main groups:

  1. Non-modifiable risk factors: Advanced age, male gender, family history of premature heart disease, and genetic predisposition.
  2. Modifiable risk factors: Hypertension, cigarette smoking, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidaemia, obesity, and sedentary lifestyles.

Acute heart attacks are frequently precipitated by sudden triggers that increase myocardial oxygen demand against a narrowed vessel lumen. Common triggers include heavy physical exertion, severe emotional stress, heavy meals, or exposure to cold temperatures. Midwives must screen antenatal and postnatal clients with chronic conditions like gestational hypertension for long-term cardiovascular risks.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Assessment

The classic presentation of a myocardial infarction is sudden, severe retrosternal chest pain often described as crushing, squeezing, or a heavy pressure. This discomfort frequently radiates to the left arm, neck, jaw, or back.

Associated autonomic symptoms include profuse diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, lightheadedness, and profound shortness of breath. Midwives should remain aware that atypical presentations occur frequently in women, older adults, and individuals with diabetes. These patients might present primarily with unexplained fatigue, sharp pleuritic pain, indigestion, or sudden syncope without classic chest pressure. Rapid clinical assessment using a focused history and physical examination is vital for early detection.

Diagnostic Evaluation: ECG, Biomarkers, and Imaging

Confirming a myocardial infarction requires a combination of electrical, biochemical, and imaging assessments. The 12-lead electrocardiogram is the cornerstone test performed within ten minutes of arrival at a health facility. It distinguishes between ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI).

Blood tests measure cardiac biomarkers that leak into the bloodstream when myocardial cells break down:

  • Troponin T and I: Highly specific markers that remain elevated for days.
  • CK-MB: Rises and falls faster, useful for detecting re-infarction.

Echocardiography helps visualize wall motion abnormalities and overall pumping efficiency of the left ventricle, guiding ongoing management.

Emergency Medical and Pharmacological Management

Immediate medical management aims to restore blood flow, relieve pain, and limit the extent of myocardial damage. The standard immediate approach follows the acronym MONA: Morphine for pain and anxiety, Oxygen if saturation is low, Nitroglycerin to dilate blood vessels, and Aspirin to prevent further platelet aggregation.

For STEMI patients, emergency reperfusion therapy is paramount. This is achieved either through primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or through fibrinolytic drugs when PCI is unavailable within recommended timeframes. Anticoagulants and beta-blockers are also administered to stabilize the patient's heart rhythm and reduce myocardial workload.

Nursing Care Plan and Continuous Monitoring

Once stabilized, the patient requires meticulous nursing care in a monitored setting. Continuous cardiac telemetry is essential to detect dangerous arrhythmias like ventricular fibrillation early. Nurses must monitor vital signs frequently, paying close attention to blood pressure drops that indicate cardiogenic shock.

Pain management remains a priority; recurring chest pain suggests ongoing ischaemia. Fluid balance must be tracked carefully to prevent fluid overload and pulmonary oedema in a weakened heart. Midwives and nurses also manage patient anxiety, promote physical rest, and educate clients on gradual mobilization before discharge.

Potential Complications and Post-Infarction Recovery

Recovery from a myocardial infarction can be complicated by both acute and chronic issues. Common complications include heart failure, cardiogenic shock, mechanical defects such as papillary muscle rupture, and lethal arrhythmias.

Long-term recovery focuses on cardiac rehabilitation, lifestyle modification, and medication adherence. Patients are counseled on quitting smoking, adopting heart-healthy diets, engaging in supervised exercise programs, and taking prescribed medications like statins and ACE inhibitors. Midwives and community health workers play a key role in supporting patients and their families through lifestyle changes to prevent future cardiac events.
