Angina Pectoris

Understanding Angina Pectoris

Angina pectoris is the clinical term for chest pain or discomfort that occurs when your heart muscle does not get enough oxygen-rich blood. Think of it as a warning alarm from the myocardium—the muscular wall of the heart. It happens when coronary arteries, which supply blood to the heart, become narrowed or blocked due to atherosclerosis. While angina itself is not a heart attack, it is a major warning sign that the heart is under stress and that a more serious cardiac event could happen if underlying issues are ignored. In Nigerian clinical practice, midwives caring for pregnant women with pre-existing heart disease or older adult clients must recognize that chest pain should never be dismissed as mere heartburn or gas.

Pathophysiology and the Oxygen Supply-Demand Mismatch

The heart relies on a constant, steady stream of oxygen to perform its continuous pumping work. Angina occurs when there is an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand.

  • Oxygen Supply: Determined by the patency of coronary arteries, blood pressure, and hemoglobin levels.
  • Oxygen Demand: Driven by heart rate, myocardial contractility, and the workload placed on the ventricles.

When a person exercises, feels stressed, or eats a heavy meal, the heart rate and workload go up. If a coronary artery is narrowed by fatty plaques, it cannot expand to let extra blood through. The heart muscle quickly runs out of oxygen, switches to anaerobic metabolism, and builds up lactic acid. This metabolic shift irritates local nerve fibers, sending pain signals to the brain that the patient feels as chest tightness or pressure.

Recognizing Clinical Manifestations and Triggers

Patients rarely describe angina as a sharp, stabbing pain. Instead, they use words like pressure, heaviness, squeezing, choking, or a tight band around the chest. This discomfort often radiates to the left shoulder, down the inner arm, into the jaw, or between the shoulder blades.

Associated symptoms can include shortness of breath, cool and clammy skin, dizziness, and mild nausea. Common triggers include physical exertion, heavy meals that divert blood flow to the digestive tract, exposure to cold morning air which causes blood vessels to constrict, and emotional stress. In midwifery and general nursing practice, be mindful that women, older adults, and diabetic patients may present with atypical symptoms like pure shortness of breath or fatigue rather than classic chest pain.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Cardiac Testing

Diagnosing angina requires a combination of a thorough clinical history and objective testing. The standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is often performed during an episode to look for ST-segment depression or T-wave inversion, which indicate myocardial ischemia. When the ECG is normal at rest, healthcare providers use a stress test—either on a treadmill or using pharmacological agents like dobutamine—to force the heart to work harder while monitoring electrical activity. For a definitive view of coronary blockages, a coronary angiography is performed, where a radiopaque dye is injected into the arteries during cardiac catheterization to visualize narrowing or obstructions directly.

Pharmacological Management and Emergency Interventions

Medical management focuses on stopping acute attacks, preventing future episodes, and lowering the risk of a heart attack.

  • Sublingual Nitroglycerin: The cornerstone for acute relief. It dilates blood vessels, reducing venous return to the heart and lowering myocardial oxygen demand.
  • Beta-Blockers: Lower heart rate and blood pressure, reducing long-term heart workload.
  • Calcium Channel Blockers: Relax arterial smooth muscle and prevent coronary spasms.
  • Antiplatelet Agents: Low-dose aspirin prevents blood platelets from clumping together over existing plaque.

If a patient takes one sublingual nitroglycerin tablet and the chest pain persists after five minutes, they should take a second tablet and call for emergency help. A third tablet can be taken five minutes later if pain continues, up to a maximum of three doses.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Midwives and nurses play a vital role in supporting clients with ischemic heart disease. The immediate nursing priority during an anginal attack is to stop all activity, have the patient sit or lie down, assess vital signs, and administer prescribed oxygen and nitroglycerin.

Beyond acute episodes, education centers on lifestyle modification. Help the client identify personal triggers, adopt a heart-healthy diet low in saturated fats and sodium, engage in safe, graduated exercise programs, and quit smoking. Emphasize that managing stress and taking daily prescribed medications consistently are essential habits for protecting cardiovascular health and preventing life-threatening complications.
