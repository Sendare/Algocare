Hemodialysis

Introduction to Hemodialysis and Its Role in Renal Replacement

When the kidneys fail completely, the human body can no longer filter waste products, balance electrolytes, or manage fluid volume. Hemodialysis steps in as an artificial replacement for this vital function. During the procedure, blood is pumped out of the patient's body through a specialized circuit, passed through a machine-controlled filter called a dialyzer, and then returned safely to the circulation.

This process relies on two main scientific principles: diffusion, which draws toxins and excess waste products across a semipermeable membrane from a high concentration in the blood to a lower concentration in the dialysate fluid, and ultrafiltration, which removes excess water by applying pressure gradients. Hemodialysis does not cure chronic kidney disease, but it keeps patients alive and reasonably stable until a kidney transplant becomes available, or as a lifelong maintenance therapy.

Vascular Access Preparation: AV Fistulas, Grafts, and Catheters

To clean large volumes of blood efficiently, hemodialysis requires a high-flow vascular access site. Midwives and nurses caring for patients with renal issues must understand these access types to protect them from failure and infection.

  • Arteriovenous (AV) Fistula: Created by surgically connecting an artery directly to a vein, usually in the forearm. It is considered the gold standard because it uses native vessels, lasts the longest, and has the lowest complication rate. It requires several weeks to mature before use.
  • Arteriovenous (AV) Graft: Involves inserting a synthetic tube to bridge an artery and a vein when the patient's native veins are too fragile or small. Grafts mature faster than fistulas but carry a higher risk of clotting and infection.
  • Central Venous Catheter (CVC): A temporary double-lumen tube placed into a large vein like the internal jugular or femoral vein. Used when immediate dialysis is needed, but carries a high infection risk and should not be used for long-term outpatient care.

How the Hemodialysis Machine and Dialyzer Work

The hemodialysis machine acts as the command center, monitoring pressures, blood flow rates, and fluid removal targets. Inside the machine, blood flows through thousands of tiny hollow fibers inside the dialyzer while a specialized cleansing solution called dialysate flows around the outside of these fibers in the opposite direction.

Counter-current flow ensures that the concentration gradient remains high along the entire length of the filter, maximizing the removal of waste products like urea and creatinine. The machine also warms the dialysate to body temperature to prevent patient hypothermia and includes sensitive air detectors and blood leak detectors as critical safety features to protect the patient during treatment.

Pre-Dialysis Assessment and Nursing Responsibilities

Thorough preparation before a hemodialysis session prevents sudden emergencies and ensures the prescription matches the patient's immediate clinical needs. The nurse begins by taking an accurate pre-dialysis weight, which serves as the primary guide for calculating how much fluid must be removed during the run.

Vital signs are measured to establish a baseline, paying close attention to blood pressure since fluid overload can elevate it and ultrafiltration can cause it to drop. The nurse inspects the vascular access site for any signs of infection, redness, or swelling. Finally, the nurse reviews the patient's medication chart, noting which drugs should be held until after dialysis to prevent them from being filtered out of the blood prematurely.

Intra-Dialysis Monitoring and Managing Complications

During the hemodialysis procedure, constant vigilance is required because fluid and electrolytes shift rapidly across compartments. Nurses monitor vital signs every 30 to 60 minutes and watch closely for adverse reactions.

  • Hypotension: The most common complication, caused by rapid fluid removal. It presents with dizziness, yawning, sweating, and nausea. Managed by lowering the head of the bed, stopping fluid removal temporarily, and giving normal saline boluses.
  • Muscle Cramps: Often triggered by rapid electrolyte shifts and ultrafiltration. Treated by slowing the pump and infusing saline.
  • Dialysis Disequilibrium Syndrome: A neurological condition caused by rapid drops in blood urea nitrogen, leading to cerebral edema, headache, confusion, and sometimes seizures in new patients.

Post-Dialysis Care and Patient Education

Once the session ends, the blood remaining in the dialyzer is returned to the patient using a saline rinse, and the vascular access needles are carefully removed. Firm, direct pressure is applied to the puncture sites to achieve hemostasis, taking care never to compress the access so hard that blood flow stops completely.

Post-dialysis weight is measured to confirm that the target fluid removal was achieved. The nurse assesses the patient for bleeding, dizziness, or fatigue before discharge. Patient education focuses heavily on interdialytic fluid restriction, dietary potassium and phosphorus control, and teaching the patient how to care for their fistula or graft at home.
