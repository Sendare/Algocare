Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

Understanding Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Acute Kidney Injury is a sudden decline in renal function occurring over hours or days, leading to the retention of nitrogenous wastes and disruption of fluid and electrolyte balance. In Nigerian tertiary and secondary health facilities, AKI is frequently encountered in both medical and obstetric wards. Unlike chronic kidney disease, AKI is potentially reversible if identified early.

Common triggers in our local environment differ slightly from high-income countries due to tropical disease burdens and healthcare access challenges. They include:

  • Severe volume depletion: Resulting from gastroenteritis, cholera, or severe malaria.
  • Obstetric complications: Septic abortion, postpartum hemorrhage, and eclampsia causing renal hypoperfusion.
  • Nephrotoxic exposure: Traditional herbal remedies, self-medication with unprescribed non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and inappropriate antibiotic use.
  • Sepsis: Community-acquired and hospital-acquired infections leading to systemic inflammatory response.

Pathophysiological Phases: Prerenal, Intrarenal, and Postrenal Causes

Classifying AKI etiology helps guide prompt intervention. The causes are traditionally divided into three main categories:

  1. Prerenal AKI: Caused by decreased blood flow to the kidneys without immediate parenchymal damage. Common examples include severe dehydration, hemorrhage, and septic shock.
  2. Intrarenal AKI: Involves direct damage to the kidney tissue, tubules, or glomeruli. Acute Tubular Necrosis (ATN) resulting from prolonged ischemia or nephrotoxins is the most frequent cause here.
  3. Postrenal AKI: Caused by mechanical obstruction of urine flow anywhere from the renal tubules to the urethral meatus. BPH, severe pelvic tumors, or blocked urethral catheters can trigger this type.

If prerenal insults are corrected early by restoring intravascular volume, kidney function recovers rapidly. Left untreated, prerenal azotemia progresses to intrinsic acute tubular necrosis.

Staging Systems and Diagnostic Criteria: RIFLE and KDIGO

Standardized criteria allow clinicians to grade the severity of AKI and initiate timely management. The KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) criteria are widely used today, combining changes in serum creatinine and urine output.

  • Stage 1: Serum creatinine increase of 1.5 to 1.9 times baseline, or an increase of 0.3 mg/dL or greater, with urine output less than 0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours.
  • Stage 2: Serum creatinine increase of 2.0 to 2.9 times baseline, with urine output less than 0.5 mL/kg/h for 12 hours.
  • Stage 3: Serum creatinine increase of 3.0 times baseline, or increase to 4.0 mg/dL or greater, or initiation of renal replacement therapy, with urine output less than 0.3 mL/kg/h for 24 hours.

Tracking baseline creatinine is critical; however, in emergency admissions where baseline values are unknown, clinicians often estimate a baseline assuming normal kidney function.

Clinical Manifestations and Fluid-Electrolyte Imbalances

As renal clearance fails, waste products and fluid accumulate in the body, triggering systemic symptoms.

  • Uremia: Anorexia, nausea, fatigue, metallic taste, and in severe cases, encephalopathy or pericardial friction rub.
  • Fluid Overload: Peripheral edema, pulmonary congestion presenting with dyspnea and crackles, and elevated jugular venous pressure.
  • Hyperkalemia: The most life-threatening electrolyte shift, causing muscle weakness, cardiac conduction abnormalities, and fatal arrhythmias.
  • Metabolic Acidosis: Compensatory hyperventilation or Kussmaul breathing as the kidneys fail to excrete hydrogen ions and reabsorb bicarbonate.

Urine output may vary. While oliguria (urine output < 400 mL/day) is classic, non-oliguric AKI also occurs and carries a better overall prognosis.

Medical and Pharmacological Management Strategies

Management focuses on identifying the underlying cause, halting further renal damage, and supporting failing organ systems. Fluid management must be carefully tailored; fluid challenges are given for prerenal hypovolemia, whereas fluid restriction and loop diuretics are used for volume overload.

Hyperkalemia requires urgent stepped management:

  1. Stabilize the myocardial membrane using intravenous calcium gluconate.
  2. Shift potassium intracellularly using regular insulin with 50% dextrose, or inhaled beta-2 agonists.
  3. Remove potassium from the body using binding resins or dialysis.

Nephrotoxic drugs such as aminoglycosides and NSAIDs must be stopped immediately or their doses adjusted according to estimated clearance.

Indications for Emergency Dialysis

When conservative medical management fails to control life-threatening complications, renal replacement therapy (RRT) becomes necessary. In settings where continuous renal replacement therapy is scarce, intermittent hemodialysis or acute peritoneal dialysis is utilized.

The classic mnemonic AEIOU summarizes emergency indications:

  • Acidosis: Severe metabolic acidosis (pH < 7.1) refractory to medical treatment.
  • Electrolytes: Severe hyperkalemia unresponsive to medical therapy.
  • Ingestions: Toxicity from dialyzable poisons or overdoses (e.g., lithium, ethylene glycol).
  • Overload: Volume overload causing pulmonary edema unresponsive to diuretics.
  • Uremia: Uremic complications such as encephalopathy, pericarditis, or bleeding diathesis.

Critical Nursing Care and Monitoring

Rigorous nursing surveillance is essential for detecting sudden deterioration and managing fluid balance.

  • Strict Fluid Charting: Measure all intake and output hourly. In oliguric patients, calculate fluid allowances based on measured losses plus an estimate for insensible losses (approximately 400-500 mL/day).
  • Daily Weights: The most sensitive indicator of fluid gain or loss. A sudden weight gain of 1 kg typically equates to roughly 1 liter of retained fluid.
  • Vital Signs: Monitor blood pressure frequently. Hypotension worsens renal ischemia, while hypertension can reflect fluid overload.
  • Aseptic Technique: Care for urinary catheters and vascular access lines with strict infection control to prevent secondary sepsis.

Prevention and Prognosis in Low-Resource Settings

Improving outcomes for AKI patients in resource-constrained environments depends heavily on community education, early identification, and timely referral. Many patients present late to tertiary centers with advanced uremia and multi-organ failure because they first seek care from traditional healers or use unregulated herbal concoctions.

Key preventive measures include:

  • Public health campaigns against the indiscriminate use of herbal remedies and OTC pain relievers.
  • Prompt resuscitation and management of diarrheal diseases, malaria, and obstetric emergencies in primary health clinics.
  • Pre-hydration protocols before exposing patients to contrast media or known nephrotoxic drugs.

While complete renal recovery is possible for many survivors, a subset of patients may progress to chronic kidney disease, highlighting the need for long-term follow-up of kidney function post-AKI.
