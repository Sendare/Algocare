Glomerulonephritis

Understanding Glomerulonephritis and Renal Function

Glomerulonephritis is an inflammation affecting the glomeruli, the tiny filtering units inside the kidneys. Each kidney contains millions of these delicate blood vessel clusters. Their main job is to filter waste products, extra fluid, and electrolytes from the blood while keeping vital substances like proteins and red blood cells inside the circulatory system. When these filters become inflamed due to an immune response, they cannot do their job properly. Protein and blood cells leak through the damaged walls and end up in the urine. Fluid builds up in the body because the kidneys fail to clear it effectively. Understanding this basic filtering failure helps midwives and nurses connect structural damage in the kidneys to the physical signs seen in patients, such as swelling and changes in urine color.

Etiology and Pathophysiological Mechanisms

The most common trigger for acute glomerulonephritis in children and young adults is a preceding group A beta-hemolytic streptococcal infection, such as strep throat or a skin infection. The body creates antibodies to fight the bacteria. Sometimes, these antibodies form immune complexes that travel through the bloodstream and get trapped in the tiny kidney filters. This trapped material causes an immune reaction that recruits white blood cells, triggers inflammation, and harms the glomerular membrane. Other causes include systemic conditions like lupus, viral infections, or vasculitis. Once the immune complexes lodge in the glomeruli, the resulting swelling reduces the overall filtration rate of the kidneys.

Clinical Presentation: Recognizing Signs and Symptoms

Patients with glomerulonephritis present with a classic set of physical findings that reflect fluid overload and impaired renal clearance. Midwives and nurses must look out for the following clinical features:

  • Hematuria: Urine appears smoky, cola-colored, or rust-colored due to the presence of red blood cells.
  • Edema: Swelling starts typically around the eyes in the face, especially in the morning, and can spread to the lower limbs and abdomen.
  • Hypertension: Blood pressure rises because the kidneys cannot excrete sodium and water properly.
  • Oliguria: Urine output drops significantly as the filtration rate slows down.
  • Fatigue: Patients feel unusually tired due to waste buildup and mild anemia.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Confirming glomerulonephritis requires a combination of blood tests, urine analysis, and sometimes imaging or biopsy. Urinalysis reveals microscopic or gross hematuria alongside protein and cellular casts. Blood tests show elevated levels of urea and creatinine, indicating that waste products are building up because filtration is sluggish. Doctors often check anti-streptolysin O titers if a recent streptococcal infection is suspected. Serum complement levels might also be checked, as they frequently drop during active immune-mediated glomerular injury. A renal ultrasound helps rule out other structural abnormalities and confirms that both kidneys are present and normal in size.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Medical treatment focuses on controlling the underlying cause, managing symptoms, and preventing further kidney damage. If a streptococcal infection is still present, a full course of antibiotics like penicillin is prescribed to eradicate the bacteria. Blood pressure management is critical; doctors frequently use antihypertensive medications, such as ACE inhibitors or diuretics, to reduce fluid overload and lower pressure on the remaining healthy nephrons. Dietary modifications are equally important, often involving restrictions on sodium, potassium, and fluid intake to ease the workload on the kidneys while they heal.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Monitoring

Midwives and nurses caring for patients with glomerulonephritis must maintain vigilant monitoring of vital signs and fluid balance. Accurate daily weight measurement is the most reliable indicator of fluid retention or loss; an abrupt weight gain signals worsening edema. Fluid intake and output must be recorded meticulously every shift. Nurses should monitor blood pressure regularly and report sudden spikes immediately. Skin care is also essential because edematous tissues break down easily. Providing clear dietary guidance to the patient and their family ensures adherence to salt and fluid restrictions after discharge.

Potential Complications and Long-term Prognosis

While many children recover completely from acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, complications can arise if the condition is severe or left untreated. Acute kidney injury can develop rapidly, requiring temporary dialysis support. Severe hypertension can lead to hypertensive encephalopathy or heart failure due to extreme fluid overload. In some individuals, the inflammation does not resolve, leading to chronic glomerulonephritis and permanent loss of kidney function over time. Regular follow-up appointments involving urine tests and blood pressure checks are essential to catch any lingering kidney damage early.
