Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)

Understanding Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) in Pregnancy

Diabetic ketoacidosis is a life-threatening medical emergency characterized by uncontrolled hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and increased total body ketone concentration. While more common in type 1 diabetes, it can also occur in type 2 diabetes during periods of severe metabolic stress. Pregnancy is a naturally diabetogenic state. Normal physiological changes include progressive insulin resistance driven by placental hormones like human placental lactogen, cortisol, and progesterone, especially during the second and third trimesters.

In pregnant women, DKA can develop much faster and at lower blood glucose levels compared to non-pregnant individuals, sometimes under 200 mg/dL. This phenomenon, known as euglycemic DKA, poses a unique diagnostic challenge in antenatal and labour wards across Nigeria. Because the maternal and fetal circulations are inextricably linked, maternal acidosis directly compromises uteroplacental perfusion, leading to rapid fetal hypoxia, distress, and potential intrauterine demise if intervention is delayed.

Pathophysiology: Why Insulin Deficiency Triggers Ketosis

Insulin is the primary anabolic hormone that facilitates cellular glucose uptake and suppresses fat breakdown. When insulin is severely deficient, cells cannot access circulating glucose for energy, triggering a false state of starvation despite high blood sugar levels.

The body responds by activating alternative energy pathways:

  • Glycogenolysis: The liver breaks down stored glycogen into glucose, worsening hyperglycemia.
  • Gluconeogenesis: The liver synthesizes new glucose from non-carbohydrate sources, further elevating blood sugar.
  • Lipolysis: Adipose tissue releases large amounts of free fatty acids into the bloodstream.

The liver converts these free fatty acids into acidic ketone bodies, specifically acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate. As these organic acids accumulate, they overwhelm the body's bicarbonate buffering system, resulting in severe high anion gap metabolic acidosis.

Recognizing Early Warning Signs and Clinical Presentation

The clinical presentation of DKA evolves from mild symptoms of uncontrolled hyperglycemia into profound systemic collapse. Midwives must recognize these warning signs early during antenatal visits or emergency admissions.

  • Polyuria and Polydipsia: Excessive urination occurs as high blood glucose spills into the urine, pulling water with it, which leads to intense thirst.
  • Gastrointestinal Distress: Nausea, vomiting, and diffuse abdominal pain often mimic surgical emergencies like acute appendicitis.
  • Respiratory Compensation: Kussmaul breathing, characterized by deep, rapid, and labored respirations, develops as the lungs try to blow off carbon dioxide to reduce acid levels.
  • Acetone Breath: A distinctive, sweet, or fruity odor on the breath caused by exhaled acetone.
  • Neurological Changes: Lethargy, confusion, drowsiness, and in extreme cases, coma resulting from severe dehydration and electrolyte shifts.

Emergency Assessment and Diagnostic Criteria

Immediate laboratory and clinical assessment confirms the diagnosis of DKA and guides urgent resuscitation. Midwives working in emergency obstetric settings must coordinate rapid investigations.

Diagnostic ParameterDKA Threshold Value
Blood GlucoseUsually > 250 mg/dL (can be lower in pregnancy)
Arterial pH< 7.3 (indicating metabolic acidosis)
Serum Bicarbonate< 18 mEq/L
Serum Ketones / Urine KetonesModerate to high positive
Anion GapElevated (> 12 mEq/L)

Bedside capillary blood glucose testing provides a quick initial check, but venous blood gas analysis, serum electrolytes, urea, creatinine, and ketone evaluations are essential to determine the severity of the crisis and establish a baseline for treatment.

Immediate Medical Management and Fluid Resuscitation

Restoring circulating blood volume and tissue perfusion is the absolute first priority in managing DKA. Severe osmotic diuresis leads to profound dehydration and electrolyte depletion.

Initial fluid resuscitation typically begins with isotonic sodium chloride (0.9% normal saline) infused rapidly to restore intravascular volume and improve renal perfusion. Once blood glucose falls to around 200 mg/dL in pregnant patients, intravenous fluids are switched to 5% dextrose in normal saline to prevent sudden maternal hypoglycemia and minimize the risk of fetal distress. Careful monitoring of fluid input and output via an indwelling urinary catheter is vital to prevent fluid overload and pulmonary edema, which can be catastrophic in pregnancy.

Insulin Therapy Protocol and Electrolyte Correction

Insulin therapy halts lipolysis, clears ketone bodies, and gradually lowers blood glucose. Regular human insulin administered via continuous intravenous infusion is the standard approach.

A common pitfall is initiating insulin before adequate fluid resuscitation, which can cause a rapid drop in blood pressure as fluid shifts back into cells. Furthermore, as insulin drives glucose and potassium back into intracellular spaces, serum potassium levels can plummet dangerously. Potassium replacement must be added to intravenous fluids once serum potassium falls below normal thresholds, provided adequate urine output is confirmed. Regular monitoring of electrolytes prevents fatal cardiac arrhythmias.

Fetal Monitoring and Obstetric Considerations

When managing DKA in pregnancy, care must be dual-focused on both the mother and the fetus. Continuous electronic fetal monitoring provides real-time data on fetal well-being during the acute maternal crisis.

Maternal acidosis and dehydration often cause non-reassuring fetal heart rate patterns, such as late decelerations or reduced variability. However, emergency delivery is rarely indicated solely for fetal distress unless the mother fails to stabilize or obstetric complications arise. Resolving the maternal acid-base imbalance and restoring hydration usually restores normal fetal status. Obstetricians and neonatologists must be involved early, as severe DKA increases the risk of preterm labour and intrauterine demise.

Midwifery Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive midwifery care extends far beyond acute stabilization. Once the crisis resolves, the focus shifts to recovery, prevention, and empowerment through education.

  • Vital Signs and Charting: Monitor maternal temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and respiratory rate hourly alongside strict fluid balance charts.
  • Capillary Blood Glucose: Check blood sugar levels every hour while on the sliding scale infusion.
  • Self-Management Education: Teach the patient and her family how to test blood glucose regularly and check urine for ketones during minor illnesses.
  • Sick-Day Rules: Emphasize that insulin must never be stopped during illness, even if oral intake is poor due to nausea.
  • Multidisciplinary Support: Involve dietitians, endocrinologists, and medical social workers to ensure holistic long-term support upon discharge.
