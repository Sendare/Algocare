Cushing's Syndrome

Introduction to Cushing's Syndrome and Adrenal Physiology

Cushing's syndrome occurs when the body experiences prolonged exposure to excess levels of the hormone cortisol. To understand this condition, we must first look at the adrenal glands, two small pyramid-shaped organs resting on top of each kidney. Each adrenal gland consists of an inner medulla and an outer cortex. The cortex is divided into three zones, with the innermost zone, the zona fasciculata, responsible for manufacturing glucocorticoids, primarily cortisol.

Cortisol is vital for survival. It helps the body manage stress, regulates metabolism by increasing blood glucose, controls blood pressure, and reduces inflammation. Under normal physiological conditions, cortisol release is strictly controlled by the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis. The hypothalamus secretes corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which stimulates the anterior pituitary gland to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH then travels through the bloodstream to the adrenal cortex, prompting the release of cortisol. When cortisol levels in the blood rise high enough, they signal the brain to slow down CRH and ACTH production through a negative feedback loop. When this delicate feedback system fails or is overridden by external factors, cortisol production continues unchecked, leading to Cushing's syndrome.

Etiology: Endogenous vs. Exogenous Causes of Cortisol Excess

The causes of Cushing's syndrome are broadly categorized into exogenous and endogenous sources. Exogenous Cushing's syndrome is by far the most common cause seen in clinical practice. It happens when patients take long-term, high-dose glucocorticoid medications—such as prednisone, dexamethasone, or hydrocortisone—to manage chronic inflammatory, autoimmune, or allergic conditions like systemic lupus erythematosus, severe asthma, or rheumatoid arthritis. The body reacts to these external steroids just like natural cortisol.

Endogenous Cushing's syndrome, on the other hand, originates from inside the patient's body due to an overproduction of cortisol by the adrenal glands. This can be driven by a few distinct mechanisms:

  • Cushing's Disease: A benign tumor (adenoma) in the anterior pituitary gland produces too much ACTH, which in turn overstimulates the adrenal glands.
  • Adrenal Tumors: A benign adenoma or a malignant carcinoma directly inside the adrenal cortex pumps out massive amounts of cortisol independently of pituitary ACTH.
  • Ectopic ACTH Syndrome: Non-pituitary tumors, most commonly small cell lung cancer, secrete ACTH from outside the normal regulatory pathway.

Pathophysiology and the Impact of Chronic Hypercortisolemia

Chronic exposure to high cortisol levels alters almost every major metabolic pathway in the human body. Because cortisol is a natural glucocorticoid, it stimulates gluconeogenesis—the creation of glucose from non-carbohydrate sources like proteins—in the liver. This process, combined with peripheral resistance to insulin, frequently leads to hyperglycemia and secondary diabetes mellitus.

Protein synthesis is severely inhibited while protein breakdown increases. This catabolic state strips protein away from supporting structures, causing muscle wasting, especially in the extremities, thin skin, and weakened bones. Concurrently, lipid metabolism is altered through an unclear mechanism that shifts fat deposition away from the limbs and relocates it centrally into the abdomen, upper back, and face.

Furthermore, high levels of cortisol can cross-react with mineralocorticoid receptors in the kidneys. This causes the renal tubules to reabsorb excess sodium and water while excreting potassium and hydrogen ions, paving the way for hypertension, hypokalemia, and metabolic alkalosis. Finally, chronic hypercortisolemia suppresses the immune system by inhibiting white blood cell function and inflammatory mediators, leaving patients highly vulnerable to infections.

Clinical Presentation: Recognizing Signs and Symptoms

The clinical picture of Cushing's syndrome is distinct and progressive, making it recognizable once a clinician connects the physical changes to hormonal excess. Patients often describe a changing body shape and unexplained weight gain.

  • Truncal Obesity: Accumulation of fat around the abdomen and chest while the arms and legs remain relatively thin.
  • Moon Face and Buffalo Hump: Rounding of the facial contours (moon face) and a noticeable fatty pad developing over the cervical spine and upper back (buffalo hump).
  • Dermal Changes: Thin, translucent skin that bruises easily, accompanied by reddish-purple stretch marks called purple striae across the abdomen, hips, and thighs.
  • Musculoskeletal Weakness: Proximal muscle weakness, making it difficult for the patient to rise from a deep chair or climb stairs without assistance.
  • Cardiovascular and Metabolic Signs: Persistent high blood pressure and signs of fluid retention.
  • Psychological Effects: Mood swings, irritability, anxiety, depression, and occasional psychosis.
  • Endocrine Disruptors: Hirsutism (excess facial and body hair growth) and menstrual irregularities in women due to elevated adrenal androgen production.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Workup

Diagnosing Cushing's syndrome involves a careful, multi-step process. The goal is first to confirm that chronic hypercortisolemia exists, and second, to find its exact anatomical source.

  1. Late-Night Salivary Cortisol: Normal cortisol follows a circadian rhythm, peaking in the morning and dropping to very low levels around midnight. Loss of this normal dip is an early indicator of Cushing's syndrome.
  2. 24-Hour Urinary Free Cortisol (UFC): Measures the total amount of free cortisol excreted by the kidneys over a full day. A level three times higher than normal strongly points toward Cushing's syndrome.
  3. Low-Dose Dexamethasone Suppression Test: Patients take a small dose of a synthetic steroid (dexamethasone) late at night, and blood cortisol is measured the next morning. In healthy individuals, the drug suppresses ACTH and lowers cortisol. In Cushing's syndrome, cortisol levels remain high because the feedback mechanism is broken.
  4. Plasma ACTH Measurement: Once excess cortisol is proven, measuring ACTH helps differentiate causes. Low ACTH points to an adrenal tumor, while normal or high ACTH points to a pituitary adenoma or ectopic source.
  5. Imaging Studies: MRI of the pituitary gland and CT scans of the adrenal glands and chest help locate tumors.

Medical and Surgical Management Strategies

Treatment for Cushing's syndrome depends entirely on the underlying cause. The primary goal is to return cortisol levels to normal, reverse physical symptoms, and treat any underlying tumor.

When the cause is an adenoma on the pituitary gland, the treatment of choice is transsphenoidal surgery—removing the tumor through the nasal cavity and sphenoid sinus without opening the skull. For adrenal tumors, surgeons perform a laparoscopic or open adrenalectomy to remove the diseased adrenal gland. If an ectopic tumor cannot be completely removed, or while waiting for surgery, doctors use steroidogenesis inhibitors like ketoconazole, metyrapone, or mitotane to block the enzymes responsible for cortisol synthesis in the adrenal cortex.

Patients with exogenous Cushing's caused by necessary steroid medication require a careful, supervised tapering schedule. Doctors gradually reduce the external dose over weeks or months to allow the patient's own adrenal glands time to wake up and start producing natural cortisol again, preventing dangerous acute adrenal insufficiency.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education for Adrenal Disorders

Nursing care for patients with Cushing's syndrome requires attention to physical safety, infection prevention, and emotional support. Because high cortisol weakens bones and causes muscle wasting, nurses must institute fall precautions and help patients perform safe, gentle physical activity to preserve muscle mass without fracturing fragile bones.

Infection control is paramount. Since cortisol suppresses the immune system, standard hand hygiene and monitoring for subtle signs of infection—such as low-grade fever or mild redness—are critical, as classic signs of inflammation may be masked. Nurses should also monitor fluid balance daily through intake and output records, daily weights, and assessments for peripheral edema or elevated blood pressure.

Patient education must focus on medication adherence, dietary modifications, and psychological coping. Midwives and nurses should teach patients to eat a diet high in protein, calcium, and vitamin D while restricting sodium and simple sugars to manage weight gain, hyperglycemia, and bone loss. Finally, patients who undergo adrenalectomy need thorough education regarding lifelong or temporary hormone replacement therapy and the critical importance of wearing a medical alert bracelet.
