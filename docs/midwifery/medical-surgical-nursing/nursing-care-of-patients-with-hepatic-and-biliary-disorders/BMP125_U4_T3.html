Liver Failure

Understanding Liver Failure and Its Classification

Liver failure happens when large parts of the liver become damaged beyond repair and the organ can no longer keep up with its normal functions. Midwives and nurses in Nigerian clinical settings often encounter this condition as either acute or chronic. Acute liver failure develops rapidly within days or weeks in a person who had no prior liver disease, frequently triggered by viral hepatitis or certain medications like paracetamol overdose. Chronic liver failure, on the other hand, progresses slowly over months or years, usually as the final end-stage result of long-term scarring from conditions like chronic hepatitis B or C, or alcohol-related damage.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The liver is responsible for filtering toxins, producing clotting factors, and regulating metabolism. When toxins, viruses, or continuous inflammation destroy hepatocytes, scar tissue replaces healthy liver cells. This disruption blocks normal blood flow through the organ, causing portal hypertension. In Nigeria, viral hepatitis (especially hepatitis B and C) remains the leading driver of chronic liver destruction, alongside heavy alcohol consumption and exposure to aflatoxins found in improperly stored grains.

Clinical Manifestations and Multi-System Effects

As liver function fails, the entire body suffers from a buildup of toxins and a lack of essential proteins. Patients typically present with characteristic signs:

  • Jaundice: Yellow discoloration of the sclera and skin due to high bilirubin levels.
  • Ascites: Fluid accumulation in the abdominal cavity caused by low albumin and portal hypertension.
  • Coagulopathy: Easy bruising and bleeding because the liver cannot make enough clotting factors.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy: Confusion, personality changes, and tremors caused by ammonia reaching the brain.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Confirming liver failure requires a combination of blood tests, imaging, and clinical assessment. Laboratory investigations look for elevated liver enzymes (ALT and AST), high serum bilirubin, and prolonged clotting times (INR). Serum albumin levels drop significantly because the damaged liver can no longer synthesize this crucial protein. Abdominal ultrasound is often performed to assess liver size, detect scarring, and check for fluid collection in the peritoneal cavity.

Medical Management and Supportive Care

Medical management focuses on stabilizing the patient, protecting remaining liver tissue, and treating complications. Healthcare providers restrict dietary protein temporarily if hepatic encephalopathy is present, to reduce ammonia production by gut bacteria. Diuretics are used cautiously to manage ascites and peripheral edema. Vitamin K injections may be administered to help correct clotting defects, though results vary depending on how severely damaged the liver cells are.

Complications of Liver Failure and Emergency Interventions

Liver failure triggers dangerous, life-threatening complications that require immediate emergency response. Hepatorenal syndrome causes rapid kidney failure secondary to poor liver perfusion. Esophageal varices can rupture suddenly, causing massive, catastrophic upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Midwives and nurses must recognize these emergencies early, secure intravenous access, administer prescribed volume expanders or blood products, and prepare the patient for urgent transfer to a high-dependency unit.

Specialized Nursing Care and Patient Monitoring

Careful, continuous monitoring is vital for saving lives in liver failure wards. Nurses must track strict fluid intake and output charts, measure abdominal girth daily to monitor ascites progression, and perform neurological checks every few hours to catch early signs of worsening encephalopathy. Skin care is paramount because edema and jaundice make tissues fragile and prone to painful pressure sores. Providing clear, gentle explanations to the family helps reduce anxiety during a very critical hospital stay.
