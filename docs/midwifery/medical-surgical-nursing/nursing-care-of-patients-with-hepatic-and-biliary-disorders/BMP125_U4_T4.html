Cholecystitis

Pathophysiology and Causes of Cholecystitis

Cholecystitis is the inflammation of the gallbladder wall, most commonly triggered by a mechanical obstruction in the biliary system. In over ninety percent of cases, a gallstone—medically termed a calculus—gets trapped in the cystic duct. This blockage prevents normal bile drainage, causing bile to accumulate, concentrate, and irritate the mucosal lining of the gallbladder. The trapped bile and subsequent distension lead to increased intraluminal pressure, compromising local blood flow and lymphatic drainage.

While calculous cholecystitis is the primary presentation, a small percentage of patients develop acalculous cholecystitis without gallstones. This severe form typically arises in critically ill individuals due to prolonged fasting, severe trauma, or systemic ischemia, resulting in biliary stasis and secondary bacterial invasion. Left unchecked, both forms can progress from chemical irritation to acute tissue ischemia, necrosis, and bacterial proliferation involving organisms like Escherichia coli and Klebsiella.

Risk Factors and Clinical Presentations

A classic mnemonic used in clinical teaching for gallstone formation is the 'Fair, Fat, Forty, and Fertile' rule, though clinicians must remember that patients of any demographic can present with the condition. Key risk factors include female gender, advancing age, rapid weight loss, high-fat diets, and a family history of biliary disease.

Patients typically present with severe, steady pain in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen. This discomfort often radiates toward the right shoulder or the right scapula due to phrenic nerve irritation. Other hallmark clinical signs include:

  • Murphy's sign: Sharp arrest of inspiration when the examiner palpates the right subcostal area during deep breathing.
  • Nausea and vomiting: Triggered by biliary colic and peritoneal irritation.
  • Low-grade fever: Indicates mild systemic inflammatory response to tissue irritation or early infection.
  • Jaundice: May appear if a stone obstructs the common bile duct alongside the cystic duct.

Diagnostic Investigations and Nursing Assessments

Confirming cholecystitis requires a combination of laboratory blood tests and imaging studies. Midwives and nurses play a vital role in preparing patients for these investigations and monitoring the results for signs of worsening infection or obstruction.

Full Blood CountElevated white blood cell count (leukocytosis)
Liver Function TestsMildly elevated bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and transaminases
Abdominal UltrasoundGallstones, thickened gallbladder wall, and fluid around the organ
HIDA ScanFailure of the tracer to fill the gallbladder, indicating a blocked cystic duct

Alongside diagnostics, ongoing nursing assessments focus on monitoring vital signs for tachycardia and fever, gently palpating the abdomen for changes in tenderness, and tracking fluid intake and output to prevent dehydration.

Medical Management and Pain Relief Strategies

Initial medical management aims to rest the gastrointestinal tract, control infection, and relieve excruciating pain. Most patients are kept on strict bowel rest with nothing by mouth to reduce the release of cholecystokinin, a hormone that stimulates gallbladder contraction. Intravenous fluids are administered to maintain hydration and electrolyte balance.

Pain relief is a primary clinical priority. Clinicians typically prescribe non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or potent analgesics such as opioids (e.g., pethidine or morphine) depending on the severity of the colic. Intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics are also initiated promptly if there is any suspicion of secondary bacterial infection or systemic involvement.

Surgical Interventions and Perioperative Care

Definitive treatment for symptomatic cholecystitis is the surgical removal of the gallbladder, known as a cholecystectomy. Today, the gold standard procedure is laparoscopic cholecystectomy, which utilizes small incisions and a camera for minimal tissue trauma, quicker recovery, and reduced postoperative pain. In complicated cases with severe inflammation, necrosis, or anatomical distortion, the surgeon may convert to an open cholecystectomy with a larger subcostal incision.

Perioperative nursing care involves thorough preoperative counseling, checking baseline lab results, and ensuring informed consent. Postoperatively, nurses monitor the surgical site for bleeding or signs of infection, encourage early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis, manage incisional pain, and monitor bowel sounds until normal diet is safely resumed.

Potential Complications and Emergency Nursing Actions

If acute cholecystitis is left untreated or fails to respond to initial medical therapy, dangerous complications can develop rapidly. Midwives and nurses must remain vigilant for signs of clinical deterioration that require emergency medical or surgical intervention.

  • Gangrene of the gallbladder: Results from prolonged ischemia and tissue death, leading to a high risk of rupture.
  • Gallbladder perforation: Causes localized abscess formation or generalized peritonitis, presenting with rigid, board-like abdomen and shock.
  • Empyema: Accumulation of pus inside the trapped gallbladder, causing high spiking fevers and severe toxicity.
  • Cholangitis or pancreatitis: Occurs if gallstones migrate out of the gallbladder and obstruct the common bile duct or pancreatic duct.

Emergency nursing actions include notifying the medical team immediately, maintaining strict intake and output records, preparing for emergency exploratory surgery, and supporting vital organ perfusion with IV fluids and oxygen therapy.

Patient Education on Diet and Long-Term Recovery

Recovery education focuses heavily on dietary adjustments and lifestyle modifications. Without a gallbladder, bile is no longer stored and concentrated between meals; instead, it trickles continuously into the small intestine. While the liver quickly adapts to this continuous flow, patients may experience mild fat intolerance or loose stools initially.

Midwives and nurses should advise patients to adopt a low-fat diet during the first few weeks following surgery, gradually reintroducing healthy fats as tolerated. Patients must also learn to recognize and report red-flag symptoms such as persistent fever, worsening abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or yellowing of the skin and eyes. Emphasizing regular physical activity and maintaining a healthy weight helps prevent future digestive discomfort and promotes overall wellness.
