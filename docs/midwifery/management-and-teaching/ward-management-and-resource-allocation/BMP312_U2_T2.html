Material Resource Management: Inventory Control, Requisition, Storage, and Equipment Maintenance

Introduction to Ward Material Resource Management

Material resource management in a Nigerian health facility involves planning, acquiring, storing, and safely maintaining the physical tools and consumables required to deliver quality midwifery care. A well-managed ward ensures that delivery trays, sterile packs, IV fluids, and essential drugs are always available when a woman presents in labor. Without systematic oversight, items expire on the shelf, vital equipment breaks down unnoticed, and stockouts compromise emergency obstetric care. Midwives carry a dual clinical and administrative responsibility here, balancing hands-on patient care with the duty of stewardship over public and institutional property.

Inventory Control Techniques

Inventory control keeps track of all items on the ward to prevent waste, theft, and shortages. Midwives utilize specific systematic approaches to manage stock levels effectively:

  • Stock Ledger Cards: A physical or digital running record kept with every item batch, updated immediately upon receipt or usage.
  • FIFO (First-In, First-Out): A stock rotation rule ensuring items received earliest are dispensed first to prevent expiration.
  • Bin Cards: Tags attached directly to storage shelves showing minimum, maximum, and current stock quantities at a glance.
  • Buffer Stock: A reserve quantity maintained specifically to absorb sudden spikes in emergency admissions.

The Requisition Process

Requisition is the formal administrative procedure used to request replacement supplies from the central hospital store or pharmacy. To avoid emergency delays, requests must be timed accurately based on consumption rates and lead times—the duration it takes for an order to be processed and delivered. A proper requisition form requires precise descriptions, quantities, and the signature of the ward manager or senior midwife in charge. Junior staff should understand that inaccurate or inflated requests disrupt hospital-wide distribution and trigger strict administrative queries.

Storage Principles and Environmental Controls

Proper storage preserves the physical and chemical integrity of medical consumables and drugs. The ward storage area must be clean, dry, well-lit, and secure against unauthorized access. Medications requiring cold chain maintenance, such as oxytocin and ergometrine, must be stored within strict temperature ranges in functional refrigerators with daily temperature logs. Heavy items like boxes of IV fluids should be placed on lower pallets rather than high shelves to prevent structural collapse and worker injury. Direct sunlight and high humidity accelerate drug degradation, rendering life-saving uterotonics ineffective during postpartum hemorrhage management.

Equipment Maintenance and Fault Reporting

Medical devices such as fetal dopplers, suction machines, and autoclaves require active upkeep to function during obstetric emergencies. Maintenance is divided into daily cleaning by nursing staff and scheduled servicing by biomedical engineering technicians. When a device malfunctions, the ward manager must submit an immediate formal fault report detailing the serial number and exact nature of the problem. Makeshift repairs using adhesive tape or unauthorized local wiring create severe electrical hazards and violate hospital safety standards.

Auditing, Accountability, and Waste Reduction

Resource auditing involves periodic physical counts of all ward stock to reconcile ledger records with actual items present. Discrepancies point toward pilferage, poor record-keeping, or unrecorded spoilage. Midwives can curb waste by practicing careful portioning, avoiding opening sterile packs before confirming absolute need, and monitoring expiry dates closely. Accountability means every staff member recognizes they are handling public goods financed by taxpayers or patient out-of-pocket spending, making resource stewardship a core professional duty.
