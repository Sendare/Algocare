Simulation

Understanding Simulation-Based Education in Midwifery

Simulation-based education replicates real clinical environments to teach midwifery students practical skills, critical thinking, and teamwork without putting actual patients at risk. In Nigerian midwifery training, where clinical placement sites can sometimes be overcrowded, simulation provides a reliable way to ensure every learner gets hands-on practice. Instead of waiting for a rare obstetric emergency to happen on the ward, students can experience and manage it in a controlled setting. This method bridges the gap between theoretical classroom lectures and actual bedside care, building both competence and confidence.

Types of Simulation Tools

Midwifery educators use a wide variety of tools depending on learning objectives and available resources.

  • Low-fidelity simulators: Basic anatomical models, such as pelvic phantoms or simple perineal repair pads, used for learning isolated mechanical skills like suturing or Leopold's maneuvers.
  • Mid-fidelity simulators: Task trainers that offer more realism or partial interactive features, such as models that bleed or basic electronic fetal monitoring simulators.
  • High-fidelity simulators: Computer-driven, full-body manikins that blink, breathe, bleed, have heart sounds, and can even give birth while responding physiologically to student interventions.
  • Standardized patients: Healthy actors trained to portray pregnant women experiencing specific emotional or clinical scenarios, excellent for practicing communication and history-taking.

Designing a Realistic Simulation Scenario

An effective simulation scenario requires careful planning and clear learning objectives. A poorly designed scenario confuses students and wastes valuable time. When building a case study—such as managing postpartum hemorrhage or shoulder dystocia—the educator must define the clinical prompt, set up the environment with appropriate props, and assign clear roles to the participating students. The complexity of the case should match the current skill level of the learners, ensuring they are challenged without becoming overwhelmed.

Facilitating the Simulation Session

Running a smooth simulation requires establishing a safe psychological contract with students before the session begins. Educators must explicitly state that the simulation room is a safe space for making mistakes and learning. During the active scenario, the facilitator should resist the urge to jump in and rescue struggling students too early; allowing them to work through a minor misstep often sparks deeper problem-solving. Clear briefing sets expectations, manages anxiety, and reminds participants to treat the manikin or actor with professional realism.

The Art of Debriefing

Debriefing is widely considered the most critical phase of simulation-based education. It is the structured conversation that takes place immediately after the scenario, where students reflect on their actions, emotions, and clinical decisions. Using conversational models like the plus-delta or advocacy-inquiry, facilitators help learners uncover why they made certain choices. Good debriefing shifts the focus from what went wrong to how the team can improve patient outcomes in future clinical practice.

Overcoming Challenges in Resource-Limited Settings

Implementing simulation training in Nigerian institutions often comes with hurdles such as erratic power supply, lack of funds for expensive high-tech manikins, and large student cohorts. However, resource limitations should not stop educators from using simulation. Low-cost, locally improvised models—like using fruit or locally sourced fabrics for pelvic and perineal repair models—can teach mechanical skills effectively. Peer-led role-play and faculty creativity often matter far more for effective learning than expensive computerized manikins.
