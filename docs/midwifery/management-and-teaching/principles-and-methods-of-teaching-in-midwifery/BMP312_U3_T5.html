Demonstration

Understanding the Demonstration Method in Midwifery Education

The demonstration method is a core teaching strategy used in midwifery education to bridge the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical clinical application. At its heart, demonstration involves showing students *how* to perform a specific procedure step-by-step while explaining the underlying principles. In a Nigerian school of midwifery, this method is indispensable for teaching psychomotor skills such as setting up a delivery tray, performing abdominal palpation, or administering an intramuscular injection.

Demonstration follows a clear pedagogical rationale often summarized as 'watch one, do one, teach one' or structured learning cycles. Students first observe a skilled practitioner perform the task accurately, note the correct posture, equipment handling, and safety precautions, and then replicate the action themselves. Without this visual and physical bridge, students struggle to translate flat textbook diagrams into live clinical scenarios where precision matters for maternal and neonatal survival.

Preparing for a Clinical Skill Demonstration

A successful clinical demonstration begins long before the students enter the skills laboratory. Proper preparation prevents embarrassing mid-procedure hitches, such as missing instruments or expired supplies, which disrupt learning and compromise professional standards.

  • Equipment Checklist: Gather all necessary tools, materials, and models beforehand. If you are demonstrating catheterization, ensure you have sterile gloves, the correct catheter size, a clean dummy, and appropriate cleansing agents.
  • Environment Setup: Arrange the room so every student has an unobstructed view of the procedure table. Ensure adequate lighting and ventilation.
  • Rehearsal: Practice the procedure mentally or physically beforehand to time the session and smooth out any awkward movements.
  • Learning Objectives: Clearly state what specific competency the students should achieve by the end of the session.

Step-by-Step Execution of the Live Demonstration

Executing a live demonstration requires deliberate pacing, clear narration, and constant engagement with the student audience. Midwifery educators must avoid rushing through complex tasks.

Start by performing the procedure at normal speed once so learners see the complete flow. Then, repeat the procedure slowly, breaking it down into distinct steps. As you work, narrate your actions aloud—explain *what* you are doing and *why* you are doing it. For instance, when demonstrating cord clamping, explain the distance from the newborn's abdomen and the rationale for inspecting the vessels.

Maintain eye contact with your students periodically to gauge their understanding and check if anyone looks confused. Encourage them to ask questions immediately if a step is unclear, rather than waiting until the end when details might be forgotten.

Guiding Student Return Demonstration and Practice

Watching a demonstration is passive; doing is active. The return demonstration phase requires students to perform the learned skill themselves under direct supervision. This is where competence is truly built.

Organize students into small groups to maximize hands-on practice time, especially in resource-constrained settings where models or manikins may be limited. Step back and allow the student to lead, intervening only if they commit an error that threatens safety or sterility. Encourage peer coaching, where observing students gently point out missed steps. Ensure every student gets adequate time to practice the physical movements until muscle memory begins to develop.

Providing Constructive Feedback and Assessment

Assessment during practical sessions should be supportive, structured, and timely. Feedback given weeks after a poor return demonstration loses all its educational value.

Use objective evaluation checklists that list every critical step of the midwifery procedure. This keeps grading fair and transparent, preventing personal bias. When delivering feedback, start by highlighting what the student did well to build confidence. Next, address areas needing improvement with specific, actionable suggestions rather than vague criticism. For example, instead of saying 'your technique was messy,' say 'remember to drop the used needle directly into the safety box immediately after injection to avoid needle-stick injury.'

Common Pitfalls and How to Avoid Them in the Skills Lab

Even experienced educators can fall into traps that reduce the effectiveness of laboratory teaching. Being aware of these pitfalls helps maintain high training standards.

Common PitfallEducational ConsequencePreventive Strategy
Passive CrowdingStudents at the back see nothing and lose focusDivide class into manageable rotations around stations
Rushing the StepsLearners miss critical safety nuancesPace the demo deliberately and invite questions
Lack of EquipmentSimulation feels unrealistic and frustratingInspect and improvise safely before the session
Harsh CriticismStudents grow anxious and fear making mistakesFocus feedback on corrective actions, not personal flaws
