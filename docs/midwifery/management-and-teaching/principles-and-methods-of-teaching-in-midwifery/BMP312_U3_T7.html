Small Group Discussion

Introduction to Small Group Discussion in Midwifery Education

Small group discussion is an interactive teaching method where a facilitator divides a larger cohort of student midwives into smaller units—typically numbering between four and eight participants—to explore specific clinical scenarios, ethical dilemmas, or theoretical concepts together. Unlike a traditional lecture where information flows strictly from the teacher to the passive learner, small group work requires active participation, verbal articulation of thoughts, and collaborative peer learning. In the context of Nigerian midwifery training, where future practitioners must eventually communicate effectively within multidisciplinary healthcare teams, mastering the art of dialogue early in the classroom is invaluable. This method moves away from rote memorization, encouraging student midwives to defend their clinical reasoning out loud, listen critically to alternative viewpoints, and construct professional knowledge collectively under the gentle guidance of an educator.

Pedagogical Rationale and Benefits for Student Midwives

The educational justification for utilizing small group discussions rests on constructivist learning theory, which asserts that learners build new understanding best when they actively engage with material and social contexts. For student midwives navigating complex topics such as postpartum hemorrhage management protocols or maternal autonomy rights, hearing how peers interpret a case study breaks down cognitive barriers.

  • Critical Thinking: Students must analyze clinical data and justify their choices to skeptical peers.
  • Communication Skills: Articulating midwifery concepts out loud builds the confidence needed for real-world patient handovers and ward rounds.
  • Peer Support: Quieter students often find lower-stakes environments less intimidating than answering questions in front of a massive lecture hall.
  • Attitude Shift: Collaborative problem-solving encourages professional humility and respect for different perspectives.

Structuring and Preparing a Small Group Session

A successful small group discussion does not happen by accident; it requires meticulous planning by the midwifery educator. Preparation begins with defining a clear, manageable learning objective, such as evaluating the causes of obstructed labor in a primigravida. The educator must then formulate a stimulating trigger task—be it a written clinical vignette, a prompt question, or a brief illustrative case study.

Physical space matters immensely. Chairs should be arranged in a tight circle or around a table to encourage eye contact among all group members, rather than traditional rows facing a chalkboard. Clear time boundaries must be established right at the beginning, letting students know how many minutes are allocated for brainstorming versus plenary feedback. Finally, distributing preparatory reading materials or clinical guidelines days in advance ensures that students arrive with baseline knowledge ready to debate.

Small Group Discussion Facilitation

The role of the educator shifts dramatically during a small group session; instead of being a traditional sage on the stage, the lecturer becomes a facilitator on the sidelines. Effective facilitation requires balancing active supervision with respectful distance. Walking too closely or hovering over a group can make students self-conscious and shut down conversation.

Instead, the educator should circulate quietly, listening for moments where clinical misconceptions arise or where a group gets completely stuck. Rather than immediately supplying the correct answer when students ask for help, the skilled facilitator uses probing questions: "What physiological sign would you check next?" or "Have you considered the local standing orders for eclampsia?" This scaffolding empowers students to think critically and solve the clinical dilemma on their own terms.

Managing Common Group Dynamics and Challenges

Even with careful preparation, small groups occasionally experience interpersonal friction or dysfunctional participation patterns. A common challenge in midwifery student cohorts is the dominant speaker who monopolizes the conversation, overshadowing quieter colleagues who may have brilliant clinical insights. Conversely, social loafing occurs when a student sits silently, letting others do all the cognitive labor.

Handling these dynamics requires tact and emotional intelligence. To curb a dominant speaker, the facilitator can institute a rule allowing everyone to speak once before anyone takes a second turn, or gently direct a question explicitly to a quieter member: "Amina, what is your view on managing this breech presentation?" Establishing ground rules for respectful communication at the very start of the course helps prevent disruptive behaviors before they become entrenched.

Assessment and Debriefing Strategies

The concluding phase of a small group discussion is just as critical as the activity itself. Without a proper debrief, students may leave the classroom holding onto incorrect clinical assumptions or half-formed ideas. Plenary sessions allow different groups to summarize their findings for the wider class, highlighting diverse approaches to the same midwifery problem.

Assessment in this context is typically formative rather than summative. Educators often evaluate students based on their active engagement, peer respect, and contribution to group problem-solving rather than grading a final product. Self-reflection and peer evaluation forms completed at the end of the session encourage student midwives to take ownership of their professional growth and recognize areas where their teamwork or clinical reasoning can improve.
