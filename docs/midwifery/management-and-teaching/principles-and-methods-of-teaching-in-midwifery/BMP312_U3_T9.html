Written Exams

Introduction to Written Examinations in Midwifery Education

Written examinations remain a cornerstone of evaluation in Nigerian midwifery training institutions, serving to assess both theoretical knowledge and the cognitive frameworks underpinning safe clinical practice. A well-constructed written exam allows educators to gauge a student's grasp of complex obstetric concepts, pharmacological calculations, and public health guidelines without the immediate pressure of the clinical environment. However, written exams alone cannot measure hands-on dexterity or bedside manner; they form one crucial leg of a comprehensive assessment strategy that must be paired with objective structured clinical examinations and continuous clinical monitoring. Midwifery educators must design these tests not merely to penalize errors, but to verify that future practitioners possess the foundational facts required to make life-saving decisions during antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care.

Objective vs. Subjective Question Formats

Midwifery exams typically utilize two broad categories of written questions: objective and subjective. Each format serves a distinct evaluative purpose.

  • Objective items: Include multiple-choice questions, matching, and true-false formats. They allow for wide content sampling across the midwifery curriculum and ensure rapid, bias-free grading, though they can sometimes reward rote memorization.
  • Subjective items: Include essay questions and short-answer problems. These require students to synthesize information, construct coherent clinical arguments, and explain the rationale behind specific midwifery interventions, though they are more time-consuming to grade and prone to marker subjectivity.

Designing Exams and Applying Reliable Grading Rubrics

Creating a fair written examination requires deliberate planning, blueprinting, and structured marking guides. A test blueprint acts as a table of specifications, ensuring that question distribution matches the amount of teaching time dedicated to each topic. When grading subjective papers, standardizing the evaluation process is essential to minimize lecturer bias. Midwifery departments should utilize detailed marking schemes or rubric grids that award specific points for every critical clinical step mentioned in a student's answer. Anonymous grading, where student names are covered during marking, further protects the integrity of the score, ensuring that a student's past academic reputation does not unfairly influence their current grade.

Aligning Test Items with Clinical Competencies

Every question on a midwifery exam should link directly to a specific learning objective or clinical competency mandated by regulatory bodies like the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria. If a syllabus emphasizes active management of the third stage of labor to prevent postpartum hemorrhage, the examination must feature items testing this exact protocol. When test items disconnect from actual clinical demands, students resort to memorizing isolated trivia that bears little relevance to saving mothers' lives in rural primary health centers or tertiary teaching hospitals. Examiners should routinely review questions against course outlines to guarantee that passing the exam truly signifies readiness for safe clinical practice.

Avoiding Common Pitfalls in Question Construction

Poorly phrased questions frustrate students and invalidate test results by measuring reading comprehension rather than actual knowledge of midwifery. Common construction errors include using trick questions, double negatives, ambiguous phrasing, or providing implausible distractors in multiple-choice options. For example, a multiple-choice item should contain only one indisputably correct answer while other options serve as plausible clinical errors commonly made by learners. Educators must also avoid copying textbook sentences verbatim, which encourages mindless memorization rather than true comprehension of obstetric principles.

Ensuring Reliability and Validity in Midwifery Assessments

For an examination to serve its purpose, it must demonstrate both high reliability and high validity. Validity means the test actually measures what it claims to measure—such as competency in managing shoulder dystocia rather than general English proficiency. Reliability refers to consistency; if the same student takes a similar test under identical conditions on two different occasions, or if two different lecturers grade the same paper, the resulting scores should be nearly identical. Midwifery educators enhance reliability and validity through peer review of test questions, pre-testing items on pilot groups, and maintaining strict security standards before examination day.
