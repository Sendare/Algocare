Curriculum Development and Lesson Planning

Educational Principles and Curriculum Foundations in Midwifery

Midwifery education requires a careful blend of foundational educational theory and practical clinical training. A curriculum serves as the master blueprint guiding what student midwives learn, how they learn it, and how their competence is measured. In Nigeria, midwifery curricula must align with national health goals, addressing prevalent challenges like maternal and neonatal mortality through targeted training.

Effective curriculum design rests on clear guiding principles. Educators must balance theoretical classroom instruction with hands-on clinical practice in maternity wards and primary health care centres. When building an educational programme, curriculum planners consider the needs of the learners, the expectations of regulatory bodies like the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN), and the health needs of the communities where graduates will eventually practice.

Designing Competency-Based Midwifery Curricula

Modern midwifery education has shifted away from traditional, time-based memorisation toward competency-based education (CBE). In a competency-based model, students must demonstrate specific, measurable skills and professional behaviours before progressing. It is not enough to simply sit through a certain number of lectures on postpartum haemorrhage; the student must accurately perform active management of the third stage of labour under supervision.

  • Core Competencies: Essential clinical abilities defined by regulatory bodies, such as conducting a normal vaginal delivery or performing neonatal resuscitation.
  • Formative Assessment: Ongoing feedback given during clinical rotations to guide improvement rather than assign a final grade.
  • Summative Assessment: Final evaluations verifying that a student has mastered a required skill or body of knowledge.

Formulating Measurable Learning Objectives

A well-written learning objective tells the educator what to teach and tells the student what is expected of them. Objectives must be specific, realistic, and measurable. Vague statements like 'students will understand labour' make it impossible to test whether learning actually occurred.

When writing objectives, midwifery educators use action verbs drawn from frameworks like Bloom's Taxonomy. For cognitive knowledge, verbs such as *describe*, *explain*, or *calculate* work well. For psychomotor clinical skills, such as performing a sterile vaginal examination, action verbs must be observable—such as *demonstrate*, *measure*, or *perform*.

A strong objective contains three elements: the audience (the student midwife), the observable behaviour (what they will do), and the condition or criteria (how well or under what circumstance they will do it).

Anatomy of an Effective Lesson Plan

A lesson plan is the instructor's roadmap for a single teaching session, whether a one-hour lecture on pre-eclampsia or a clinical demonstration on catheterisation. Without a plan, teaching wanders off course, valuable time is wasted, and crucial information gets left out.

Standard components of a clinical lesson plan include:

  1. Title and Objectives: What specific topic is covered and what students will achieve.
  2. Introduction: A hook to capture attention and link the new topic to previous lessons.
  3. Body / Core Content: The structured sequence of teaching points, key concepts, and explanations.
  4. Teaching Methods: The strategies used, such as group discussion, lecture, or role-play.
  5. Evaluation / Assessment: Questions or practical checks to confirm students grasped the material before dismissal.

Selecting Teaching Methods and Educational Resources

No single teaching method fits every midwifery topic. A lecture works well for introducing the physiology of the placenta to a large hall, but teaching how to repair an episiotomy requires interactive clinical simulation and close supervision.

Educators must choose methods that match the learning domain:

  • Lectures and Seminars: Best for theoretical knowledge, legal frameworks, and maternal health policies.
  • Demonstrations and Simulations: Essential for clinical skills training using pelvic models, anatomical charts, or mannequins before touching actual patients.
  • Case Studies: Excellent for developing clinical reasoning by analyzing real or simulated patient scenarios, such as managing postpartum haemorrhage in a rural primary health centre.

Visual aids, anatomical models, and digital resources enhance retention by appealing to different learning styles.

Evaluating Student Learning and Curriculum Effectiveness

Evaluation closes the educational loop. It determines whether student midwives have acquired the necessary competencies and whether the curriculum itself achieves its intended goals. Evaluation occurs at multiple levels.

At the student level, written examinations assess cognitive retention, while Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCEs) evaluate practical competence through timed stations. At the curriculum level, faculty review student pass rates, clinical logbooks, and feedback from clinical preceptors and graduates.

When evaluation data reveals persistent weaknesses—such as high failure rates in neonatal resuscitation—educators do not simply blame the students. They inspect the curriculum, lesson plans, and teaching methods to identify gaps and implement quality improvements.
