Fayol

Introduction to Henri Fayol and Classical Management

Henri Fayol was a French mining engineer and director whose practical experiences shaped modern business administration. Writing in the early 20th century, he sought to find a systematic way to run large industrial organizations. Before Fayol, management was mostly unstructured and relied heavily on the personal traits of individual leaders. He argued that management was a distinct skill that could be taught, learned, and applied across different settings. His ideas laid the groundwork for the classical school of management, which emphasizes formal structures, clear chains of command, and specialized labor.

The Five Functions of Management

Fayol proposed that all managers perform five core activities to keep an organization running smoothly. These functions provide a structured framework for daily administrative duties.

  • Planning: Examining the future and drawing up a concrete plan of action for the facility or unit.
  • Organizing: Providing the necessary materials, human resources, and structural layout to execute the plan.
  • Commanding: Maintaining activity among personnel, ensuring staff understand their duties, and getting the best possible work from them.
  • Coordinating: Binding together, unifying, and harmonizing all operational activities and efforts.
  • Controlling: Verifying that everything occurs in accordance with established rules, accepted policies, and overall goals.

The 14 Principles of Management

Fayol developed fourteen foundational guidelines to help managers navigate organizational challenges. While rigid application can cause friction, these concepts remain useful benchmarks for institutional governance.

  1. Division of Work: Breaking tasks down so workers specialize and build expertise.
  2. Authority and Responsibility: Giving managers the right to give orders, paired with accountability for results.
  3. Discipline: Ensuring obedience, energy, and outward respect through clear agreements.
  4. Unity of Command: Employees should receive orders from only one direct supervisor.
  5. Unity of Direction: Teams working on similar goals must be directed by one plan and one head.
  6. Subordination of Individual Interest: Organizational goals must take precedence over personal desires.
  7. Remuneration: Fair financial and non-financial compensation for staff work.
  8. Centralization: The degree to which decision-making authority is concentrated at the top.
  9. Scalar Chain: The unbroken line of authority from top management down to the lowest rank.
  10. Order: Materials and people should be in the right place at the right time.
  11. Equity: Kindness and justice demonstrated by managers to inspire loyalty.
  12. Stability of Tenure of Personnel: Low staff turnover promotes efficiency and institutional memory.
  13. Initiative: Allowing all staff to conceive and execute plans with creative freedom.
  14. Esprit de Corps: Promoting team spirit, harmony, and unity among personnel.

Application of Fayol's Principles in Modern Healthcare Settings

Although created for industrial factories a century ago, Fayol's principles translate well into clinical environments like hospitals and primary health centers. The scalar chain is visible in the clear reporting hierarchy from chief nursing directors down to student midwives on clinical rotation. Unity of direction keeps the maternity unit focused on lowering maternal mortality targets without departments working at cross-purposes. Principles of order ensure that delivery kits, resuscitation equipment, and pharmaceuticals are consistently stocked and easily accessible during obstetric emergencies. Fair remuneration and equity build staff morale, reducing burnout among overworked clinical teams.

Critique and Relevance of Fayol's Theory in Midwifery Administration

While Fayol provides a helpful roadmap for institutional organization, modern midwifery administration requires flexibility that strict classical theories sometimes lack. His heavy emphasis on top-down command can discourage creative problem-solving among frontline midwives who best understand patient bedside needs. Healthcare delivery is dynamic, requiring collaborative teamwork rather than rigid adherence to a scalar chain when a patient crashes. Contemporary nurse managers blend Fayol's structural clarity with modern participatory leadership styles, encouraging staff initiative while maintaining safe clinical standards.
