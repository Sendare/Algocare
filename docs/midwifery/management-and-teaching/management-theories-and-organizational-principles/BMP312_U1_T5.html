Functions of Management: Planning, Organizing, Staffing, Directing, Controlling

Introduction to Healthcare Management Functions

Management in midwifery is not just about holding an administrative title; it is the active process of guiding human and material resources to deliver safe care. Whether running a busy antenatal clinic in Lagos or a primary health centre in a rural community, midwives constantly apply management principles. The classic framework breaks management down into distinct yet connected activities: planning, organizing, staffing, directing, and controlling. Understanding these functions helps midwives transition from purely clinical thinkers to effective leaders who can improve maternal and child health outcomes at the facility level.

Planning

Planning is the foundational step where a midwife manager looks ahead and decides what needs to be done, who will do it, and how it will be accomplished. In a maternity unit, this involves setting goals such as reducing postpartum hemorrhage rates or ensuring a 24-hour supply of oxytocin. Effective plans anticipate challenges, like rainy season transport delays or seasonal surges in deliveries, and build contingencies. Without a clear plan, shifts become chaotic and reactive, putting mothers and babies at risk.

Organizing

Once a plan is in place, organizing arranges the physical environment, equipment, and workflow to make that plan happen. In a labor ward, this means arranging delivery beds, sterilizing instruments, and setting up clear pathways for emergency referrals. Organizing establishes a clear chain of command so every healthcare worker knows who reports to whom during a crisis. A well-organized unit reduces wasted time, eliminates confusion, and ensures that life-saving tools are exactly where they are needed.

Staffing

Staffing involves getting, keeping, and developing the right number of qualified personnel to run the health facility. Midwife managers must look at staff strengths, training needs, and burnout levels when creating duty rosters. In Nigerian practice, staffing challenges often mean managing with fewer hands than ideal, making fair scheduling and supportive supervision crucial. Proper staffing ensures that skilled attendants are present round-the-clock for every mother in labor.

Directing

Directing is the day-to-day leadership that guides, inspires, and communicates with staff to keep them motivated and focused on patient care. A nurse-midwife manager directs by setting a good professional example, providing clear instructions during shifts, and listening to team concerns. Good direction builds morale, reduces workplace conflict, and encourages junior staff to speak up if they notice a patient deteriorating.

Controlling

Controlling is the process of monitoring performance, comparing it against established standards, and taking corrective action when things go wrong. In midwifery, this includes reviewing partograph completion rates, auditing maternal mortality cases, or checking infection control practices. If a standard is not being met—such as delayed administration of magnesium sulfate in severe pre-eclampsia—the manager investigates the cause and retrains or reassigns staff as needed. Controlling closes the loop, ensuring quality improvement is continuous.

Interdependence of Management Functions in Midwifery Practice

Management functions do not happen in isolation; they flow into one another in a continuous cycle. A plan cannot succeed without organizing resources and staffing the unit properly. Even the best staff need inspiring direction and vigilant controlling to maintain high standards of care. When one function fails—such as poor staffing leading to exhaustion—planning and directing also break down. Successful midwifery leaders master all five functions to create stable, high-performing maternal health services.
