Concepts of Management, Administration, and Organizational Structures in Healthcare

Introduction to Healthcare Leadership in Midwifery

Midwifery practice extends far beyond direct clinical care. Every midwife in a Nigerian health facility takes on leadership responsibilities, whether running a busy antenatal clinic, supervising junior staff, or coordinating emergency obstetric care. Healthcare leadership involves guiding, influencing, and supporting a team to achieve the best possible health outcomes for mothers and newborns.

In resource-constrained settings, effective leadership means making optimal use of available drugs, equipment, and personnel. Midwives must advocate for their patients while also managing workplace relationships. A good leader fosters teamwork, resolves conflicts fairly, and maintains high morale even during stressful night shifts or emergencies.

Core Concepts of Management

Management is the process of getting things done effectively and efficiently with and through other people. In a healthcare setting, management focuses on execution, coordination, and day-to-day operations. The classical functions of management include:

  1. Planning: Deciding in advance what needs to be done, such as creating staff duty rosters or booking antenatal schedules.
  2. Organizing: Arranging resources, tasks, and personnel to carry out the plan effectively.
  3. Directing: Guiding, supervising, and motivating staff during routine clinical duties.
  4. Controlling: Monitoring performance against set standards and correcting deviations, like checking partograph usage or auditing maternal mortality records.

Core Concepts of Administration

While management deals with day-to-day execution, administration is concerned with high-level decision-making, policy formulation, and setting broad organizational goals. In a hospital or primary health care board, administrators establish the rules, secure funding, and define the overall mission of the institution.

Administration determines *what* needs to be done and *why*, whereas management focuses on *how* and *when* it gets done. Administrators interact with government bodies, community leaders, and boards of directors to ensure the health facility aligns with national health policies and legal frameworks.

Key Differences Between Management and Administration

Students often confuse management and administration because the terms are frequently used interchangeably in everyday hospital language. However, looking at their scope, authority level, and primary functions reveals distinct differences:

FeatureAdministrationManagement
LevelTop-level (Board, Chief Medical Director)Middle and lower-level (Ward Sister, Matron)
Primary RoleDetermining policies and objectivesExecuting plans and running operations
Decision MakingCrucial, broad decisionsRoutine, operational decisions
Key FocusResource generation and governanceDirecting people and workflow

In a typical Nigerian teaching hospital, the board of management and directors handle administration, while the senior nursing officer or chief nursing officer manages the actual ward floor.

Organizational Structures in Healthcare

An organizational structure defines how roles, power, and responsibilities are assigned, controlled, and coordinated, and how information flows between levels of management. In healthcare, structure directly affects how quickly emergency decisions are made for obstetric complications.

  • Bureaucratic (Hierarchical): Features a clear chain of command with tall reporting lines. Common in large federal medical centers where decisions pass through multiple administrative layers.
  • Flat Structure: Has fewer management layers between staff and executives, promoting quicker communication and decentralized decision-making, often seen in primary health care clinics.
  • Matrix Structure: Employees report to multiple managers (such as a clinical supervisor and a program coordinator), which is common in specialized public health programs or research units.

Application in Nigerian Maternity Units

Applying management and administrative principles in Nigerian maternity units is essential for reducing maternal and perinatal mortality. Midwife managers must balance clinical duties with administrative oversight, ensuring that task shifting, emergency referral protocols (such as the three delays model), and commodity management are properly handled.

Challenges such as erratic power supply, stockouts of oxytocinergic drugs, and staff shortages require midwives to demonstrate adaptive leadership. Good unit organization ensures that a pregnant woman presenting with postpartum hemorrhage receives immediate, coordinated care without waiting for bureaucratic clearance from distant hospital authorities.
