Postpartum Depression: Risk Factors, Screening (EPDS), Manifestations and Nursing Care

Understanding Postpartum Depression in the Nigerian Context

Postpartum depression (PPD) is a mood disorder that can develop after childbirth, extending far beyond the temporary emotional vulnerability of the "baby blues." While transient crying spells and mild anxiety typically resolve within two weeks postpartum, PPD persists, interfering with a mother's ability to function and bond with her baby. In Nigeria, cultural expectations heavily influence how mental health is perceived and reported. Mothers face immense societal pressure to appear strong, joyous, and capable, which often masks deep emotional distress.

Stigma remains a major barrier to care. Mental health challenges are frequently misinterpreted as spiritual attacks or personal weakness rather than legitimate medical conditions. Consequently, many women suffer silently, hiding their symptoms from family members and healthcare providers alike. Midwives working in primary healthcare clinics, maternity hospitals, and community health posts are uniquely positioned to bridge this gap. By offering a non-judgmental, empathetic environment, midwives can help normalize maternal mental health struggles and encourage early disclosure.

Underlying Risk Factors and Vulnerabilities

Developing postpartum depression rarely stems from a single cause; rather, it arises from a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and social factors. Hormonal fluctuations, specifically the sharp drop in estrogen and progesterone following placental delivery, trigger chemical shifts in the brain. Beyond biology, specific social stressors prevalent in many Nigerian settings increase vulnerability.

  • History of Mental Health Issues: Women with a personal or family history of depression or anxiety face a significantly higher risk.
  • Marital Conflict and Poor Support: Lack of emotional or financial backing from a partner or extended family leaves new mothers overwhelmed.
  • Financial Strain: Economic hardship and the high cost of childcare create chronic stress during the transition to motherhood.
  • Unplanned or Unwanted Pregnancies: Societal judgment combined with personal ambivalence can complicate emotional attachment.
  • Traumatic Birth Experiences: Emergency procedures, severe postpartum hemorrhage, or loss of the baby elevate psychological vulnerability.

Clinical Manifestations and Presentation

The clinical presentation of postpartum depression extends far beyond persistent sadness. Symptoms manifest across emotional, physical, and behavioral domains, often disrupting daily functioning. Midwives must look beyond routine physical checks during postnatal visits to observe maternal demeanor and behavioral cues.

  • Emotional Signs: Persistent feelings of emptiness, severe mood swings, excessive crying, and a pervasive sense of inadequacy as a mother.
  • Behavioral Changes: Withdrawal from family members, lack of interest in the newborn, or conversely, excessive, anxious preoccupation with the baby's health.
  • Cognitive Difficulties: Trouble concentrating, memory lapses, and indecisiveness.
  • Physical Symptoms: Chronic fatigue that sleep does not cure, severe insomnia, and significant changes in appetite.

Crucially, midwives must remain vigilant for intrusive thoughts of harming the infant or self-harm. While rare, these thoughts represent a psychiatric emergency requiring immediate intervention.

Using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)

Universal screening is the most effective tool for catching postpartum depression early. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) is a simple, ten-question self-report questionnaire specifically validated for use during pregnancy and the postnatal period. It screens for symptoms experienced over the past seven days.

Each question is scored from 0 to 3, yielding a maximum total score of 30. A higher score indicates greater depressive symptoms. While a cut-off score of 12 or 13 typically suggests probable depression, any score indicating thoughts of self-harm—specifically question number 10—requires immediate clinical attention regardless of the total score. Midwives should administer the tool calmly, explaining that it is a routine part of postnatal wellness checks to ensure emotional health is just as strong as physical recovery.

Comprehensive Nursing Care and Support Strategies

Managing postpartum depression requires a compassionate, multidisciplinary approach that addresses both psychological and social needs. Midwives serve as the first line of support, establishing a therapeutic relationship built on active listening and empathy. Mothers need reassurance that they are not failing and that postpartum depression is an illness that can be treated successfully.

Interventions extend beyond the clinic walls. Midwives should involve supportive family members—such as partners, mothers, or sisters—in care planning, educating them on how to share household chores and infant care duties. For moderate to severe cases, timely referral to psychiatric services, clinical psychologists, or specialized counseling is essential. Support groups where mothers share experiences can also diminish feelings of isolation. Regular follow-up visits ensure adherence to treatment plans and monitor the safety and well-being of both mother and child.
