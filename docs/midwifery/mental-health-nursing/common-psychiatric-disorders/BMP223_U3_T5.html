Panic Disorder

Understanding Panic Disorder and Childbearing Women

Panic disorder is a specific type of anxiety condition marked by unexpected and repeated panic attacks. These are sudden surges of intense fear or severe discomfort that reach a peak within minutes. For pregnant women and new mothers in Nigeria, the physiological changes of childbearing can sometimes mimic or trigger these episodes. Midwives must understand that a panic attack is not just ordinary worry; it involves a profound physical reaction that can feel deeply terrifying to the mother, often making her fear she is dying or losing her mind.

Recognizing Acute Panic Attacks vs. General Anxiety

Telling the difference between generalized anxiety and an acute panic attack is vital for proper midwifery assessment. Generalized anxiety involves persistent, excessive worrying about many different aspects of daily life. In contrast, panic disorder features discrete episodes with overwhelming somatic symptoms.

  • Palpitations: A pounding or racing heart that mimics a cardiac event.
  • Shortness of breath: A smothering sensation or feeling of choking.
  • Chest pain: Discomfort in the chest that often brings women rushing to the emergency ward.
  • Dizziness: Lightheadedness or unsteadiness that can lead to fears of fainting.

Screening and Clinical Assessment in Midwifery Practice

Routine antenatal and postnatal booking visits provide ideal opportunities to screen for mental health challenges. Midwives should integrate simple screening questions regarding anxiety and panic into standard history-taking. Asking direct questions about unexplained racing heartbeats, dizziness, or sudden bouts of terror helps break the stigma. When assessing a mother, always rule out organic medical causes first, such as thyroid disorders or severe anemia, which can present with similar autonomic symptoms.

Impact on Pregnancy, Labour, and the Postpartum Period

Untreated panic disorder can significantly influence obstetric outcomes and maternal well-being. Chronic high anxiety may contribute to poor self-care, inadequate nutrition, or missed antenatal appointments. During labour, the intense physical environment can trigger severe panic attacks, sometimes leading to prolonged second-stage labour due to hyperventilation and exhaustion. In the postpartum period, mothers with panic disorder face a higher risk of bonding difficulties and postpartum depression if their anxiety remains unmanaged.

Non-Pharmacological Management and Coping Strategies

First-line management for mild to moderate panic disorder relies heavily on psychological and supportive interventions. Midwives can teach mothers grounded breathing techniques to combat hyperventilation during an acute attack. Psychoeducation is equally powerful; explaining that panic symptoms, though frightening, are not life-threatening helps reduce anticipatory anxiety. Encouraging regular rest, peer support groups, and cognitive behavioral strategies empowers women to regain a sense of control over their bodies.

Pharmacological Considerations and Breastfeeding Safety

When non-pharmacological methods are insufficient, medication may be necessary, especially for severe panic disorder. Midwives must work closely with medical officers and psychiatrists to balance maternal mental health against fetal and neonatal safety. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors are frequently used, but clinicians must weigh potential risks against the benefits of maternal stability. For breastfeeding mothers, drug transfer into breast milk requires careful evaluation, ensuring the chosen treatment supports safe nursing practices.

Multidisciplinary Care and Emergency Support

Effective care for severe panic disorder requires a strong network involving midwives, general practitioners, psychiatrists, and psychiatric nurses. Midwives serve as the vital link who can identify distress early and coordinate referrals. In an acute emergency where a mother experiences overwhelming panic that does not respond to grounding techniques, rapid access to mental health support is crucial. Providing a safe, quiet space during labor or clinic visits can de-escalate crisis situations and ensure the mother feels protected.
