Major Depressive Disorder

Understanding Major Depressive Disorder in Midwifery Practice

Major Depressive Disorder is far more than passing sadness or the normal emotional adjustments of pregnancy. It is a persistent mood disorder characterized by pervasive low mood, loss of interest in daily activities, and significant impairment in daily functioning lasting for at least two weeks. In Nigerian midwifery practice, recognizing this condition requires looking beyond physical complaints like tiredness, which are often brushed aside as normal pregnancy fatigue. Pregnant and postpartum women face massive hormonal shifts, social pressures, and financial stressors that can trigger or worsen depressive episodes. Midwives are often the primary healthcare providers seeing these women during antenatal visits, making them the first line of defense in identifying mental health struggles before they spiral into severe crises. Overlooking mental health during maternity care leaves new mothers vulnerable to dangerous complications that affect both her and her baby.

Diagnostic Criteria and Clinical Presentation

Diagnosing Major Depressive Disorder relies on identifying a specific cluster of emotional and physical symptoms. According to standard diagnostic criteria, a woman must exhibit a depressed mood or a marked loss of pleasure in all or almost all activities, alongside other associated signs.

  • Mood and Interest: Persistent sadness, emptiness, or complete apathy towards hobbies and newborn care.
  • Sleep Disturbances: Insomnia or sleeping excessively, even when the baby is resting.
  • Energy Changes: Severe fatigue, heaviness in the limbs, and a total lack of drive.
  • Cognitive Shifts: Trouble concentrating, poor memory, and extreme difficulty making simple decisions.
  • Appetite Shifts: Significant weight loss or weight gain driven by changes in eating habits.
  • Guilt and Worthlessness: Severe self-blame, feeling like a bad mother, or harboring excessive guilt.

In clinical reality, somatic symptoms like aches, pains, and chronic exhaustion frequently overshadow emotional complaints in many cultural settings, leading to missed diagnoses if the midwife only screens for sadness.

Pathophysiological and Risk Factors in Childbearing Women

The development of depression during the childbearing years stems from a complex mix of biological, psychological, and social factors. Biologically, the abrupt drop in estrogen and progesterone levels immediately following childbirth disrupts neurotransmitter systems regulating mood, particularly serotonin and norepinephrine. Thyroid dysfunction, common in the postpartum period, can also mimic or trigger depressive symptoms.

Beyond biology, several high-risk social and personal vulnerabilities increase susceptibility:

  • A personal or family history of mood disorders.
  • History of physical, emotional, or sexual abuse.
  • Poor social support systems and marital conflict.
  • Unplanned, unwanted, or complicated pregnancies.
  • Financial hardship and socioeconomic stress, which are prominent concerns in many Nigerian communities.

Understanding these triggers helps midwives identify vulnerable women proactively rather than waiting for severe symptoms to manifest.

Screening and Assessment Tools for Antenatal and Postnatal Depression

Routine screening is the most effective way to catch hidden perinatal depression. Midwives should integrate validated screening tools into standard antenatal and postnatal booking and follow-up visits. The most widely accepted and utilized instrument globally is the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale. This self-report questionnaire consists of ten short statements designed to capture how the mother has felt over the past seven days. It explicitly screens for depressive symptoms while minimizing false positives from normal physical discomforts of pregnancy like tiredness. Another useful instrument is the Patient Health Questionnaire, which directly maps onto diagnostic criteria for depression. When a screening tool flags a high score, the midwife must conduct a clinical assessment, explore the context of her symptoms, and specifically ask about thoughts of self-harm or harming the baby. Screening is not a one-time event; a woman can develop depression at any point during pregnancy or the first postpartum year.

Pharmacological and Psychotherapeutic Management Strategies

Managing Major Depressive Disorder requires a balanced approach tailored to the severity of the illness and whether the woman is pregnant or breastfeeding. Mild to moderate depression often responds well to non-pharmacological interventions. Psychological therapies, such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy and structured interpersonal counseling, help women challenge negative thought patterns and adjust to their new maternal roles. Support groups also offer immense relief by reducing feelings of isolation.

For moderate to severe depression, or when therapy alone fails, pharmacological treatment with Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors may be necessary. Midwives do not prescribe these medications independently, but they play a crucial role in collaborating with medical doctors and psychiatrists. Midwives educate mothers on medication safety during lactation, monitor for potential side effects in both mother and baby, and strongly discourage abrupt discontinuation, which can trigger severe withdrawal and relapse.

Impact on Maternal-Infant Bonding and Child Development

Untreated maternal depression ripples outward, profoundly affecting the developing relationship between mother and infant. A depressed mother may struggle to respond sensitively to her baby's cues, often displaying emotional flatness, detachment, or irritability. This disrupted interaction hinders healthy attachment, a vital psychological milestone where the infant learns that the world is safe and responsive.

Infants of depressed mothers frequently show altered behavioral patterns:

  • Reduced eye contact and muted facial expressions.
  • Increased fussiness, inconsolable crying, and sleep disruptions.
  • Delays in cognitive, language, and emotional milestones as they grow.

When a mother is consumed by despair, the delicate dance of mutual gazing, smiling, and talking breaks down. Midwives can support this bond by actively pointing out the baby's positive responses, encouraging skin-to-skin contact, and reassuring mothers that treatment can restore their emotional connection with their child.

Nursing Care, Safety, and Crisis Prevention

The ultimate priority in managing severe Major Depressive Disorder is ensuring the safety of both mother and child. Depression can sometimes escalate into suicidal ideation or, in rare severe cases like postpartum psychosis, thoughts of harming the infant. Midwives must maintain a non-judgmental, empathetic stance that encourages women to speak openly about dark thoughts without fear of stigma or punishment.

Crisis management requires clear protocols. If a woman expresses active suicidal intent or thoughts of harming her baby, the midwife must never leave her unattended. Immediate psychiatric referral, hospital admission, and mobilization of a multidisciplinary team are mandatory. Creating a safe physical environment, involving trusted family members in safety planning, and providing compassionate reassurance form the bedrock of excellent midwifery care during psychiatric emergencies.
