Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT): Indications and Pre/Post-Procedure Nursing Care

Introduction to Electroconvulsive Therapy in Psychiatric Care

Electroconvulsive Therapy, commonly known as ECT, is a safe and highly effective medical treatment used primarily for severe mental health disorders. It involves passing a carefully controlled, brief electrical current through the brain to trigger a controlled seizure. This process alters brain chemistry and neural circuitry, which can rapidly reverse symptoms of debilitating psychiatric illnesses when other treatments have failed.

Clinical Indications for ECT

Midwives and psychiatric nurses encounter patients with diverse mental health needs. ECT is not used for everyday anxiety or mild depression; it is reserved for specific, severe conditions.

  • Severe Major Depression: Especially when accompanied by psychotic features, extreme suicidal intent, or refusal to eat.
  • Catatonia: A state of unresponsiveness, immobility, or purposeless agitation where patients fail to care for their basic physical needs.
  • Bipolar Mania: Acute, treatment-resistant manic episodes that pose an immediate danger to the patient or others.
  • Schizoaffective Disorder: Cases featuring severe affective symptoms combined with psychotic episodes.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Thorough preparation prevents complications during and after the treatment session. The nursing team must execute a strict pre-procedure protocol before the patient is taken to the treatment suite.

  1. NPO Status: Ensure the patient has nothing by mouth for at least six to eight hours to prevent aspiration under general anesthesia.
  2. Baseline Vitals: Measure and record blood pressure, pulse, respiration rate, and oxygen saturation.
  3. Medical Clearance: Review recent laboratory results, electrocardiogram (ECG) reports, and medical histories, paying close attention to cardiovascular and neurological status.
  4. Physical Preparation: Remove dentures, eyeglasses, hairpins, and hearing aids. Ensure the patient voids their bladder immediately before transfer to the suite.

Informed Consent and Psychological Support

Because ECT involves a controversial history and temporary memory side effects, obtaining true informed consent is a legal and ethical necessity. The psychiatrist must explain the procedure, expected benefits, and risks. Nurses reinforce this education by listening to fears, clearing up misconceptions about painful shocks, and involving family members in discussions. Providing a calm, reassuring environment helps ease pre-procedure anxiety for both the patient and their loved ones.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities and Monitoring

During the actual procedure, the nurse works alongside the psychiatrist and anesthetist in the treatment room. The anesthetist administers a short-acting general anesthetic and a muscle relaxant, such as succinylcholine, to prevent severe bone fractures or muscle tears during the seizure.

The nurse assists with applying electrodes, monitoring continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) rhythms, tracking oxygen levels via pulse oximetry, and ensuring airway patency. Once the seizure is induced, the nurse observes the physical seizure activity, timing its duration through a isolated limb technique if a muscle relaxant is used.

Post-Procedure Nursing Care and Immediate Recovery

Immediately following the seizure, the patient is transferred to the recovery area. Nurses manage the airway until spontaneous breathing resumes and supplemental oxygen can be discontinued. Vital signs are checked every fifteen minutes until the patient is fully stable and oriented. Because confusion and disorientation are common upon waking, nurses speak in a calm, reassuring tone, reorienting the patient to time, place, and person as needed.

Managing Common Side Effects and Potential Complications

While generally safe, ECT can produce temporary and occasionally lasting side effects. Memory loss is the most frequent complaint, involving retrograde amnesia for events leading up to treatment and anterograde difficulty forming new memories. Fortunately, cognitive function usually improves within weeks to months after the course of treatment ends. Other immediate side effects include dull headaches, muscle soreness, and mild nausea. Nurses manage these symptoms with simple analgesics, antiemetics, and supportive care.
