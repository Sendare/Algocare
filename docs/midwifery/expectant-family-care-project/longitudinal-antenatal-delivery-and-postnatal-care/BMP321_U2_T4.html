Postnatal and Neonatal Surveillance Visits Up to Six Weeks Postpartum

Introduction to Postnatal and Neonatal Surveillance

The postnatal period, or puerperium, begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six full weeks. This phase is a critical transition window where both the mother and the newborn undergo rapid physiological adaptations. Postnatal and neonatal surveillance refers to the structured, ongoing clinical assessment and supportive care provided during these crucial weeks to prevent, detect, and manage complications that could lead to maternal or infant morbidity and mortality.

In many Nigerian communities, traditional practices emphasize caring for the newborn while the mother's own recovery is sometimes overlooked. Midwives bridge this gap by educating families that maternal health directly impacts infant survival. Surveillance is not a single event but a continuum of care that requires proactive home visits, facility-based check-ups, and clear communication between healthcare providers and the family unit.

Immediate Postpartum Care and the First Vital Contact

The first 24 hours following childbirth carry the highest risk for life-threatening complications, particularly primary postpartum hemorrhage. Immediate surveillance involves continuous monitoring of vital signs, uterine tone, and vaginal bleeding.

  • Vital Signs: Check blood pressure, pulse, and temperature every 15 minutes for the first two hours, then hourly.
  • Uterine Palpation: Ensure the fundus remains firm and contracted to prevent excessive blood loss.
  • Bladder Management: Encourage spontaneous micturition early, as a full bladder displaces the uterus and inhibits effective contractions.
  • Immediate Newborn Care: Dry the baby immediately, initiate skin-to-skin contact, assess APGAR scores, and encourage early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life.

The Routine Surveillance Schedule: Day 3, Day 7, and Week 6

To catch complications early, the World Health Organization and national guidelines recommend a specific schedule of postnatal contacts. Midwives must ensure women and babies are seen at these critical milestones:

  1. Day 3 (48 to 72 hours): Focuses on checking for early signs of jaundice, assessing breastfeeding attachment, evaluating cord stump healing, and checking maternal bleeding.
  2. Day 7 to Day 14: Evaluates ongoing involution, milk production, perineal wound healing, and infant weight gain.
  3. Week 6 Postpartum: The comprehensive final postnatal check. This visit evaluates complete systemic recovery, provides definitive family planning counselling, reviews immunization status, and offers psychological screening.

Assessing Maternal Recovery and Involution of the Uterus

Involution is the physiological process by which the uterus returns to its pre-pregnant size and weight. Midwives assess this through abdominal palpation, noting the height of the fundus daily in the early days, until it becomes a pelvic organ and is no longer palpable abdominally by about two weeks postpartum.

Day 1One centimeter below the umbilicus
Day 5Midway between the umbilicus and symphysis pubis
Day 10Pelvic organ, no longer palpable abdominally

Midwives also inspect lochia—the postpartum uterine discharge. Lochia changes character over time: lochia rubra (red, days 1-4), lochia serosa (pinkish-brown, days 5-9), and lochia alba (whitish-yellow, from day 10 onward). A sudden return to bright red bleeding or a foul odor suggests retained products of conception or infection.

Screening for Postpartum Complications and Danger Signs

Midwives must educate mothers and their family members to recognize critical danger signs that require immediate return to a health facility. Postpartum hemorrhage, puerperal sepsis, thromboembolism, and hypertensive disorders can arise after discharge.

  • Severe headache and visual changes: Indicate postpartum pre-eclampsia.
  • Fever, chills, and foul-smelling lochia: Suggest uterine infection or endometritis.
  • Calf pain, swelling, and redness: Point toward deep vein thrombosis.
  • Heavy vaginal bleeding: Soaking more than two pads per hour for two consecutive hours.

Regular temperature checks and perineal inspections during home and clinic visits help catch these conditions early before they become life-threatening emergencies.

Neonatal Examination, Feeding, and Growth Monitoring

Neonatal surveillance ensures the baby is transitioning smoothly to extrauterine life. The midwife performs a head-to-toe examination at every contact.

Check the umbilical stump for signs of redness, moisture, or foul odor. Inspect the skin for jaundice, rashes, or birthmarks. Assess newborn reflexes like Moro and sucking.

Feeding support is paramount. Exclusive breastfeeding on demand for the first six months is the gold standard. Midwives evaluate latch, positioning, and swallowing to prevent nipple trauma and ensure the baby receives adequate nutrition. Weight checks are vital; newborns normally lose up to 10% of their birth weight in the first week due to fluid shifts, but they should regain their birth weight by day 10 to 14.

Mental Health and Psychological Well-being of the Mother

The hormonal shifts, physical fatigue, and emotional adjustments following childbirth leave many women vulnerable to psychological distress. Midwives must distinguish between normal postpartum experiences and clinical conditions.

  • Baby Blues: Mild mood swings, tearfulness, and anxiety occurring in the first few days, resolving spontaneously within two weeks.
  • Postpartum Depression: Persistent sadness, severe anxiety, feelings of worthlessness, and inability to bond with the baby, requiring professional mental health support.
  • Postpartum Psychosis: A rare, severe psychiatric emergency characterized by hallucinations, delusions, and erratic behavior that requires immediate psychiatric referral.

Asking open-ended, non-judgmental questions about how the mother is coping emotionally is a routine responsibility during every postnatal visit.

Immunization Schedules and Family Planning Counselling

Postnatal visits provide a vital window for preventive health interventions. Midwives educate mothers on infant immunization and discuss future reproductive health.

At birth, babies receive the BCG vaccine against tuberculosis and the oral polio vaccine (OPV zero dose), along with Hepatitis B immunization. Midwives explain the schedule for subsequent routine immunizations at 6, 10, and 14 weeks.

Family planning counselling must begin before hospital discharge and be reinforced during postnatal visits. Ovulation can return before the resumption of menstruation, even in exclusively breastfeeding women. Midwives discuss options ranging from progestin-only pills, injectables, and barrier methods to long-acting reversible contraception, ensuring the mother makes an informed choice.

Documentation, Referral Pathways, and Discharge Planning

Accurate record-keeping ensures continuity of care between home visits, community clinics, and tertiary referral centers. Midwives must document all findings in both the mother's and infant's health records, noting vital signs, feeding status, and any counselling provided.

When complications arise that exceed primary care capabilities, clear referral pathways must be activated promptly. This involves contacting the receiving facility, stabilizing the patient, organizing safe transport, and sending a comprehensive referral note.

Discharge planning from the postnatal surveillance program culminates at the six-week visit. The midwife ensures the mother knows where to access routine child welfare clinics, family planning services, and emergency care whenever needed in the future.
