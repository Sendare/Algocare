Writing the Final Dissertation/Thesis and Oral Defense Before Internal/External Examiners

Structuring and Writing the Final Dissertation or Thesis

Writing up your midwifery research requires a clear, standardized structure that guides the reader logically from your initial question to your final conclusions. Traditionally, a dissertation is divided into five main chapters: Introduction, Literature Review, Methodology, Results, and Discussion/Conclusion.

  • Chapter 1 (Introduction): Sets the background, problem statement, research questions, and the significance of the study to maternal and child health in Nigeria.
  • Chapter 2 (Literature Review): Synthesizes existing global and local studies related to your topic, highlighting gaps your research addresses.
  • Chapter 3 (Methodology): Details your study design, population, sampling method, data collection tools, and data analysis plans.
  • Chapter 4 (Results): Presents your findings objectively using text, tables, and graphs without personal opinions.
  • Chapter 5 (Discussion & Conclusion): Interprets what your findings mean, compares them with previous literature, outlines study limitations, and gives practical recommendations.

Maintain an academic yet accessible writing style. Use the past tense when describing your methodology and results, as these events have already occurred. Avoid overusing long, convoluted sentences; clarity is paramount for examiners wading through dozens of pages.

Preparing Your Presentation Slide Deck

Your oral defense presentation is a concise summary, not a reading of your entire 100-page dissertation. Aim for a lean slide deck—roughly 10 to 15 slides—that you can comfortably present within a strict 15-to-20-minute window.

Clarity beats decoration. Avoid dense paragraphs of text; instead, use bullet points, clear headings, and clean charts or graphs to communicate data at a glance. Stick to a readable font size, and ensure high contrast between your text and background.

  • Slide 1: Title and presenter's name.
  • Slide 2-3: Background and problem statement.
  • Slide 4: Objectives of the study.
  • Slide 5: Brief methodology overview.
  • Slide 6-9: Key findings and data charts.
  • Slide 10-12: Discussion of findings, limitations, and recommendations.
  • Slide 13: Conclusion and acknowledgement.

Practice timing yourself multiple times. Running out of time before reaching your results leaves examiners with a poor impression of your synthesis skills.

Navigating the Oral Defense Process

The oral defense, or viva voce, is your opportunity to prove that you are the absolute master of your research. Dress professionally, arrive early, and bring printed copies of your dissertation along with your presentation notes.

The session typically begins with a brief introduction by the panel chair, followed by your uninterrupted presentation. Once you finish, the internal and external examiners take turns asking questions, challenging your assumptions, and probing your methodology.

Listen carefully to each question without interrupting. Take brief notes as they speak so you do not miss any part of a multi-part question. Address the panel respectfully, maintaining a calm, confident, and professional demeanor, even if a question feels pointed or critical. Remember that examiners are testing your scientific maturity and your ability to defend your professional choices under scrutiny.

Anticipating Questions from Internal and External Examiners

Examiners generally focus on predictable areas of your research. Anticipating their lines of inquiry helps you prepare calm, evidence-based responses. Common areas of scrutiny include:

  • Methodological choices: Why did you choose a cross-sectional design instead of a cohort study? How did you calculate your sample size?
  • Data anomalies: Why did response rates drop in certain health facilities? What accounts for an unexpected statistical outlier?
  • Clinical relevance: How do your findings impact everyday midwifery practice in Nigerian healthcare centers?
  • Limitations: What were the shortcomings of your study, and how might they affect the generalization of your results?

If you genuinely do not know the answer to a question, do not guess or fabricate data. Acknowledge the gap politely, noting that it presents a valuable area for future research.

Handling Critique, Feedback, and Post-Defense Revisions

Receiving heavy critique can feel personal, but examiners' comments are meant to elevate the scholarly standard of your work. After the defense, the panel will deliberate and categorize the outcome: pass without changes, minor revisions, major revisions, or re-examination.

Keep a meticulous record of every correction requested by both internal and external examiners. Create a tracking table listing the examiner's comment, the specific page number, and how and where you addressed it in the revised manuscript.

Work closely with your primary supervisor to implement these changes accurately before submitting the final bound copies. Meeting these final administrative requirements promptly ensures a smooth path toward graduation and licensure endorsement.
