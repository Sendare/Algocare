Ethical Review Board Clearance and Pilot Testing of Instruments

Introduction to Midwifery Research Governance

Midwifery research directly impacts mothers and newborns, making ethical oversight a fundamental requirement before any study begins. Research governance ensures that human participants are treated with dignity, protected from harm, and that their rights are fully respected. In Nigeria, ethical committees evaluate proposals to confirm compliance with national and international health research guidelines. Midwives undertaking research must understand that ethical clearance is not just a bureaucratic hurdle, but a moral safeguard confirming that the study's benefits outweigh any potential risks to vulnerable populations like pregnant women.

Preparing the Protocol and Consent Documents

A well-structured research protocol forms the foundation of a successful ethical review. The application package typically includes the main proposal, data collection tools, recruitment materials, and the informed consent form. The informed consent form must be written in simple, clear language—often translated into local languages like Yoruba, Igbo, or Hausa depending on the study setting—so participants fully understand the study's purpose, risks, and voluntary nature. Midwives must explicitly state that participants can withdraw from the study at any point without losing their regular clinical care.

Navigating the Ethical Review Board Process

Once the application is submitted to an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Health Research Ethics Committee (HREC), it undergoes administrative screening followed by a full board or expedited review. Committee members examine participant vulnerability, recruitment methods, and data privacy safeguards. Common pitfalls that delay approval include vague descriptions of study procedures, missing translation of questionnaires, or inadequate plans for protecting patient data. Midwives should respond promptly and respectfully to any queries or requested modifications raised by the reviewers.

Designing and Preparing Data Collection Tools

Research instruments such as questionnaires, interview guides, and observation checklists must align directly with the study's research questions and objectives.

  • Questionnaires: Structured sets of written questions used to gather quantitative data from a large sample.
  • Interview Guides: Semi-structured prompts used during qualitative sessions to explore deep insights and personal experiences.
  • Observation Checklists: Structured tools allowing researchers to systematically record observed behaviors or clinical practices in real-time.

Items should be concise, avoid leading questions, and maintain cultural sensitivity, especially when addressing sensitive reproductive health topics.

Pilot Testing of Instruments

Before launching full-scale data collection, the research instruments must undergo pilot testing—a small-scale trial run conducted with a group similar to the actual study population. Pilot testing helps identify ambiguous phrasing, confusing layouts, or questions that take too long to answer. In midwifery studies, testing a questionnaire on a small group of postnatal mothers in a nearby clinic can reveal whether terms related to maternal complications are easily understood. It also helps estimate the average time required to complete the tool, ensuring respondents do not experience undue fatigue.

Refining Instruments Based on Pilot Feedback

Feedback gathered during the pilot phase provides actionable insights for refining research tools. If multiple respondents struggle with a specific question or interpret it differently than intended, the researcher must reword or split the item. Additionally, tracking the time taken to complete a questionnaire helps trim redundant items that contribute to respondent fatigue. Once modifications are finalized, the revised instrument ensures higher data reliability and validity, strengthening the overall credibility of the midwifery research project.
