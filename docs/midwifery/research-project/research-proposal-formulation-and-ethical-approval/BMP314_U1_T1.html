Problem Identification, Statement of the Problem, and Formulation of Objectives

Finding a Meaningful Midwifery Research Topic

Midwifery research begins by observing real-world clinical challenges in labor rooms, antenatal clinics, and postnatal wards across Nigeria. A good topic addresses a genuine gap in maternal or neonatal care, rather than just repeating what is already well-known. Midwives often encounter recurring issues like delayed referral of obstructed labor, poor documentation of partographs, or low uptake of postnatal services in rural primary health centers.

When searching for a topic, focus on areas where clinical outcomes can be directly improved. Talk to senior colleagues, review ward audit logs, and examine previous research recommendations. Your clinical curiosity is the starting point for meaningful investigation.

Crafting the Statement of the Problem

The statement of the problem is the core argument of your research proposal. It convinces the reader that a specific issue is serious enough to warrant scientific study. A well-written problem statement moves logically from the broad global or national picture down to the specific local facility where the study will take place.

  1. The Ideal Situation: Describe how maternal and newborn care ought to function according to international or national standards.
  2. The Reality: Present current data showing how the actual situation falls short of the ideal.
  3. The Consequences: Explain the negative impact of this gap on patients, staff, or the health system.
  4. The Knowledge Gap: State clearly what is missing in current practice or literature that your proposed study will uncover.

Writing Clear General and Specific Objectives

Objectives translate your research problem into concrete actions. Every proposal must have one main objective and several specific objectives.

  • General Objective: A broad statement reflecting the overall direction and ultimate goal of the study.
  • Specific Objectives: Detailed, step-by-step milestones that break down the general objective into manageable pieces of investigation.

Use strong, measurable action verbs like determine, assess, evaluate, identify, or compare. Avoid vague verbs like appreciate, understand, or know, because they cannot be easily measured through data collection.

Aligning Objectives with Research Questions and Hypotheses

A common mistake in student proposals is misalignment between the various sections. Your research questions, specific objectives, and hypotheses must mirror each other perfectly. If you have four specific objectives, you should typically have four corresponding research questions.

Proposal SectionPrimary PurposeExample Focus
ObjectiveTo determine the prevalence of anemia among booked ANC clientsAssess exact figures
Research QuestionWhat is the prevalence of anemia among booked ANC clients?Inquire about figures
HypothesisThere is no significant relationship between parity and anemiaTest relationships

When these components align seamlessly, your methodology and data analysis plan write themselves without confusion.

Avoiding Common Pitfalls in Nigerian Midwifery Proposals

Student researchers often struggle with recurring errors that delay ethical approval and proposal defense. Recognizing these traps early saves time and frustration.

  1. Scope Overload: Trying to study too many variables in a single undergraduate project, such as combining maternal nutrition, mental health, and labor outcomes all at once.
  2. Outdated Literature: Relying heavily on references older than ten years without acknowledging recent clinical guidelines.
  3. Local Context Disconnect: Copying problem statements from foreign textbooks without referencing Nigerian maternal mortality indices or local facility realities.
  4. Unmeasurable Objectives: Writing objectives that cannot be evaluated with standard quantitative or qualitative tools.
