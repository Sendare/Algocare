Patient Rights, Confidentiality and Informed Consent

Introduction to Healthcare Ethics in Nigerian Nursing Practice

Midwifery practice in Nigeria is built upon a strong foundation of moral principles and professional codes. Midwives do not just deliver babies; they navigate complex human lives where clinical skill must always walk hand-in-hand with moral integrity. The Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) sets clear codes of professional conduct that guide everyday choices in the labour ward, antenatal clinic, and community health post.

Healthcare ethics provides the framework to decide what is right when faced with difficult choices. For instance, when resources are scarce in a rural primary health centre, ethical principles help staff distribute care fairly. Understanding these rules protects both the patient, who deserves respectful care, and the midwife, who needs legal and professional protection while carrying out her duties.

Core Patient Rights Under National and International Frameworks

Every pregnant woman and newborn infant enters the healthcare system with inherent human rights. National guidelines, alongside international declarations like the Patient's Bill of Rights, guarantee that individuals seeking medical attention retain their dignity, autonomy, and bodily integrity.

  • Right to Information: Patients must receive clear explanations of their diagnosis, treatment options, and expected outcomes in a language they understand.
  • Right to Dignity: Care must be delivered without physical abuse, verbal abuse, or degrading treatment—a persistent challenge in busy public maternity wards.
  • Right to Refuse Treatment: Competent adults have the legal backing to decline any medical intervention, even if staff believe it is best for the pregnancy.

Midwives frequently encounter friction between traditional hospital routines and patient autonomy. Respecting these rights is not optional; it is a fundamental requirement of modern healthcare delivery.

Maintaining Confidentiality and Privacy in Clinical Settings

Confidentiality is the sacred trust between a healthcare provider and a patient. Anything a woman shares during her antenatal booking, labour, or postnatal recovery must remain strictly private. This principle covers spoken words, written case notes, and digital information shared on personal phones.

Breaching confidentiality destroys trust and discourages vulnerable women from seeking help, especially regarding sensitive issues like HIV status or teenage pregnancies. Midwives must guard patient records by keeping charts locked away, avoiding clinical discussions in public corridors or staff canteens, and refusing to disclose patient details to curious family members without explicit permission from the client herself.

The Legal and Ethical Foundations of Informed Consent

Informed consent is far more than a signature scribbled on a hospital form; it is an active, ongoing dialogue between the midwife and the patient. For consent to be legally valid, it must meet three core criteria: voluntariness, capacity, and information.

Voluntariness means the patient agrees without coercion from family members, traditional rulers, or medical staff. Capacity requires that the individual is of legal age and sound mind to understand the choices presented. Information entails disclosing the nature of the procedure, potential risks, expected benefits, and available alternatives. When a midwife rushes a patient through a consent form without explanation, the legal protection of that consent vanishes.

Handling Exceptions, Emergencies, and Incapacity in Consent

Real-world clinical practice frequently presents situations where obtaining standard informed consent is impossible. In life-threatening obstetric emergencies—such as severe postpartum hemorrhage or eclamptic seizures—where an expectant mother is unconscious, the emergency doctrine applies. Under this legal and ethical principle, the law assumes a reasonable person would consent to life-saving treatment.

When a patient lacks capacity due to severe illness, intellectual disability, or age (minors), a legally authorized representative or guardian must step in. However, midwives must remember that emergency overrides apply strictly to immediate threats to life or limb; routine procedures must wait until the patient regains full capacity.

Navigating Ethical Dilemmas and Patient Advocacy

Midwives often stand at crossroads where professional duties, cultural expectations, and hospital policies clash. An ethical dilemma occurs when two or more moral principles compete—such as a mother's cultural wish to discharge early against medical advice versus the midwife's duty to protect the newborn from sepsis.

In these moments, the midwife acts as a patient advocate, protecting the client's rights while balancing medical safety. Effective advocacy requires active listening, cultural sensitivity, and fearless communication with senior medical staff. Midwives must champion safe, respectful maternity care, ensuring that institutional convenience never overrides the fundamental rights of women and children.
