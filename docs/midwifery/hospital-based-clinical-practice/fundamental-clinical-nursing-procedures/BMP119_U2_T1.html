Hand Hygiene Techniques

Introduction to Infection Control and Hand Hygiene

Hand hygiene is universally recognized as the single most critical intervention for preventing healthcare-associated infections. Microorganisms on the hands of healthcare workers are easily transmitted to patients during routine clinical procedures, physical assessments, and basic nursing care. Pathogens generally fall into two distinct categories: transient flora, which are picked up through direct contact with patients or contaminated environmental surfaces and cause most hospital infections, and resident flora, which permanently dwell deep within skin layers and are rarely removed by simple washing. Effective hand hygiene physically removes transient microorganisms and significantly reduces the microbial load on the skin, interrupting the chain of infection before pathogens can colonize vulnerable patients.

The Five Moments of Hand Hygiene

The World Health Organization framework outlines five specific moments when healthcare providers must clean their hands:

  1. Before touching a patient: Clean hands before entering the patient's personal space to protect them from harmful germs carried on your hands.
  2. Before clean or aseptic procedures: Perform hand hygiene immediately before any clean procedure, such as administering medications, dressing wounds, or inserting a urinary catheter, to prevent introducing pathogens into vulnerable body sites.
  3. After body fluid exposure risk: Wash hands right after any activity that risks contact with blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions, or medical waste, removing gloves immediately beforehand.
  4. After touching a patient: Clean hands after leaving the patient's bedside or finishing a physical examination to protect yourself and the environment from the patient's flora.
  5. After touching patient surroundings: Perform hand hygiene after touching inanimate objects near the patient, such as bed rails, bedside tables, infusion pumps, or call buttons, even if you did not touch the patient directly.

Handwashing with Soap and Water

Handwashing with plain or antimicrobial soap and running water remains mandatory in specific clinical scenarios. The complete procedure should take between 40 to 60 seconds.

  • Wetting: Wet hands under clean, running water.
  • Lathering: Apply enough soap to cover all hand surfaces.
  • Friction: Rub hands palm to palm, interlace fingers, clean thumbs, scrub the back of hands, and clean under fingernails.
  • Rinsing: Rinse thoroughly under running water to wash away loosened debris and soap residue.
  • Drying: Dry completely with a single-use paper towel, and use that same towel to turn off the water faucet to avoid recontaminating clean hands.

Alcohol Rubs and Sanitizer Application

Alcohol-based hand rubs containing 60% to 95% alcohol are the gold standard for routine hand antisepsis in clinical settings. They act rapidly to kill most bacteria, fungi, and enveloped viruses. Apply a palmful of product to cover all surfaces completely, then rub your hands together until they are completely dry, which usually takes about 20 to 30 seconds. Do not wipe your hands on a towel or rinse them with water, as drying time is essential for the antimicrobial action to take full effect.

Comparing Handwashing and Alcohol Rubs: When to Use Which

Choosing between soap and water and an alcohol-based rub depends entirely on the clinical context and the type of contamination present.

Clinical ScenarioPreferred MethodReason
Hands are visibly dirty or soiled with blood/body fluidsSoap and WaterMechanical action and water are needed to physically remove heavy soil and organic material.
Caring for a patient with suspected Clostridioides difficileSoap and WaterAlcohol rubs do not destroy bacterial spores produced by certain organisms.
Routine patient care with unsoiled handsAlcohol RubFaster, highly effective against most pathogens, and less drying to skin.
Moving between multiple patients rapidly on the wardAlcohol RubAccessible at the bedside and saves time without compromising safety.

Common Technique Errors and Skin Integrity Maintenance

Frequent hand hygiene can sometimes lead to contact dermatitis, skin dryness, and cracking, which paradoxically increases infection risk by creating entry points for bacteria. To protect skin integrity, apply hospital-approved hand lotions or emollient creams regularly, usually at the end of shifts or during breaks. Avoid washing hands with hot water, as heat strips natural oils faster. Furthermore, healthcare workers must avoid common technique errors such as wearing artificial nails, keeping natural nails long, or wearing wrist jewelry and rings, all of which harbor high concentrations of microorganisms and prevent proper cleaning.
