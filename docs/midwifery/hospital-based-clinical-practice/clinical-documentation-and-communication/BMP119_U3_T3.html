Patient Admission, Inventory Taking and Discharge Documentation

Introduction to Ward Routines and Midwifery Documentation

Accurate record keeping forms the backbone of safe midwifery practice in Nigerian healthcare facilities. Every entry in a client's case note provides a chronological trail of care, ensuring continuity between shifts and among different healthcare providers. Midwives manage busy wards where verbal handovers alone cannot be trusted to capture complex clinical details.

Standard documentation requires clarity, legibility, and promptness. Writing must occur as close to the time of care as possible to prevent memory lapses. In public and private hospitals alike, the case note serves as both a clinical guide and a legal document. When documentation is missing or vague, the legal principle stands that the care was never given.

The Patient Admission Process

Admitting a pregnant woman or a new mother into the ward involves more than just assigning a bed. The process begins with a warm, professional greeting that helps reduce anxiety in an unfamiliar hospital environment. Midwives must collect a comprehensive baseline assessment, including vital signs, obstetric history, and any current complaints.

  1. Reception: Welcome the client and her relatives courteously, establishing a trusting relationship from the onset.
  2. Identification: Verify the client's identity using hospital bracelets or direct questioning to prevent errors.
  3. Baseline Assessment: Record temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure, and fetal heart rate where applicable.
  4. Documentation: Open the admission sheet, noting the exact time and date of arrival along with the mode of admission.

Inventory Taking of Personal and Ward Belongings

Inventory management during admission protects both the client and the hospital. Pregnant women often arrive with valuables, wrappers, jewelry, and personal medications. Midwives must conduct a joint inventory of these items in the presence of the client or her relative.

Unrecorded items frequently lead to avoidable disputes and accusations of theft if belongings go missing during labor or surgery. Every listed item must be clearly documented in the admission register or personal property form. Both the midwife and the client should sign the inventory list to confirm its accuracy. Ward equipment inventory, such as blood pressure cuffs and thermometers, follows a similar routine during shift changeovers to ensure tools are accounted for and functional.

Discharge Documentation and Client Education

Discharge marks the formal transition of a mother and her baby from hospital care back to the community. Thorough discharge documentation ensures that the next level of care provider, such as a primary health care clinic, understands the hospital course and ongoing needs.

  • Discharge Summary: A concise report detailing the diagnosis, treatment given, procedures performed, and maternal-neonatal condition upon leaving.
  • Medication Instructions: Clear written guidance on postnatal drugs, including analgesics and hematinics.
  • Danger Signs: Education on warning signs for both mother (like heavy bleeding or fever) and baby (like poor feeding or jaundice).
  • Follow-up Appointments: Specific dates given for postnatal checks and infant immunization.

Legal and Ethical Responsibilities in Record Keeping

Midwives hold legal accountability for every word they write in a client's medical record. In courts of law, documentation stands as primary evidence of whether standard care was delivered. Falsifying records, back-dating entries, or using correction fluid constitutes professional misconduct.

Errors in documentation must never be erased or scribbled out completely. Instead, a single neat line should be drawn through the mistake, allowing the original text to remain readable, followed by the correct entry, date, time, and signature. Maintaining patient confidentiality is an ethical duty; case notes must never be left exposed where unauthorized persons can read them. Respecting privacy builds trust between the client and the midwifery profession.
