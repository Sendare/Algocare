Definitions

Introduction to Pharmacology in Midwifery Practice

Midwives in Nigeria routinely administer and manage various medications, ranging from oxytocics to prevent postpartum hemorrhage to anti-malarials and routine hematics. Pharmacology is the foundational science that studies how chemical substances interact with living systems. In maternal and child health, understanding this science is not optional; it is a core competency for safe clinical decision-making. Every drug given to a pregnant or breastfeeding woman crosses biological barriers to some degree, potentially affecting both mother and child. Safe administration requires knowing why a drug is given, how it works, and what adverse reactions to anticipate.

Core Branches of Pharmacology

To master drug therapy, pharmacology is divided into specific branches that examine different phases of a drug's journey and effects.

  • Pharmacodynamics: What the drug does to the body, including its cellular and physiological mechanisms.
  • Pharmacokinetics: What the body does to the drug, tracking its movement through absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion.
  • Toxicology: The study of adverse effects, poisons, and drug safety limits.
  • Pharmacotherapeutics: The clinical use of drugs to prevent and treat diseases during conditions like pregnancy and labor.

Pharmacodynamics: Drug Action and Receptors

Drugs rarely create new functions in the body; instead, they modify existing physiological processes. Most medications achieve this by binding to specific protein structures called receptors located on cell membranes or inside cells.

  • Agonists: Drugs that bind to a receptor and trigger a biological response, mimicking natural body substances. For example, oxytocin binds to uterine receptors to stimulate contractions.
  • Antagonists: Drugs that bind to receptors but block them, preventing natural substances or other drugs from activating them.

Pharmacokinetics: The Four Key Processes

Once a drug enters the body, it undergoes four distinct phases collectively known by the acronym ADME.

  1. Absorption: How the drug enters the bloodstream from its site of administration, whether oral, intramuscular, or intravenous.
  2. Distribution: How the drug travels through the bloodstream to reach body tissues and target organs.
  3. Metabolism: How the body chemically alters the drug, primarily taking place in the liver.
  4. Excretion: How the body eliminates the drug and its byproducts, mainly through the kidneys into the urine.

Therapeutics and Clinical Safety in Pregnancy

Pharmacotherapeutics applies pharmacological principles to treat and prevent diseases. In midwifery, this requires special caution because physiological changes in pregnancy—such as increased blood volume, altered liver enzyme activity, and faster kidney filtration—drastically change how drugs behave. Midwives must always weigh the therapeutic benefits of a medication against potential risks to the developing fetus, ensuring that every prescription or administered dose promotes safe maternal-foetal outcomes.
