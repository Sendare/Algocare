Drug Naming

Introduction to Pharmacology and Medication Nomenclature

Pharmacology is the study of how drugs interact with living systems. To communicate clearly about these potent substances, healthcare professionals rely on a standardized system of medication nomenclature. Every drug has multiple names, which can easily confuse a student midwife during clinical rotations in busy Nigerian hospitals. Understanding why drugs carry different names helps prevent administration mistakes and ensures safe maternal and neonatal care.

Chemical Names

The chemical name describes the exact atomic and molecular structure of a drug using complex organic chemistry rules. For example, acetylsalicylic acid is the chemical name for aspirin. While crucial for chemists and researchers, these names are far too long and complicated for everyday clinical use in a maternity ward. Midwives rarely encounter chemical names on patient charts or medication bottles.

Generic Names

The generic name—also known as the non-proprietary name—is the official, unique name assigned to a drug before it receives approval for public market sale. National and international bodies, such as the World Health Organization, assign these names. Generic names often feature specific stems or suffixes that hint at the drug class. For instance, drugs ending in "-caine" are local anesthetics, while those ending in "-lol" are beta-blockers. Learning these common stems helps midwives quickly identify an unfamiliar drug's functional family.

Proprietary (Brand) Names

The proprietary name, commonly called the brand name or trade name, is chosen by the pharmaceutical company that manufactures and markets the drug. These names are protected by trademark law, meaning other companies cannot use them. Brand names are often short, catchy, and capitalized on prescription forms. A single generic drug—such as paracetamol—may have dozens of different brand names depending on which laboratory produced the tablets.

Comparing Drug Naming Systems in Clinical Practice

Navigating both generic and brand names is a daily reality in Nigerian healthcare facilities. Midwives must understand how these systems compare during prescription review, drug dispensing, and patient education.

Relying solely on brand names can lead to confusion if a hospital switches its pharmaceutical supplier and stocks a familiar drug under an unfamiliar commercial title.

Preventing Medication Errors Related to Drug Names

Many medication errors happen because different drugs have look-alike or sound-alike names (LASA). For example, hydralazine and hydroxyzine sound very similar when spoken quickly in a noisy antenatal ward, yet they have entirely different uses. To prevent these dangerous mix-ups, hospitals use tall-man lettering (like chlorproMAZINE and chlorproPAMIDE) to highlight differences. Midwives must carefully verify drug orders, check packaging twice, and never hesitate to clarify an ambiguous prescription with a pharmacist.
