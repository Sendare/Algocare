Factors Modifying Drug Action

Introduction to Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics Variability

Pharmacology teaches us how drugs work in the human body, but in clinical midwifery practice, two patients rarely react identically to the exact same dose of a medication like oxytocin or magnesium sulfate. Pharmacokinetics refers to what the body does to the drug—encompassing absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. Pharmacodynamics describes what the drug does to the body, including receptor binding and cellular response. Variability occurs because physiological processes differ widely across human populations. A drug that achieves a therapeutic plasma level in a healthy postpartum woman might cause toxicity in a woman with impaired renal function. Understanding these underlying differences prevents medication errors and ensures safer obstetric care in Nigerian clinical settings.

Physiological Changes Across the Life Span

Age significantly alters how drugs behave in the body.

  • Neonates and Infants: Possess immature liver enzyme systems and reduced glomerular filtration rates, making them highly vulnerable to drug accumulation and toxicity.
  • Pregnant Women: Experience increased plasma volume, higher cardiac output, and altered renal clearance, which often necessitates higher doses of renally cleared medications.
  • The Elderly: Show a progressive decline in hepatic blood flow, renal function, and total body water, increasing the risk of adverse drug reactions from standard adult doses.

Body Weight and Composition in Drug Dosage

Body size directly influences drug distribution. Standard adult doses assume an average weight of around 70 kilograms, but actual patient weights vary widely. Obese patients store lipophilic drugs in adipose tissue, which can prolong drug action and delay elimination. Conversely, malnourished patients or those with severe muscle wasting may have altered plasma protein binding. Midwives must calculate weight-based dosages carefully—especially for neonates or when administering potent drugs where a small miscalculation leads to severe overdose.

Genetic Polymorphisms and Metabolic Variations

Inherited genetic differences influence how rapidly patients metabolize drugs. Liver enzymes, particularly the cytochrome P450 system, show genetic variations across different human populations. Some individuals are extensive metabolizers, processing drugs normally, while others are poor metabolizers who break medications down so slowly that standard doses accumulate dangerously. Conversely, ultra-rapid metabolizers clear drugs before they can exert a therapeutic effect. While routine genetic testing is rare in many routine Nigerian primary health clinics, midwives should remain alert to unexpected drug responses or treatment failures.

Impact of Underlying Pathology on Drug Clearance

Disease states alter pharmacokinetic pathways dramatically. Renal disease impairs the excretion of water-soluble drugs and metabolites, causing dangerous buildup if doses are not reduced. Similarly, hepatic conditions like viral hepatitis or cirrhosis compromise the liver's ability to synthesize plasma proteins and metabolize xenobiotics. Cardiovascular disorders reduce perfusion to vital organs, slowing both the absorption of intramuscular injections and the delivery of drugs to their target receptors. Midwives caring for women with pre-eclampsia or severe systemic infections must anticipate these altered clearance rates.

Environmental, Dietary, and Extrinsic Influences

External factors outside the internal physiology also shape drug responses. Dietary components can interact directly with medications; for instance, traditional herbal preparations and local dietary items can inhibit or induce hepatic enzymes. Co-administration of multiple drugs leads to drug-drug interactions, where one medication alters the absorption, protein binding, or excretion of another. Midwives should routinely ask antenatal and postnatal clients about herbal remedies, dietary supplements, and over-the-counter medications to prevent unexpected adverse outcomes.
