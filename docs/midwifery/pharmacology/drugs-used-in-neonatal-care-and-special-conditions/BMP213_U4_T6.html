Antiretroviral Drugs for PMTCT (Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission of HIV)

Understanding PMTCT in Nigerian Midwifery Practice

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission, commonly known as PMTCT, is a core component of maternal and child health services across Nigeria. Without medical intervention, the risk of passing the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) from a mother to her baby during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding ranges between twenty and forty-five percent. With timely and effective antiretroviral interventions, this transmission rate can drop below five percent.

Midwives often serve as the first point of contact for pregnant women attending antenatal care. Routine opt-out HIV counseling and testing is offered to every woman at her first booking visit. Early identification allows for immediate commencement of combination antiretroviral therapy (ART), which protects the mother's health while shielding the unborn child from vertical transmission.

Principles of Antiretroviral Therapy during Pregnancy

The fundamental principle of managing HIV-positive pregnant women in Nigeria is the immediate and lifelong continuation of combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), regardless of the woman's CD4 count or clinical stage. This strategy, known globally as Option B+, simplifies clinical guidelines and ensures continuous maternal health.

  • TDF/3TC/DTG Regimen: The preferred first-line fixed-dose combination consists of Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), Lamivudine (3TC), and Dolutegravir (DTG). This regimen is highly effective, well-tolerated, and has a high genetic barrier to viral resistance.
  • Viral Suppression: The ultimate therapeutic goal is achieving and maintaining an undetectable viral load, which nearly eliminates the risk of intrapartum transmission.

Maternal Prophylaxis and Treatment Continuity

Maintaining consistent adherence to antiretroviral drugs is the single most important factor in keeping maternal viral loads suppressed. Midwives must actively assess adherence at every antenatal clinic visit and provide continuous counseling on the benefits of uninterrupted treatment.

Pregnant women often experience physiological challenges such as nausea and vomiting, particularly during the first trimester, which can interfere with drug intake. Midwives should offer practical coping strategies, such as taking medications with light snacks or changing the timing of the dose, rather than advising the patient to stop treatment. Furthermore, ensuring an uninterrupted supply of drugs at the facility prevents treatment interruptions that could lead to viral rebound and drug resistance.

Neonatal Prophylaxis Regimens

Every newborn exposed to HIV in utero or during labor requires post-exposure prophylaxis to neutralize any virus that may have crossed the placental barrier. The choice of the neonatal regimen depends primarily on the mother's viral load status and how long she has been on effective ART before delivery.

  • Low-Risk Infants: Babies born to mothers who achieved viral suppression for at least four weeks prior to delivery receive Syrup Nevirapine (NVP) daily for the first six weeks of life.
  • High-Risk Infants: Babies born to mothers with unsuppressed viral loads, late diagnosis, or new infections during breastfeeding receive Syrup Zidovudine (ZDV) and Nevirapine combined for six weeks, sometimes accompanied by additional expert consultations.

Administering these syrups correctly and promptly after birth is a critical nursing and midwifery duty in the labor ward.

Managing Infant Feeding and ART Exposure

Infant feeding counseling for HIV-positive mothers requires a careful balance between providing optimal nutrition and preventing postnatal transmission through breast milk. National guidelines in Nigeria promote exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life, provided the mother is virally suppressed and adherent to her ART.

Breast milk contains antiretroviral drugs in small quantities when the mother takes her medication, which provides additional protection to the nursing infant. Midwives must educate mothers never to practice mixed feeding—giving breast milk alongside water, formula, or other foods before six months—because mixed feeding damages the infant's gut lining and significantly increases the risk of HIV transmission.

Midwife's Role in Adherence Support and Follow-Up

The responsibilities of a midwife extend far beyond the delivery room. Long-term success in PMTCT relies heavily on structured tracking, psychosocial support, and scheduled infant virological testing.

Midwives coordinate Early Infant Diagnosis (EID) using Dried Blood Spot (DBS) polymerase chain reaction testing at six weeks of age. Retention in care requires compassionate counseling, addressing stigma, and involving male partners where possible. Facility peer support groups led by mentor mothers also play an invaluable role in encouraging women to keep clinic appointments and collect drug refills on schedule.
