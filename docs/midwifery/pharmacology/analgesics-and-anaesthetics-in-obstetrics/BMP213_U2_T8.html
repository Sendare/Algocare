Epidural and Spinal Anaesthesia in Obstetrics: Midwifery Monitoring and Side Effect Management

Introduction to Regional Anaesthesia in Obstetrics

Regional anaesthesia provides pain relief during labour and childbirth by blocking nerve signals from the lower spinal segments. Unlike general anaesthesia, it allows the mother to remain awake and participate actively in the birth process. Epidural and spinal blocks are the two primary techniques used in obstetric practice across Nigeria. Midwives play a central role in educating women, preparing them for the procedure, and recognizing the baseline physiological status of both mother and foetus before any local anaesthetic is administered.

Key Differences Between Epidural and Spinal Blocks

Understanding the structural differences between an epidural and a spinal block helps midwives anticipate their onset times and clinical uses. An epidural involves placing a fine catheter into the epidural space, allowing continuous medication infusion for long labours or a Caesarean section top-up. A spinal block involves a single injection directly into the cerebrospinal fluid, providing dense, rapid-onset anaesthesia ideal for planned surgeries.

  • Epidural block: Slower onset, uses a catheter for continuous dosing, flexible for normal labour pain relief.
  • Spinal block: Immediate onset, single-shot injection, commonly used for scheduled Caesarean sections.

Essential Midwifery Monitoring Protocols

Rigorous monitoring is non-negotiable following the administration of regional anaesthesia. Midwives must track maternal blood pressure every three to five minutes for the first fifteen minutes after insertion, and regularly thereafter, because sympathetic blockade frequently triggers sudden hypotension. Foetal heart rate monitoring is equally critical to detect uteroplacental hypoperfusion early. Additionally, midwives assess the sensory block level using cold touch or pinprick tests to ensure the anaesthesia is not creeping too high toward the respiratory muscles.

Managing Common Side Effects

Regional anaesthesia brings predictable physiological side effects that demand prompt midwifery intervention. Maternal hypotension is managed with intravenous fluid boluses and vasopressins like ephedrine as ordered. Pruritus, often linked to the use of intrathecal opioids, responds well to prescribed antihistamines or low-dose opioid antagonists. Urinary retention is another frequent consequence due to temporary paralysis of the bladder nerves, necessitating regular bladder palpation and catheterisation protocols when the patient cannot void spontaneously.

Recognizing and Responding to Obstetric Emergencies

While regional anaesthesia is generally safe, severe complications can arise unexpectedly. Total spinal anaesthesia occurs when local anaesthetic tracks too high up the spinal column, causing profound respiratory paralysis and unconsciousness. Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) happens if medication is accidentally injected directly into a blood vessel, leading to seizures, metallic taste, and cardiac arrest. Midwives must immediately call for senior medical help, ensure a patent airway, administer high-flow oxygen, and prepare emergency resuscitation equipment.

Post-Procedure Recovery and Maternal Support

As regional anaesthesia wears off, comprehensive postpartum care remains vital. Midwives monitor motor function recovery, ensuring lower limb strength returns fully before encouraging unassisted mobilization to prevent falls. Post-dural puncture headache, caused by cerebrospinal fluid leakage after a spinal needle puncture, can be managed with hydration, simple analgesics, and in severe cases, an epidural blood patch. Throughout recovery, offering emotional reassurance and assisting with early skin-to-skin contact helps promote successful bonding and breastfeeding.
