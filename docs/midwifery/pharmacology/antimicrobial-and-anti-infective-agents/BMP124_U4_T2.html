Penicillins

Overview of Penicillins in Midwifery Practice

Penicillins are among the oldest and most vital classes of antibiotics used in maternity care across Nigeria. Derived from Penicillium fungi, these beta-lactam agents play a frontline role in treating and preventing bacterial infections during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period. Midwives frequently encounter penicillins when managing conditions such as urinary tract infections, chorioamnionitis, and postpartum wound infections following a caesarean section or perineal tear repair. Understanding their properties ensures that maternal infections are managed safely without harming the developing foetus.

Mechanism of Action and Bacterial Target

Penicillins are bactericidal agents, meaning they actively kill bacteria rather than merely stopping their multiplication. Their primary target is the bacterial cell wall.

  • Peptidoglycan Synthesis: Bacteria require a strong mesh-like cell wall made of peptidoglycan to survive osmotic pressure inside the human body.
  • Penicillin-Binding Proteins (PBPs): Penicillins bind to specific enzymes on the bacterial cell membrane, halting the cross-linking of peptidoglycan strands.
  • Cell Lysis: With a weakened wall, water enters the bacterium freely, causing it to swell and burst.

Human cells do not have cell walls made of peptidoglycan, which is why penicillins are selectively toxic to bacteria while sparing maternal and foetal tissues.

Classification and Key Subgroups of Penicillins

The penicillin family is divided into distinct subgroups based on their spectrum of activity and chemical structure. Midwives should be familiar with these categories to understand prescription choices:

  1. Natural Penicillins: Includes Penicillin G and Penicillin V. Highly effective against many streptococci and treponemes (such as syphilis), but easily destroyed by bacterial enzymes.
  2. Aminopenicillins: Includes Ampicillin and Amoxicillin. These have a broader spectrum, covering more gram-negative organisms, and are commonly used for urinary and respiratory tract infections.
  3. Penicillinase-Resistant Penicillins: Includes Cloxacillin. Designed specifically to resist destruction by penicillinase enzymes produced by stubborn bacteria like Staphylococcus aureus.
  4. Antipseudomonal Penicillins: Includes Piperacillin. Often combined with beta-lactamase inhibitors for severe hospital-acquired or postpartum sepsis.

Indications for Use During Pregnancy and Puerperium

In obstetric practice, penicillins are indicated for several common clinical scenarios. Group B Streptococcus (GBS) screening and intrapartum prophylaxis rely heavily on intravenous penicillin G or ampicillin to prevent early-onset neonatal sepsis. Syphilis diagnosed during antenatal booking clinics is treated successfully with Benzathine penicillin G to prevent congenital syphilis and stillbirth. Additionally, oral amoxicillin or ampicillin treats asymptomatic bacteriuria and acute cystitis, protecting the pregnant woman from ascending pyelonephritis and premature uterine contractions.

Administration, Dosage, and Safety Considerations

Safe administration of penicillins requires careful attention to routes, timing, and dosage adjustments. While many penicillins are well absorbed orally, severe infections require intravenous or intramuscular delivery. Midwives must check the patient's renal function, as the kidneys clear most penicillins, and dosage adjustments may be necessary if renal clearance is impaired during pregnancy complications like pre-eclampsia. Furthermore, oral amoxicillin can be taken with or without food, though giving it with meals helps reduce mild gastrointestinal upset in pregnant women.

Hypersensitivity Reactions and Management of Anaphylaxis

Allergic reactions are the most significant adverse risk associated with penicillins. Midwives must take a thorough drug allergy history before any dose is given. Reactions range from mild delayed skin rashes to life-threatening anaphylactic shock, which involves severe bronchospasm, laryngeal oedema, and cardiovascular collapse.

  • Immediate management of anaphylaxis: Stop the penicillin infusion immediately.
  • Adrenaline administration: Give intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) without delay into the mid-outer thigh.
  • Supportive care: Maintain airway, administer high-flow oxygen, and establish intravenous access for fluid resuscitation.

Antimicrobial Resistance Patterns in Obstetric Care

Antimicrobial resistance poses a major threat to effective maternity care globally and locally in Nigeria. Many common bacteria, such as Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus, have developed mechanisms to resist standard penicillins through the production of beta-lactamase enzymes that destroy the antibiotic molecule before it can work. To combat this, clinicians often combine penicillins with beta-lactamase inhibitors like clavulanic acid (as seen in co-amoxiclav). Midwives play an essential role in curbing resistance by promoting rational drug use, discouraging self-medication during pregnancy, and ensuring patients complete their full prescribed course.
