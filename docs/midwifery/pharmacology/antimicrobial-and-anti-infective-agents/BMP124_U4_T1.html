Principles of Antimicrobial Therapy and Resistance Prevention

Introduction and Core Principles of Antimicrobial Therapy

Antimicrobial therapy involves using medications to treat infections caused by microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites. In midwifery practice across Nigeria, midwives frequently encounter infections ranging from urinary tract infections in pregnancy to postpartum wound sepsis. Successful treatment relies on several fundamental principles:

  • Selective Toxicity: The drug must target the invading pathogen without causing severe harm to the maternal or fetal host cells.
  • Empiric vs. Directed Therapy: Treatment often starts empirically based on the most likely pathogen before laboratory culture results return, then shifts to targeted therapy.
  • Spectrum of Activity: Choosing between narrow-spectrum drugs that target specific bugs or broad-spectrum drugs that cover multiple types of bacteria.
  • Optimal Dosing and Duration: Administering the correct dose at the right intervals and completing the full course to eradicate the infection completely.

Mechanisms of Antimicrobial Resistance

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when microorganisms evolve and survive exposure to medicines that once killed them. Bacteria utilize several clever biological mechanisms to outsmart antimicrobial drugs:

  1. Enzyme Inactivation: Pathogens produce specific enzymes, such as beta-lactamases, that destroy the active chemical structure of the drug before it can work.
  2. Target Modification: Bacteria alter the binding sites where the drug normally attaches, rendering the medication ineffective.
  3. Reduced Permeability: Changes in the bacterial cell wall decrease the entry channels, preventing the drug from accumulating inside the cell.
  4. Active Efflux Pumps: Germs develop specialized transport proteins that pump the drug out of the cell almost as fast as it enters.

Clinical Impact and Drivers of Resistance in Maternal and Newborn Care

Resistant infections pose a grave threat during pregnancy, childbirth, and the neonatal period. When common first-line antibiotics fail, treatment costs soar, hospital stays lengthen, and the risk of severe maternal morbidity, septic shock, and neonatal mortality climbs sharply.

In many resource-limited settings, several distinct factors drive this dangerous trend:

  • Over-the-counter availability of potent antimicrobials without a proper prescription.
  • Inappropriate use of broad-spectrum agents for mild viral ailments.
  • Substandard or falsified medicines containing low active ingredients.
  • Incomplete treatment courses due to financial constraints or premature symptom relief.

Midwifery Stewardship: Prescribing and Administration Best Practices

Antimicrobial stewardship refers to coordinated interventions designed to measure and improve the appropriate use of anti-infective agents. Midwives play a frontline stewardship role in maternity units and primary healthcare clinics.

Good stewardship starts with rigorous infection prevention and control practices, including strict hand hygiene, clean delivery techniques, and proper instrument sterilization, which stop infections before they start. When prescribing or administering medications, midwives must verify indications, check gestational safety profiles, calculate accurate weight-based doses for neonates, and adhere strictly to institutional treatment guidelines. Never administer antibiotics on routine demand without clear clinical signs of bacterial infection.

Educating Patients and Communities on Responsible Drug Use

Clinical stewardship extends beyond hospital walls into antenatal classes, postnatal visits, and community outreach. Midwives are trusted educators who can dispel common misconceptions about medicines.

Common MisconceptionFact / Correct Guidance
Antibiotics cure malaria and viral flus.Antibiotics only target bacteria; they have zero effect on viruses or malaria parasites.
Stopping drugs when feeling better saves medication.Stopping early leaves resilient bacteria alive to cause a stronger relapse.
Leftover drugs can treat future illnesses.Stored drugs lose potency and may not match the new infection type.

Midwives should counsel mothers during antenatal care to finish every dose, avoid sharing prescriptions, and discard expired medications safely.
