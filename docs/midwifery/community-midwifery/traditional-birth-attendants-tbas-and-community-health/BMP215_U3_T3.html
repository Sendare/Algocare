Traditional Practices in Pregnancy and Childbirth: Beneficial vs Harmful Practices

Introduction to Cultural Context in Nigerian Maternal Health

Cultural beliefs deeply influence how women in Nigeria experience pregnancy, labor, and postpartum recovery. Long-standing traditions often dictate what an expectant mother should eat, how she should dress, and who is allowed to attend her birth. Midwives working in Nigerian communities must recognize that these practices are not merely random behaviors; they carry profound emotional and social meaning for families. Respecting this cultural backdrop helps build trust between orthodox healthcare providers and the community. When midwives dismiss traditional beliefs outright, pregnant women may hide their practices or avoid antenatal clinics entirely. Understanding the local worldview allows health workers to separate harmless or helpful customs from those that endanger maternal and fetal health.

Identification and Categorization of Cultural Practices

To provide safe and effective care, midwives need to categorize traditional practices into those that support health and those that cause harm. Beneficial practices improve maternal comfort and psychological well-being without introducing physical risks. Harmful practices, conversely, directly threaten the physiological safety of the mother or newborn.

  • Nutritional taboos: Restricting protein-rich foods like eggs or meat during pregnancy based on the belief that it causes large babies or difficult labor.
  • Abdominal massage: Deep manual manipulation of the pregnant uterus, which can precipitate placental abruption or uterine rupture.
  • Uterine fundal pressure: Applying heavy downward force during the second stage of labor to hasten delivery, risking severe perineal tearing and uterine rupture.
  • Oral ingestion of local concoctions: Drinking herbal brews to speed up labor contractions, which can lead to hyperstimulation and fetal distress.

Promotion of Beneficial Traditions in Local Communities

Not all traditional practices are detrimental; many promote wellness and should be actively encouraged. Midwives should support customs that provide emotional reassurance, physical comfort, and nutritional value.

  • Continuous companion support: Allowing female family members to stay with the laboring woman provides vital emotional backing and reduces fear.
  • Postpartum warmth and rest: Practices that encourage adequate bed rest and warm baths help the new mother recover her strength.
  • Use of local soups and herbs: Incorporating culturally accepted, nutrient-dense local foods that boost lactation and restore blood volume after birth.

By validating these positive traditions, midwives foster a collaborative environment where mothers feel proud of their heritage while still accepting skilled medical interventions.

Modification and Cessation of Harmful Practices

Stopping harmful traditions requires patience, education, and empathy rather than direct confrontation or insults. When a midwife encounters a dangerous practice, lecturing the community usually causes defensiveness and drives women away from healthcare facilities. Instead, effective behavior change communication focuses on explaining the physiological risks in simple, relatable terms. Midwives can offer safer alternatives that satisfy the underlying cultural need. For instance, if a woman wants to use a herbal drink to ease birth pains, the midwife can substitute safe pain-relief breathing techniques or gentle massage of non-hazardous areas. Engaging community leaders and traditional rulers in these health talks ensures the message gains widespread acceptance.

Bridging the Gap: Working with Traditional Birth Attendants

Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs) remain the first point of contact for many pregnant women in rural Nigerian communities due to geographic and financial barriers. Rather than viewing TBAs as enemies of orthodox medicine, progressive health programs train them to recognize danger signs and refer complications early. This collaborative model transforms TBAs from competing practitioners into effective community-level links. TBAs can manage normal, low-risk deliveries while promptly escorting women experiencing obstructed labor or postpartum hemorrhage to the nearest primary health center. Joint workshops and referral partnerships save countless lives by reducing the delays that lead to maternal mortality.
