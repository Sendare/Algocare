Training, Supervision, and Integration of TBAs into PHC Systems

The Role of Traditional Birth Attendants in Nigerian Communities

Traditional Birth Attendants, widely known as TBAs, occupy a deeply respected and accessible position in many rural Nigerian communities. Long before the establishment of modern primary health care centers, these community-based practitioners provided vital maternity care rooted in local cultural traditions. Women often turn to TBAs because they share the same language, respect local customs, and offer continuous emotional and physical presence during labor.

  • Cultural Continuity: TBAs incorporate indigenous practices, prayers, and dietary advice that align with the community's worldview.
  • Physical Accessibility: Operating directly within villages, they remove the barrier of long-distance travel to distant health facilities.
  • Trust and Rapport: Families often have multi-generational relationships with their local TBA, fostering deep trust that formal health facilities sometimes struggle to build initially.

Capacity Building and Essential Training Modules for TBAs

Training traditional birth attendants does not aim to replace their cultural value, but rather to equip them with life-saving knowledge and the ability to recognize danger signs. Midwifery educators design these training programs to be simple, highly visual, and practically focused, avoiding dense medical jargon.

  1. Recognition of Danger Signs: Teaching TBAs to spot excessive bleeding, prolonged labor, severe headache, or fever so they can refer clients early.
  2. Hygiene and Infection Prevention: Emphasizing clean cord cutting, hand washing, and the avoidance of harmful substances applied to the newborn's umbilical stump.
  3. Basic Newborn Resuscitation: Instructing participants on how to dry, stimulate, and clear the airway of a baby who fails to breathe immediately at birth.

Establishing Effective Supervisory Frameworks

Training alone is insufficient without ongoing, respectful supervision. Midwives and Community Health Extension Workers from local Primary Health Care centers must maintain regular contact with trained TBAs. Supervision should feel like mentorship rather than policing, encouraging open dialogue about recent deliveries, challenges faced, and cases referred.

Regular supervisory visits allow health workers to audit birth outcomes, replenish basic clean delivery kits, and reinforce clinical lessons. When supervision is collaborative, TBAs feel valued as part of the formal health team rather than sidelined or threatened by the formal system.

Linking TBAs with Primary Health Care Networks

Integration bridges the gap between community-level care and formal health facilities. A functional linkage means that when a TBA identifies a woman with obstructed labor or severe pre-eclampsia, a clear referral pathway is already in place. This includes designated contact persons at the primary health center, pre-arranged transport options, and a feedback loop where the clinic informs the TBA about the mother's recovery.

Without these formal bridges, referred patients often arrive at health facilities too late, or get lost between the community and the clinic due to transport delays and financial hurdles.

Overcoming Barriers to Collaborative Maternal Care

Integrating traditional and modern health systems presents notable challenges. Mutual suspicion often exists; biomedical health workers may view TBAs as uneducated and dangerous, while TBAs might fear that formal clinics will steal their clients or criminalize their trade.

Bridging this gap requires intentional community dialogue involving village leaders, health administrators, and the practitioners themselves. Financial barriers, such as referral transport costs and clinic user fees, must also be addressed to ensure that poverty does not block a referred woman from receiving lifesaving emergency care at the primary health center.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and Quality Assurance in Integrated Systems

To determine if integrating traditional birth attendants improves maternal and child health indices, robust monitoring mechanisms must be put in place. Local health authorities track key indicators such as the number of referrals made by TBAs, maternal near-miss events, and facility delivery rates.

Data collection should be kept simple so that community birth attendants can participate without being overwhelmed by paperwork. Regular review meetings bring midwives and TBAs together to analyze maternal outcomes, celebrate successes, and correct recurring mistakes, ensuring continuous quality improvement across the entire primary health care network.
