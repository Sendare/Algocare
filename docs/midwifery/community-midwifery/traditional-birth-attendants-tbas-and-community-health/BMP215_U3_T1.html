Role and Status of TBAs in Rural Communities

Introduction to Traditional Birth Attendants in Rural Nigeria

Traditional Birth Attendants, commonly known as TBAs, are community-based practitioners who provide care during pregnancy, labor, and the postnatal period. In many rural parts of Nigeria, they serve as the primary and sometimes only source of maternity care due to the severe shortage of formal health facilities. These practitioners acquire their skills through apprenticeship, ancestral lineage, or perceived spiritual callings rather than formal medical training. Understanding their prevalence requires recognizing the structural gaps in the public health system that leave rural women with very few options when labor begins.

Deep-Rooted Cultural Significance and Belief Systems

The influence of TBAs extends far beyond physical childbirth support because their practice is deeply intertwined with local cosmology and spiritual beliefs. Many rural communities view pregnancy and childbirth not merely as physiological events, but as spiritual journeys that require metaphysical protection. Cultural beliefs often dictate that complications arise from spiritual transgressions, malicious spirits, or broken taboos. TBAs naturally integrate local prayers, ritual herbs, and protective charms into their care, providing psychological comfort that biomedical clinics rarely address.

Maternal Care Roles Provided in Rural Practice

TBAs perform a wide range of services spanning the entire continuum of the childbearing cycle. During pregnancy, they monitor fetal position through external abdominal palpation and administer various herbal decoctions intended to strengthen the womb or ease labor pains. During labor, they offer continuous, one-on-one emotional encouragement and physical positioning support. In the postnatal period, they often assist with newborn cord care, infant bathing, and maternal abdominal binding.

Socioeconomic Drivers of TBA Utilization

Economic barriers play a massive role in steering pregnant women toward Traditional Birth Attendants. Formal healthcare, even where nominally free, often incurs hidden costs such as purchasing drugs, gloves, and diagnostic materials. TBAs, by contrast, operate on flexible payment structures, accepting payment in installments, farm produce, livestock, or small tokens of appreciation. Furthermore, transportation hurdles—such as impassable roads and lack of vehicles—make traveling to distant health facilities nearly impossible during emergencies.

Community Perception, Trust, and Social Status

Within their communities, respected TBAs hold high social standing as pillars of wisdom, custodians of heritage, and essential caregivers. This high status stems from their lifelong availability, empathetic communication style, and cultural proximity to the villagers. They speak the local dialect fluently, respect traditional norms, and treat patients with a familiar warmth that clinical staff, perceived as elitist or judgmental, sometimes lack. This profound level of trust often makes community members heed TBA advice over warnings from distant public health authorities.

Limitations and Associated Maternal Risks

Despite their social value, traditional birth attendance carries substantial clinical risks due to the absence of formal diagnostic and sterile procedures. Common hazards include severe postpartum hemorrhage, prolonged obstructed labor from cephalopelvic disproportion, and neonatal tetanus resulting from unsterile cord cutting tools. TBAs lack the training to identify early warning signs of obstetric emergencies or administer life-saving interventions such as oxytocin, blood transfusions, or caesarean sections. Consequently, reliance on TBAs contributes significantly to preventable maternal and perinatal mortality rates in rural Nigeria.

Bridging the Gap: Integration and Collaboration with Formal Healthcare

Recognizing that outright bans on TBAs are ineffective, modern public health strategies increasingly focus on training and collaboration. Rather than fighting traditional practitioners, many health ministries involve them as community health scouts whose primary duty is to identify pregnant women and prompt timely referrals to clinics. Training programs teach TBAs to recognize danger signs like severe bleeding, fever, or prolonged labor and to abandon harmful practices such as aggressive abdominal massage. Successful integration respects their community influence while shifting actual delivery responsibilities to skilled birth attendants.
