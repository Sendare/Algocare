Preparation and Conduct of Safe Home Delivery

Assessing Suitability and Preparing the Home Environment

Conducting a delivery outside a health facility requires careful prior assessment. Not every pregnant woman is a suitable candidate for a home birth. Midwives must screen clients during antenatal visits, confirming low-risk status, a single fetus in cephalic presentation, and an uncomplicated gestational age. When the home is chosen, environmental preparation is crucial.

  • Clean space: A well-lit, private room free from clutter and domestic animals.
  • Water supply: Clean, running water or adequate stored potable water for handwashing and washing the perineum.
  • Waste disposal: Designated receptacles for placenta and contaminated materials.
  • Bedding: A clean bed or mattress covered with clean plastic sheeting and fresh linen.

Essential Equipment and Emergency Preparedness

A domiciliary midwife must carry a well-stocked midwifery kit capable of handling normal physiological childbirth and immediate emergencies. Relying on household items for clinical procedures compromises safety.

  • Clean delivery kit: Sterile cord ties, sterile blades or scissors, and clean gauze.
  • Resuscitation gear: A neonatal bag-valve-mask device with appropriate newborn mask sizes.
  • Pharmacological agents: Oxytocin, active management drugs, and intravenous infusion sets.
  • Monitoring tools: Fetoscope or Doppler, and a reliable sphygmomanometer.

Emergency preparedness also means having a written transport plan. The midwife must identify a functional referral hospital, confirm vehicle availability, and ensure fuel or transport funds are ready before labor establishes.

Strict Adherence to Clean Delivery Practices

Puerperal sepsis is a major historical cause of maternal mortality that remains entirely preventable through strict infection prevention practices. In a home setting where environmental sterility is harder to control than in a hospital theater, the midwife creates a clean zone.

Hand hygiene is non-negotiable. The midwife must perform thorough handwashing with soap and clean water or use an alcohol-based hand rub before touching the client. All instruments entering the sterile field must be autoclaved or high-level disinfected. The perineum is washed with clean water. Unnecessary vaginal examinations are avoided, as each examination introduces pathogens from the lower vagina into the upper reproductive tract.

Intrapartum Monitoring of Mother and Fetus

Monitoring labor at home mirrors facility standards. The midwife tracks maternal vital signs—blood pressure, pulse, and temperature—to detect physiological exhaustion, dehydration, or pre-eclampsia early. Fetal well-being is assessed by auscultating the fetal heart rate using a fetoscope or Doppler immediately after a uterine contraction.

Contractions are monitored for frequency, duration, and intensity. Progress is documented using a partograph whenever possible. Cervical dilation rates help identify prolonged labor early, prompting timely discussion about transfer before fetal distress or uterine rupture occurs.

Managing the Third Stage of Labor at Home

The third stage—from the birth of the baby until the delivery of the placenta—carries the highest risk of postpartum hemorrhage. Active management of the third stage (AMTSL) is standard practice even outside the hospital.

  1. Uterine tonic: Administer 10 international units of oxytocin intramuscularly within one minute of infant delivery, ensuring the baby is not a second twin.
  2. Cord traction: Apply controlled cord traction with counter-traction on the uterus once the uterus is well contracted.
  3. Uterine massage: Massage the uterine fundus immediately after placental delivery and monitor tone regularly.

Immediate Newborn Care at Home

The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life requires prompt, gentle assessment and care. The newborn is dried immediately with a clean, warm towel to prevent hypothermia. Assessment of breathing, heart rate, and color is conducted simultaneously.

Delayed cord clamping—waiting 1 to 3 minutes or until cord pulsation stops—improves infant iron stores. Eye prophylaxis and Vitamin K administration are given according to local protocols. The infant is placed skin-to-skin on the mother's chest, encouraging early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life.

Indications and Protocols for Emergency Transfer

Recognizing when a home birth is exceeding normal physiological limits is the hallmark of a skilled domiciliary midwife. Hesitation in transferring a client can result in catastrophe.

  • Maternal red flags: Persistent postpartum hemorrhage not responding to uterotonics, severe pre-eclampsia symptoms, retained placenta exceeding 30 minutes, or severe perineal lacerations.
  • Fetal/Neonatal red flags: Persistent fetal heart rate abnormalities, birth asphyxia failing initial resuscitation, or severe congenital anomalies.

The midwife accompanies the client whenever possible, keeps intravenous lines running if established, and brings records, equipment, and supportive family members to ensure continuity of care at the receiving facility.
