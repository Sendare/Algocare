Emergency Referral Protocols from Home to Secondary/Tertiary Facilities

The Reality of Obstetric Emergencies in Home Births

Home births supervised by midwives in Nigeria often proceed smoothly, yet severe complications can arise with very little warning. Obstetric emergencies like postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, or obstructed labor do not always respect a planned facility delivery. When these crises happen outside a hospital setting, time becomes the most critical factor in survival. Domiciliary midwives must maintain a high index of suspicion and remain constantly prepared to shift from routine caregiving to emergency management. Recognizing that home environments lack surgical intervention capability means acknowledging that prompt referral is lifesaving, not a failure of domiciliary care.

Identification of Obstetric Emergencies

Spotting the early warning signs of danger allows the midwife to act before total collapse occurs. Midwives must assess vital signs and physical symptoms continuously during labor and the immediate postpartum period.

  • Postpartum hemorrhage: Blood loss exceeding 500ml vaginally, accompanied by a soft, boggy uterus and signs of hypovolemia.
  • Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia: Persistent severe headache, blurred vision, sudden facial swelling, and dangerously high blood pressure sometimes leading to convulsions.
  • Obstructed labor: Strong contractions with no progress in cervical dilation, severe lower abdominal pain, and fetal distress or a palpable contraction ring.
  • Sepsis: High fever, shivering, foul-smelling lochia, and rapid maternal pulse during labor or postpartum.

Stabilizing the Mother Before Transfer

Before moving a client from the home to a secondary or tertiary facility, the midwife must perform essential stabilization measures. Transporting an unstable patient drastically worsens her prognosis.

Initiate intravenous access using a large-bore cannula and commence normal saline or Ringer's lactate infusion if shock threatens. Empty the bladder either spontaneously or via catheterization to help the uterus contract in cases of hemorrhage. Administer emergency uterotonics such as oxytocin or misoprostol if heavy bleeding is the issue. Keep the patient warm, position her on her side if she is unconscious or fitting to protect her airway, and ensure an accompanying person stays ready to assist.

Communication Protocols

Clear communication bridges the gap between the remote home setting and the receiving hospital team. As soon as an emergency is identified and initial stabilization begins, the midwife or a designated relative must contact the receiving facility.

State the midwife's name, exact location, and clinical findings clearly. Provide the patient's parity, gestational age, vital signs, and the specific nature of the complication. Advance warning allows the receiving obstetric unit to prepare blood products, an operating theatre, or specialized personnel before the ambulance arrives. Document the exact time the call was made and the name of the health worker who received the message.

Transport and Handover Procedures

Moving the patient safely requires a reliable vehicle, an accompanying knowledgeable birth attendant, and organized logistics. Relying on informal transport can waste precious minutes, so pre-arranged community transport schemes are invaluable in Nigerian domiciliary practice.

Transport PhaseKey Midwife Action
DepartureEnsure IV lines are secured, bladder is drained, and emergency drugs are packed.
TransitMonitor vital signs continuously, watch for IV dislodgement, and maintain airway positioning.
HandoverGive a concise verbal summary using SBAR while handing over physical records and the patient.

Documenting the Referral for Continuity of Care

Comprehensive documentation protects both the client and the midwife while ensuring the receiving medical team understands the full clinical picture. The referral note must be concise, legible, and written immediately before departure.

Include the time of onset of the complication, exact vital signs recorded, all medications administered with dosages and times, and the clear reason for transfer. Keep a duplicate copy or stub of the referral note in the domiciliary practice records. Thorough records prevent duplication of treatments, highlight trends in vital signs, and provide a legal safeguard for professional midwifery practice.
