Postnatal Home Visits and Maternal-Infant Surveillance Schedule

Introduction to Domiciliary Postnatal Care

Domiciliary postnatal care brings professional midwifery services directly to the mother and newborn's home environment. Hospital discharges happen very early in many Nigerian communities due to bed shortages or maternal preference, making home visits a vital bridge for continuity of care. Midwives assess the home setting, observe family dynamics, and deliver individualized clinical support where the woman feels most comfortable. Catching early complications like postpartum hemorrhage or neonatal hypothermia prevents emergency hospital admissions. This home-based approach also reassures new mothers and builds confidence in their changing domestic routines.

Postnatal Visit Timeline and Scheduling

A structured schedule ensures that the midwife assesses the mother and baby during critical physiological transition windows.

  • First Visit (Day 1-2): Conducted within twenty-four to forty-eight hours of discharge to check immediate postpartum bleeding, perineal healing, and early breastfeeding attachment.
  • Second Visit (Day 3-5): Focuses heavily on the physiological milk transition, infant weight check, and screening for neonatal jaundice.
  • Third Visit (Day 10): Evaluates overall involution of the uterus, umbilical cord separation, and maternal emotional well-being.
  • Final Routine Visit (Week 6): The classic postnatal check covering contraception, final physical clearance, and infant developmental milestones.

Postpartum Maternal Assessment

Evaluating the postnatal mother requires a systematic head-to-toe physical and psychosocial check. Midwives check vital signs, noting any unexpected spikes in blood pressure that might signal postpartum pre-eclampsia. Palpating the abdomen confirms proper uterine involution, ensuring the fundus remains firm and midline. Perineal inspections assess episiotomy or tear healing, looking closely for signs of hematoma or wound breakdown. Lochia is evaluated for color, amount, and foul odor which suggests infection. Finally, conversational screening for mood changes helps identify early signs of baby blues or postpartum depression.

Neonatal Surveillance and Care

Newborn surveillance in the home centers on thermal regulation, umbilical care, and general behavioral assessment. Midwives measure infant temperature to rule out hypothermia or occult fever, which are both dangerous in early life. The umbilical stump is inspected for dryness, bleeding, or signs of omphalitis like redness spreading to the abdominal skin. Skin color is checked under natural light to monitor for progressive jaundice spreading down the trunk or limbs. Midwives also observe general muscle tone, cry strength, and voiding patterns to ensure the baby is thriving.

Evaluating Breastfeeding and Infant Nutrition

Observing a live breastfeeding session remains the gold standard for assessing nutritional adequacy. Midwives look for effective latch indicators: wide-open mouth, more areola visible above the top lip than below, and audible swallowing sounds. Mothers are guided on responsive feeding cues rather than strict clock-watching. The midwife checks infant hydration by counting wet diapers and observing fontanelle depression. Addressing common challenges like engorged breasts or sore nipples early prevents premature cessation of exclusive breastfeeding.

Identifying Danger Signs and Red Flags

Midwives must educate families on urgent warning signs requiring immediate medical intervention.

  • Maternal Red Flags: Heavy vaginal bleeding soaking more than one large pad per hour, severe throbbing headaches with blurred vision, high fever, and sudden calf pain.
  • Neonatal Red Flags: Poor suck or total refusal to feed, persistent vomiting, convulsions, lethargy, and jaundice reaching the palms and soles.

Spotting these signs quickly during a home visit allows for immediate stabilization and emergency transport.

Documentation and Community Referral Pathways

Accurate record-keeping completes every home visit cycle, ensuring legal protection and continuity across different healthcare providers. Midwives document all assessment findings, treatments administered, and educational advice given in the mother-infant home-based record booklet. When complications exceed domiciliary care capacity, clear referral pathways must be activated immediately. This involves communicating directly with the receiving primary health center or secondary hospital, arranging transport, and providing an accompanying referral note detailing the clinical findings and urgent needs.
