Evaluating Community Health Indicators and Submitting National Health Records

Introduction to Community Health Metrics in Nigerian Midwifery

Community health metrics provide the empirical foundation required for safe, effective midwifery practice at the grassroots level in Nigeria. Midwives do not operate in a clinical vacuum; every antenatal booking, normal delivery, and immunization session generates vital statistical data that reflects the broader health status of a community. Within the primary health care system, these numbers transform individual patient encounters into public health intelligence. When midwives accurately capture and report community health metrics, they enable the National Health Management Information System (NHMIS) to allocate resources, track disease outbreaks, and measure progress toward national health targets. Understanding how these metrics function is therefore just as clinically relevant as knowing how to manage postpartum hemorrhage or perform a sterile vaginal examination.

Core Maternal and Child Health Indicators to Measure

Midwives track specific quantitative measures to evaluate the health and survival of mothers and children within their catchment areas. These indicators act as numerical warning signs or success markers for community interventions.

  • Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR): The number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births, reflecting obstetric safety and emergency referral efficiency.
  • Neonatal Mortality Rate (NMR): The number of deaths during the first 28 days of life per 1,000 live births, highlighting immediate postnatal care quality.
  • Antenatal Care (ANC) Coverage: The percentage of pregnant women who attend at least four targeted visits with a skilled birth attendant.
  • Immunization Dropout Rate: The proportion of infants who start a vaccination schedule, such as BCG or Pentavalent-1, but fail to complete the final doses.

Data Collection Tools at Primary Health Care (PHC) Level

Accurate reporting begins at the point of care using standardized paper and digital registers. Midwives must master these registers to prevent data loss before electronic submission takes place.

Step-by-Step Guide to the DHIMS2 Platform

The District Health Information Management System version 2 (DHIMS2) is the designated web-based software used across Nigeria for routine health data reporting. Midwives at facility levels often input monthly summary totals into this platform.

  1. Obtain Secure Access: Log in using your assigned credentials provided by the Local Government Area (LGA) Monitoring and Evaluation officer.
  2. Select the Correct Facility: Navigate to your specific primary health care facility organization unit on the hierarchical tree.
  3. Choose the Reporting Period: Select the exact month and year corresponding to the paper summary forms you are entering.
  4. Input Data Datasets: Enter numerical values corresponding to maternal health, child health, and disease surveillance fields, ensuring no blank fields are left ambiguous.
  5. Run Validation Checks: Use the built-in validation rules to spot outliers, negative numbers, or missing mandatory fields.
  6. Complete Submission: Click submit and notify your LGA health information officer that your facility report is locked and ready for review.

Common Reporting Errors and How to Prevent Them

Inaccurate health data distorts national planning, leading to drug stockouts or misplaced medical supplies. Midwives must actively avoid common reporting mistakes during data collation.

  • Transcription Inaccuracies: Mistyping tally marks from paper registers into monthly summary sheets. Prevent this by cross-checking totals with a colleague before transcription.
  • Late Submissions: Failing to submit reports by the designated deadline (usually the fifth day of the new month). Establish a strict facility timeline that finishes tallying two days prior to the deadline.
  • Double Counting: Recording a client twice because they visited two different service points on the same day without noting previous registration. Always verify client cards during registration.
  • Omitting Outreach Data: Forgetting to include mobile clinic or community outreaches in the facility monthly total. Always collect tally sheets from community health extension workers immediately after outreach programs.

Using Local Data for Actionable Community Health Planning

Data is not just an administrative burden for higher levels of government; it is a powerful local tool for midwives. When a midwife reviews her facility's monthly indicators, she can immediately spot operational gaps. For instance, if data shows a sudden drop in antenatal clinic attendance during the rainy season, the midwife can work with traditional leaders to organize farm-gate health talks or adjust clinic hours. If immunization registers show high dropout rates for measles vaccines, community mobilization can be targeted specifically at market days and religious gatherings. By interpreting local trends, midwives transition from passive record keepers to proactive community health strategists.
