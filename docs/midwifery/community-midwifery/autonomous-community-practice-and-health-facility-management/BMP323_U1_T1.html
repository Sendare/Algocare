Managing Primary Health Center (PHC) Maternity Units and Birthing Centers

Introduction to Primary Health Care Maternity Units

Primary Health Care (PHC) maternity units form the bedrock of maternal and child healthcare delivery within Nigerian communities. These centers serve as the first point of contact for pregnant women seeking antenatal care, uncomplicated deliveries, and postnatal services. Operating at the grassroots level, a well-managed PHC maternity unit significantly reduces maternal and neonatal mortality by bringing skilled birth attendants closer to rural populations. Midwives in charge must balance clinical duties with basic administrative responsibilities to ensure the facility remains functional 24 hours a day. Understanding the scope, limitations, and community expectations of a PHC setting helps midwives deliver safe, culturally acceptable, and equitable care.

Core Operational Standards and Facility Readiness

Maintaining a functional maternity unit requires strict adherence to operational standards. Facility readiness encompasses infrastructure, basic utilities, and functional equipment necessary to conduct clean deliveries.

  • Power Supply: Reliable backup sources such as solar panels or generators are mandatory to ensure lighting during night deliveries.
  • Water Supply: Clean running water or treated borehole systems must be available continuously for infection control and perineal hygiene.
  • Functional Equipment: Essential items include working fetoscopes, BP apparatus, adult and neonatal resuscitation kits, and sterilizing units.

A daily facility checklist helps the midwife-in-charge identify broken equipment or depleted resources before an emergency arises.

Human Resource Management and Staff Rostering

Staffing shortages in rural primary health centers demand creative and fair roster management. Midwives often work in small teams, making burnout and fatigue significant risks to patient safety. The midwife-in-charge must create transparent duty rotas that balance experienced staff across all shifts, ensuring that every shift has at least one skilled birth attendant capable of managing a primary postpartum haemorrhage or neonatal asphyxia. Cross-training Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) to assist with vital signs, record keeping, and patient preparation optimizes the nursing workforce. Regular team meetings foster open communication, clear delegation of duties, and higher morale among healthcare workers.

Supply Chain and Essential Medicines Management

Stockouts of life-saving commodities can turn a normal delivery into a tragedy. Managing the maternity supply chain requires diligent inventory tracking using the 'First-Expired, First-Out' (FEFO) principle to minimize wastage. Essential uterotonics like oxytocin and misoprostol must be stored within recommended temperature ranges—often requiring functional cold chain storage. Midwives must monitor consumption rates and place timely orders through the state drug management agency to prevent sudden shortages. Accountability records for controlled drugs and vaccines prevent pilferage and ensure that subsidised maternal medicines actually reach the targeted pregnant women.

Infection Prevention and Control (IPC)

Standard precautions protect both healthcare workers and patients from hospital-acquired infections. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective intervention to break the chain of transmission. Maternity units must maintain dedicated handwashing stations with soap and clean water at the entrance of the delivery suite and post-natal ward. Proper segregation of infectious waste—such as placenta remains, blood-soaked gauze, and used syringes—into colour-coded bins prevents accidental needle-stick injuries and environmental contamination. Decontamination of reusable metal instruments in chlorine solutions immediately after use protects staff during subsequent washing and sterilization stages.

Emergency Obstetric Referral Pathways and Transport

Recognizing the limits of primary care is vital for saving lives. When complications like prolonged obstructed labour, severe pre-eclampsia, or postpartum haemorrhage surpass PHC capacity, prompt referral is mandatory. Establishing a functional emergency transport system—such as community tricycle ambulances, fuel-backed vehicles, or structured driver networks—eliminates transit delays. The referring midwife must stabilize the mother, start an intravenous line, administer loading doses of critical drugs where indicated, and write a clear referral note detailing findings, interventions performed, and the reason for transfer.

Data Collection, Health Records, and Maternal Audit

Accurate record keeping transforms daily clinical activities into actionable public health data. Midwives must meticulously document all antenatal visits, labour progressions on the partograph, and delivery outcomes in official registers. Monthly summary reports inform local government health authorities about disease trends, birth rates, and maternal complications. Furthermore, conducting confidential maternal death reviews and near-miss audits helps the healthcare team learn from adverse events without fear of punishment. These audits identify systemic gaps—such as delayed referrals or drug stockouts—allowing clinics to implement preventive changes and improve future care quality.
