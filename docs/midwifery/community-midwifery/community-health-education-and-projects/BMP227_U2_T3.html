Organizing Community Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) Sensitization Programs

Introduction to Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH)

Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene, collectively known as WASH, form the absolute bedrock of public health, especially in resource-constrained communities across Nigeria. Safe water prevents internal waterborne pathogens, proper sanitation stops the fecal-oral transmission route in the environment, and hygiene behaviors like handwashing create a personal shield against daily infections. When midwives step outside the maternity ward to engage the wider community, understanding these three pillars allows us to address the root environmental causes of neonatal sepsis, severe childhood diarrhea, and maternal illnesses. Clean water supply, hygienic excreta disposal, and behavior change communication must always be addressed together, because fixing only one yields very little long-term health benefit.

Understanding Safe Drinking Water Sources and Management

Access to safe drinking water is a fundamental human right, yet many rural and peri-urban Nigerian communities rely on unprotected streams, shallow hand-dug wells, or distant rivers. Contaminated water is a primary vehicle for pathogens causing cholera, typhoid, and dysentery.

  • Protected Boreholes: Groundwater tapped through a drilled hole with a fitted handpump or motorized system, generally safer if located away from pit latrines.
  • Rainwater Harvesting: Collecting runoff from clean roofs into covered storage tanks, requiring regular cleaning of gutters and first-flush diverters.
  • Point-of-Use Treatment: Household water purification methods including boiling, chemical chlorination, solar disinfection (SODIS), and ceramic filtration.

Midwives must teach mothers that water that looks clear can still harbor invisible microorganisms. Source protection keeps contaminants out, while safe storage inside narrow-necked, covered jerrycans prevents post-collection recontamination from dirty hands and cups.

Promoting Proper Environmental Sanitation

Environmental sanitation encompasses the safe management of human excreta, solid waste, and drainage. Open defecation remains a stubborn public health challenge in parts of Nigeria, contaminating soil and water sources during the rainy season through runoff. Midwives must promote the construction and correct usage of improved sanitation facilities, such as ventilated improved pit (VIP) latrines and water-closet systems connected to safe septic tanks. Furthermore, safe management of household refuse prevents breeding sites for malaria-transmitting mosquitoes and rodents. Community sensitization should address proper waste disposal, clearing of overgrown bushes around homes, and maintaining clear drainage channels to prevent stagnant water accumulation.

Core Hygiene Practices for Disease Prevention

Personal hygiene is the behavioral line of defense that breaks the chain of infection. Handwashing with soap at critical times—specifically after using the toilet, after cleaning a child's bottom, and before preparing food or eating—reduces diarrheal morbidity by nearly half and significantly lowers respiratory infection rates. Midwives should demonstrate the proper technique using Tippy-Taps or Veronica buckets in community settings, emphasizing that rinsing with plain water is insufficient to remove grease and pathogens. Beyond handwashing, personal cleanliness includes regular bathing, washing clothes, and maintaining safe food hygiene practices such as washing raw vegetables, keeping cooked food covered, and separating raw meat from ready-to-eat items.

Designing Effective Community Sensitization Programs

A successful WASH sensitization program requires careful preparation and participatory methods rather than lecturing community members. Midwives must begin with a rapid assessment or community dialogue to identify local beliefs, existing water sources, and specific hygiene gaps. Interactive communication methods—such as drama, songs, storytelling, and practical demonstrations—hold audience attention and encourage active participation. Visual aids should be culturally appropriate, clear, and easy to understand for both literate and non-literate participants. Sessions must be scheduled at times that do not conflict with farming or market days to ensure high attendance across all demographic groups.

Mobilizing Stakeholders and Engaging Local Leaders

Community ownership is vital for the sustainability of any WASH intervention. Midwives cannot achieve lasting behavioral change working in isolation; we must actively identify and involve key stakeholders including traditional rulers, religious leaders, women's group heads, youth leaders, and local government health officers. Traditional and religious leaders hold immense influence over community norms and can endorse new practices or grant permission for communal labor projects like clearing drainage paths or building pit latrines. Involving local leaders early builds trust, ensures cultural alignment, and secures community backing long after external project facilitators have departed.

Evaluating the Impact of WASH Interventions

Evaluation measures whether a WASH sensitization program achieved its intended goals and identifies areas for future improvement. Midwives should evaluate programs using both quantitative indicators—such as the number of newly constructed household latrines, increased volume of treated drinking water, or attendance figures—and qualitative feedback gathered through focus group discussions about behavior changes. Comparing health records before and after the intervention, such as tracking clinic visits for childhood diarrhea, provides concrete evidence of impact. Sharing these findings with community members and local authorities celebrates successes and reinforces ongoing commitment to safe water, sanitation, and hygiene practices.
