Identifying and Referring High-Risk Pregnant Women from Rural Communities

Understanding High-Risk Pregnancies in Rural Contexts

A high-risk pregnancy is one where the mother, the developing fetus, or both face an increased chance of health problems or death before, during, or after birth. In rural Nigerian communities, midwives encounter unique challenges because basic diagnostic tools are scarce and distances to comprehensive emergency obstetric care (CEmOC) are vast.

Risk factors generally fall into distinct categories that a community midwife must screen for during every antenatal visit:

  • Medical history: Chronic conditions like hypertension, diabetes, sickle cell disease, or previous severe cardiac issues.
  • Obstetric history: Past history of postpartum hemorrhage, severe pre-eclampsia, recurrent miscarriages, or previous caesarean sections.
  • Current pregnancy complications: Multiple gestations, abnormal fetal lie after 36 weeks, severe anemia (hemoglobin below 7 g/dL), or persistent vaginal bleeding.
  • Demographic and social factors: Extremes of maternal age (under 18 or over 35 years), grand multiparity (delivering five or more times), and severe malnutrition.

Spotting Red Flags: High-Risk Identification at the Primary Care Level

At primary health care (PHC) clinics, midwives rely on careful clinical observation, basic equipment, and thorough questioning. Spotting danger signs early prevents minor issues from turning into life-threatening emergencies.

Midwives must routinely check for the following critical red flags:

  • Severe vaginal bleeding at any stage of gestation, which may signal placenta previa or abruptio placentae.
  • Persistent severe headache, blurred vision, and right upper quadrant abdominal pain, classic signs of impending eclampsia.
  • Fever, chills, and foul-smelling vaginal discharge, pointing toward systemic infection or chorioamnionitis.
  • Marked swelling of the face, hands, and feet occurring suddenly, distinct from normal dependent edema.
  • Reduced or absent fetal movements reported by the mother during the third trimester, indicating potential fetal compromise.

Overcoming Barriers to Care in Remote Rural Communities

Identifying a high-risk woman is only the first step. In many rural Nigerian settlements, structural barriers often prevent patients from reaching higher levels of care. Midwives must understand these obstacles to design realistic management plans.

Financial constraints remain a primary hurdle, as out-of-pocket payments for transport and hospital fees delay decision-making. Geographic isolation, seasonal road impassability during heavy rains, and a scarcity of motorized transport compound the problem. Furthermore, cultural preferences for traditional birth attendants (TBAs) and spiritual homes often keep women away from orthodox facilities until complications become catastrophic. Bridging these gaps requires community health education, building local transport funds, and fostering respectful partnerships with traditional leaders.

Establishing Effective Emergency Referral Systems

When a complication exceeds the capacity of a primary health clinic, a well-coordinated emergency referral system can save a life. Delays in this chain often lead to the 'three delays': delay in deciding to seek care, delay in reaching a facility, and delay in receiving adequate treatment.

A proper referral protocol involves the following concrete steps:

  1. Stabilization: Provide emergency first aid (such as starting an IV line or administering loading doses of anticonvulsants) before moving the patient.
  2. Communication: Contact the receiving secondary or tertiary hospital in advance using mobile phones or radio devices to ensure they are prepared.
  3. Documentation: Write a clear, concise referral note detailing the patient's history, vital signs, findings, and treatments already given.
  4. Accompanying Personnel: Ensure a trained health worker or knowledgeable relative accompanies the patient to monitor vital signs during transit.

Community Engagement and Pre-Referral Stabilization

Effective maternal health care extends beyond the four walls of the clinic. Midwives must actively involve community structures—such as youth groups, women's associations, and local transporters—to build functional emergency transport schemes.

Before transferring a compromised woman, basic stabilization measures buy critical time. For instance, if a woman is actively convulsing from eclampsia, administer magnesium sulfate and clear her airway. If she suffers from severe postpartum hemorrhage, secure two large-bore IV lines, infuse normal saline rapidly, and massage the uterus. Never transport an unstable patient without taking these emergency steps, as the journey itself can worsen shock.
