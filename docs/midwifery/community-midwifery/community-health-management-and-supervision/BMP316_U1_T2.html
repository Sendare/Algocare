Organization of Domiciliary Midwifery Services and Emergency Referral Schemes

Introduction to Domiciliary Midwifery in Nigeria

Domiciliary midwifery involves providing maternal and newborn care directly within the home and community setting. In Nigeria, home-based care bridges the massive gap between formal health facilities and rural populations constrained by geography, finance, or cultural preferences. Midwives practicing in the community must respect local traditions while holding fast to professional safety standards. Success requires building trust with community leaders, traditional birth attendants, and family decision-makers long before labour begins. This care model focuses on normal physiological birth, early detection of complications, and maintaining an active bridge to higher-level facilities when danger arises.

Equipping the Home Delivery Bag: Essential Tools and Supplies

A well-organized home delivery bag is a community midwife's mobile clinic. Every item must be sterile, functional, and checked before every call-out.

  • Sterile instruments: Cord clamp, scissors, and needle holders for perineal repair.
  • Medications: Oxytocin, misoprostol, vitamin K, and eye ointment.
  • Diagnostics: Fetoscope or Doppler, blood pressure cuff, and adult thermometer.
  • Hygiene supplies: Disposable gloves, hand sanitizer, waterproof sheets, and heavy-duty waste bags.

Community Entry and Home Antenatal Risk Assessment

Before conducting home births, midwives must perform proper community entry through local leaders. Antenatal assessments done inside homes require keen observation. The midwife checks vital signs, palpates the abdomen to determine foetal lie and presentation, and screens for danger signs like severe headache, blurred vision, or swelling. Home visits also allow the midwife to evaluate the domestic environment for cleanliness, water access, and a designated clean birth space. Identifying risk factors early helps the midwife decide whether a home birth is appropriate or if the woman must book for facility-based delivery.

Intrapartum Care and Immediate Postpartum in the Community

Managing labour at home demands high clinical vigilance and patience. The midwife monitors contractions, foetal heart rate, and maternal progress using a partograph even outside the hospital. Clean delivery practices prevent puerperal sepsis. Immediately after the baby is born, active management of the third stage of labour is carried out to prevent postpartum haemorrhage. The newborn is dried, assessed using the Apgar scoring system, kept warm through skin-to-skin contact, and initiated on early breastfeeding within the first hour.

Identifying Obstetric Emergencies Requiring Evacuation

Not all complications can be managed at home. Midwives must recognize warning signs that demand immediate transfer to a comprehensive facility. These red flags include prolonged or obstructed labour, antepartum or postpartum haemorrhage, severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia, retained placenta, and signs of acute foetal distress. Recognizing these conditions early, before maternal collapse occurs, saves lives. The midwife must stabilize the client as much as possible while simultaneously activating the emergency transport plan.

Designing and Operating an Effective Local Referral Chain

An emergency referral scheme links the community midwife, traditional transporters, primary health clinics, and secondary hospitals. Communication is the backbone of this chain; reliable phone numbers and community alert systems ensure that receiving facilities are notified before the patient arrives. Midwives should conduct regular emergency drills with community members so everyone knows their role during a crisis. A well-coordinated referral system eliminates deadly delays in reaching advanced medical care.

Overcoming Logistical and Transport Barriers in Rural Nigeria

Rural Nigerian settings frequently feature bad roads, flooded rivers, fuel scarcity, and a lack of motorized vehicles. Midwives must plan ahead by partnering with local motorcycle riders, tricycle unions, and community emergency transport schemes. Financial barriers are another hurdle; community emergency funds or micro-insurance schemes can cover transport costs so families do not delay care while searching for cash. Addressing these logistics proactively ensures that physical distance does not translate into a maternal death sentence.
