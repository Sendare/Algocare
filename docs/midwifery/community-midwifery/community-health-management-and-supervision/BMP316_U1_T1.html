Supervision and Evaluation of Community Health Workers and Traditional Birth Attendants

Introduction to Community-Level Maternal Health Cadres

In many parts of Nigeria, formal health facilities are miles away from pregnant women in rural settings. To bridge this geographic gap, community-level maternal health cadres step in as the first point of contact. These workers live within the communities they serve, understanding local languages, cultural norms, and transport challenges.

  • Community Health Workers (CHWs): Trained healthcare providers belonging to the formal or semi-formal health system, deployed to offer basic curative and preventive services at the grassroots.
  • Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs): Practitioners who learn their skills through apprenticeship, cultural heritage, or spiritual calling, historically attending to women during childbirth within their communities.

Recognizing these cadres is crucial because most maternal deaths occur at home or during transit. Midwives cannot manage community health alone. Integrating local cadres creates a continuous safety net, bringing basic screening, health education, and timely referral closer to the doorsteps of rural mothers.

The Role of Community Health Workers and Traditional Birth Attendants in Primary Care

Community health workers and traditional birth attendants perform distinct yet overlapping functions at the primary healthcare level. Their daily tasks focus heavily on health promotion, basic monitoring, and navigating community members to higher centers.

CadrePrimary FocusKey Activities
Community Health WorkersPreventive and basic curative careImmunization mobilization, ante-natal health talks, rapid diagnostic testing, and vital signs recording.
Traditional Birth AttendantsPregnancy support and childbirth deliveryEmotional support during labor, traditional massages, herbal preparations, and identifying complicated labor.

A common pitfall among student midwives is dismissing traditional birth attendants entirely. Instead, recognizing their profound trust among villagers allows formal health workers to transform TBAs from potential competitors into active birth companions who facilitate early referrals.

Designing Effective Supportive Supervision Frameworks

Traditional supervision often relies on fault-finding, intimidation, and strict checklist inspections that leave community workers demoralized. Supportive supervision replaces this punitive approach with guidance, joint problem-solving, and continuous capacity building.

An effective framework involves regular, scheduled visits where midwives and supervisors work alongside community workers. Instead of merely auditing registers, a supportive supervisor observes patient interactions, provides immediate constructive feedback, listens to logistical challenges like lack of supplies, and ensures continuous mentorship. This approach fosters mutual respect, boosts morale, and directly enhances the quality of care delivered at the grassroots.

Integration, Mentorship, and Bridging Traditional and Formal Practices

Bridging the gap between orthodox medicine and traditional practices requires patience and cultural humility. Many traditional birth attendants hold deep-seated beliefs and practices passed down through generations. Forcing an abrupt halt to all traditional methods usually drives practices underground, increasing maternal risks.

Successful integration involves respectful engagement. Midwives invite TBAs to local health facility meetings, train them to recognize danger signs like severe postpartum hemorrhage or malpresentation, and encourage them to accompany laboring women to health clinics. Mentorship sessions focus on eliminating harmful practices—such as abdominal pressure during labor or applying unhygienic substances to the umbilical cord stump—while acknowledging and respecting their role in providing emotional comfort and local postpartum care.

Performance Metrics, Monitoring, and Evaluation Tools

Evaluating community-level maternal health cadres requires practical tools that capture real impact without overwhelming frontline workers with paperwork. Monitoring tracks routine activities, while evaluation measures long-term changes in community health outcomes.

  1. Activity Logs: Simple tally sheets or register books tracking numbers of pregnant women registered, home visits conducted, and referrals made.
  2. Referral Completion Rate: Tracking the proportion of referred women who actually arrive at the comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility.
  3. Community Feedback Meetings: Regular interactive sessions with village elders, mothers, and local leaders to discuss quality of care and attitude of health workers.

Supervisors must ensure that tools are simple enough for cadres with basic literacy to complete accurately, turning data collection into a useful guide for action rather than a bureaucratic chore.
