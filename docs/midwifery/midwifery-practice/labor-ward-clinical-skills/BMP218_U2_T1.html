Admission and Assessment of Laboring Patients

Initial Reception and Welcoming

The first moments a woman arrives in the labor ward set the tone for her entire birth experience. Anxiety and fear can spike adrenaline levels, which may slow down labor progress by reducing effective uterine contractions. Greet her warmly, introduce yourself by name and professional role, and address her respectfully. In many Nigerian hospitals, family presence is valued, so welcoming her chosen birth companion helps reduce her stress. Assess her general physical condition immediately with a quick glance: is she walking calmly, breathing heavily through strong contractions, or showing signs of distress? Offer a comfortable seat or guide her directly to the examination area depending on the urgency of her presentation.

Establishing the Clinical History

Taking a focused history helps determine how far labor has advanced and flags potential risks early. Ask about the estimated due date (EDD), how the contractions started, and whether her membranes have ruptured. If water has broken, note the time and the color of the fluid—green or brown staining suggests meconium passage, which requires urgent attention. Review her antenatal card carefully, checking blood pressure trends, hemoglobin levels, blood group, and results for infections like HIV or syphilis. Inquire about past pregnancies, previous caesarean sections, and any underlying medical conditions such as sickle cell disease or hypertension.

Abdominal Examination and Leopold's Maneuvers

Abdominal assessment reveals the position, presentation, and estimated size of the fetus before internal examination. Ensure the woman empties her bladder first to avoid discomfort and inaccurate palpation findings.

  • First maneuver (Fundal height): Palpate the upper abdomen to identify which fetal pole occupies the fundus.
  • Second maneuver (Lateral grip): Feel down the sides of the uterus to locate the smooth, hard fetal back versus the smaller, irregular limbs.
  • Third maneuver (Pawlik's grip): Grasp the lower abdomen just above the pubic symphysis to determine what part is presenting and whether it is engaged.
  • Fourth maneuver (Pelvic grip): Face the patient's feet and slide your fingers down the sides of the presenting part to check its descent.

Evaluating Uterine Contractions and Fetal Well-being

Assessing contractions involves timing their frequency, duration, and intensity by placing a warm hand gently on the mother's abdomen. Count the number of contractions in a ten-minute window to check frequency, and time how many seconds each contraction lasts. Feel the firmness of the uterine wall at the peak of a contraction to gauge intensity—mild, moderate, or strong. Simultaneously, monitor the fetal heart rate (FHR) using a Pinard stethoscope or a Doppler ultrasound device. Listen for a full minute immediately after a contraction ends to catch any late decelerations that hint at uteroplacental insufficiency.

Performing the Vaginal Examination

The vaginal examination is a crucial skill that confirms whether true labor has started by assessing cervical changes. Practice strict aseptic technique using sterile gloves and clean antiseptic solution to clean the vulva from front to back. Gently insert the index and middle fingers into the vagina to evaluate cervical dilatation in centimeters, effacement (thinning) as a percentage or length, and consistency. Determine the station of the presenting part relative to the ischial spines and check for the presence of the amniotic sac. Avoid frequent vaginal exams after membranes have ruptured to minimize the risk of ascending infection.

Documentation and Communication of Findings

Accurate record-keeping is both a clinical necessity and a legal safeguard in midwifery practice. Plot all findings clearly on the partograph once active labor is established, noting cervical dilatation, fetal heart rate, and contraction patterns. Communicate your findings effectively to the doctor or senior midwife on duty, especially if any abnormal signs like fetal bradycardia or thick meconium are spotted. Clear handovers ensure the entire healthcare team remains aware of the woman's condition and can act promptly if complications arise.
