Assisting and Conducting Normal Vaginal Deliveries

Preparation and Essential Equipment for Delivery

Getting ready for a normal vaginal delivery requires a systematic approach to ensure both maternal and neonatal safety. Midwives must assemble all necessary tools before the second stage of labor peaks. A well-prepared delivery tray reduces panic and prevents delays if complications arise.

  • Delivery Tray: Contains artery forceps, episiotomy scissors, needle holders, and tissue forceps.
  • Suction Apparatus: Used to clear the newborn's airway if excessive mucus or meconium is present.
  • Cord Clamp Material: Sterile ties or plastic clamps to secure the umbilical cord.
  • Warm Receiving Towels: Essential for drying the baby and preventing rapid heat loss immediately after birth.
  • Suturing Materials: Local anesthetic, catgut or synthetic absorbogenic sutures, and sterile gauze for perineal repair.

Monitoring Maternal and Fetal Well-Being During the Second Stage

During the second stage, the midwife acts as a vigilant observer while the mother actively pushes. Fetal heart rate monitoring must occur frequently, typically auscultated using a Pinard stethoscope or a Doppler device immediately after every contraction. This helps catch early signs of fetal distress, such as prolonged decelerations.

Maternal well-being is equally paramount. Assess her vital signs, including blood pressure, pulse, and temperature, at regular intervals. Watch for signs of exhaustion, dehydration, or emotional distress. Encourage adequate hydration with sips of water and guide her on effective pushing techniques synchronized with uterine contractions to avoid premature maternal fatigue.

Assisting Normal Vaginal Deliveries

Assisting a birth requires clear communication and precise coordination with the primary birth attendant or managing the supporting role under supervision. The assistant ensures the sterile field remains intact, hands over instruments promptly, and keeps a close eye on blood loss.

Positioning the mother correctly is a major responsibility of the assistant. Lithotomy or left lateral positions are common depending on local clinical protocols and maternal preference. The assistant also provides psychological reassurance, keeping the room calm and focused so the mother can channel her energy into effective expulsive efforts.

Conducting Normal Vaginal Deliveries

Conducting a delivery means taking direct charge of managing the birth of the baby as the head crowns. The midwife protects the perineum to prevent severe lacerations and controls the speed of the baby's head delivery.

  1. Crowning: The widest diameter of the fetal head stretches the vulvar ring and does not recede between contractions.
  2. Head Delivery: Control the head with gentle upward or outward pressure to encourage extension and prevent rapid expulsion.
  3. Checking for Cord: Feel gently around the baby's neck for a nuchal cord. If present and loose, slip it over the head; if tight, clamp and cut it.
  4. Shoulder Delivery: Allow spontaneous restitution, then guide the anterior shoulder down under the pubic arch, followed by lifting the baby up to deliver the posterior shoulder.
  5. Complete Birth: The rest of the body usually follows swiftly with minimal assistance.

Immediate Care of the Newborn at Birth

The moment a baby is born marks the critical transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life. The midwife must act quickly yet calmly to evaluate the newborn.

Place the baby immediately on the mother's abdomen for skin-to-skin contact, provided there are no signs of distress requiring resuscitation. Dry the baby thoroughly with a warm towel while simultaneously assessing breathing, tone, and color. Assign the initial Apgar score at one minute and again at five minutes. Delay routine procedures like weighing and bathing to protect the sacred first hour of bonding and successful initiation of breastfeeding.

Management of the Third Stage of Labor

The third stage spans from the birth of the baby until the complete expulsion of the placenta and membranes. This is a high-risk period due to the potential for postpartum hemorrhage.

Active management of the third stage of labor is standard practice in most clinical settings to reduce bleeding risks. It involves three key steps: administering a uterotonic drug (such as oxytocin) within one minute of birth, performing controlled cord traction while counter-pressure is applied to the uterus (the Brandt-Andrews maneuver), and massaging the uterus after the placenta is delivered to ensure it remains well-contracted. Inspect the delivered placenta carefully to ensure no lobes or membranes are missing.

Postpartum Assessment and Immediate Maternal Care

Once the placenta is delivered, the immediate postpartum period begins. This time requires frequent observation of the mother's vital signs and uterine tone to catch early complications.

Palpate the uterus every fifteen minutes during the first hour to ensure it stays firm and contracted (often called the 'contraction of safety'). Monitor lochia flow for color, amount, and presence of large clots. Check the perineal repair or intact perineum for swelling, hematoma formation, or bleeding. Promote early mobilization, offer a comforting meal or drink, and facilitate bonding while supporting the mother's transition into the postnatal ward.
