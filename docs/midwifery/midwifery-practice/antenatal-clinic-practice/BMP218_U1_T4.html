Documentation in Antenatal Cards and Registers

Introduction to Antenatal Record Keeping

Antenatal record keeping forms the backbone of safe maternal and child health services in Nigerian healthcare facilities. Every clinical interaction, observation, and intervention during pregnancy must be systematically recorded. Good documentation provides a continuous medical history, ensuring that any midwife or doctor taking over care can instantly understand the client's risk status and ongoing needs. Without reliable records, continuity of care breaks down, making it difficult to spot developing complications like pre-eclampsia or intrauterine growth restriction early enough to intervene effectively.

The Antenatal Card: Structure and Purpose

The antenatal card, often called the mother's card or clinic card, acts as a portable medical summary held by the pregnant woman. She must bring this card to every clinic appointment and carry it with her wherever she goes in case of emergency.

  • Biodata and Social History: Captures name, age, address, occupation, and next of kin.
  • Obstetric History: Details previous pregnancies, parity, mode of delivery, and complications.
  • Current Pregnancy Details: Records gestational age, fundal height, fetal heart rate, and urine test results at each visit.
  • Emergency Contact Plan: Outlines transport arrangements and blood donor availability for sudden complications.

Facility Antenatal Registers

While the antenatal card stays with the client, clinic registers remain permanent legal books kept securely within the health facility. These facility registers compile data for all clients attending the clinic on a daily and monthly basis. They are vital for generating health management information system (HMIS) reports, tracking clinic attendance trends, and calculating maternal mortality or morbidity indicators for public health planning. Midwives must enter details immediately after attending to each client to avoid omission.

Capturing Essential Information During Booking and Return Visits

The booking visit is the most comprehensive assessment a pregnant woman receives. Midwives must record baseline investigations, blood group, genotype, serology results, and a complete medical, surgical, and family history. Subsequent visits focus on monitoring changes. At every return visit, the midwife must record weight, blood pressure, urinalysis, symphysis-fundal height, fetal lie and presentation, and fetal heart tone. Missing any of these routine parameters can delay the detection of gestational hypertension or fetal distress.

Legal, Ethical, and Professional Standards in Documentation

Midwifery documentation is a legal record that can be subpoenaed in a court of law during medico-legal investigations. Entries must be accurate, legible, factual, and signed with the date and time clearly indicated. Midwives must never falsify records, backdate entries, or use correction fluid (tippex) to cover up mistakes. Ethical practice demands client confidentiality; clinical records must never be left exposed where unauthorized persons can view sensitive personal and medical data.

Common Errors and Quality Assurance Strategies

Common documentation errors include missing signatures, omitted dates, illegible handwriting, and failing to record negative findings. Quality assurance requires regular clinic audits where senior midwives and facility managers review selected antenatal cards and registers to ensure compliance with standards. Midwives should embrace constructive feedback from these audits to improve their record-keeping habits and safeguard clinical excellence across the unit.
