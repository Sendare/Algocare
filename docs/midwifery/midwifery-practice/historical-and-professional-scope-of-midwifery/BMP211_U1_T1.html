History and Evolution of Midwifery Globally and in Nigeria

Ancient Roots and Traditional Midwifery Practices Worldwide

Childbirth has always required assistance, long before modern hospitals or medical degrees existed. In ancient civilizations like Egypt, Greece, and Mesopotamia, community women with practical experience attended to laboring mothers. These early attendants relied on herbal remedies, massage, and gravity-assisted positions to manage childbirth. Birth was viewed primarily as a natural, domestic, and spiritual event managed entirely by women within the community. Midwives passed their knowledge down orally from generation to generation, serving as the sole guardians of maternal health for centuries.

The Institutionalization and Medicalization of Childbirth in the West

During the 18th and 19th centuries in Europe and North America, childbirth shifted from the home to the hospital. The rise of modern medicine introduced male physicians into a space previously reserved for female attendants. Instruments like forceps and the widespread use of anesthesia changed how labor was managed. While this medical shift reduced some causes of maternal death, it also marginalized traditional midwives and turned a normal physiological process into a medical condition requiring institutional control.

Indigenous Maternity Care and Traditional Birth Attendants in Pre-Colonial Nigeria

Long before the arrival of colonial administration, diverse ethnic groups across Nigeria had well-established systems of maternal care. Traditional Birth Attendants, often older women respected for their wisdom and spiritual insight, managed pregnancies and deliveries. They utilized local herbs, abdominal massage, ritual prayers, and dietary advice to support the mother. These practitioners held a vital social and health role within their villages, ensuring that pregnant women received continuous community support throughout gestation and the postpartum period.

Introduction of Western-Style Midwifery during the Colonial Era in Nigeria

British colonial rule in the late 19th and early 20th centuries brought formal western medicine to Nigeria, primarily to serve colonial officers and expatriates. Missionaries and colonial governments later established the first hospitals and maternity homes for the local population. This era saw the introduction of organized midwifery training aimed at replacing or regulating traditional practices. Pioneering training schools began teaching Western hygiene, basic anatomy, and safe delivery techniques to Nigerian women, laying the groundwork for professional nursing and midwifery.

Post-Independence Reforms and the Formalization of Nursing and Midwifery Education

Following Nigeria's independence in 1960, healthcare expansion became a national priority. The government and regional health authorities worked to scale up training institutions to produce indigenous healthcare professionals. Nursing and midwifery education underwent structured reforms, unifying curricula and setting clearer entry requirements. Training programs shifted from basic apprenticeship models to more rigorous, classroom-and-hospital-based curricula designed to meet international standards while addressing local disease burdens.

Evolution of Regulatory Bodies and Professional Associations in Nigeria

To maintain professional standards, protect the public, and guide practice, regulatory bodies were established in Nigeria. The creation of the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria stands as a major milestone, taking charge of examining, registering, and disciplining practitioners. Alongside regulation, professional associations like the National Association of Nigerian Nurses and Midwives emerged to advocate for practitioner welfare and professional ethics. These organizations ensure that practitioners maintain competence throughout their careers.

Modern Midwifery: Bridging Global Standards with Local Realities in Nigeria

Today, Nigerian midwifery faces the challenge of aligning international best practices with local infrastructural realities. Midwives operate in diverse settings, from well-equipped urban teaching hospitals to under-resourced rural primary health centers. Efforts like task-shifting policies, community midwifery programs, and digital health tools aim to reduce high maternal and newborn mortality rates. Modern midwives are not just delivery attendants; they are public health advocates, educators, and leaders working to ensure safe motherhood for every Nigerian woman.
