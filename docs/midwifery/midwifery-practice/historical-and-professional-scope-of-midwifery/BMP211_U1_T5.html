Midwife-Led Care Models and Continuity of Care

Understanding Midwife-Led Care Models and Continuity of Care

Midwife-led care is a professional approach where a midwife, or a team of midwives, takes primary responsibility for planning, organizing, and delivering care during pregnancy, labor, and the postnatal period. This model is built on the philosophy that pregnancy and birth are normal physiological processes requiring minimal medical intervention unless complications arise.

At the heart of this philosophy is continuity of care, which ensures that a woman receives support from a known midwife or a small, familiar group of midwives throughout her childbearing journey. Unlike fragmented care where a patient meets a different practitioner at every antenatal visit, continuity builds a trusting relationship. This sustained connection reduces anxiety, fosters open communication, and allows midwives to pick up subtle physical or emotional changes early. In Nigeria, where healthcare settings can sometimes feel intimidating or overcrowded, this personalized relationship gives women a comforting anchor through one of life's most vulnerable transitions.

Core Principles of Continuous Midwifery Support

Continuous midwifery support relies on several foundational pillars that guide clinical decisions and interpersonal interactions. These principles distinguish midwifery practice from traditional disease-oriented medical models.

  • Woman-centred care: Placing the individual needs, cultural preferences, and personal values of the mother at the center of all decision-making.
  • Empowerment: Providing clear, evidence-based information so women can make informed choices about their own bodies and birth plans.
  • Physiological norm protection: Actively safeguarding the natural progress of labor through non-pharmacological comfort measures before resorting to artificial augmentation.
  • Partnership: Working alongside the woman and her family rather than adopting a paternalistic, directive stance.

Clinical and Psychological Benefits for Mother and Newborn

Extensive global research demonstrates that continuous care models yield remarkable outcomes for mothers and babies. When women feel secure and respected by their birth attendants, physiological stress decreases, leading to smoother labors.

Beyond these clinical markers, mothers report higher satisfaction scores because they feel heard and respected during critical moments of decision-making.

Caseload vs. Team Midwifery: Delivery Structures in Practice

Implementing continuity requires organizing how midwives work. Two primary structural models dominate global practice: caseload midwifery and team midwifery.

In caseload midwifery, a primary midwife carries a defined caseload of women—often around 40 per year—and provides their antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care. A backup midwife, known to the client, steps in when the primary midwife is off duty. This model achieves the highest levels of true continuity.

In team midwifery, a group of four to eight midwives works together to care for a larger pool of women. While the woman may not know every midwife on the team, she will meet a familiar face during antenatal visits and have a high likelihood of knowing the attendant on duty at birth. Team midwifery offers better work-life balance and prevents the severe burnout frequently seen in individual caseload models.

Integrating Midwife-Led Models into the Nigerian Healthcare System

Adapting Western-style midwife-led care to the Nigerian context requires navigating unique systemic realities. Nigeria's healthcare landscape features a mix of primary health care (PHC) centres, secondary general hospitals, and tertiary teaching hospitals, alongside a vibrant informal sector of traditional birth attendants (TBAs).

Primary health centres are the ideal grassroots setting for midwife-led care, especially given task-shifting policies designed to bridge severe workforce shortages. However, successful integration faces hurdles such as erratic power supply, poor referral logistics, and infrastructural deficits. Furthermore, many urban Nigerian women lean toward high-technology obstetric care in tertiary facilities out of a belief that 'more technology equals better safety.' Midwives working in Nigeria must actively build community trust, demonstrate the safety of physiological birth, and establish robust, functioning referral pipelines to secondary and tertiary centres when complications arise.

Overcoming Operational and Interprofessional Challenges

The expansion of midwife-led care often encounters institutional friction, particularly from medical colleagues who may misunderstand the scope of midwifery practice. Interprofessional rivalry can lead to defensive medical practices, restricted clinical autonomy, and bottlenecks in referral pathways.

Operational challenges compound these professional barriers. Midwives face heavy workloads, irregular shift hours, and inadequate compensation, which threaten the sustainability of continuity models. Overcoming these obstacles requires deliberate institutional reforms:

  1. Clear protocols: Establishing mutually agreed-upon guidelines that define exact thresholds for obstetric referral.
  2. Interprofessional education: Training medical and midwifery students together to foster mutual respect and collaborative teamwork.
  3. Administrative backing: Securing policy support from hospital management to protect protected time and fair remuneration for on-call midwives.
