Definition, Role, and Scope of Practice of a Midwife

Official Definition of a Midwife

A midwife is a recognized healthcare professional who has successfully completed a midwifery education program that is duly recognized in the country where it is located. This program must be based on the essential competencies for basic midwifery practice as set out by the International Confederation of Midwives (ICM). Once licensed, the practitioner acquires the full scope of profile practices for midwifery. The definition emphasizes partnership with women to provide the necessary support, care, and advice during pregnancy, labour, and the postpartum period. Midwives also conduct births on their own responsibility and provide care for the newborn and the infant. This care includes preventative measures, the promotion of normal birth, the detection of complications in mother and child, and the access to medical or other appropriate assistance.

Core Roles and Responsibilities in Maternal and Child Health

The duties of a midwife extend far beyond the delivery room. They encompass preventative health care, antenatal screening, intrapartum monitoring, and postnatal rehabilitation. Midwives serve as primary educators for families and communities, promoting breastfeeding, family planning, and proper infant nutrition.

  • Antenatal Care: Monitoring fetal growth, screening for gestational hypertension, and providing routine immunizations.
  • Intrapartum Care: Managing the first, second, and third stages of labor, performing vaginal examinations, and identifying signs of fetal distress.
  • Postnatal Care: Assessing uterine involution, checking lochia, supporting early initiation of breastfeeding, and screening for postpartum depression.
  • Neonatal Care: Conducting immediate newborn resuscitation, checking for congenital abnormalities, and promoting thermal care.

The Scope of Practice: What a Midwife Can and Cannot Do

A midwife's scope of practice defines the boundaries of professional action permitted by law and professional regulatory bodies like the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN). Understanding these boundaries keeps mothers safe and protects the practitioner from legal liability.

  • What a Midwife Can Do: Diagnose and manage normal pregnancies, perform routine episiotomies and simple perineal repairs, administer specified oxytocic drugs in the third stage of labor, and provide family planning counselling.
  • What a Midwife Cannot Do: Perform elective Caesarean sections, manage severe eclampsia without medical consultation, prescribe medications outside the approved midwifery pharmacopeia, or perform surgical procedures unrelated to normal obstetric care.

Autonomous Practice and Collaborative Care

Midwifery is recognized as an autonomous profession, meaning a licensed midwife takes full professional responsibility for their own clinical decisions during normal childbearing processes. Autonomy empowers midwives to admit clients, manage normal labor independently, and discharge healthy mother-baby dyads.

However, midwifery does not exist in isolation. Collaborative care is vital because pregnancy can transition from normal to high-risk very quickly. Effective communication and clear referral pathways ensure that when complications arise—such as severe pre-eclampsia, malpresentation, or prolonged obstructed labor—the client is seamlessly transferred to a medical officer or obstetrician. True professional maturity involves knowing when to manage independently and when to call for multidisciplinary backup.

Legal, Ethical, and Professional Boundaries in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the practice of midwifery is strictly regulated by the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) established under the Nursing and Midwifery Registration Act. The Council sets the curriculum, conducts professional examinations, issues practicing licenses, and enforces discipline through the disciplinary tribunal.

Ethical practice requires midwives to maintain patient confidentiality, respect cultural beliefs, obtain valid informed consent, and practice within their acquired competence. Professional misconduct, such as negligence, illegal abortion, or practicing with an expired license, attracts severe legal penalties including the withdrawal of the practicing license. Midwives must renew their licenses periodically and complete mandatory continuing professional development (MCPD) to stay current with modern evidence-based practices.
