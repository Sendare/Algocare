Management of Major Postpartum Haemorrhage (PPH Bundle, B-Lynch, Tamponade)

Immediate Recognition and Escalation of Major PPH

Postpartum haemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal mortality across Nigeria. Recognizing the transition from normal physiological blood loss to a life-threatening emergency requires constant vigilance. Midwives must never rely solely on visual estimation, as blood loss is notoriously underestimated during delivery.

  • Primary PPH: Blood loss exceeding 500 mL following a vaginal birth, or 1,000 mL following a caesarean section, occurring within the first 24 hours.
  • Major PPH: Cumulative blood loss reaching 1,000 mL or more, or any blood loss accompanied by signs of clinical shock.
  • Clinical Shock Signs: Tachycardia, falling blood pressure, tachypnoea, delayed capillary refill, and altered maternal consciousness.

Immediate escalation saves lives. The moment blood loss crosses the threshold or maternal vital signs begin to drift, the midwife must sound the emergency alarm, mobilize the multidisciplinary team, and initiate the standardized PPH response bundle.

Executing the PPH Resuscitation Bundle

When major PPH strikes, systematic action replaces panic. The resuscitation bundle follows the MEMO or standard emergency checklist approach: Music to silence, or rather, communication that is clear and direct, focusing on the 4 Ts (Tone, Tissue, Trauma, Thrombin).

Two large-bore intravenous cannulas (16G or 18G) must be sited immediately. Draw blood for urgent laboratory investigations: full blood count, clotting profile, cross-matching at least four units of blood, and kidney function tests. Infuse warmed crystalloids rapidly while awaiting blood products.

Call for help early—inform the obstetrician, anaesthetist, blood bank, and senior nursing staff. Keep the mother warm using blankets to prevent hypothermia, which worsens coagulopathy. Administer high-flow oxygen via a face mask to maintain tissue perfusion.

Uterine Massage and Medical Therapy

Uterine atony accounts for the vast majority of postpartum haemorrhage cases. The initial mechanical response is bimanual compression and continuous bimanual uterine massage to stimulate muscular contraction.

Medical management follows a tiered pharmacological ladder:

  • Oxytocin: First-line uterotonic. Administer 10 IU slow IV bolus or 40 IU in 1,000 mL crystalloid infusion.
  • Ergometrine: Second-line agent (0.5 mg IM or slow IV). Contraindicated in women with severe hypertension or pre-eclampsia.
  • Misoprostol: Prostaglandin E1 analogue, given as 800 mcg to 1,000 mcg sublingually or rectally when injectables are unavailable.
  • Carboprost: 15-methyl prostaglandin F2-alpha (250 mcg intramyometrial or deep IM). Avoid in asthmatic patients due to bronchospasm risk.
  • Tranexamic Acid (TXA): Administer 1 gram IV over 10 minutes within 3 hours of birth to inhibit clot breakdown.

Intrauterine Balloon Tamponade Techniques

When medical therapy fails to control uterine atony, mechanical tamponade provides a rapid, uterus-preserving solution. An intrauterine balloon applies outward hydrostatic pressure directly against the bleeding myometrial sinuses, halting capillary and venous oozing.

Specific devices like the Bakri balloon or improvised alternatives such as a condom catheter tied securely to a Foley catheter are utilized. The device is inserted transcervically into the uterine cavity under aseptic conditions. Once inside, the balloon is filled with sterile normal saline (typically between 300 to 500 mL) until resistance is felt or bleeding stops.

Continuous drainage through the central lumen of the device is vital. If blood accumulates silently above the balloon, the uterus can re-expand unnoticed, masking ongoing internal haemorrhage. Broad-spectrum prophylactic antibiotics are given to reduce infection risks during balloon retention, which usually lasts for 24 hours before gradual deflation.

Surgical Intervention: The B-Lynch Brace Suture

If medical management and balloon tamponade fail, surgical conservation of the uterus remains a priority, especially for women desiring future fertility. The B-Lynch brace suture is a compression technique designed to mechanically compress the anterior and posterior walls of the uterus together.

Performed during laparotomy, the procedure involves using a large absorbable suture (such as No. 2 Chromic Catgut) on a round-bodied needle. The suture enters the lower uterine segment, loops over the fundus, compresses the cavity vertically, and ties securely.

Success depends on accurate placement and prior confirmation that the bleeding is purely atonic and not caused by uncorrected coagulopathy or trauma. Complications, though rare, can include uterine necrosis if stitches are tied excessively tight or tissue erosion.

Escalation to Devascularization and Hysterectomy

When conservative surgical measures fail to achieve haemostasis, escalation is mandatory to save the mother's life. Delaying definitive surgery while a patient exsanguinates is a fatal error.

  • Uterine and Ovarian Artery Ligation: Stepwise surgical ligation of the ascending branches of the uterine artery, ovarian vessels, or internal iliac (hypogastric) arteries to reduce pelvic pulse pressure.
  • Emergency Obstetric Hysterectomy: The definitive life-saving treatment. Total or subtotal hysterectomy removes the bleeding organ entirely when all conservative interventions are exhausted.

Clear communication with the operating theatre team and blood bank is critical as massive transfusion protocols are enacted simultaneously.

Multidisciplinary Team Roles and Post-Resuscitation Care

Managing major PPH requires seamless teamwork. The midwife manages the airway, monitors vitals, and administers drugs; the obstetrician performs surgical interventions; the anaesthetist manages fluid resuscitation and blood pressure; and laboratory scientists ensure rapid blood product availability.

Once the bleeding stops and the patient is stable, post-resuscitation care begins:

  • Transfer the patient to the Intensive Care Unit or High Dependency Unit for close monitoring.
  • Continue monitoring lochia, uterine tone, urine output via an indwelling catheter, and vital signs every 15 minutes, spacing out as stability is proven.
  • Provide psychological debriefing for the mother and her family, as near-miss traumatic experiences often lead to postpartum post-traumatic stress disorder.
