Resuscitation of Patients in Obstetric Shock

Understanding Obstetric Shock and the Nigerian Context

Obstetric shock is a life-threatening condition characterized by the failure of the circulatory system to maintain adequate perfusion to vital organs during pregnancy or the puerperium. In Nigeria, this condition remains a major driver of maternal mortality, heavily linked to postpartum hemorrhage, severe sepsis, and ruptured uterus. When a parturient loses a significant volume of blood or suffers profound systemic vasodilation, compensatory mechanisms such as tachycardia and peripheral vasoconstriction activate to preserve blood flow to the heart and brain. Midwives must recognize that a pregnant woman's body masks blood loss remarkably well due to her expanded blood volume. By the time her blood pressure drops visibly, she may have already lost over thirty percent of her circulating blood volume. Resource constraints in secondary and primary health facilities across Nigeria, including erratic power supply and delayed blood banking services, make early detection and rapid stabilization the most critical interventions to save a mother's life.

Identifying Early Warning Signs and Clinical Presentation

The clinical presentation of obstetric shock evolves through distinct stages. Early recognition relies on spotting subtle shifts long before the patient collapses. Midwives should routinely assess the following key indicators:

  • Tachycardia: Often the very first sign of hypovolemia as the heart beats faster to maintain cardiac output.
  • Tachypnea: Rapid, shallow breathing triggered by cellular oxygen hunger and metabolic acidosis.
  • Altered Mental Status: Restlessness, anxiety, confusion, or lethargy indicate reduced cerebral perfusion.
  • Capillary Refill Time: Sluggish return of color to the nail beds beyond two seconds points to peripheral vasoconstriction.
  • Oliguria: Urine output dropping below 30 mL per hour signals that the kidneys are being deprived of adequate blood flow.

Immediate Actions and the ABCD Approach

When faced with an obstetric emergency, immediate and structured action saves lives. The standard ABCD resuscitation mnemonic provides a reliable framework for midwives to stabilize the patient while simultaneously calling for multidisciplinary backup.

  1. Airway: Ensure the airway is clear. If the patient is unconscious, position her in the left lateral tilt to prevent aortocaval compression by the gravid uterus.
  2. Breathing: Assess respiratory effort and administer high-flow oxygen via a tight-fitting face mask to maximize tissue oxygenation.
  3. Circulation: Secure two large-bore intravenous cannulas (16G or 14G) immediately. Draw blood for baseline investigations, cross-matching, and coagulation studies while starting fluid resuscitation.
  4. Disability and Dysfunction: Evaluate the conscious level using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale, and check for immediate sources of bleeding or sepsis.

Fluid Resuscitation Protocols and Blood Product Administration

Restoring circulating volume is a cornerstone of managing hypovolemic obstetric shock. Crystalloids such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate should be initiated rapidly, but clinicians must avoid excessive fluid administration, which can dilute clotting factors and worsen bleeding. When massive hemorrhage is suspected, whole blood or component therapy including packed cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets should be requested without delay. In many Nigerian centers where blood banking delays occur, family replacement donors or hospital emergency blood stock must be mobilized instantly. Midwives must monitor vital signs continuously during transfusions to detect transfusion reactions or fluid overload early.

Addressing Underlying Causes: Hemorrhage, Sepsis, and Pre-eclampsia

Resuscitating a patient in shock will fail if the primary underlying trigger is left uncorrected. Midwives must simultaneously treat the root cause while performing general resuscitation.

CauseImmediate Midwifery Intervention
Postpartum HemorrhageBimanual uterine compression, uterotonic drugs (oxytocin, misoprostol), uterine massage, and exploring for retained tissue.
Septic ShockPrompt administration of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, taking blood cultures, and removing the source of infection (such as retained products of conception).
Severe Pre-eclampsia/EclampsiaControlling blood pressure with antihypertensives and administering magnesium sulfate to prevent or stop seizures.

Monitoring, Complications, and Inter-Facility Referral

Continuous reassessment is mandatory following the initial resuscitation phase. Midwives should utilize a modified early warning chart to track trends in pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and urine output. Complications such as acute kidney injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and multi-organ failure can develop rapidly if tissue perfusion is restored too late. When a patient's condition exceeds the capacity of the primary or secondary health facility, prompt and safe inter-facility referral is necessary. The referral process requires stabilizing the patient first, ensuring an IV line and oxygen remain running during transit, and sending a trained healthcare provider along with a detailed referral note detailing all interventions given.
