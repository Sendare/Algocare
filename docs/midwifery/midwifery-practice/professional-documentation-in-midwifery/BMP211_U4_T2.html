Partograph Design, Components, and Plotting Principles

Introduction to the Partograph in Nigerian Midwifery Practice

The partograph is a pictorial overview of labor used to monitor the progress of childbirth and record maternal and fetal well-being. In Nigerian maternity units, it serves as a vital clinical tool to prevent prolonged and obstructed labor, which remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and obstetric fistula. Midwives are expected to commence plotting as soon as a woman enters the active phase of labor. Routine utilization of this chart aids early detection of deviations from normal progress, guiding timely referrals from primary health centers to secondary or tertiary facilities.

Structural Design and Layout of the Standard Partograph

The standard World Health Organization partograph layout is divided into distinct sections to capture all necessary clinical observations on a single sheet of paper.

  • Patient Identification: Administrative details such as name, hospital number, parity, and time of rupture of membranes.
  • Fetal Condition: Top grid tracking heart rate, amniotic fluid color, and cranial molding.
  • Progress of Labor: Middle grid featuring cervical dilatation curves, descent of the head, and uterine contractions.
  • Maternal Condition: Lower section recording pulse, blood pressure, temperature, urine output, and drugs given.

Core Components: Fetal Condition Monitoring

Monitoring the unborn baby involves three primary parameters recorded on the upper tiers of the partograph.

  1. Fetal Heart Rate: Counted and recorded every 30 minutes using a fetoscope or Doppler. Normal range is 110 to 160 beats per minute.
  2. Amniotic Fluid: Assessed during vaginal examinations and designated with specific letters: I for intact membranes, C for clear liquor, M for meconium-stained liquor, and B for blood-stained liquor.
  3. Molding: Indicates how much the fetal skull bones overlap as they navigate the pelvis, graded from zero (separated bones) to plus three (severely overlapping bones locked together).

Core Components: Progress of Labor and Cervical Dilatation

The middle section of the chart tracks the mechanical progress of labor. Cervical dilatation is measured in centimeters during vaginal examinations and plotted with an 'X' on the graph. Descent of the fetal head is measured using abdominal palpation in fifths palpable above the pelvic brim, plotted with a circle ('O'). Uterine contractions are assessed over a ten-minute window, noting their frequency and duration, and represented on the grid using shading or hatching boxes.

Core Components: Maternal Condition and Drug Administration

Maternal well-being is just as critical as fetal monitoring and occupies the lower portion of the partograph. Pulse rate is recorded every 30 minutes with a dot, while blood pressure is measured every four hours and marked with vertical arrows. Temperature is checked every two to four hours. Urinalysis for protein and ketone levels is documented whenever urine is passed. All medications, especially oxytocin and intravenous fluids, along with the exact dosage and time administered, must be clearly entered in the corresponding rows at the bottom.

Standard Rules and Principles for Accurate Plotting

Accurate partograph recording relies on consistency, precision, and adherence to established documentation rules. Time is recorded in elapsed hours or actual clock time depending on the local hospital policy, but whichever is chosen must remain consistent. Vaginal examinations should be performed strictly every four hours unless a clinical indication warrants a more frequent check. Every entry must be made promptly following clinical assessment to prevent recall errors. Midwives must never falsify entries or retroactively plot data if observations were missed.

Navigating Alert and Action Lines to Detect Prolonged Labor

The graphical grid for cervical dilatation contains two critical diagonal reference lines that guide clinical decision-making.

  • Alert Line: Starts at four centimeters of cervical dilatation and moves rightward at a rate of one centimeter per hour. If the patient's plotting line crosses to the right of the alert line, it serves as a warning that labor may be prolonged, and the woman should be transferred to a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility if she is at a primary health clinic.
  • Action Line: Placed four hours to the right of and parallel to the alert line. When the dilatation line reaches or crosses the action line, definitive medical intervention, such as augmentation or cesarean section, must be undertaken.
