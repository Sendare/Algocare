Incident Reporting and Adverse Event Management in Maternity Care

Understanding Clinical Risk and Safety in Maternity Units

Maternity care involves high-stakes environments where two lives are managed simultaneously, making clinical risk management a daily priority for midwives in Nigeria. A clinical risk is any event or circumstance that could lead to unintended physical or psychological harm to a mother or her baby. Maternity units face unique vulnerabilities due to the rapid, unpredictable nature of labor and delivery. When safety protocols are weak or communication breaks down between the obstetric team, midwives, and auxiliary staff, preventable adverse events occur.

Building a safe maternity unit relies on recognizing that errors are rarely caused by a single careless individual. Instead, system vulnerabilities—such as staff shortages, broken equipment, or missing supplies—usually create the conditions for mistakes. Midwives must look beyond immediate blame and understand how these underlying workplace factors contribute to patient risk.

Defining Incidents, Near Misses, and Adverse Events

Clear definitions help maternity teams categorize safety events accurately. Without standard terms, staff struggle to report issues consistently.

  • Adverse Event: An incident that results in unintended physical or psychological harm to the mother or baby, ranging from minor trauma to severe morbidity or mortality.
  • Near Miss: An unplanned event that had the potential to cause harm, but missed the patient purely by chance or through timely intervention. For example, administering a wrong drug dose that is caught by a second midwife before entering the IV line.
  • Sentinel Event: A severe, unexpected occurrence involving death or serious physical or psychological injury, such as a uterine rupture leading to neonatal demise.
  • No-Harm Incident: An event that reached the patient but caused no discernible injury, like receiving an incorrect blood type without experiencing a transfusion reaction due to rapid stopping.

Immediate Actions and Patient Safety First Aid

When an adverse event or acute crisis occurs in the labor ward, the immediate priority is always clinical stabilization. Midwives must pivot instantly from routine monitoring to emergency intervention.

  1. Secure Patient Safety: Immediately remove the immediate source of harm, such as stopping an incorrect infusion or moving away a faulty monitor.
  2. Provide Clinical Care: Call for emergency medical assistance and initiate resuscitation protocols for mother or baby as required.
  3. Inform Senior Staff: Alert the lead consultant, matron, or hospital management without delay.
  4. Preserve Evidence: Do not discard broken equipment, empty ampoules, or contaminated packaging, as these are vital for subsequent investigation.

Incident Reporting Procedures and Documentation

An incident report is an official administrative record used by healthcare institutions to track safety breaches and improve system performance. In many Nigerian hospitals, fear of punishment prevents junior staff from completing these reports honestly.

Midwives must understand that incident forms are distinct from clinical case notes. The official incident report goes to the hospital quality improvement department, while the clinical notes must contain an objective, factual record of the medical care provided. Avoid writing emotional commentary, blame, or speculation in either record. State clearly what was observed, what actions were taken, and how the patient responded, maintaining professionalism at all times.

Root Cause Analysis and Investigating Near Misses

To stop repeat errors, maternity units use Root Cause Analysis (RCA), a structured investigative method that looks deep into the operational layers of an event. Instead of asking 'Who made the mistake?', RCA asks 'Why did the system allow this mistake to happen?'

Investigators look at multiple contributing factors including communication breakdowns, heavy workloads, lack of training, and equipment design flaws. Investigating near misses using the same rigorous framework offers high preventive value. Because near misses happen much more frequently than actual adverse events, analyzing them provides a window into hidden system flaws before a mother or baby suffers permanent harm.

Open Disclosure and Communicating with Families

Open disclosure is the transparent, honest process of discussing an adverse event with the affected mother and her family. When things go wrong during childbirth, families deserve a truthful explanation without corporate defensiveness or medical jargon.

Midwives and obstetricians must coordinate to deliver the news in a private, quiet space as soon as the patient is clinically stable. The conversation should include an expression of sincere regret for what happened, a clear account of the known facts, an outline of immediate support services, and an assurance that an investigation is underway. Practicing open disclosure builds trust and helps families process trauma, reducing the likelihood of adversarial legal disputes.

Learning Culture and Implementing Preventive Systems

Reporting incidents and conducting investigations hold no value unless hospitals use the findings to drive real change. Moving from a punitive culture—where staff hide mistakes out of fear—to a learning culture is essential for modern maternity units.

Once an RCA identifies a system vulnerability, management must implement corrective safeguards. These can include upgrading outdated obstetric equipment, standardizing emergency drug trays, introducing structured shift handovers (such as SBAR), or organizing mandatory drills for postpartum hemorrhage and shoulder dystocia. Sustained safety improvements depend on regular audit cycles to verify whether new preventive systems are working effectively across all shifts.
