History Taking and Obstetric History Formulation

Introduction to Midwifery History Taking and Obstetric History Formulation

History taking is the foundational clinical skill in midwifery practice. It is the systematic gathering of a pregnant woman's past and present health information to guide safe clinical care. In Nigerian maternity settings, where resources can vary between primary health centres and tertiary hospitals, a well-taken history often provides the first critical clues about potential complications.

Obstetric history formulation goes beyond simply asking a few questions. It is the structured process of synthesizing biomedical, psychosocial, and cultural details into a coherent clinical picture. This process allows midwives to assess baseline risk, tailor antenatal care, and anticipate emergencies such as postpartum haemorrhage or obstructed labour before they occur.

Establishing Rapport and Creating a Safe Environment

A successful consultation begins long before the first clinical question is asked. Establishing rapport creates a therapeutic bridge between the midwife and the pregnant woman. In many Nigerian communities, pregnant women may feel anxious about hospital procedures, judgmental attitudes, or language barriers.

Creating a safe environment requires active listening, culturally sensitive communication, and maintaining strict confidentiality. Midwives should greet the woman respectfully, use her preferred language or title, and ensure physical privacy. When a woman feels respected and heard, she is much more likely to disclose sensitive information, such as previous traditional treatments, domestic abuse, or herbal use, which directly impacts obstetric safety.

Structuring the General Medical and Social History

The general medical and social history uncovers underlying health conditions that may complicate pregnancy or childbirth. Midwives must methodically review body systems while also exploring the woman's home and community environment.

  • Past Medical History: Document chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, sickle cell disease, and kidney disorders. Inquire about previous blood transfusions and major surgeries.
  • Surgical History: Pay close attention to any prior uterine surgeries, such as myomectomy or previous caesarean sections, as these increase uterine rupture risks.
  • Social History: Assess housing, occupation, and financial stability. Inquire about support systems at home.
  • Lifestyle and Substance Use: Gently ask about alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and the use of over-the-counter or herbal remedies, which are common across many regions.

Decoding the Obstetric History: Parity, Gravidity, and Past Pregnancies

Calculating obstetric history accurately is a core competency for every midwife. Ambiguity in parsing numbers can lead to poor clinical decisions during labour emergencies.

  • Gravida: The total number of times a woman has been pregnant, regardless of outcome or gestational age, including the current pregnancy.
  • Parity: The number of pregnancies that reached 28 weeks of gestation or more, whether the baby was born alive or stillborn.

In many West African clinical settings, parity is further detailed using the TPAL system or local modifications to track term births, preterm births, abortions or miscarriages, and living children. Midwives must meticulously interrogate the outcome of every past pregnancy, noting birth weights, modes of delivery (such as vacuum extraction or caesarean section), and postpartum complications like severe bleeding or sepsis.

Uncovering Details of the Present Pregnancy

The present pregnancy history focuses on confirming gestational age, tracking foetal well-being, and identifying current symptoms. The cornerstone of this section is establishing the Last Menstrual Period (LMP) to calculate the Estimated Due Date (EDD) using Naegele's rule.

Midwives must ask about the date of quickening (the first time the mother felt baby movements), results of any ultrasound scans performed, and any early pregnancy warning signs. Common current complaints such as nausea, vomiting, dizziness, or urinary symptoms must be documented and evaluated to differentiate normal physiological adaptations from pathological conditions like pre-eclampsia or gestational diabetes.

Identifying Risk Factors and Red Flags

A critical objective of obstetric history formulation is risk stratification. Midwives must actively hunt for warning signs that can rapidly escalate into obstetric emergencies.

  • Vaginal Bleeding: Any bleeding in pregnancy must be investigated to rule out placenta praevia, placental abruption, or ectopic pregnancy.
  • Severe Headaches and Visual Disturbances: These are classic warning signs of impending eclampsia or severe gestational hypertension.
  • Reduced Foetal Movements: A decrease in foetal kicks can indicate chronic hypoxia or intrauterine foetal compromise.
  • History of Severe Postpartum Haemorrhage: Women with a prior history of excessive bleeding after birth require active management of the third stage of labour in subsequent deliveries.

Documentation and Clinical Communication in Practice

Recording the history is only half the task; effective documentation ensures continuity of care across different shifts and healthcare providers. Every entry in the maternity record must be legible, accurate, and dated with the exact time.

Midwives must avoid vague descriptions and record objective findings alongside the woman's subjective statements. Good documentation protects both the patient and the midwife legally. Furthermore, concise verbal handovers using structured communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) ensure that critical risk factors identified during history taking are successfully communicated to the wider multidisciplinary team.
