Palpation Techniques: Fundal Height, Lie, Presentation, Position, Engagement

Introduction to Abdominal Palpation in Midwifery

Abdominal palpation is a systematic method used by midwives to evaluate fetal growth, well-being, and anatomical relations within the maternal uterus. Performed primarily using the bimanual technique through Leopold's maneuvers, this physical assessment relies on touch to gather crucial clinical data without invasive procedures. Midwives begin these maneuvers after 24 weeks of gestation, a point when fetal parts become distinct enough to identify through the abdominal wall.

Before starting, ensure the mother empties her bladder to promote comfort and improve the accuracy of uterine palpation. Position her comfortably in a semi-recumbent position with her knees slightly bent and abdomen fully exposed from the xiphisternum to the pubic symphysis. Always warm your hands before touching the maternal abdomen to prevent involuntary muscle guarding, which can obscure distinct fetal outlines.

Fundal Height Measurement

Fundal height measurement is a simple yet vital tool for monitoring fetal growth and estimating gestational age. Using a non-elastic tape measure, the midwife places the zero mark at the upper border of the pubic symphysis and stretches the tape along the midline of the abdominal wall to the highest point of the uterine fundus.

  • 12 weeks: Fundus reaches the level of the pubic symphysis.
  • 24 weeks: Fundus reaches the level of the umbilicus.
  • 36 weeks: Fundus reaches the xiphisternum.

Between 20 and 36 weeks of pregnancy, the measurement in centimeters usually matches the gestational age in weeks, plus or minus two centimeters. A discrepancy greater than two centimeters warrants further investigation, such as an ultrasound scan, to rule out conditions like intrauterine growth restriction, polyhydramnios, or multiple gestation.

Determining Fetal Lie

Fetal lie describes the relationship between the long axis of the fetus and the long axis of the maternal uterus. Recognizing the lie tells the midwife how the baby is oriented inside the womb before labor begins.

  • Longitudinal lie: The fetal spine runs parallel to the maternal spine. This is the normal and expected orientation for birth.
  • Transverse lie: The fetal spine lies perpendicular across the maternal abdomen. Vaginal delivery is impossible in this orientation.
  • Oblique lie: The fetal spine rests at an angle across the uterus. This is usually an unstable lie that frequently converts to a longitudinal or transverse lie as labor approaches.

Midwives determine the lie during the first and second Leopold's maneuvers by palpating the fundus and the lateral walls of the abdomen to locate the fetal head and breech.

Identifying Fetal Presentation

Fetal presentation refers to the specific anatomical part of the fetus that leads the way through the birth canal. Identifying the presenting part helps the midwife plan for the mode of delivery.

Cephalic presentation occurs when the head presents first, which includes the vertex, brow, and face types. Breech presentation happens when the buttocks or lower extremities enter the pelvis first, categorized as complete, frank, or footling breech.

To identify presentation, the midwife performs the first and third Leopold's maneuvers. The first maneuver checks the fundus to see what pole occupies the upper uterus. The third maneuver, known as Pawlik's grip, uses the thumb and fingers of one hand just above the pubic symphysis to grasp the lower pole of the uterus, assessing whether it is hard and ballotable like a head, or softer and irregular like the breech.

Assessing Fetal Position

Fetal position describes the relationship of a designated point on the presenting part—known as the denominator—to the right or left side of the maternal pelvis. For cephalic presentations, the denominator is the occiput; for breech presentations, it is the sacrum.

Midwives perform the second Leopold's maneuver to assess position. Facing the mother's head, the midwife slides both hands down the sides of the abdomen, applying gentle pressure on one side while stabilizing the uterus with the other. One side will feel smooth and firm, indicating the fetal back, while the opposite side will feel bumpy and irregular due to small fetal limbs.

Common positions include left occipito-anterior and right occipito-posterior. Knowing the position helps predict the mechanism of labor and guides where to best auscultate the fetal heart rate.

Evaluating Fetal Engagement

Engagement means that the widest diameter of the presenting part has successfully passed through the pelvic inlet. In cephalic presentations, this widest diameter is the biparietal diameter.

To evaluate engagement, the midwife performs the fourth Leopold's maneuver. Facing the mother's feet, the midwife places both hands on the lower abdomen near the groin and moves them downward toward the pelvis. If the fingers converge easily and the presenting part is still movable above the brim, the head is floating and unengaged. If the fingers diverge and the presenting part is fixed deep in the pelvis, engagement has occurred.

Midwives also use the rule of fifths by palpating how many centimeters or finger-breadths of the fetal head remain palpable above the pelvic brim. If two fifths or less of the head can be felt abdominally, the head is considered engaged.

Documenting and Interpreting Findings

Accurate documentation of abdominal palpation findings is essential for tracking fetal progress and sharing information across the healthcare team. Every assessment should clearly record fundal height in centimeters, estimated fetal lie, presentation, position, and engagement status.

Midwives must also note maternal vital signs, fetal heart rate patterns, and any reported fetal movements during the examination. Any unexpected deviations—such as a transverse lie in late pregnancy or a fundal height that does not match expected growth curves—must be reported immediately to the attending physician or obstetrician. Proper records protect the clinical team and ensure continuity of safe care for both mother and baby.
