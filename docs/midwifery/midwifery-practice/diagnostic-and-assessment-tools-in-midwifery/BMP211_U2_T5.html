Vaginal Examination Principles, Indications, and Contraindications

Introduction and Purpose of Vaginal Examination

A vaginal examination (VE), often referred to by midwives as an internal examination, involves inserting gloved fingers into the vagina to assess the lower female reproductive tract. In midwifery practice across Nigeria, this procedure remains a fundamental diagnostic tool used primarily during labor, antenatal care, and the postpartum period. By performing a VE, a midwife gathers vital physical data that cannot be obtained through abdominal palpation alone.

The main clinical objectives of a vaginal examination include:

  • Evaluating cervical status: Determining cervical dilation, effacement, consistency, and position.
  • Assessing fetal progress: Checking the station of the presenting part and determining the fetal position and presentation.
  • Monitoring labor progress: Confirming the onset of true active labor and identifying potential cephalopelvic disproportion.
  • Diagnosing complications: Identifying abnormal bleeding, cord presentation, or premature rupture of membranes.

While ultrasound and other modern technologies offer non-invasive alternatives, the physical VE provides direct tactile feedback essential for immediate intrapartum decision-making. Midwives must weigh the diagnostic benefits against the inherent discomfort and infection risks of the procedure.

Core Principles for Performing a Safe Vaginal Examination

Safety and accuracy during a vaginal examination depend on strict adherence to established clinical principles. A poorly executed examination causes unnecessary physical trauma, increases infection rates, and severely breaches patient trust.

Key principles include:

  • Justification: Never perform a VE out of routine curiosity; every examination must have a clear clinical indication that directly alters care management.
  • Gentleness: Movements must be deliberate and smooth. Forceful insertion or aggressive cervical sweeping causes severe pain and tissue damage.
  • Asepsis: Strict sterile technique prevents ascending pathogens from reaching the uterine cavity.
  • Frequency limitation: Limit the number of examinations during labor—typically every four hours or when a significant clinical change occurs—to minimize infection risks.

A common mistake among student midwives is performing examinations too frequently during early labor, which exhausts the patient and raises intrauterine infection risks without providing useful clinical data.

Clinical Indications: When and Why to Perform the Examination

Midwives must clearly understand the valid reasons for performing an internal examination. Performing the procedure without a valid clinical reason violates ethical guidelines and patient autonomy.

Whenever a clinical decision relies on knowing the exact station of the fetal head or the precise state of the membranes, a vaginal examination is clinically justified.

Absolute and Relative Contraindications

Not every pregnant or laboring woman should undergo a vaginal examination. Midwives must identify contraindications before putting on gloves, as ignoring them can lead to catastrophic bleeding or fetal death.

Absolute Contraindications

  • Placenta Praevia: If the placenta covers or lies very close to the internal cervical os, digital examination can dislodge it and trigger massive, life-threatening maternal hemorrhage.
  • Unexplained Antepartum Hemorrhage: Until placental location is confirmed via ultrasound, inserting fingers into the vagina is strictly prohibited.

Relative Contraindications

  • Known or Suspected Preterm Labor with Intact Membranes: Frequent manipulation can stimulate prostaglandins and worsen uterine irritability.
  • Active Genital Herpes Lesions: Internal examination can spread the virus to ascending tissues or the newborn during passage, though specific clinical protocols guide this when delivery is imminent.
  • Patient Refusal: A competent woman has the absolute right to decline the examination at any point.

Infection Prevention and Universal Precautions

Because a vaginal examination introduces fingers directly into the sterile environment of the upper reproductive tract, rigorous infection prevention practices are mandatory. Failing to maintain asepsis places both mother and newborn at high risk of puerperal sepsis.

Essential infection prevention steps include:

  • Hand Hygiene: Perform thorough handwashing with soap and water or use an alcohol-based hand rub before and after patient contact.
  • Barrier Protection: Wear clean, sterile examination gloves on both hands throughout the procedure.
  • Proper Positioning: Position the woman comfortably in the dorsal or lithotomy position with adequate draping to maintain her dignity while exposing the perineum.
  • Antiseptic Cleansing: Clean the vulva and perineum with an appropriate antiseptic solution from front to back before inserting fingers, depending on local institutional guidelines.
  • Safe Disposal: Immediately discard used gloves and contaminated materials into appropriate waste receptacles following universal precaution standards.

Patient Communication, Consent, and Privacy

A vaginal examination is an intimate and potentially invasive procedure that can cause psychological distress or trigger past trauma if handled insensitively. Midwives must prioritize effective communication and respect patient autonomy at all times.

Communication best practices include:

  • Explaining the Reason: Clearly explain why the examination is needed, what it involves, and what the woman will feel.
  • Obtaining Informed Consent: Always ask for explicit verbal permission before touching the patient. Never assume consent is implied just because she is in labor.
  • Ensuring Privacy: Draw curtains, close doors, and use proper draping to shield the woman from unnecessary exposure.
  • Providing Continuous Support: Invite a support person of her choice to stay by her side, and talk her through every step of the procedure in a calm, reassuring tone.
