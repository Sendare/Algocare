Auscultation of Fetal Heart Sounds (Pinard Stethoscope and Doppler)

Introduction to Fetal Surveillance in Nigerian Midwifery Practice

Fetal surveillance forms the bedrock of safe maternity care across Nigeria, from bustling tertiary teaching hospitals to remote primary health care clinics. Midwives carry the primary responsibility for assessing fetal well-being during antenatal visits and labor using basic, accessible tools. Regular auscultation helps detect early signs of fetal compromise, particularly oxygen deprivation, allowing for timely referrals before catastrophic birth injuries occur. Resource constraints mean that low-cost, reliable methods remain essential alongside modern electronic gadgets. Clinical competence in listening to the fetal heart bridges the gap between normal physiological progression and obstetric emergencies.

Anatomical Landmarks and Finding the Fetal Heart

Before placing any listening device on the maternal abdomen, a midwife must perform Leopold's maneuvers to determine fetal lie, presentation, and position. The fetal heart is transmitted best through the convex part of the fetus, usually the back in a longitudinal lie.

  • Vertex presentation (Occipito-anterior): The fetal heart is typically heard in the lower abdominal quadrant, below the maternal umbilicus.
  • Breech presentation: The sound is usually located at or above the level of the maternal umbilicus.
  • Transverse lie: Auscultation is generally difficult and unreliable; prompt obstetric review is required.

Pinard Stethoscope: Mechanics and Technique

The Pinard stethoscope is a specially designed acoustic horn that uses bone conduction and air column resonance to transmit fetal heart sounds. To use it correctly, the wide flared end is placed firmly perpendicular to the maternal abdomen over the fetal back, while the midwife presses their ear tightly against the narrow end.

A common mistake among student midwives is holding the Pinard with fingers resting on the bell, which muffles the sound and transmits the maternal pulse instead. The midwife must remove their hand entirely from the bell once positioned, steadying the instrument only with light head pressure, and concurrently feel the maternal radial pulse to differentiate maternal from fetal heart rates.

Doppler Ultrasound: Technology and Electronic Auscultation

A Doppler ultrasound device emits high-frequency sound waves that bounce off the moving fetal heart valves, translating the frequency shift into an audible sound through a built-in speaker. Midwives apply a coupling gel to the transducer to eliminate air pockets that block sound waves.

Doppler technology allows for rapid location of the fetal heart, making it exceptionally useful in busy labor wards or with clients who have a higher body mass index. However, midwives must remember that Doppler devices display a numerical heart rate calculated by software algorithms, which can sometimes pick up maternal blood flow in large pelvic vessels if positioned too low.

Comparative Analysis: Pinard Versus Doppler in Clinical Settings

Both tools have distinct advantages and limitations in clinical midwifery practice.

AccuracyDependent on quiet environment and skillFast detection, but risk of artifact counting

Midwives should master both instruments, ensuring quality care even during power cuts.

Interpreting Fetal Heart Rate and Rhythm

A normal baseline fetal heart rate (FHR) ranges between 110 and 160 beats per minute. Midwives must auscultate the heart rate for a full minute immediately after a uterine contraction to catch decelerations.

  • Tachycardia: Sustained baseline above 160 beats per minute, often indicating maternal fever, chorioamnionitis, or fetal hypoxia.
  • Bradycardia: Sustained baseline below 110 beats per minute, signaling severe fetal distress or acute oxygen deprivation.
  • Accelerations and Decelerations: Temporary increases or drops associated with fetal movement or cord compression.

Documentation and Communication of Findings

Accurate record-keeping is a legal and clinical necessity in midwifery. Every auscultation finding must be documented immediately in the partograph or patient case note, recording the exact baseline rate, rhythm, and timing relative to contractions. Vague descriptions like 'heartbeat heard' are unacceptable. If an abnormality such as bradycardia or persistent tachycardia is identified, the midwife must communicate the findings clearly and urgently to the medical officer using structured handover tools, ensuring prompt emergency intervention.
