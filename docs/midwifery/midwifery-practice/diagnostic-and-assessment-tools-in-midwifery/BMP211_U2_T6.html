Use of Ultrasonic Diagnostics in Midwifery Care

Introduction and Principles of Obstetric Ultrasound

Ultrasonic diagnostics in midwifery rely on high-frequency sound waves that are well above the limit of human hearing. A transducer sends these sound waves into the maternal abdomen, where they bounce off different tissues and structures inside the uterus. The machine then measures how long the echoes take to return and turns that data into a moving grayscale image on a screen. Unlike X-rays, ultrasound equipment does not use ionizing radiation, which makes it safe for routine monitoring during pregnancy. Midwives in Nigeria frequently use handheld or clinic-based machines to quickly assess fetal presence, heartbeat, and position during routine antenatal visits.

First Trimester Scans: Dating, Viability, and Early Complications

Early pregnancy scans are vital for establishing a reliable estimated due date and confirming that the pregnancy is progressing normally inside the uterine cavity. 1. Crown-Rump Length (CRL): Measured between 8 and 13 weeks, this measurement provides the most accurate estimation of gestational age, often within a margin of a few days. 2. Cardiac Activity: A pulsing fetal heart tube should be clearly visualized once the embryo reaches a certain size, confirming viability. 3. Ectopic Pregnancy: Scans help rule out extra-uterine implantations, particularly in the fallopian tubes, which pose life-threatening hemorrhage risks. 4. Multiple Gestations: Early scanning determines chorionicity and amnionicity, which dictate the specific monitoring schedule needed for twin or triplet pregnancies.

Second and Third Trimester Assessments: Anatomy and Growth

Mid-pregnancy and late-pregnancy scans evaluate structural development and monitor ongoing fetal well-being. The anomaly scan, usually performed around 18 to 22 weeks, systematically examines the fetal head, spine, heart, stomach, kidneys, and limbs to detect congenital malformations. Later in the third trimester, biometric measurements such as the biparietal diameter, head circumference, abdominal circumference, and femur length are combined to estimate fetal weight and track growth trajectories. Serial scans are frequently ordered in Nigerian practice if a mother has conditions like gestational diabetes or hypertension to ensure the baby is growing adequately.

Evaluating Placental Position and Amniotic Fluid Volume

Assessing the placenta and amniotic fluid is a critical component of every routine obstetric scan. The placenta's lower edge is measured relative to the internal cervical os; if it covers or lies very close to the os late in pregnancy, placenta previa is diagnosed, prohibiting vaginal delivery. Amniotic fluid is evaluated either subjectively or quantitatively using the Amniotic Fluid Index (AFI) or the Single Deepest Pocket method. Low fluid volume, known as oligohydramnios, or excessive fluid, known as polyhydramnios, both serve as important warning signs that prompt further investigations into fetal or maternal well-being.

The Midwife's Role in Patient Counselling and Preparation

Midwives bridge the gap between complex diagnostic technology and the pregnant woman's understanding. Before an ultrasound, the midwife explains the procedure, addresses any cultural fears or misconceptions, and advises whether a full bladder is necessary—often required for early transabdominal scans to push the uterus upward. After the scan, the midwife interprets the findings in simple language, reassuring the mother when results are normal or preparing her for specialist referral if an anomaly is detected. Clear, empathetic communication helps alleviate maternal anxiety and ensures active participation in her own care plan.

Safety, Limitations, and Ethical Considerations

While ultrasound is an invaluable tool, midwives must understand its limitations and safety boundaries. Diagnostic ultrasound emits thermal and mechanical energy, so scans should be performed using the ALARA principle—As Low As Reasonably Achievable—limiting exposure time, particularly with Doppler applications in the first trimester. Furthermore, ultrasound is operator-dependent; image quality and diagnostic accuracy rely heavily on the skill of the person holding the probe. Midwives must also counsel women against obtaining non-medical keepsake scans or souvenir videos from unregulated operators, ensuring that every scan serves a clear clinical purpose.
