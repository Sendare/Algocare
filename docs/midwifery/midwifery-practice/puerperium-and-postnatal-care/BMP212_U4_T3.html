Postnatal Assessment of Mother and Infant (Day 1 to 6 Weeks)

Introduction to the Puerperium and Postnatal Objectives

The puerperium is the critical period following childbirth during which the maternal anatomical and physiological changes of pregnancy revert back to the non-pregnant state. This phase typically lasts for six weeks, or forty-two days. Midwives view this transition not merely as a recovery period, but as a foundational window for establishing lifelong health for both mother and newborn.

Care objectives during this time focus on preventing complications such as postpartum hemorrhage and puerperal sepsis, supporting successful lactation, monitoring infant growth, and promoting psychological adjustment. Structured clinical assessments at specific milestones ensure that timely interventions are provided when deviations from normal recovery occur.

Immediate Postnatal Assessment on Day 1 in the Ward

The first twenty-four hours after birth carry the highest risk for severe maternal complications, making rigorous ward surveillance vital. Midwives must perform frequent observations to detect primary postpartum hemorrhage, bladder distension, and cardiovascular instability.

  • Vital signs: Check blood pressure, pulse, and temperature every four hours initially to spot early signs of shock or infection.
  • Uterine tone: Palpate the fundus to ensure it remains firm and well-contracted, which prevents excessive bleeding.
  • Lochia check: Assess the amount, color, and clots in the vaginal discharge, weighing pads if heavy blood loss is suspected.
  • Perineal inspection: Look for increasing edema, expanding hematomas, or severe pain around a tear or episiotomy repair.
  • Voiding status: Encourage spontaneous urination within six hours of delivery to prevent urinary retention and uterine atony.

Evaluating Maternal Physiological Involution and Healing

Involution is the rapid shrinking of the uterus back to its normal pelvic size after childbirth. Immediately after placental delivery, the fundus sits about midway between the symphysis pubis and the umbilicus. By day ten, the uterus descends into the true pelvis and becomes impalpable abdominally.

As the internal wound left by the placenta heals, vaginal discharge known as lochia changes in character. It begins as lochia rubra (bright red) for the first few days, transitions to lochia serosa (pinkish-brown) around days four to ten, and finishes as lochia alba (whitish-yellow) until involution completes. Any foul odor or sudden return of bright red bleeding after the first week suggests retained products of conception or endometritis.

Assessing Lactation Success and Infant Feeding

Breastfeeding establishment requires careful observation of both the mother's breasts and the infant's feeding behavior. Midwives inspect the breasts for engorgement, cracked nipples, and blocked ducts, especially around days three to five when milk volume increases significantly.

Observation of a complete feed is the gold standard of lactation assessment. The baby should take a wide mouthful of the breast, not just the nipple, with the lower lip turned outward. Swallowing sounds should be audible, and the mother should feel a firm tugging sensation rather than sharp, piercing pain. Addressing latch issues early prevents severe nipple trauma and mastitis.

Screening for Postpartum Psychological Well-being

Emotional vulnerability is common in the puerperium due to rapid hormonal shifts, sleep deprivation, and the demands of newborn care. Midwives must distinguish between normal mood disturbances and clinical conditions requiring mental health referrals.

  • Postpartum blues: A transient state of tearfulness, anxiety, and mood lability occurring in the first few days, resolving spontaneously within two weeks without formal psychiatric treatment.
  • Postpartum depression: A persistent depressive state starting within weeks or months after birth, characterized by profound sadness, inability to bond with the infant, and feelings of worthlessness.
  • Postpartum psychosis: A psychiatric emergency featuring hallucinations, delusions, severe confusion, and rapid mood swings, requiring immediate hospital admission.

Neonatal Transition, Growth, and Developmental Milestones

Adapting to extrauterine life requires profound cardiopulmonary and metabolic changes in the newborn. Midwives monitor vital signs, skin color, cord separation, and elimination patterns during every postnatal contact.

Healthy term newborns typically lose up to ten percent of their birth weight in the first few days due to fluid loss and meconium passage, but they should regain this weight by day ten to fourteen. Cord care involves keeping the stump clean and dry until it naturally separates. Midwives also evaluate basic neurological reflexes like the Moro and suck reflexes, ensuring the infant responds appropriately to environmental stimuli.

The 6-Week Final Postnatal Consultation and Family Planning

The six-week postnatal visit marks the official conclusion of the puerperium. This comprehensive consultation evaluates complete physical recovery, reviews infant feeding and immunization status, and provides an opportunity to discuss sexual health and family planning.

During the physical exam, the midwife checks abdominal muscle tone, pelvic floor integrity, and performs a speculogynaelogical or bimanual assessment if indicated to ensure the uterus has fully involuted. Because ovulation can resume before the return of menstruation, discussing and initiating a chosen contraceptive method is a vital component of this visit, tailored to whether the mother is exclusively breastfeeding.
