Contraception Counseling in Postpartum Period

Why Postpartum Contraception Matters for Nigerian Mothers and Newborns

Childbearing places significant physiological demands on a woman's body. In Nigeria, where maternal and infant mortality rates remain a public health concern, birth spacing is a powerful lifesaving intervention. When pregnancies occur too close together, the mother's nutritional stores, especially iron and folate, do not fully recover. This depletion contributes directly to severe anemia, premature rupture of membranes, and low birth weight infants.

Adequate birth spacing allows the uterine muscle and lining to regain complete structural integrity, drastically reducing the risk of uterine rupture in subsequent labors. Furthermore, healthy intervals between births give older siblings a better chance at sustained breastfeeding and maternal care during their critical first two years of life. Midwives occupy a trusted frontline position in community health structures, making them uniquely suited to help families understand that child spacing is not about population control, but about preserving the health and survival of both mother and child.

Understanding Return to Fertility After Childbirth

A common misconception among postpartum women is that pregnancy cannot occur before the return of regular menstrual bleeding. Ovulation consistently precedes the first postpartum menses. In non-breastfeeding mothers, the ovarian cycle can resume as early as four to six weeks following delivery.

Even for women who practice exclusive breastfeeding, the timing of the first ovulation is unpredictable and varies widely based on suckling frequency and hormonal fluctuations. Prolactin suppresses gonadotropin-releasing hormone, but as the infant grows and feeds less frequently at night, or introduces complementary feeds, prolactin levels drop. This hormonal shift allows the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis to reactivate. Midwives must counsel every postnatal mother that fertility can return before she sees her first postpartum period.

Postpartum Family Planning and Contraception Counseling

Effective counseling in the puerperium requires a client-centered, non-judgmental approach tailored to the woman's unique lifestyle, cultural background, and reproductive goals. Midwives should integrate family planning discussions across multiple touchpoints, starting during antenatal care, continuing through the immediate postnatal ward stay, and reinforcing sessions at the six-week postnatal clinic.

Using the Method Eligibility Checklist helps the midwife quickly rule out medical conditions that restrict certain contraceptive choices. Listening actively to the client's concerns regarding side effects, future fertility desires, and partner approval builds trust and improves long-term continuation rates. The goal is informed choice, ensuring the mother selects a method she understands and feels confident using.

Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM): Harnessing Breastfeeding as Contraception

The Lactational Amenorrhea Method is a highly effective, temporary contraceptive option that relies on the natural physiological suppression of fertility induced by breastfeeding. For LAM to remain effective with a failure rate of less than two percent, three strict criteria must be met simultaneously:

  • Amenorrhea: The mother must not have experienced any vaginal bleeding after 56 days postpartum.
  • Full or Nearly Full Breastfeeding: The baby must receive mostly breastmilk, with very rare or no supplements of water, formula, or solid food.
  • Frequent Feeding: The infant must nurse frequently day and night, with intervals between feeds generally not exceeding four hours during the day and six hours at night.

Once any of these three conditions changes, LAM is no longer reliable, and the mother must transition to another modern contraceptive method immediately.

Navigating Hormonal and Barrier Options in the Puerperium

Selecting hormonal contraceptives in the postnatal period requires careful attention to the timing of breastmilk production and thromboembolic risks. Estrogen-containing methods, such as combined oral contraceptive pills and combined injectables, are generally avoided during the first three weeks postpartum. During this window, the risk of deep vein thrombosis is naturally elevated, and estrogen can also interfere with the establishment of a robust milk supply.

Progestogen-only methods—including progestogen-only pills, depot medroxyprogesterone acetate injections, and subdermal implants—are safe to initiate immediately after delivery, regardless of breastfeeding status. They do not compromise milk volume or composition. Barrier methods like male and female condoms offer dual protection against both pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections, making them an excellent choice for postpartum couples, provided they are used consistently.

Intrauterine Devices in the Immediate and Interval Postpartum Period

Intrauterine devices provide long-acting, reversible contraception that can be inserted at various stages of the puerperium. Postpartum IUD insertion can occur within ten minutes of placental delivery during a caesarean section or a vaginal delivery, known as immediate postpartum insertion. Alternatively, it can be placed as an interval insertion after four to six weeks once the uterus has fully involuted.

While immediate insertion is convenient and takes advantage of the hospital stay, it carries a slightly higher rate of spontaneous expulsion because the cervix is dilated and the lower uterine segment is stretched. Midwives performing or assisting with this procedure must ensure proper fundal placement using specialized instruments. Women appreciate IUDs because they require no daily action and remain effective for years, yet fertility returns immediately upon removal.

Overcoming Cultural Myths and Societal Pressures in Midwifery Practice

Providing effective contraceptive care in Nigeria requires navigating a rich landscape of cultural beliefs, religious values, and societal expectations. Many communities hold deep-seated myths—such as the belief that modern contraceptives cause permanent sterility, congenital malformations, or accumulate dangerously inside the stomach. Husbands and mothers-in-law often exert strong influence over a woman's reproductive choices.

Midwives must counter misinformation with respectful, culturally sensitive dialogue rather than dismissive correction. Involving partners in counseling sessions whenever possible demystifies family planning and encourages shared decision-making. By partnering with respected community and religious leaders, midwives can help dispel harmful rumors and frame child spacing as a vital tool for maternal health and family prosperity.
