Pre-Operative Preparation and Post-Operative Management of Emergency Caesarean Section Patients

Indications and Urgency Classification for Emergency Caesarean Section

An emergency Caesarean section is performed when maternal or fetal life is threatened and vaginal delivery is unsafe or impossible. Midwives must understand the clinical urgency behind each case, as response times dictate perinatal outcomes. Category 1 emergencies demand immediate delivery within thirty minutes due to severe compromise like cord prolapse or suspected uterine rupture. Category 2 cases involve maternal or fetal compromise that is not immediately life-threatening, while Category 3 represents patients needing early delivery without immediate threat.

  • Category 1: Immediate threat to life, including severe fetal distress and massive placental abruption.
  • Category 2: Maternal or fetal compromise, such as failure to progress in labor with reassuring fetal status.
  • Category 3: Needing early delivery due to conditions like pre-labor rupture of membranes at term without distress.

Rapid Pre-Operative Assessment, Stabilization, and Consent

Time is precious during an emergency, but basic resuscitation and assessment cannot be bypassed. Midwives must quickly check baseline vital signs, establish reliable intravenous access with a large-bore cannula, and draw blood for a complete blood count and crossmatching. Urine output should be monitored, and an indwelling catheter is typically inserted.

Informed consent must be obtained swiftly yet clearly. The midwife or doctor explains the indication, the procedure, and the anesthesia type, ensuring the patient or her legal surrogate signs the consent form. Psychological support is equally vital; panic can hinder cooperation. Speak in a calm, reassuring voice, explaining each urgent step as it happens to reduce maternal anxiety.

Immediate Safety Checks and Theatre Preparation Protocols

Before transferring the patient to the operating theatre, final safety checklists must be completed to prevent avoidable errors. The midwife confirms the patient's identity using her hospital wristband, checks allergy status, and verifies that baseline investigations are complete.

All jewelry, dentures, and underwear must be removed, and the abdomen should be cleaned and clipped if necessary. Ensure the urinary bladder is emptied, usually via a Foley catheter, to prevent accidental bladder injury during the uterine incision. Handing over the patient securely to the perioperative team with an accurate verbal summary completes this critical phase.

Intra-Operative Midwifery Support and Neonatal Care

The midwife's role does not end at the theatre doors; intra-operative support bridges obstetric surgery and neonatal resuscitation. In the theatre, the scrub or circulating midwife assists the team, ensures a sterile environment, and maintains accurate counts of instruments, swabs, and needles.

Simultaneously, the midwife prepares for the newborn. A warm resuscitation space with functioning suction, oxygen, and bag-valve-mask apparatus must be ready before delivery. Once the baby is delivered, the midwife receives the infant, evaluates the Apgar score, provides immediate drying and stimulation, and facilitates early skin-to-skin contact if the mother's condition permits.

Immediate Post-Operative Recovery Room Care

The immediate post-operative period requires vigilant observation in the recovery ward or high-dependency unit. The primary danger during these first few hours is primary postpartum hemorrhage due to uterine atony. Midwives must monitor vital signs—blood pressure, pulse, respiration, and temperature—every fifteen minutes for the first hour, and hourly thereafter until stable.

Palpate the uterine fundus regularly to ensure it remains well-contracted and hard. Check the surgical dressing for active bleeding and monitor lochial flow. Assess the return of sensory and motor function if regional anesthesia was used, and maintain adequate intravenous fluid infusion as prescribed.

Routine Post-Operative Management: Pain Relief, Fluids, and Wound Care

Managing the post-operative recovery phase involves structured supportive care to promote healing and prevent complications. Pain control is paramount; multimodal analgesia combining regular paracetamol, NSAIDs, and scheduled opioids ensures the mother is comfortable enough to breathe deeply and move.

Intravenous fluids are typically tapered once bowel sounds return and oral intake is tolerated. The urinary catheter is usually removed within twenty-four hours to encourage normal voiding. Inspect the abdominal surgical wound daily for signs of hematoma, redness, or discharge, keeping the dressing clean and dry.

Early Ambulation, Lactation Support, and Complication Recognition

Promoting recovery extends to encouraging early ambulation and supporting successful breastfeeding. Getting out of bed within twenty-four hours significantly lowers the risk of venous thromboembolism. Midwives should assist the mother during her first attempts at sitting and walking.

Breastfeeding should be initiated as soon as possible. Support the mother with comfortable positioning, such as the rugby-ball hold, to keep pressure away from her abdominal incision. Finally, remain vigilant for red flags: spiking fevers, foul-smelling lochia, severe calf pain, and worsening lower abdominal tenderness, which point to infection or thromboembolic disease.
