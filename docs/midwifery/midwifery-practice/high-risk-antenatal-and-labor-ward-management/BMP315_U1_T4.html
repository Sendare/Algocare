MROP

Understanding Manual Removal of Placenta (MROP)

Manual Removal of Placenta, commonly known as MROP, is an emergency obstetric procedure performed when the placenta fails to deliver spontaneously within the normal third stage of labor. In Nigerian maternity units, the standard definition marks prolonged third stage at 30 minutes following the birth of the baby with active management, or sooner if excessive bleeding occurs. Delaying intervention exposes the mother to profound postpartum hemorrhage and shock. The procedure involves the midwife or doctor introducing a gloved hand directly into the uterine cavity, locating the placental edge, and performing a sweeping motion with the side of the hand to separate the placenta cleanly from the uterine wall.

Indications and Urgency in the Labor Ward

  • Primary Retained Placenta: Failure of the placenta to deliver within 30 minutes of childbirth despite active management.
  • Secondary Hemorrhage Risk: Heavy bleeding occurring anytime before the placental delivery, even if the 30-minute mark has not been reached.
  • Placental Accreta Spectrum: Abnormal invasion of the placenta into the uterine myometrium, often diagnosed or suspected during the third stage.
  • Previous Uterine Scar: Women with prior cesarean sections or uterine surgery have a higher risk of morbidly adherent placentas requiring skilled surgical MROP.

Essential Preparations and Aseptic Technique

MROP is a painful and invasive intrauterine procedure that requires careful preparation to prevent infection and manage pain. Adequate analgesia or regional anesthesia must be established before the hand enters the vagina. Intravenous access with a large-bore cannula is mandatory to allow rapid fluid replacement and administration of uterotonic drugs. Strict aseptic technique is non-negotiable because the open uterine wound is highly vulnerable to ascending bacteria. Midwives must wear sterile long-sleeved gowns and gloves that reach past the elbows, ensuring the entire forearm entering the birth canal remains sterile.

Step-by-Step Procedure for MROP

  1. Abdominal Support: Place one sterile hand on the lower abdomen to support and steady the fundus, preventing uterine inversion during the procedure.
  2. Uterine Entry: Gently insert the other hand, shaped like a cone with fingers held close together, following the umbilical cord into the vagina and through the cervix.
  3. Locating the Plane: Once inside the uterine cavity, locate the insertion site of the umbilical cord and slide your fingers along the placental surface to find the placental edge.
  4. Cleavage and Separation: Using the ulnar edge of your hand like a knife blade, make gentle sweeping motions between the placenta and the uterine wall until the entire placenta is detached.
  5. Extraction and Inspection: Grasp the detached placenta, slowly withdraw your hand from the uterus, and inspect the maternal surface to ensure no cotyledons or membranes remain behind.

Intraoperative and Post-Procedure Complications

While MROP is lifesaving, it carries significant risks that every maternity clinician must monitor closely. Uterine perforation can occur if excessive or unguided force is used, particularly with a softened postpartum myometrium. Postpartum hemorrhage may persist if pieces of the placenta are left inside or if the uterine muscle fails to contract after extraction. Severe intrauterine infection or endometritis is another major hazard due to the introduction of organisms during manual manipulation. Clinicians must also watch for signs of profound shock resulting from blood loss and pain.

Immediate Post-Operative Care and Monitoring

Following a successful MROP, the patient requires intensive monitoring to catch early signs of secondary bleeding or infection. Immediately administer prescribed uterotonics such as oxytocin or misoprostol to maintain firm uterine contraction. Monitor vital signs—blood pressure, pulse rate, and temperature—every 15 minutes for the first hour, then according to hospital protocol. Inspect lochia color, amount, and odor regularly, and check the uterine fundus for tone and height. Administer prophylactic antibiotics as ordered to protect against ascending genital tract infections.
