Vacuum Delivery Assistance

Indications and Prerequisites for Vacuum Extraction

Vacuum extraction is an operative vaginal delivery method used to shorten the second stage of labor when spontaneous pushing is insufficient or unsafe. Before applying the vacuum cup, specific clinical conditions must be met to ensure both maternal safety and successful delivery.

  • Full cervical dilatation: The cervix must be completely retracted and dilated to 10cm to prevent severe cervical lacerations.
  • Membranes ruptured: The amniotic sac must be broken so the cup can make direct contact with the fetal scalp.
  • Fetal head engagement: The leading part of the skull must be at station 0 or lower, specifically no more than 2/5 palpable abdominally.
  • Known fetal position: The occiput position must be accurately determined, usually via sagittal suture and fontanelle palpation.
  • Adequate pelvis: There must be no signs of cephalopelvic disproportion.
  • Bladder emptied: A full bladder can obstruct descent and risks injury during application, so catheterization is often performed.

Common indications include prolonged second stage, fetal distress in the second stage, or maternal exhaustion where pushing is no longer effective.

Equipment Check and Preparation

Before any operative vaginal birth, verifying the working condition of all instruments prevents critical failures during the procedure. The vacuum system consists of a traction handle, a vacuum pump (manual or electric), tubing, and the vacuum cup itself.

Midwives must test the suction pressure by blocking the cup with the palm of the hand and generating negative pressure up to the recommended limit, usually 0.8 kg/cm² or 80 kPa. Check all connections for air leaks and ensure the release valve functions smoothly.

Preparation also involves gathering appropriate analgesia, checking neonatal resuscitation equipment, and briefing the delivery room team. Reassuring the mother and explaining each step helps reduce anxiety and secures her vital cooperation during contractions.

Cup Placement and Technique

Correct placement of the vacuum cup is the single most important factor in preventing detachment and scalp injury. The cup must be centered over the flexion point, which lies about 3 cm in front of the posterior fontanelle along the sagittal suture. This placement promotes physiological flexion of the fetal head as traction is applied, presenting the smallest diameter of the skull to the birth canal.

Technique steps:

  1. Separate the labia and gently insert the cup sideways into the vagina.
  2. Slide the cup over the fetal scalp, sweeping it away from the maternal soft tissues.
  3. Palpate all around the rim of the cup to ensure no maternal vaginal wall or cervix is trapped underneath.
  4. Apply negative pressure gradually, building up to the required suction level in stages.

Avoid placing the cup over the anterior fontanelle, as this encourages deflexion and increases the risk of scalp tearing or cephalohematoma.

Traction and Delivery of the Fetal Head

Traction during vacuum extraction must be synchronized with maternal pushing and uterine contractions. Pulling between contractions wastes energy and increases the risk of cup detachment and fetal trauma.

The line of pull must always be perpendicular to the plane of the cup, following the downward curve of Carus corresponding to the birth canal axis. Never use rotational force with a vacuum cup, unlike some forceps applications; the vacuum is designed for traction only. As the fetal head crowns, the operator directs the movement upward to allow the suboccipital region to pivot under the pubic symphysis. Once the head is delivered, the vacuum is released, and the rest of the body is born normally.

Safety Limits and Abandoning the Procedure

Vacuum extraction is not an indefinite procedure; strict time and pull limits protect mother and baby from harm. If progress is not evident despite correct technique, certain stop rules must be obeyed.

  • Time limit: The total application time should ideally not exceed 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Pull limit: A maximum of three pull failures or three cup detachments dictates abandonment.
  • Lack of progress: If the head does not descend with each proper traction effort, stop immediately.

When these limits are reached, the procedure must be abandoned, the vacuum released, and an alternative plan executed, usually conversion to an emergency cesarean section if delivery is not imminent. Persistence beyond safety limits dramatically increases rates of severe scalp injury, subgaleal hemorrhage, and maternal trauma.

Post-Procedure Maternal and Neonatal Care

Following a vacuum-assisted birth, heightened vigilance is required for both the mother and the newborn.

For the mother, midwives must routinely inspect the vagina, cervix, and perineum for lacerations or hematomas that commonly occur due to rapid instrumental delivery. Monitor vital signs closely and assess postpartum blood loss to catch primary postpartum hemorrhage early. Offer adequate analgesia and psychological reassurance, acknowledging the stressful nature of an assisted birth.

For the neonate, examine the scalp immediately for a chignon (the harmless localized swelling formed inside the cup), bruising, abrasions, or signs of a cephalohematoma. Monitor the baby closely over the first 24 to 48 hours for signs of jaundice or neurological changes that could point to intracranial bleeding.
