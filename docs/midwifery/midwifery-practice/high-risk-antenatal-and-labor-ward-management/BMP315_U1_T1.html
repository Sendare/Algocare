Management of High-Risk Pregnant Women in Antenatal Wards

Understanding High-Risk Pregnancies in the Antenatal Ward

A high-risk pregnancy is one where the mother, the unborn baby, or both face a significantly higher chance of health problems or death compared to a normal pregnancy. In the context of Nigerian healthcare, common contributors include severe pre-eclampsia, sickle cell disease, grand multiparity, multiple gestations, and severe malarial anaemia. When these patients are admitted to the antenatal ward, the main goal shifts from routine screening to intensive surveillance and timely intervention.

Midwives must recognize that high-risk status is rarely static. A stable patient with gestational diabetes can rapidly transition into a medical emergency if ketoacidosis develops. Therefore, understanding the underlying pathophysiology of each patient's condition guides the frequency and type of care provided in the ward setting.

Initial Assessment and Risk Stratification on Admission

The moment a high-risk client arrives in the antenatal ward, a systematic admission process begins. This sets the baseline for all future care.

  • History Taking: Detailed review of obstetric, medical, surgical, and social history, paying close attention to previous pregnancy losses or severe complications.
  • Physical Examination: A head-to-toe clinical assessment, prioritizing blood pressure measurement, assessment for generalized oedema, and evaluation of uterine size against gestational age.
  • Baseline Investigations: Ordering and reviewing crucial laboratory tests such as full blood count, urinalysis for protein, blood grouping and cross-matching, and random blood sugar.
  • Risk Stratification: Categorizing the severity of the condition to determine the frequency of vital sign monitoring and level of medical staff involvement.

Multidisciplinary Team Approach and Ward Routine

Managing complex antenatal cases requires seamless teamwork. The midwife acts as the central coordinator, bridging communication between the pregnant woman, obstetricians, resident doctors, anaesthetists, neonatologists, and laboratory scientists.

Daily ward rounds provide a structured platform where the multidisciplinary team reviews each patient's progress, adjusts medication, and plans the optimal timing for delivery. Midwives contribute vital observational data gathered over the previous twenty-four hours—such as subtle behavioral changes, fluid balance trends, and patient concerns—that short doctor visits might miss. Maintaining clear, contemporaneous nursing notes ensures that every team member shares an accurate understanding of the clinical picture.

Monitoring Maternal Vital Signs and Fetal Well-being

Surveillance in the high-risk ward relies on regular, accurate tracking of both maternal and fetal indicators. Maternal blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and temperature must be checked according to the specific protocol for each condition—frequently every four hours or even hourly for unstable cases. Fluid intake and output charts are mandatory for women with pre-eclampsia, severe sepsis, or hyperemesis.

Fetal monitoring balances intermittent auscultation with electronic fetal monitoring where indicated. Midwives assess fetal heart rates, monitor fetal movements through daily kick counts, and watch for intrauterine growth restriction using serial symphysis-fundal height measurements and ultrasound scans. Spotting an abnormal fetal heart rate pattern early can prevent intrauterine demise.

Specific Medical and Obstetric Interventions

Pharmacological management in the antenatal ward targets the specific pathology threatening the pregnancy. Common interventions include administering antihypertensives like labetalol or methyldopa for chronic hypertension or pre-eclampsia, anticonvulsants such as magnesium sulphate for seizure prophylaxis, and corticosteroids to accelerate fetal lung maturity when preterm delivery is anticipated.

Obstetric interventions may involve cervical length monitoring, placement of cervical cerclage for incompetent cervix, or scheduling timely elective deliveries. Midwives must understand the correct dosages, administration routes, and expected side effects of these potent medications. For instance, monitoring deep tendon reflexes and respiratory rate is non-negotiable when a patient receives magnesium sulphate infusions.

Emergency Preparedness and Crisis Management

Despite careful monitoring, emergencies do occur in the high-risk antenatal ward. Conditions like eclamptic seizures, antepartum haemorrhage from placenta praevia or abruption, and cord prolapse demand rapid, coordinated action.

Every ward must have readily accessible emergency equipment, including working suction machines, oxygen delivery systems, emergency drug trays, and functional call bells. Midwives must be confident in executing emergency drills, such as calling for immediate backup, stabilizing the mother using the left lateral position where appropriate, securing intravenous access, and preparing for emergency transfer to the operating theatre or labor ward. Quick thinking saves lives.

Psychological Support and Patient Communication

Physical care in the high-risk ward is inseparable from psychological support. Prolonged hospital stay away from family, financial strain, and constant fear of losing the baby cause immense anxiety, depression, and emotional fatigue among admitted women.

Midwives should provide empathetic listening, clear explanations of procedures before they are performed, and involve family members in discussions where appropriate. Respectful maternity care reassures patients and builds trust. Addressing fears openly helps lower maternal stress hormones, which has a positive physiological benefit for both mother and fetus.

Discharge Planning and Transition to Labor Ward

Discharge planning begins on the day of admission. For patients whose conditions stabilize, preparation for safe discharge home involves comprehensive education on warning signs—such as severe headache, blurred vision, reduced fetal movements, or vaginal bleeding—and scheduling timely follow-up visits.

Conversely, when a high-risk pregnancy reaches term, or when maternal or fetal complications necessitate delivery, the midwife facilitates a smooth handover to the labor ward or operating theatre. This transition requires a comprehensive written handover note, verbal briefing of the labor ward team, and ensuring that all baseline lab results and blood cross-matches accompany the patient.
