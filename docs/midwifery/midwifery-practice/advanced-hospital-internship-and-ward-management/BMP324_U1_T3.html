Supervision

The Role of Supervision in the Midwifery Unit

Supervision in a busy Nigerian maternity ward is not merely about policing staff or waiting for mistakes to happen. It is an active, continuous process aimed at guiding clinical practice, maintaining safety standards, and supporting professional growth. When an acting ward manager provides consistent oversight, maternal and neonatal morbidity rates drop because potential complications are caught early. Good supervision bridges the gap between the theory learned in midwifery school and the chaotic reality of the labor ward. It creates a safe environment where junior staff feel comfortable asking questions rather than hiding errors.

Clinical vs. Administrative Supervision on the Ward

A ward manager must balance two distinct types of oversight. Clinical supervision focuses on direct patient care skills, partograph interpretation, aseptic technique during deliveries, and immediate neonatal resuscitation. Administrative supervision deals with duty rosters, leave schedules, equipment inventory, and compliance with hospital policies. Both are vital for a functioning ward. If a manager spends all shift in the office sorting rosters, clinical standards slip. Conversely, ignoring administrative duties leads to supply stockouts and staff burnout from poor scheduling.

Fostering a Culture of Continuous Learning and Mentorship

A progressive maternity unit treats every shift as an opportunity to learn. Ward managers should encourage bedside teaching, brief post-shift debriefs, and peer review. Mentorship pairs experienced midwives with younger colleagues to pass down tacit knowledge that textbooks miss, such as how to manage shoulder dystocia with calm authority. Instead of blaming junior staff for a poor outcome, a learning-focused supervisor holds a constructive audit meeting to examine what the entire team can do better next time.

Delegation and Accountability in Maternity Care

Delegation is a core competency for an acting ward manager. Because maternity units are often short-staffed, knowing who to assign to specific tasks is a matter of safety.

  • Assessment: Evaluate the competency level of the staff member and the clinical stability of the patient before delegating.
  • Communication: Give clear, specific instructions regarding what needs to be done and when to report back.
  • Supervision: Maintain oversight without micro-managing every single action.
  • Accountability: Remember that while a task can be delegated, the overall responsibility for patient outcome remains with the delegating manager.

Addressing Performance Gaps and Providing Constructive Feedback

When a staff member repeatedly fails to monitor blood pressure correctly or submits incomplete records, the ward manager must intervene promptly. Waiting for the annual appraisal is useless. Feedback should be delivered privately, focusing on specific behaviors rather than attacking the person's character. Use the sandwich method or a direct, supportive coaching conversation: highlight a strength, discuss the gap objectively, and agree on a clear action plan for improvement with a follow-up date.

Evaluating Junior Staff and Student Midwives

Evaluation is more than ticking boxes on a grading sheet. For student midwives and newly posted junior staff, evaluation measures clinical competence, professional attitude, and readiness for independent practice. A good evaluation relies on direct observation over time rather than a single snap judgment made on a stressful day. Involve the learners in their own assessment by asking them to appraise their own performance first. This builds critical self-awareness, which is essential for safe, lifelong midwifery practice.
