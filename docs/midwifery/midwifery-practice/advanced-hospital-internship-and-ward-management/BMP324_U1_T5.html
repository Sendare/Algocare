Directing Obstetric Emergency Resuscitation Teams

The Role of the Midwife Leader in Obstetric Emergencies

During a sudden obstetric crisis such as a massive postpartum haemorrhage or severe eclamptic seizure, chaos easily overtakes the labor ward. The midwife leader steps back from direct hands-on tasks to maintain situational awareness. This macroscopic view allows the leader to spot failing interventions, track total blood loss or seizure frequency, and anticipate the next clinical phase before it arrives. Stepping back is not passive; it is the most active cognitive task in the room. A good leader assigns themselves the role of coordinator rather than primary proceduralist, ensuring that resources flow where they are most needed without bottlenecks.

Structuring Roles and Delegating Tasks Under Pressure

A high-stress resuscitation room requires pre-assigned roles to prevent confusion. When everyone knows their duty, response times drop significantly.

  • Airway Manager: Maintains a patent airway and administers high-flow oxygen.
  • Circulatory Lead: Secures large-bore intravenous access and draws blood samples.
  • Recorder/Timekeeper: Documents exact times of interventions, medication dosages, and fluid volumes.
  • Runner: Fetches emergency drugs, blood products, and specialized equipment from the pharmacy or lab.

Delegation must be explicit. Pointing at a specific colleague, calling their name, and stating the exact task prevents diffusion of responsibility where everyone assumes someone else is doing it.

Clear Communication Protocols in Critical Moments

Standard conversational speech fails in noisy, high-adrenaline emergency situations. Teams must adopt closed-loop communication to ensure zero misinterpretation of orders. When the leader gives an instruction, the receiver repeats it back verbatim. Once the task is completed, the receiver reports back that the action is done.

For example:

  • Leader: "Staff Nurse Amina, give ten units of oxytocin intramuscularly now."
  • Receiver: "Ten units of oxytocin intramuscularly now, understood."
  • Receiver (after action): "Oxytocin ten units given intramuscularly."

Keep verbal commands concise, use names, and avoid ambiguous slang or quiet phrasing.

Managing the Golden Hour in Postpartum Haemorrhage and Eclampsia

The first sixty minutes following a major obstetric complication dictate whether a woman survives with minimal morbidity or suffers multi-organ failure. In postpartum haemorrhage, the leader must simultaneously drive uterine massage, bimanual compression, uterotonic infusion, and fluid resuscitation. In eclampsia, airway protection and controlled blood pressure reduction take priority alongside magnesium sulfate administration. Speed without structure breeds error. The midwife leader balances urgency with methodical checklists, ensuring no critical step like catheterization or baseline blood work is forgotten in the rush.

Post-Resuscitation Debriefing and Documentation

Once the patient stabilizes, the work is still incomplete. The resuscitation team must conduct a short, structured debriefing session while memories are fresh. This conversation focuses on system performance, equipment availability, and communication gaps rather than personal blame. Following the debrief, contemporaneous documentation must occur. Every action, fluid volume, drug administration time, and clinical response must be recorded accurately in the patient's chart. Proper notes protect the healthcare team legally and provide a clear baseline for the ongoing intensive care nursing staff.
