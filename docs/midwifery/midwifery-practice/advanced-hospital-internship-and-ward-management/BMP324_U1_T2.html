Delegation

Understanding the Concept of Delegation in Midwifery Ward Management

Delegation is the process by which a midwife manager transfers authority to perform a specific nursing or midwifery task to a competent healthcare worker, while retaining ultimate responsibility for the outcome. In a busy Nigerian maternity ward, managing patient care single-handedly is impossible. Ward managers must distribute workloads safely among staff nurses, student midwives, and ward attendants. True delegation means handing over both the work and the authority to do it, but never the final accountability. If a delegated task goes wrong, the manager still carries the professional responsibility for that assignment decision.

The Five Rights of Clinical Delegation

Safe delegation relies on a universally accepted framework known as the Five Rights. Ignoring even one of these rights can endanger a patient's life and put your license at risk.

  1. Right Task: The activity must be suitable to pass on to someone else, such as routine vital signs rather than initial clinical assessments.
  2. Right Circumstance: Assess the patient's condition and ward environment before assigning the duty.
  3. Right Person: Ensure the assigned worker actually possesses the right skills and training.
  4. Right Direction/Communication: Provide clear, concise instructions without medical jargon.
  5. Right Supervision/Evaluation: Monitor the task and follow up to ensure it was done correctly.

Scope of Practice and Legal Accountability in Nigerian Healthcare

Every healthcare cadre in Nigeria operates within strict legal boundaries defined by regulatory bodies like the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN). As a ward manager, you must know what tasks are legally permitted for registered midwives, registered nurses, community health extension workers (CHEWs), and ward orderlies. Delegating a clinical procedure—such as administering intravenous oxytocin—to an unqualified or unauthorized cadre is illegal and professional misconduct. If litigation arises from a botched procedure, the court examines whether the task fell within the worker's legal scope of practice and whether you exercised sound judgment in assigning it.

Assessing Staff Competency Before Task Assignment

Never assume a staff member can perform a duty simply because of their job title or years on the payroll. Competence is demonstrated ability, combining knowledge, skill, and attitude. A newly qualified staff nurse might have learned a procedure in school but lacks hands-on confidence in a chaotic labour ward. Before assigning a clinical task, directly observe the worker or review their recent competency sign-offs. Match the complexity of the task directly to the verified capability of the individual staff member.

Effective Communication and Clear Briefing Techniques

Poor communication is the leading cause of failed delegation. When giving instructions, avoid vague statements like 'take care of bed four.' Instead, use a structured briefing that specifies the exact task, expected timelines, parameters for reporting back, and any specific patient peculiarities. Encourage the receiver to repeat the instructions back in their own words to confirm complete understanding. Maintain an approachable demeanor so junior staff feel safe asking clarifying questions if they feel overwhelmed.

Monitoring, Supervision, and Providing Constructive Feedback

Delegation does not end when you hand over a task; monitoring is an ongoing responsibility. Ward managers must strike a balance between overbearing micromanagement and total abandonment. Check in periodically to gauge progress, offer guidance, and answer emerging questions. Once the task is completed, provide immediate, constructive feedback. Acknowledge what was done well and gently correct any procedural errors before they become ingrained bad habits.

Overcoming Barriers and Avoiding Improper Delegation

Ward managers often encounter mental and systemic barriers to delegation. Some managers suffer from the 'I can do it better myself' syndrome, leading to severe burnout. Others fear losing control or distrust junior colleagues. Conversely, improper delegation occurs when managers dump undesirable chores onto junior staff without regard for safety or competence. To build a healthy ward culture, foster mutual trust, delegate developmental tasks to eager learners, and address workload imbalances fairly across the entire nursing team.
