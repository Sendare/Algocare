Nursing and Midwifery Care during First Stage: Pain Relief, Hydration, Vital Signs Monitoring

Baseline Assessment and Vital Signs Monitoring

Monitoring a laboring woman during the first stage provides vital clues about maternal and fetal well-being. Midwives must establish a reliable baseline by checking the blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiration rate upon admission. During the latent and active phases of labor, maternal blood pressure and pulse are typically assessed every four hours, though more frequent checks are necessary if abnormal findings emerge or if regional analgesia is in use.

Temperature requires monitoring every four hours, or every two hours if the amniotic membranes have ruptured, to catch early signs of chorioamnionitis. Fetal heart rate auscultation is equally critical; listen using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler ultrasound immediately after a contraction every thirty minutes in the active phase, and every fifteen minutes during the second stage. Documenting these observations clearly helps the healthcare team spot subtle deviations before they escalate into emergencies.

Maintaining Maternal Hydration and Nutrition

Labor is physically demanding, comparable to running a marathon, which means energy and fluid balance matter deeply. Historically, strict fasting was enforced during labor out of fear of aspiration under general anesthesia. Modern midwifery guidelines encourage oral hydration with water or isotonic sports drinks for low-risk women to prevent ketosis and dehydration.

Solid food restriction remains common in many Nigerian health facilities due to the unpredictable need for emergency operative delivery, though light, easily digestible carbohydrates like porridge or tea are often permitted in early labor. Intravenous fluids, such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate, are reserved for women who cannot take oral fluids, those experiencing prolonged labor, or patients receiving epidural analgesia that causes maternal hypotension.

Understanding the Physiology of Labor Pain

Pain during the first stage of labor stems primarily from uterine contractions and cervical dilation. Visceral pain fibers transmitting these sensations enter the spinal cord at the T10 to L1 levels. During the latent phase, discomfort feels similar to severe menstrual cramps, localized in the lower abdomen and lower back.

As the cervix effaces and dilates further in the active phase, the stretching of the lower uterine segment and pelvic tissues intensifies this visceral ache. Fear, anxiety, and tension can amplify a woman's perception of this pain by triggering the release of circulating catecholamines, which in turn reduce uterine blood flow and prolong labor. A skilled midwife acknowledges this physiological loop, offering reassurance alongside physical comfort measures to ease anxiety.

Non-Pharmacological Pain Relief Methods

  • Continuous Labour Support: Having a dedicated companion, doula, or midwife present provides continuous reassurance and significantly reduces the need for medical pain relief.
  • Hydrotherapy: Warm showers or immersion in a birth pool during the active phase relaxes tense muscles and reduces pain perception.
  • Positioning and Movement: Encouraging the mother to walk, rock on a birth ball, or assume upright postures utilizes gravity to assist fetal descent while easing back pain.
  • Massage and Counterpressure: Applying steady pressure to the sacral area during contractions relieves the intense backache associated with posterior fetal positions.
  • Breathing Techniques: Controlled, paced breathing helps the woman focus her mind and manage each contraction actively.

Pharmacological Pain Relief Options

When non-pharmacological methods prove insufficient, pharmacological options offer targeted relief. Systemic opioids, such as pentazocine or tramadol, are commonly administered intramuscularly or intravenously in Nigerian clinical settings. While opioids take the edge off pain, midwives must watch closely for maternal sedation, nausea, and neonatal respiratory depression if delivery occurs soon after administration.

Regional analgesia, particularly epidural blocks, provides superior pain relief by blocking nerve impulses in the lower spinal segments. Epidurals require careful monitoring of maternal blood pressure because sympathetic blockade can lead to sudden hypotension, which compromises placental blood flow. Nitrous oxide gas mixed with oxygen is another viable, patient-controlled option that takes the peak off contractions without putting the baby to sleep.

Documenting Care and Evaluating Interventions

Clear documentation is the legal and professional safety net of intrapartum midwifery care. Every assessment, vital sign, pain relief method used, and its subsequent effect must be recorded accurately on the partograph and nursing notes. Midwives should evaluate whether an intervention worked; for instance, noting if sacral massage successfully eased back pain or if a dose of opioid medication provided adequate rest between contractions.

Failing to document timely assessments or interventions leaves clinical staff vulnerable during adverse outcomes. Furthermore, handovers between shifts require concise, factual communication of the labor progress, maternal coping strategies, and any emerging risk factors to ensure continuity of safe care.
