Monitoring Labor Progress using the Partograph (Alert and Action Lines)

Introduction to the Partograph and Its Purpose

The partograph is a standardized, low-cost graphical tool used during the active phase of labor to monitor maternal and fetal well-being as well as the progress of cervical dilation. Invented by Philpott and Castle and recommended globally by the World Health Organization, its primary purpose is to provide an early visual warning when labor deviates from normal parameters. By plotting key clinical observations on a single sheet of paper, midwives can quickly spot prolonged labor, identify maternal or fetal distress early, and make timely decisions about referral or intervention. In busy Nigerian maternity units, the partograph serves as a vital communication link between shifts and healthcare cadres, reducing cases of obstructed labor, uterine rupture, and associated maternal and neonatal morbidities.

Components of the Partograph

A standard partograph chart is divided into distinct sections that capture the entire clinical picture of labor. Midwives must record observations systematically across these sections:

  • Fetal Condition: Fetal heart rate, status of amniotic membranes and liquor, and degree of fetal skull molding.
  • Progress of Labor: Cervical dilatation plotted in centimeters, descent of the fetal head, and uterine contractions frequency and strength.
  • Maternal Condition: Pulse rate, blood pressure, temperature, and urine output, including protein and acetone levels.
  • Medications and Fluids: Oxytocin administration, IV fluids given, and drugs administered during labor.

The Alert Line and Its Clinical Significance

The alert line starts at 4 cm of cervical dilatation and runs diagonally upward to the right at a rate of 1 cm per hour, reaching the point of expected full dilatation. Crossing to the right of the alert line means that labor is progressing slower than the average normal rate of 1 cm per hour. In primary health care settings or peripheral clinics in Nigeria, crossing the alert line serves as a warning signal indicating the need for careful evaluation and preparation for possible transfer to a higher-level facility where comprehensive emergency obstetric care is available. It does not automatically mean surgical intervention is required right away, but it demands heightened vigilance.

The Action Line and When to Intervene

The action line is parallel to the alert line and is drawn four hours to the right of it. When the cervical dilatation plot crosses the action line, it indicates severe delay in labor progress. This crossing is a critical trigger for immediate clinical action. Midwives working in secondary or tertiary facilities must assess the cause of the delay—often secondary to cephalopelvic disproportion or poor uterine contractions—and institute decisive management. Interventions at the action line typically include artificial rupture of membranes if intact, augmentation with oxytocin if contractions are weak and CPD is ruled out, or preparation for operative delivery.

Practical Interpretation and Decision Making

Interpreting the partograph requires integrating multiple findings rather than looking at the cervical curve in isolation. If the alert line is crossed, the midwife checks the strength and frequency of contractions, the state of the membranes, and the fetal heart rate. A common pitfall in busy wards is failing to plot findings regularly, leading to retrospective data entry which defeats the purpose of early detection. When evaluating descent of the fetal head alongside cervical dilatation, a lack of descent despite full cervical dilation or poor progress warns of potential obstruction. Clear documentation ensures that every member of the healthcare team shares the same understanding of the labor trajectory.
