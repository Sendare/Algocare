Administering Postpartum Medications and Immunizations

Introduction to Postnatal Pharmacotherapy and Immunity

Postnatal drug administration requires an understanding of physiological shifts after childbirth. Blood volume begins returning to pre-pregnant levels, and renal clearance alters drug excretion rates. Midwives must remain vigilant because medications pass into breastmilk. Evaluating infant safety alongside maternal recovery guides every prescription and over-the-counter recommendation on the ward. Drug pharmacokinetics change rapidly in the first week following delivery, demanding careful observation.

Uterotonics for Preventing Postpartum Hemorrhage

Postpartum hemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal mortality in Nigeria. Uterotonic agents contract the myometrium, compressing open blood vessels at the placental site.

  • Oxytocin (Syntocinon): First-line prophylactic agent, given via slow intravenous injection or intramuscularly immediately after delivery.
  • Misoprostol: Prostaglandin analogue used orally or sublingually, especially valuable where cold storage for oxytocin is unavailable.
  • Ergometrine: Stimulates sustained uterine contraction but is contraindicated in women with severe hypertension.

Always check maternal blood pressure before administering ergometrine to prevent dangerous spikes.

Pain Relief and Analgesic Management

Managing postnatal pain improves early ambulation and bonding. Discomfort typically stems from uterine contractions afterpains, perineal lacerations, or abdominal incisions following a caesarean section. A stepped approach using non-opioid analgesics like paracetamol and ibuprofen forms the foundation of care. For severe surgical pain, short courses of weaker opioids may be added, keeping watch for maternal sedation and neonatal drowsiness during breastfeeding.

Administering Rho(D) Immune Globulin

Rh incompatibility poses a severe risk in subsequent pregnancies if an Rh-negative mother is exposed to Rh-positive fetal blood during delivery. Midwives must check the blood group and antibody screen of every Rh-negative woman giving birth. Administering Rho(D) immune globulin within 72 hours of delivery prevents maternal sensitization and the formation of antibodies that attack future fetuses. A Kleihauer-Betke test may be needed if large fetal-maternal hemorrhages are suspected, ensuring the standard dose is sufficient.

Postnatal Immunizations

The postnatal ward serves as a key contact point for catching up on maternal immunizations. Women with non-immune status documented during antenatal booking receive the Rubella vaccine, usually combined as MMR, before discharge, provided they avoid pregnancy for the next month. Tetanus toxoid boosters are administered if the mother's last dose was outside the recommended window or if delivery occurred in an unhygienic environment. Midwives educate mothers that these vaccines are safe while breastfeeding.

Midwifery Safety Protocols and Patient Education

Safe medication administration relies on strict adherence to the rights of drug administration: right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, and right time. Midwives must document every dose immediately in the postnatal chart, noting batch numbers for biologicals and vaccines. Patient education ensures the mother understands potential side effects, such as lochia changes or mild fever after immunizations, reducing unnecessary anxiety and preventing medication errors at home.
