Routine Postnatal Assessment of Mother and Infant

Introduction to the Postnatal Period and Assessment Goals

The postnatal period, or puerperium, begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six weeks. During this critical phase, the mother's body undergoes profound anatomical and physiological changes as organs return to their pre-pregnant state, while the newborn transitions to extrauterine life. Midwives play a central role in monitoring this recovery to ensure both mother and baby remain healthy and safe.

Routine postnatal assessments are designed to achieve several key objectives:

  • Detect early signs of postpartum complications such as hemorrhage or infection.
  • Monitor the involution of the uterus and the healing of any perineal trauma.
  • Assess infant feeding, weight changes, and general newborn well-being.
  • Support maternal emotional health and facilitate family bonding.

Creating a Therapeutic Environment and Preparing the Mother

Before starting any physical examination, the midwife must establish a calm, respectful, and private space. Postnatal women are often exhausted, physically sore, and emotionally vulnerable. Begin by greeting the mother warmly, introducing yourself, and explaining every step of the assessment procedure before touching her or the baby.

Ensure the room is warm, well-lit, and free from drafts, as newborns lose heat rapidly. Ask the mother to empty her bladder prior to the abdominal check, as a full bladder displaces the uterus and causes discomfort. Privacy and cultural sensitivity must be maintained at all times, ensuring the mother feels safe and respected throughout the interaction.

Postnatal Assessment of Mother (The BUBBLE-EE Approach)

The systematic evaluation of the postpartum mother follows a structured clinical mnemonic known as BUBBLE-EE, ensuring no vital body system is missed during the shift assessment.

  • Breasts: Inspect for symmetry, engorgement, nipple cracks, and softness.
  • Uterus: Palpate to confirm firm consistency, midline position, and steady involution.
  • Bladder: Monitor urinary frequency, output, and signs of retention or infection.
  • Bowel: Check for the passage of flatus, bowel movements, and absence of constipation.
  • Lochia: Evaluate color, amount, odor, and presence of clots.
  • Episiotomy / Perineum: Inspect surgical repair or tears for REEDA (Redness, Edema, Ecchymosis, Discharge, Approximation).
  • Emotions: Observe mood, affect, rest levels, and signs of the 'baby blues' or depression.
  • Education: Reinforce self-care, hygiene, nutrition, and danger signs.

Postnatal Assessment of Infant

Assessing the newborn requires a gentle, head-to-toe approach while keeping the baby warm and secure. Begin by observing general appearance, skin color, activity level, and cry. Check the fontanelles for abnormal depression or bulging. Inspect the eyes for signs of ophthalmia neonatorum and examine the cord stump for bleeding, foul odor, or redness.

Monitor the baby's elimination patterns closely. The first passage of meconium and urine should occur within the first twenty-four to forty-eight hours of life. Weigh the baby daily to monitor fluid balance and nutritional intake, remembering that an initial weight loss of up to ten percent is normal in the first week due to fluid shifts.

Evaluating Maternal-Infant Bonding and Breastfeeding

Observing how the mother interacts with her baby provides valuable insight into her psychological well-being and attachment process. Watch whether she holds the infant close, makes eye contact, responds to cries, and refers to the baby by name. Positive attachment lays the foundation for healthy child development.

Simultaneously, evaluate breastfeeding technique to prevent common problems like sore nipples and engorgement. Check that the baby's mouth is wide open, taking in a large portion of the lower areola, with lips flanged outward and a rhythmic sucking and swallowing pattern audible. Address any latching difficulties immediately to support successful exclusive breastfeeding.

Documenting Findings and Recognizing Red Flags

Accurate documentation is a legal and clinical necessity. Every assessment finding must be recorded promptly and clearly in the mother's and baby's healthcare charts, noting date, time, and specific observations such as lochia volume or infant feeding frequency.

Midwives must remain vigilant for warning signs that require immediate medical intervention.

  • Maternal Red Flags: Heavy vaginal bleeding (soaking a pad in under an hour), severe headache with visual changes, high fever, foul-smelling lochia, or severe calf pain.
  • Infant Red Flags: Persistent lethargy, poor feeding, yellow jaundice appearing in the first 24 hours, difficulty breathing, or abnormal temperature instability.
