Backache

Anatomy and Hormonal Shifts Behind the Discomfort

Backache during the antenatal period is rarely just about weight gain. Two main factors drive this common complaint: changing hormones and shifting physical alignment.

During pregnancy, the body produces high levels of relaxin and progesterone. Relaxin softens the ligaments throughout the pelvis, preparing the birth canal for delivery. Unfortunately, this softening also affects the ligaments supporting the lower back and sacroiliac joints, making them less stable.

As the uterus grows, the woman's center of gravity shifts forward. To keep from falling over, she naturally leans backward, increasing the curve in the lower spine. This postural change places continuous stress on the lumbar muscles and spinal joints.

Identifying Common Triggers in Daily Antenatal Life

Pregnancy backache often flares up due to specific daily activities that strain an already vulnerable musculoskeletal system. Midwives must help women pinpoint these triggers during antenatal visits.

  • Prolonged standing: Standing for long hours at market stalls or in clinic queues forces the lower back muscles to work overtime without rest.
  • Improper lifting: Bending from the waist instead of the knees when picking up toddlers or heavy loads places direct shear stress on the lumbar spine.
  • Inadequate footwear: Wearing flat shoes with zero support or high heels forces an unnatural posture that worsens spinal curves.
  • Fatigue and poor sleep: Lack of restful sleep prevents overworked postural muscles from recovering overnight.

Clinical Assessment and Red Flags to Watch Out For

Most backaches in pregnancy are benign musculoskeletal discomforts, but midwives must perform a careful assessment to rule out serious obstetric or medical conditions.

Always ask about the exact location, onset, and radiation of the pain. Check if the pain wraps around the abdomen or is accompanied by uterine tightening, which could indicate preterm labor. Assess for urinary symptoms like frequency, urgency, or fever, which point toward a urinary tract infection or pyelonephritis.

Be alert for red flags that require immediate medical referral:

  • Pain accompanied by vaginal bleeding or fluid leakage
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the legs (possible nerve root compression)
  • A history of trauma or a fall
  • Fever combined with localized spine tenderness

Practical Management and Non-Pharmacological Relief

Management of physiological backache focuses on comfort, self-care, and safe physical support. Drug therapy is rarely needed and should be used cautiously.

Intervention TypePractical MethodHow It Helps
Heat and ColdWarm compress or pack on the lower backRelaxes tense muscles and eases localized ache
Support GarmentsMaternity pelvic support beltsTakes the physical weight of the uterus off the spine
Rest and PositioningSide-lying with a pillow between kneesAligns the pelvis and relieves lumbar pressure
Gentle ExercisePrenatal yoga or pelvic tilt exercisesStrengthens core and back muscles safely

Analgesics like paracetamol can be used if approved by the physician, but non-drug measures remain the foundation of relief.

Counseling Patients on Posture and Ergonomics

Prevention is always better than cure. Midwives have a great opportunity during routine antenatal education sessions to teach women how to protect their backs during daily tasks at home and work.

Advise women to avoid standing in one place for too long; if their work requires standing, they should rest one foot alternately on a small box or stool. When sitting, chairs should provide good lumbar support; placing a small rolled-up towel behind the lower back makes a big difference.

Teach women to avoid wearing high heels or completely flat shoes with no arch support. Supportive walking shoes with a low, wide heel provide the best stability for the changing body.
