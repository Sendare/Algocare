Heartburn

Why Pregnant Women Experience Heartburn

Heartburn, medically known as pyrosis, is a burning sensation felt behind the sternum resulting from the regurgitation of gastric acid into the lower esophagus. In pregnancy, it ranks among the most frequent minor disorders, affecting a large percentage of expectant mothers, particularly during the second and third trimesters. While rarely life-threatening, it causes significant physical distress and can severely impair sleep quality and daily nutrition.

Hormonal and Anatomical Changes Contributing to Reflux

Two primary physiological forces drive reflux during gestation: hormonal relaxation and mechanical displacement.

  • Progesterone: Elevated levels of this hormone relax smooth muscle throughout the body, including the lower esophageal sphincter (LES). When this valve remains slack, stomach contents easily slip upward.
  • Anatomical Displacement: As the fetus grows, the expanding uterus physically pushes the stomach upward and alters intra-abdominal pressure, forcing gastric acid past the weakened LES.

Recognizing Symptoms and Discomfort in Antenatal Patients

Midwives must listen closely when women describe their discomfort during antenatal visits. Typical signs include a burning chest pain that often worsens after eating, bending over, or lying down. Some women report a sour or bitter taste in the back of the throat, frequent burping, or a feeling that food is stuck in the middle of the chest. Distinguishing these classic reflux signs from cardiac issues or severe pre-eclampsia epigastric pain is a core midwifery competency.

Lifestyle and Dietary Modifications for Relief

First-line management for pregnancy heartburn relies entirely on safe, non-pharmacological adjustments to daily habits.

  • Dietary habits: Eat small, frequent meals rather than three large ones. Avoid trigger foods such as fatty, spicy, or fried dishes, citrus fruits, chocolate, and caffeine.
  • Postural habits: Remain upright for at least an hour after eating. Elevate the head of the bed using blocks to let gravity help keep stomach acid down during sleep.
  • Clothing: Wear loose-fitting garments around the waist to avoid adding external pressure to the stomach.

Pharmacological Management and Safe Antacids

When lifestyle changes fail to control symptoms, safe medical treatments can be introduced. Antacids containing calcium carbonate or magnesium are generally considered safe during pregnancy because they neutralize existing stomach acid quickly. Midwives should caution mothers against self-medicating with sodium bicarbonate, as high sodium loads can cause fluid retention. If simple antacids are insufficient, histamine-2 blockers like ranitidine or proton pump inhibitors may be prescribed by a physician.

When to Escalate Care for Severe Symptoms

While heartburn is usually a minor discomfort, midwives must recognize warning signs that indicate a more serious condition. A client must be referred to a medical doctor if she experiences severe, unrelenting pain, difficulty swallowing, vomiting blood, or sudden weight loss. Additionally, sharp upper abdominal pain high up near the ribs must be carefully evaluated to rule out severe pre-eclampsia or HELLP syndrome, which can masquerade as severe indigestion.
