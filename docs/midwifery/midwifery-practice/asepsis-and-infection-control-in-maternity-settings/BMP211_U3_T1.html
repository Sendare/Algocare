Principles of Infection Prevention in Delivery Rooms and Wards

Understanding Infection Risks in Maternity Environments

Maternity units are high-traffic, high-risk environments where infectious agents easily spread between mothers, newborns, and healthcare personnel. During labour and childbirth, normal physiological barriers break down. Cervical dilation, ruptured fetal membranes, and surgical incisions like episiotomies or caesarean section wounds create direct entry points for bacteria. Postnatal wards also host mothers with lochial discharges and surgical wounds living in close proximity with fragile neonates who lack fully developed immune systems.

Common pathogens encountered in maternity wards include Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and group B Streptococcus. These organisms cause preventable conditions such as puerperal sepsis, wound infections, and neonatal sepsis. Recognizing these vulnerable entry points and specific transmission routes forms the foundation of all clinical infection control measures.

Core Principles of Infection Prevention in Delivery Rooms and Wards

Infection prevention and control in maternity care relies on breaking the chain of infection at every possible link. The fundamental premise is that every patient, fluid, and tissue must be treated as potentially infectious. Midwives must assume infection risks are present even when clinical signs are absent.

  • Chain of Infection: Interventions target the infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, and susceptible host.
  • Universal Risk Perception: Treating all blood, body fluids, secretions, and non-intact skin with equal caution.
  • Hierarchy of Controls: Prioritizing engineering controls and safe administrative policies alongside personal protective equipment.

Applying these core doctrines consistently protects both the healthcare worker and the vulnerable mother-infant pair across every shift.

Standard Precautions and Hand Hygiene Protocols

Hand hygiene is universally recognized as the single most effective intervention to prevent healthcare-associated infections. In busy maternity wards, midwives perform hand hygiene repeatedly between patient contacts, aseptic tasks, and administrative duties.

Hand hygiene can be performed using plain soap and water or alcohol-based hand rubs, depending on the degree of visible contamination.

  1. Before touching a patient: Protects the patient against harmful germs carried on your hands.
  2. Before clean or aseptic procedures: Protects the patient from getting harmful germs inside their body.
  3. After body fluid exposure risk: Protects yourself and the health-care environment from harmful patient germs.
  4. After touching a patient: Protects yourself and the health-care environment from harmful patient germs.
  5. After touching patient surroundings: Protects yourself and the health-care environment from harmful germs on nearby surfaces.

Fingernails must be kept short, artificial nails prohibited, and hand jewelry removed to ensure effective friction and friction-based cleansing.

Use and Disposal of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical barrier between healthcare workers and infectious materials. During childbirth, midwives face splashes of amniotic fluid, blood, and lochia. Selecting and wearing the correct item at the right time prevents direct mucous membrane and skin exposure.

  • Gloves: Wear clean non-sterile gloves for routine vaginal examinations; use sterile gloves for surgical procedures like catheterization or repair of tears.
  • Aprons and Gowns: Fluid-resistant plastic aprons protect uniforms from heavy splashes during the active second stage of labour or active bleeding.
  • Face Protection: Masks and eye visors or goggles shield the face when splashing of blood or amniotic fluid is anticipated.

Equipment must be removed carefully without touching the outer contaminated surfaces, followed immediately by hand hygiene. Improper removal contaminates clothing and skin, rendering the PPE useless.

Aseptic Techniques During Labour and Childbirth

Aseptic technique aims to minimize the presence of pathogenic microorganisms during invasive clinical procedures. In the delivery room, procedures requiring strict aseptic protocol include vaginal examinations, artificial rupture of membranes, urinary catheterization, perineal suturing, and the management of the third stage.

Maintaining an aseptic field requires sterile equipment, proper skin preparation of the perineal area using recommended antiseptics, and no-touch techniques where possible. Midwives must recognize that breaks in asepsis introduce pathogens directly into the uterine cavity, significantly increasing the likelihood of postpartum endometritis. Regular training ensures that procedural steps are performed methodically without rushing.

Environmental Cleaning, Decontamination, and Waste Management

A clean delivery room and ward environment is vital for reducing healthcare-associated infections. Surfaces, delivery beds, and reusable instruments harbour pathogens if not treated through structured decontamination protocols.

  • Decontamination: The process of making items safe to handle by removing organic matter and blood through washing with water and detergent or chlorine solutions.
  • Disinfection: Using chemical agents such as sodium hypochlorite solutions to destroy most pathogenic microorganisms on inanimate surfaces.
  • Sterilization: Complete elimination of all forms of microbial life, essential for surgical instruments coming into contact with broken skin or sterile body cavities.

Waste must be segregated at the point of generation into colour-coded bins: infectious waste (red or yellow bags for items soiled with blood or body fluids), sharps containers for needles and blades, and non-hazardous municipal waste for general paper and packaging.

Safeguarding Newborns and Postnatal Mothers from Hospital-Acquired Infections

Postnatal wards must balance family-centered care with strict protection against cross-infection. Newborns are colonized rapidly by environmental flora, and preventing colonization by virulent hospital strains is paramount. Promotion of exclusive breastfeeding provides passive maternal antibodies that guard the neonate against enteric and respiratory infections.

Visitors should be limited and screened for active respiratory or skin infections before entering postnatal rooms. Cord care protocols must emphasize clean, dry maintenance without applying harmful substances like saliva, dirty oils, or unsterilized powders. Midwives also educate mothers on early recognition of infection signs, such as poor feeding, lethargy, umbilical redness, or elevated temperature, ensuring prompt medical intervention.
