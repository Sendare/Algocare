Active Management of Third Stage of Labor (AMTSL): Uterotonic Administration, Controlled Cord Traction, Uterine Massage

Introduction to the Third Stage of Labor and AMTSL Rationale

The third stage of labor begins immediately after the birth of the baby and ends with the delivery of the placenta and fetal membranes. This phase carries the highest physiological risk for the mother due to the constant threat of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), which remains a leading cause of maternal mortality across Nigeria. During a physiological or passive third stage, the placenta separates spontaneously through the shearing of the decidua spongiosa layer, relying entirely on natural uterine contractions and maternal clotting mechanisms.

Active Management of the Third Stage of Labor, universally known as AMTSL, is a bundle of three distinct clinical interventions designed to accelerate placental separation and actively control uterine bleeding. By replacing a passive approach with timely prophylactic measures, midwives can drastically reduce blood loss and prevent primary PPH. Implementing AMTSL routinely is standard evidence-based practice for every facility-based birth, regardless of whether the mother is considered low risk on admission.

First Step: Prompt Uterotonic Administration

The cornerstone of AMTSL is the timely administration of a uterotonic drug to stimulate forceful, rhythmic uterine contractions. Oxytocin is the golden standard and drug of choice because it acts rapidly, has minimal side effects, and is generally safe for all mothers.

  • Oxytocin: 10 International Units (IU) administered intramuscularly (IM) or slowly intravenously (IV). It usually begins working within two to three minutes.
  • Misoprostol: 600 to 800 micrograms given orally or sublingually, used primarily when oxytocin is unavailable or in lower-level primary health care settings without reliable cold-chain storage.
  • Ergometrine: 0.2 milligrams IM, but strictly contraindicated in women with pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, or chronic hypertension due to its risk of causing severe, dangerous spikes in maternal blood pressure.

Administer the chosen uterotonic within one minute of the baby's birth. Always confirm by abdominal palpation that a second twin is not hiding inside the uterus before giving the medication.

Second Step: Performing Controlled Cord Traction Safely

Controlled cord traction, abbreviated as CCT, helps deliver the separated placenta smoothly while minimizing the risk of cord snapping or uterine inversion. Midwives must never pull on the umbilical cord blindly or forcefully without prior signs of placental separation and adequate uterine contraction.

  1. Clamp and Hold: Clamp the umbilical cord close to the perineum and hold it firmly with one hand.
  2. Counter-Pressure: Place your other hand flat just above the mother's pubic bone, pushing the uterus gently upward and away from the pelvis to protect against uterine inversion.
  3. Traction: Wait for a strong uterine contraction, then pull gently and steadily downward and backward following the curve of the birth canal.
  4. Delivery: As the placenta presents at the introitus, cradle it with both hands and twist it gently to ensure the membranes peel out completely without tearing.

Third Step: Post-Delivery Uterine Massage and Tone Assessment

The final step of the AMTSL bundle involves immediate and ongoing assessment of the uterine tone. As soon as the placenta and membranes are delivered, gently massage the uterine fundus through the mother's abdominal wall until the muscle becomes firm and well-contracted.

A soft, boggy uterus indicates uterine atony, which fails to compress open maternal blood vessels at the placental site and leads directly to excessive bleeding. Midwives should palpate the fundus every fifteen minutes during the first hour after birth. If the uterus feels relaxed during these checks, immediately perform firm circular massage and encourage the infant to suckle at the breast to stimulate natural oxytocin release. Never leave a newly delivered mother without checking her uterine tone regularly.

Timing, Sequencing, and Preventing Postpartum Hemorrhage

The clinical effectiveness of AMTSL relies heavily on strict adherence to proper timing and sequence. Giving the uterotonic medication too late—such as after the placenta has already been delivered manually—loses the preventative window against atony. Conversely, pulling on the cord before the uterotonic has taken effect increases the risk of tearing the umbilical cord or causing inversion.

By ensuring oxytocin is given within one minute of birth, combining it with controlled cord traction, and maintaining vigilance through fundal massage, midwives successfully manage the physiological transition. When any single step is omitted or mistimed, the risk of primary postpartum hemorrhage escalates significantly, turning a normal physiological event into a dangerous obstetric emergency.

Complications, Troubleshooting, and Midwifery Documentation

Even with perfect execution of AMTSL, complications such as partial placental separation, retained placental fragments, or persistent bleeding can arise. If the placenta fails to deliver within thirty minutes of oxytocin and CCT, suspect a retained placenta and prepare for manual removal under aseptic technique and anesthesia.

Accurate record keeping is an essential component of professional midwifery practice. The midwife must document the exact time of delivery, the specific uterotonic drug and dose administered, the time of placental expulsion, the completeness of the placental membranes and cotyledons, and the estimated blood loss. Clear documentation protects the mother's health record and provides a legal account of the immediate postpartum care provided.
