Acid-Base Concepts and pH Scale

Fundamental Acid-Base Concepts

Life in the human body depends on a very delicate chemical balance. At the core of this balance are acids and bases. An acid is a chemical substance that can donate a hydrogen ion ($H^+$) to another substance. Think of it as a donor. Conversely, a base (often called an alkali) is a substance that can accept a hydrogen ion.

In clinical practice, understanding this push and pull helps midwives and nurses grasp why body fluids must maintain a steady state. When acids accumulate or bases deplete, cell functions slow down or stop working entirely. The concentration of hydrogen ions in the blood dictates how acidic or alkaline that fluid is. A high number of free hydrogen ions makes a fluid strongly acidic, while very few hydrogen ions mean the fluid is basic.

Hydrogen Ion Concentration and the pH Scale

The pH scale is the measurement tool used to express the degree of acidity or alkalinity of a fluid. It runs from 0 to 14.

  • Neutral pH: Exactly 7.0, where hydrogen ions and base ions are perfectly balanced, just like pure water.
  • Acidic pH: Anything below 7.0. The lower the number, the stronger the acid and the higher the hydrogen ion concentration.
  • Alkaline pH: Anything above 7.0. The higher the number, the more basic the fluid.

In human blood, normal arterial pH ranges tightly between 7.35 and 7.45. If a patient's blood pH drops below 7.35, they have acidemia. If it rises above 7.45, they have alkalemia. Because the scale is logarithmic, even a tiny numerical shift represents a massive change in actual hydrogen ions in the bloodstream.

Interpreting Acidosis and Alkalosis

When body pH moves outside the normal 7.35 to 7.45 window, two main conditions can occur: acidosis or alkalosis.

Acidosis is caused by an excess of acid or a loss of base in the body. It depresses the central nervous system, leading to lethargy, confusion, or even coma in severe cases. Alkalosis arises from an excess of base or a loss of acid, such as through severe vomiting. It over-excites the nervous system, causing muscle cramps, twitching, or tingling fingers.

These imbalances are further split into two origins:

  1. Respiratory: Caused by problems with lung ventilation and carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) retention or blowing off.
  2. Metabolic: Caused by primary changes in bicarbonate ($HCO_3^-$) or non-volatile metabolic acids in the body fluids.

Physiological Buffer Systems

The body does not rely on just one mechanism to control pH; it uses three lines of defense called buffer systems.

First are the chemical buffers acting within seconds. The bicarbonate buffer system is the most important extracellular buffer, paired with phosphate and protein buffers inside cells. They instantly mop up or release hydrogen ions.

Second are the respiratory buffers, which respond within minutes. The lungs adjust the speed and depth of breathing. By exhaling more carbon dioxide (which acts as an acid in water), the lungs quickly raise the pH.

Third are the renal buffers, the slowest but most powerful system taking hours to days. The kidneys can actively excrete fixed acids into the urine and reabsorb or manufacture new bicarbonate ions to restore total chemical balance.

Clinical Implications of pH Imbalances

Recognizing acid-base disturbances is a vital skill in midwifery and emergency care. Severe shifts in pH interfere with normal enzymatic reactions and change how medications bind to proteins in the blood. For instance, low pH alters potassium balance, often pushing potassium out of cells and causing dangerous hyperkalemia that threatens the heart muscle.

When caring for women experiencing severe hemorrhage, eclampsia, or sepsis, midwives monitor vital signs and breathing patterns closely. Uncorrected acid-base disorders reduce cardiac output and weaken uterine contractions during labor. Treatment always focuses on fixing the underlying root cause—such as treating an infection, providing oxygen, or administering intravenous fluids—while letting the body's buffer systems finish the healing process.
