Acid-Base Homeostasis and Clinical Disturbances (Acidosis and Alkalosis)

Fundamental Principles of Acid-Base Homeostasis

Acid-base homeostasis refers to the precise regulation of hydrogen ion concentrations within the body fluids. The human body must maintain arterial blood pH within a very narrow physiological range of 7.35 to 7.45 to ensure optimal enzyme function and cellular metabolism. A pH falling below 7.35 indicates acidemia, while a value rising above 7.45 signifies alkalemia.

Metabolic processes constantly produce volatile and non-volatile acids. Volatile acids, primarily carbon dioxide, are end-products of aerobic respiration and are eliminated through the lungs. Non-volatile acids, such as sulfuric and phosphoric acids derived from protein catabolism, cannot be exhaled and must be excreted by the kidneys. To prevent drastic pH shifts when these acids enter the extracellular fluid, the body relies on immediate chemical buffer systems.

Renal and Respiratory Mechanisms in pH Regulation

When chemical buffers are overwhelmed, physiological regulatory systems step in to restore balance. The respiratory system acts rapidly, within minutes to hours, by altering ventilation rates. Hyperventilation blows off carbon dioxide, reducing carbonic acid and raising pH, whereas hypoventilation retains carbon dioxide to lower pH.

The renal system provides the definitive, long-term regulation of acid-base balance. Taking hours to days to fully respond, the kidneys can selectively reabsorb or generate new bicarbonate ions while excreting fixed hydrogen ions into the urine. Together, these two systems maintain the normal 20:1 ratio of bicarbonate to carbonic acid in the extracellular fluid.

Understanding Acidosis: Metabolic and Respiratory Origins

  • Respiratory Acidosis: Caused by alveolar hypoventilation leading to carbon dioxide retention and an elevated partial pressure of carbon dioxide. Common causes include chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, severe asthma attacks, and central nervous system depression from opioids.
  • Metabolic Acidosis: Arises from a primary deficit of bicarbonate or an accumulation of non-volatile organic acids. Frequent clinical triggers include diabetic ketoacidosis, severe diarrhea causing base loss, and lactic tissue hypoxia from shock.

Exploring Alkalosis: Causes and Manifestations

Alkalosis develops when body fluids lose too many hydrogen ions or gain an excess of base, pushing the pH above 7.45.

Respiratory alkalosis is driven by hyperventilation, which rapidly expels carbon dioxide. Anxiety, fever, early-stage sepsis, and mechanical ventilation set too high are frequent culprits. Patients often report tingling fingers, lightheadedness, and muscle cramps.

Metabolic alkalosis stems from a loss of acid or a gain of bicarbonate. Severe vomiting, prolonged gastric suctioning, and the overuse of loop or thiazide diuretics are classic causes. The body attempts to compensate through hypoventilation, retaining carbon dioxide to normalize the pH.

Interpreting Laboratory Findings and Nursing Implications

Analyzing arterial blood gases requires a systematic evaluation of pH, carbon dioxide, and bicarbonate levels. Midwives and nurses must correlate these laboratory values with the clinical condition of the woman or newborn under care.

When evaluating a report, first examine the pH to determine if acidemia or alkalemia is present. Next, look at the partial pressure of carbon dioxide to assess the respiratory component. Finally, check the bicarbonate level to evaluate the metabolic component. Identifying whether the primary imbalance is compensated helps guide safe clinical interventions, such as monitoring respiratory rates during magnesium sulfate therapy or replacing lost electrolytes.
