Types of Solutions, Solubility and Concentration Expressing

Understanding Solutions in Clinical Practice

A solution is a homogeneous mixture composed of two or more substances. In midwifery and general nursing, we deal primarily with aqueous solutions where water serves as the solvent and various electrolytes, minerals, or drugs serve as the solutes. Understanding how these substances mix, dissolve, and interact within the body is fundamental to safe medication administration and intravenous fluid therapy.

  • Solvent: The liquid in which a solute is dissolved, most commonly sterile water or normal saline in clinical settings.
  • Solute: The solid, liquid, or gas substance dissolved in the solvent, such as sodium chloride, potassium, or powdered antibiotics.
  • Concentration: The exact amount of solute present in a specific volume of solvent or total solution.

Types of Solutions: Isotonic, Hypotonic, and Hypertonic

Body fluids depend heavily on the tonicity of administered intravenous solutions. Tonicity describes how an extracellular solution can change the volume of a cell by affecting osmosis across the cell membrane.

  1. Isotonic Solutions: Have the same effective osmolality as body fluids. They expand the intravascular space without causing fluid to shift into or out of cells. Examples include 0.9% Normal Saline and Ringer's Lactate.
  2. Hypotonic Solutions: Have a lower osmolality than intracellular fluid, causing water to move out of the blood vessels and into the cells. 0.45% Sodium Chloride is a common example, often used to treat cellular dehydration.
  3. Hypertonic Solutions: Have a higher osmolality, drawing water out of the cells and into the bloodstream. Examples include 10% Dextrose in Water and 3% Sodium Chloride, which require careful monitoring to avoid fluid overload.

Solubility and Factors Affecting Dissolution

Solubility refers to the maximum amount of a solute that can dissolve in a given amount of solvent at a specific temperature. When mixing medications, midwives must observe whether a solute fully dissolves or leaves undissolved particles.

Several factors influence how fast and how well a substance dissolves:

  • Temperature: For most solid drugs, increasing the temperature increases solubility, though some substances behave unexpectedly.
  • Agitation: Stirring or shaking brings fresh portions of solvent into contact with the solute, speeding up dissolution.
  • Particle Size: Crushing large crystals into a fine powder increases the surface area exposed to the solvent, helping it dissolve much faster.

Methods of Expressing Concentration

Clinicians must accurately communicate how much medication is packed into a specific volume of solution. Concentration can be expressed in several standard formats:

Calculating Medication Dosages and IV Infusions

Safe midwifery practice requires routine calculation of drug doses and intravenous drop rates to prevent toxicity or under-treatment. The basic formula for calculating oral or injectable medication is:

$$\text{Desired Dose} \div \text{Stock Dose} \times \text{Volume} = \text{Amount to Administer}$|

For intravenous fluid infusions, the calculation involves the total volume, the time period, and the drop factor of the infusion set (usually measured in drops per millilitre, such as 15 drops/mL or 20 drops/mL for standard sets). Always double-check calculations with a colleague when dealing with high-risk drugs like oxytocin or magnesium sulfate.

Clinical Implications for Fluid and Electrolyte Balance

Imbalances in body fluids and electrolytes can lead to serious complications during pregnancy, labour, and the puerperium. Midwives must monitor intake and output charts, assess for signs of dehydration or oedema, and understand how IV fluids affect electrolyte shifts.

For example, infusing large volumes of 0.9% saline too quickly can lead to hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis. Similarly, administering hypotonic fluids to a woman with severe pre-eclampsia can worsen cerebral oedema. Careful selection of solutions based on individual clinical assessment protects maternal and fetal well-being.
