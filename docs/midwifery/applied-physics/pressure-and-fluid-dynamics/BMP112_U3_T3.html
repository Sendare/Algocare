Intracranial and Intraocular Pressure

Introduction to Intracranial and Intraocular Pressure

Body fluids exist within rigid or semi-rigid cavities, exerting measurable pressure against their surrounding walls. Inside the human body, two closed fluid systems require careful physiological regulation: the cranial vault and the eyeball. Intracranial pressure (ICP) measures the hydrostatic force exerted by brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid inside the skull. Meanwhile, intraocular pressure (IOP) measures the fluid pressure inside the eye chamber. When these pressures exceed normal physiological limits, vital structures suffer ischemic damage, threatening neurological function and vision.

Understanding Normal Intracranial Pressure and the Monro-Kellie Doctrine

The adult skull is a fixed, unyielding box. According to the Monro-Kellie doctrine, the total volume of the intracranial contents—consisting of brain tissue (about 80%), blood (about 10%), and cerebrospinal fluid (about 10%)—must remain constant. If the volume of one component increases, another must decrease to prevent a dangerous rise in pressure. Normal adult ICP ranges between 5 to 15 mmHg. Early compensation occurs through the displacement of cerebrospinal fluid into the spinal subarachnoid space and the reduction of intracranial venous blood volume. Once compensatory mechanisms exhaust, small volume increases trigger exponential spikes in pressure.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations of Raised Intracranial Pressure

When intracranial pressure rises past compensatory limits, cerebral perfusion pressure drops, leading to cellular hypoxia and brain tissue ischemia. Classic warning signs include headache, projectile vomiting without nausea, and a progressive decline in the level of consciousness.

  • Cushing's Triad: A late, ominous sign consisting of widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations.
  • Papilledema: Swelling of the optic disc observed during fundoscopic examination due to transmitted pressure along the optic nerve sheath.
  • Herniation: Displacement of brain tissue from one compartment to another, compressing the brainstem and threatening life.

Physiology of Aqueous Humor and Intraocular Pressure Dynamics

The eye maintains its spherical shape and internal pressure through the continuous production and drainage of aqueous humor, a clear fluid secreted by the ciliary body into the posterior chamber. Fluid flows around the lens, through the pupil into the anterior chamber, and leaves the eye through the trabecular meshwork and the canal of Schlemm. Normal intraocular pressure ranges from 10 to 21 mmHg. If fluid production outpaces drainage, or if outflow channels become blocked, pressure inside the eye rises steadily, compressing delicate retinal nerve fibers.

Pathogenesis and Presentation of Glaucoma

Elevated intraocular pressure is the primary risk factor for glaucoma, a progressive optic neuropathy that causes irreversible peripheral vision loss.

FeatureOpen-Angle GlaucomaClosed-Angle Glaucoma
OnsetGradual and silent over yearsSudden and acute emergency
Drainage angleOpen but functionally resistedPhysically blocked by the iris
SymptomsTunnel vision noticed lateSevere eye pain, halos, vomiting

In open-angle glaucoma, patients experience few symptoms until significant vision is lost. Conversely, angle-closure glaucoma creates an ocular emergency marked by excruciating eye pain, blurred vision, colored halos around lights, and corneal clouding.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring Techniques

Clinical assessment requires vigilance to detect subtle shifts in neurological and ocular status. For patients at risk of raised ICP, midwives and nurses monitor the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), pupillary light reflexes, and motor responses. Avoid procedures that cause transient pressure spikes, such as excessive suctioning, extreme neck flexion, or nursing the patient flat. For intraocular pressure, nurses use tonometry (such as applanation or non-contact puff tonometry) to screen for elevated pressures, while keeping in mind that certain systemic drugs like corticosteroids can worsen intraocular hypertension.

Emergency Interventions and Patient Management

Managing acutely elevated intracranial or intraocular pressure requires swift medical and nursing teamwork. For intracranial hypertension, interventions aim to reduce brain volume or cerebrospinal fluid. Clinicians administer osmotic diuretics like mannitol to draw fluid out of brain tissue, maintain adequate oxygenation, and elevate the head of the bed to 30 degrees. In acute angle-closure glaucoma, emergency treatment uses topical miotics (such as pilocarpine) and systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibitors to rapidly decrease aqueous humor production and force drainage angle opening before permanent blindness occurs.
