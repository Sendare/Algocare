Blood Pressure Measurement Principles

Hemodynamic Foundations: Pressure, Flow, and Resistance

Blood pressure represents the lateral force exerted by circulating blood against the walls of the arteries. This force is generated primarily by the pumping action of the heart and is modulated by the volume of blood, the elasticity of vessel walls, and the resistance offered by the peripheral vascular bed.

Flow through the circulatory system depends directly on the pressure gradient between two points and inversely on vascular resistance. When smooth muscle in the arterioles contracts, vessel diameter decreases, causing a sharp rise in resistance and a corresponding increase in systemic blood pressure. Conversely, vasodilation reduces resistance and lowers pressure. Midwives must understand these basic fluid dynamics because conditions like pre-eclampsia directly alter peripheral resistance, severely distorting blood pressure readings and requiring careful interpretation at the antenatal booking clinic.

The Physics of Laminar versus Turbulent Flow

Under normal physiological conditions, blood flows through unconstricted blood vessels in a smooth, streamlined pattern known as laminar flow. In laminar flow, the fluid particles move parallel to the vessel walls, with the fastest velocity in the center of the lumen and minimal friction against the endothelium. Because this flow is completely silent, a healthy artery makes no audible sound when auscultated with a stethoscope.

When a blood pressure cuff is inflated and partially compresses an artery, it disrupts this smooth pathway. The constriction forces the blood to move in chaotic, swirling eddies known as turbulent flow. This turbulence creates audible vibrations against the vessel wall, forming the physical basis for detecting blood pressure using a stethoscope and sphygmomanometer.

Auscultatory Method and Phase Analysis of Korotkoff Sounds

The auscultatory technique relies on listening to Korotkoff sounds as a pneumatic cuff is gradually deflated. As the cuff pressure drops below the peak systolic pressure, blood begins to spurt through the compressed vessel during the peak of the cardiac cycle, generating specific auditory phases:

  1. Phase I: The appearance of clear, tapping sounds as the first blood forces its way through the compressed artery. This marks the systolic blood pressure.
  2. Phase II: The sounds become softer and take on a swishing or murmuring quality as the cuff continues to deflate.
  3. Phase III: The sounds become crisper and louder again as blood flows more freely through the partially open vessel.
  4. Phase IV: The sounds suddenly become muffled and muted, indicating that the vessel is losing its compression.
  5. Phase V: The sounds disappear entirely as the artery remains fully open throughout the cardiac cycle. In adults, the point of complete disappearance marks the diastolic blood pressure.

Oscillometric Technique and Automated Devices

Automated blood pressure machines do not use a stethoscope or listen for Korotkoff sounds. Instead, they operate on the oscillometric technique. An electronic monitor detects tiny pressure pulses, or oscillations, transmitted to the cuff from the arterial wall as the cuff slowly deflates from an inflated state.

When the artery is fully compressed, the pulsations are very small. As the cuff pressure drops and blood begins to force its way through during systole, the oscillations suddenly increase in amplitude, reaching a maximum peak at the mean arterial pressure. As the cuff continues to deflate past the diastolic pressure, the oscillations decrease sharply. A microchip inside the device uses a mathematical algorithm to calculate the systolic, mean, and diastolic pressures based on these changing wave amplitudes. While convenient in busy labor wards, midwives should remember that these devices can struggle to give accurate readings if the pregnant woman is experiencing severe shivering or cardiac arrhythmias.

Critical Equipment Standards and Cuff Sizing Accuracy

Getting an accurate blood pressure reading depends heavily on using the correct equipment size. The inflatable bladder inside the cuff must be properly matched to the circumference of the patient's arm.

Cuff Size IssueEffect on Blood Pressure ReadingClinical Consequence
Cuff too smallArtificially high readingsMay lead to a false diagnosis of hypertension
Cuff too largeArtificially low readingsMay miss dangerous hypertension like pre-eclampsia

To ensure proper fit, the bladder width should cover approximately 40% of the upper arm circumference, and the length should wrap around at least 80% to 100% of the arm. Midwives must always inspect the equipment and select an extra-large cuff for women with higher body mass indexes, rather than forcing a standard cuff onto a conical arm.

Common Sources of Error in Clinical Practice

Even with correct equipment, many technical and physiological mistakes can distort blood pressure readings during antenatal and labour ward checks.

  • Arm positioning: Keeping the patient's arm below the level of the heart falsely raises the reading due to hydrostatic pressure, while holding it too high makes the reading artificially low.
  • Clothing interference: Rolling up a tight sleeve can act like a tourniquet, muffling sounds and altering blood flow dynamics in the limb.
  • Bladder distension: Allowing a pregnant woman to sit with a full bladder can reflexively raise her systolic and diastolic pressure by several millimeters of mercury.
  • Communication errors: Talking during the measurement or failing to allow a few minutes of rest before starting can lead to stress-induced spikes in blood pressure.
