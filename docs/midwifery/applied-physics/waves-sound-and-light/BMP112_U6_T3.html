Application of Lasers and Ultraviolet Light in Medicine

Introduction to Light-Based Therapies in Healthcare

Light-based therapies have transformed modern medicine, moving far beyond simple illumination to become powerful tools for diagnosis, surgery, and healing. In clinical practice across Nigeria, midwives and nurses encounter technology driven by light energy daily, from phototherapy units treating neonatal jaundice to precise surgical beams. Understanding how light interacts with human tissue requires a basic grasp of physics. Unlike ordinary white light, which scatters in all directions and contains many wavelengths, medical light sources are often harnessed to deliver concentrated, specific energy to targeted cells.

Light behaves as both a wave and a particle, carrying energy packets known as photons. When these photons strike biological tissue, four primary interactions occur: reflection, transmission, absorption, and scattering. Medical applications rely heavily on absorption. Specific molecules within the body, called chromophores, absorb particular wavelengths of light energy. Once absorbed, this light energy transforms into heat, chemical reactions, or mechanical disruption, allowing clinicians to cut, coagulate, destroy, or stimulate living tissue without making large traditional incisions.

Fundamentals of Laser Physics and Medical Instrumentation

The word LASER stands for Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation. Unlike ordinary lamps, a laser produces a beam that is monochromatic (single wavelength/color), collimated (travels in a straight, parallel beam with minimal spreading), and coherent (all light waves are in phase with one another). These unique properties allow laser light to be focused onto an extremely tiny spot with high power density.

Inside a medical laser machine, three core components work together:

  • Active Medium: A solid, liquid, or gas (such as carbon dioxide, argon, or neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet - Nd:YAG) that determines the wavelength of the emitted light.
  • Pumping Source: An energy input, usually electrical or optical, that excites atoms within the active medium to a higher energy state.
  • Optical Cavity: A pair of mirrors surrounding the medium that bounces the light back and forth, forcing stimulated emission and building up a powerful beam before releasing it through a partially reflective output mirror.

Surgical and Therapeutic Applications of Lasers

In the operating theatre and specialized clinics, lasers serve as precision scalpels and healing tools. The biological effect on tissue depends entirely on the wavelength used, the power output, and how long the tissue is exposed. When a continuous, high-power laser beam strikes tissue, the intense heat causes water inside the cells to instantly vaporize, cutting cleanly through flesh while simultaneously sealing small blood vessels to reduce bleeding.

Different laser types serve distinct clinical specialties:

  • Carbon Dioxide (CO2) Laser: Highly absorbed by water, making it ideal for precise cutting and vaporizing superficial skin lesions or cervical tissues.
  • Argon Laser: Emits blue-green light that is readily absorbed by hemoglobin and melanin, making it invaluable in ophthalmology for treating retinal detachments and in dermatology for vascular birthmarks.
  • Nd:YAG Laser: Penetrates deeper into tissues, useful for coagulating larger blood vessels and treating deep-seated lesions in urology or gastroenterology.
  • Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT): Uses non-thermal, low-power light to stimulate cellular metabolism, reduce inflammation, and accelerate wound healing in postpartum perineal repairs.

Properties and Sources of Ultraviolet Light in Clinical Settings

Ultraviolet (UV) light is a form of electromagnetic radiation sitting between visible light and X-rays on the spectrum, with wavelengths ranging from 100 to 400 nanometers. Because human eyes cannot see UV radiation, it is categorized into three distinct bands based on biological effects and wavelength:

  • UVA (315–400 nm): The longest wavelength, penetrates deep into the dermis, often used in phototherapy combined with sensitizing drugs for skin disorders.
  • UVB (280–315 nm): Medium wavelength, largely absorbed by the epidermis, triggers vitamin D synthesis and is effective in treating localized psoriasis.
  • UVC (100–280 nm): The shortest and most energetic wavelength, highly absorbed by cellular DNA and lethal to microorganisms, making it strictly a germicidal tool.

In clinical environments, specialized lamps generate these wavelengths. For instance, fluorescent tubes coated with specific phosphors emit UVB for treating neonatal hyperbilirubinemia or stubborn skin conditions, while quartz-mercury vapor lamps produce germicidal UVC for disinfecting air and room surfaces.

Dermatological and Sterilization Uses of Ultraviolet Light

Ultraviolet light plays a dual role in healthcare: treating specific patient conditions and maintaining sterile hospital environments. In dermatology, controlled exposure to UVB light slows down the rapid overgrowth and scaling of skin cells seen in conditions like psoriasis and vitiligo. Furthermore, in maternity wards and pediatric units, blue-green visible and low-level UV-adjacent phototherapy lights convert toxic unconjugated bilirubin in newborn infants into water-soluble isomers that the baby's liver and kidneys can easily excrete.

For infection control, germicidal UVC lamps are deployed extensively across Nigerian healthcare facilities. UVC radiation damages microbial DNA by forming thymine dimers, which prevent bacteria, viruses, and fungi from replicating. Portable UVC disinfection robots and overhead ceiling fixtures sanitize operating theatres, isolation rooms, and laboratory surfaces between procedures, significantly lowering the risk of nosocomial (hospital-acquired) infections.

Safety Protocols and Hazards for Patients and Nursing Staff

While light-based therapies offer incredible clinical benefits, they also present distinct occupational and patient hazards. High-power surgical lasers can ignite flammable materials, including surgical drapes, dry prep solutions, and even oxygen tubes, creating an immediate operating theatre fire risk. Additionally, laser beams can reflect off shiny surgical instruments, accidentally injuring nearby staff or patients.

Eye damage is a primary concern for both lasers and ultraviolet light. Direct or reflected laser beams can cause permanent retinal burns or blindness, while chronic exposure to UV radiation can lead to photokeratitis (welder's eye), cataracts, and skin cancer. Midwives, nurses, and doctors must follow strict safety protocols:

  • Eye Protection: Wearing specific wavelength-blocking protective goggles for lasers and UV-resistant face shields.
  • Controlled Access: Displaying warning signs outside treatment rooms and keeping doors locked during active laser procedures.
  • Equipment Checks: Inspecting insulation on probes and ensuring smoke evacuators are running to clear hazardous surgical plumes.
  • Patient Shielding: Covering healthy skin and sensitive areas, such as neonatal eyes and gonads, during phototherapy.
