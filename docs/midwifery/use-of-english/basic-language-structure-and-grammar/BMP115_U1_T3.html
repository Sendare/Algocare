Common Errors in English Usage and Avoidance Strategies

Introduction to Precision in Midwifery Communication

Clear communication is vital for patient safety in Nigerian healthcare settings. Midwives document vital signs, drug doses, and labour progress daily. When language is vague or incorrect, dangerous clinical misunderstandings happen. Professional writing requires correct grammar, precise terminology, and absence of slang. A midwife's notes serve as legal evidence in court. Therefore, mastering standard English usage protects both the practitioner and the mother receiving care.

Subject-Verb Agreement Errors in Clinical Records

A common mistake in medical reports is mismatching subjects and verbs. Writers often get confused when a long phrase separates the subject from the verb.

  • Singular subjects: Take singular verbs, such as "The condition of the membranes is stable."
  • Plural subjects: Take plural verbs, such as "The results of the tests are ready."

Always locate the true subject of your sentence before writing the verb, ignoring descriptive words in between.

Confusing Similar Terms in Clinical Documentation

Midwives frequently mix up words that sound alike or have similar spellings. Using the wrong word changes the clinical meaning entirely and creates confusion during handovers.

Incorrect WordCorrect TermProper Context
LossLoseTo misplace something or fail to win
AffectEffectAffect is a verb (to influence); Effect is a noun (a result)
ComplementComplimentComplement completes; Compliment praises
PrinciplePrincipalPrinciple is a rule; Principal is a head person or main thing

Carefully verifying these homophones prevents embarrassing errors in official hospital documents.

Pronoun Ambiguity and Misplaced Modifiers

Pronouns like 'it', 'they', 'he', or 'she' must clearly refer to a single noun. When a sentence contains multiple nouns, using 'she' without clarity causes dangerous ambiguity.

For example, writing: "The nurse gave the mother the baby and she was crying." Who was crying—the mother or the baby? This is unclear.

Similarly, modifiers must sit next to the word they describe. Writing: "Being fully dilated, the midwife examined the patient" wrongly suggests the midwife is fully dilated. Always place descriptive phrases directly beside the actual noun they modify.

Practical Proofreading Techniques for Busy Clinicians

Midwives work under immense pressure during shift duties, making proofreading difficult yet essential. Simple strategies help catch mistakes before closing a case file:

  1. Read aloud quietly: Hearing your notes exposes awkward phrasing and missing words that your eyes skip over.
  2. Check numbers twice: Always double-check drug dosages, dates, and times against physical charts.
  3. Avoid excessive abbreviations: Use only approved hospital abbreviations to prevent misinterpretation by incoming shift colleagues.
  4. Take a brief pause: Step away from long entries for a minute before doing a final review if time permits.
