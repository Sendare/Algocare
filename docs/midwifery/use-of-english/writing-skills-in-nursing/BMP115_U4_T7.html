Incident Reports

Understanding Incident Reports in Midwifery Practice

An incident report is a formal administrative document used in healthcare facilities to record details of an unexpected event that harmed or could have harmed a patient, visitor, or staff member. In Nigerian maternity units, these reports capture events ranging from unexpected postpartum hemorrhage to delayed emergency theater transfers. Midwives must understand that this document serves a vital purpose for quality improvement and risk management, rather than serving as a tool for punishment. It creates a factual record that helps facility management identify system weaknesses and prevent similar occurrences in the future.

When and Why an Incident Report Must Be Filed

Midwives encounter many situations daily, but not every minor hiccup requires a formal report. Clear criteria dictate when documentation is mandatory. Any event that deviates from standard maternity care protocols or results in actual or potential harm must be captured.

  • Adverse patient outcomes: Unexpected maternal collapse, birth asphyxia, or severe perineal tearing beyond normal grading.
  • Medication errors: Wrong drug, incorrect dose, or omission of prescribed uterotonics like oxytocin.
  • Equipment failures: Oxygen machine malfunction during resuscitation or broken fetal monitors.
  • Near misses: A potential error caught just before it reaches the laboring mother, such as discovering a mismatched blood transfusion packet at the bedside.

Core Elements of an Accurate Clinical Report

A well-written incident report relies on precision, objectivity, and completeness. Vague summaries weaken the institution's ability to investigate. Every report must clearly answer the basic journalistic questions without leaving room for ambiguity.

  • Who: Full names and designations of all staff involved, along with the patient's hospital number.
  • What: A factual description of the event, including specific clinical observations.
  • When: Exact date and time the incident occurred, plus the time it was reported.
  • Where: Specific location within the maternity unit, such as the labor ward, antenatal clinic, or lying-in ward.

Step-by-Step Guide to Documenting an Event

Writing an incident report requires a calm and methodical approach. Midwives should complete the documentation as soon as possible after the clinical situation stabilizes, ensuring details remain fresh in memory.

  1. Ensure immediate safety: Attend to the patient's clinical needs first before touching any paperwork.
  2. Gather facts: Verify times, dosages, and equipment serial numbers rather than relying solely on guesswork.
  3. Write objectively: Describe actions taken and immediate patient responses using plain, direct language.
  4. Submit promptly: Forward the completed document through the proper administrative chain of command without unnecessary delay.

Avoiding Common Pitfalls and Subjective Language

Language matters immensely in official hospital documentation. A common pitfall is inserting emotional language, personal opinions, or finger-pointing into the report. Terms like "lazy," "incompetent," or "rude" have no place in professional writing. Instead of writing that a doctor "panicked," the midwife should document that the clinician "spoke in a raised voice and delayed giving orders." Furthermore, midwives must never document that an incident report was filed inside the patient's actual clinical case notes, as this creates legal confusion and mixes administrative records with medical charts.

Legal Implications and Confidentiality in Healthcare

Incident reports carry significant legal weight. While they are designed for internal quality control, they can sometimes be subpoenaed in court during litigation. Because of this exposure, midwives must maintain strict confidentiality. Completed forms should never be photocopied for personal use, left lying around public nurses' stations, or discussed with unauthorized individuals outside the healthcare team. Adhering to professional secrecy protects both the institution and the rights of the patients involved while ensuring that safety investigations remain fair and protected.
