Argumentative Essay Writing

Understanding Argumentative Essays in Nursing and Midwifery

An argumentative essay is a piece of academic writing where you take a firm position on a controversial clinical issue and defend it using valid evidence. In nursing and midwifery, these essays go beyond simple description. You are not just explaining how a procedure is done; you are arguing why one clinical approach is safer, more effective, or more ethical than another based on research.

Students often confuse argumentative essays with persuasive or informative essays. While an informative essay simply gives facts, and a persuasive essay might rely on emotional appeals, an argumentative essay requires logical reasoning backed by empirical data, clinical guidelines, and professional literature.

Choosing a Defensible Clinical Topic

A strong argumentative essay begins with a well-chosen topic. In midwifery, your topic must be debatable, meaning reasonable clinicians could disagree on the best course of action. Avoid topics that are already settled facts, such as whether washing hands prevents infection. Instead, choose topics with active professional debate, such as continuous electronic fetal monitoring versus intermittent auscultation for low-risk women in labor, or water birth in public health facilities.

Your chosen topic must also be narrow enough to cover thoroughly within your word limit, and it must have enough published evidence available in journals to support your claims.

Formulating a Clear Stance and Thesis Statement

Your thesis statement is the anchor of your entire essay. Usually placed at the end of your introductory paragraph, it tells the reader exactly what your position is and the main reasons why you hold it. A weak thesis is vague or merely states a topic, such as "This essay will discuss postpartum hemorrhage." A strong thesis takes a stand: "Active management of the third stage of labor should be routine in primary healthcare centers because it significantly reduces postpartum blood loss and maternal morbidity."

Every paragraph that follows must tie directly back to supporting this central claim.

Gathering Evidence from Credible Medical Literature

An argument is only as strong as the evidence behind it. In nursing and midwifery writing, your primary sources should be peer-reviewed journal articles, clinical practice guidelines from recognized bodies like the World Health Organization or Ministries of Health, and systematic reviews.

  • Primary Research: Clinical trials and observational studies that provide direct data.
  • Systematic Reviews: High-level summaries combining results from multiple studies.
  • Clinical Guidelines: Official recommendations from professional health organizations.

Avoid relying heavily on textbooks for cutting-edge debates, as textbooks can become outdated quickly.

Structuring the Argument: Introduction, Body, and Conclusion

A well-organized essay guides the reader smoothly from your opening hook to your final recommendation.

  1. Introduction: Grabs attention, provides background context on the clinical problem, and ends with your clear thesis statement.
  2. Body Paragraphs: Each paragraph focuses on a single supporting point, introduced with a topic sentence, backed by literature evidence, and analyzed in your own words.
  3. Conclusion: Restates your thesis in a new way, summarizes your main points, and highlights the implications for midwifery practice or education.

Addressing Counterarguments and Refutations

To write a truly compelling academic argument, you must acknowledge the other side. Ignoring opposing views makes your paper look weak or biased. When you present a counterargument—the best points that critics of your stance would make—you show thorough understanding of the topic.

After stating the opposing view, you provide your refutation, explaining why your stance remains stronger or safer despite their points. For example, if you argue for early hospital discharge after normal vaginal delivery, you must acknowledge the counterargument about missed early postpartum complications, and then refute it by explaining how structured home-visit follow-ups mitigate that risk.

Maintaining an Objective and Professional Tone

Professional writing requires a calm, evidence-based voice. Avoid emotional language, dramatic exaggerations, or attacking those who hold different clinical opinions. Instead of writing, "Doctors who ignore midwives are dangerous and reckless," write, "Collaborative multidisciplinary models have been shown to improve maternal outcomes compared to isolated practice."

Keep your writing formal, use appropriate medical terminology correctly, and avoid first-person pronouns where institutional guidelines recommend third-person academic voice. Let the strength of your data carry your argument.
