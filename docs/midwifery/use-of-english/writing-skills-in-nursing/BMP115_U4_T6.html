Patient Care Notes

The Purpose and Importance of Patient Care Notes

Patient care notes form the permanent written record of a woman's journey through pregnancy, labor, and the postnatal period. These documents serve primarily as a communication bridge between shifting multidisciplinary teams, ensuring that every midwife, doctor, and nurse understands the current clinical picture without relying on memory. In busy Nigerian healthcare facilities, where staff rotations are frequent and caseloads are heavy, clear notes prevent critical details from slipping through the cracks. Beyond immediate clinical handover, these records protect both the patient and the midwife. They provide objective evidence of the care delivered, clinical reasoning applied, and the timeliness of interventions should an audit, inquiry, or legal challenge arise years later.

Core Principles of Accurate Documentation

Professional documentation relies on strict adherence to established rules of clarity, timing, and objectivity. Every entry must be contemporaneous, meaning it is written as close to the event as possible rather than at the end of a long shift when memory fades. Midwives must record observations using precise measurements and standard medical abbreviations rather than vague descriptors like "patient is fine" or "normal labor."

  • Objectivity: Record what is seen, heard, and measured, avoiding personal judgments or emotional language.
  • Contemporaneity: Write entries immediately or soon after care is delivered to prevent omissions.
  • Clarity: Use legible handwriting or approved electronic systems without leaving blank spaces that could be altered later.
  • Correction: Never use correction fluid or scribble out text completely; draw a single neat line through the error, write "error," and sign beside it.

Structure and Essential Components of a Record

A complete patient care entry requires standard administrative details before any clinical narrative begins. Every single page or electronic screen must display the patient's full name, hospital number, age, and ward location. Each individual entry must start with the exact date and time, written in the 24-hour clock format to avoid confusion between morning and evening hours.

The body of the entry follows with a clear description of the clinical encounter, maternal vital signs, fetal heart rate patterns where applicable, and the woman's verbal responses or concerns. Finally, every entry must conclude with the signature, full printed name, and professional designation of the midwife writing the note. Unsigned notes hold no legal weight and leave accountability unclear.

Using the SOAP Format in Midwifery Practice

The SOAP framework offers a structured, problem-oriented method for organizing clinical notes during antenatal, labor, or postnatal assessments. It breaks documentation down into four logical segments that guide clinical reasoning.

  1. Subjective (S): What the woman tells you in her own words, such as "I feel lower abdominal tightening" or "The baby is moving less today."
  2. Objective (O): Measurable, observable findings gathered through physical examination, such as blood pressure, urinalysis results, temperature, and fetal heart rate.
  3. Assessment (A): The clinical interpretation or working diagnosis based on the subjective and objective data, such as "Primigravida at 38 weeks gestation in active first stage of labor."
  4. Plan (P): The proposed management steps, including medications ordered, monitoring frequency, referrals made, and health education provided.

Legal, Ethical, and Confidentiality Considerations

Patient records are legally binding documents protected by strict professional ethics and national privacy laws. Midwives have a duty of professional secrecy, meaning clinical details must never be discussed in public hospital corridors, staff cafeterias, or shared on personal social media accounts. Unauthorized viewing or sharing of case notes breaches patient trust and violates professional codes of conduct set by nursing and midwifery councils. When records are requested for legal proceedings or audits, they must be handled through proper administrative channels. Altering, destroying, or falsifying a patient record is a serious professional offense that can result in the immediate erasure of a midwife's license to practice.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid When Charting

Even experienced midwives can fall into bad documentation habits that weaken the clinical record. One frequent error is recording opinions instead of facts—writing "patient is uncooperative" rather than describing the specific behavior observed. Another pitfall is delayed charting, which often leads to inaccurate timelines and forgotten clinical details. Pre-charting—documenting an intervention or medication before it is actually performed—is a dangerous practice that undermines patient safety and constitutes falsification of records. Finally, using unauthorized abbreviations can cause severe miscommunication among team members who may misinterpret symbols during emergency obstetric situations.
