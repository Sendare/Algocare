Integrating Best Evidence into Daily Nursing Care

Understanding Evidence-Based Practice in Nigerian Midwifery

Evidence-based practice (EBP) is the conscientious integration of current best research evidence, clinical expertise, and patient values when making decisions about the care of individual maternity patients. In the context of Nigerian healthcare, EBP bridges the gap between traditional routines and proven scientific methods. Midwives often encounter practices handed down through generations that may no longer serve mothers and babies well. By anchoring daily care in valid research, midwives ensure that interventions like active management of the third stage of labor or delayed cord clamping consistently yield the best health outcomes. Embracing EBP does not mean ignoring local realities; rather, it combines global scientific discoveries with a deep understanding of the Nigerian cultural and socio-economic environment.

Identifying Clinical Questions at the Bedside

Clinical inquiry begins when a midwife pauses to question routine actions during patient care. Instead of accepting the phrase "we have always done it this way," a critical thinker asks whether a current practice truly offers the safest option. Using the PICOT framework helps structure these inquiries clearly.

  • P: Patient population or problem (e.g., primigravidae in active labor)
  • I: Intervention or indicator of interest (e.g., continuous labor support)
  • C: Comparison group or intervention (e.g., standard intermittent monitoring)
  • O: Outcome desired (e.g., reduced cesarean section rates)
  • T: Timeframe for the result (e.g., during the first stage of labor)

Formulating precise questions transforms vague clinical curiosities into searchable topics.

Accessing Reliable Research Resources Locally and Globally

Finding credible evidence requires knowing where to look and how to bypass low-quality sources. Midwives can access a wealth of peer-reviewed literature through databases such as PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Hinari, which offers free or low-cost journal access to institutions in developing nations. Local Nigerian journals, nursing council publications, and West African health bulletins also provide context-specific data that international platforms might miss. When evaluating any source, midwives must appraise the study design, sample size, and methodology to ensure the findings are trustworthy before applying them to maternity care.

Overcoming Barriers to Implementation in Resource-Constrained Settings

Translating research into daily practice within Nigerian health facilities often brings unique hurdles. Common challenges include erratic electricity supply, a shortage of essential drugs, high staff-to-patient ratios, and occasional resistance from senior colleagues attached to older traditions. Midwives can navigate these obstacles by starting small, such as introducing a single updated hygiene checklist rather than overhauling an entire unit at once. Engaging hospital management early helps secure administrative backing for minor workflow modifications. Peer champions also play a vital role by encouraging hesitant team members to adopt safer, evidence-backed techniques.

Collaborative Team Approaches to Evidence Uptake

A lone midwife rarely succeeds in shifting institutional culture alone. Successful adoption of research findings relies on interprofessional collaboration involving nurses, midwives, doctors, pharmacists, and hospital administrators. Regular ward meetings, mortality reviews, and clinical audits provide safe spaces to discuss research findings and agree on new protocols. When the entire healthcare team shares responsibility for updating care standards, blame decreases and patient safety increases. Involving junior staff in these discussions also fosters a vibrant culture of continuous learning from the start of their careers.

Evaluating Patient Outcomes After Practice Changes

The evidence-based practice cycle remains incomplete without evaluating the results of any implemented change. Midwives must track whether the new intervention actually improved maternal or neonatal health indicators. Data collection does not require complex statistical software; simple tally sheets on the ward can monitor metrics such as postpartum hemorrhage rates, perineal tear severity, or newborn APGAR scores. If evaluation shows positive results, the new practice becomes the new standard operating procedure. If outcomes do not improve, the team must reassess the evidence and adjust their approach accordingly.
