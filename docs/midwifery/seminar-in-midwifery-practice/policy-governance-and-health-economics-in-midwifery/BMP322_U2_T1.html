National Health Sector Reform Policies and Maternal Health Targets

Introduction to National Health Sector Reform Policies

National health sector reform policies are deliberate, government-led efforts to overhaul and strengthen a country's health system. These structural changes aim to fix deep-seated issues like poor infrastructure, drug shortages, uneven workforce distribution, and poor health outcomes. In Nigeria, reforming the health sector is critical because maternal and child mortality rates remain unacceptably high. When a government designs a reform policy, it typically targets four core functions: financing, service delivery, human resources, and governance. For midwives, understanding these policies moves clinical practice beyond the delivery room into the broader arena of public health advocacy and health systems strengthening.

Evolution and Objectives of Nigeria's Health Reforms

Nigeria's journey through health reform spans several decades, moving from centralized colonial structures to decentralized systems, and more recently, toward achieving universal health coverage. Past frameworks like the National Health Policy have continuously evolved to tackle persistent challenges in primary healthcare delivery. The overarching objective of recent reforms is to bridge the gap between urban and rural health access, reduce out-of-pocket spending for vulnerable populations, and revitalize primary health care centres across the 774 local government areas. Despite various policy documents, implementation gaps remain a recurring hurdle that practitioners encounter daily.

Key Maternal Health Targets in National Frameworks

National strategic health plans set explicit numerical targets for maternal health, focusing heavily on reducing the maternal mortality ratio (MMR), increasing skilled birth attendance coverage, and expanding access to family planning commodities. When a country commits to these targets, it shifts maternal health from a private medical issue to a public accountability metric. Midwives are at the frontline of achieving these targets because every antenatal visit, managed third stage of labour, and postpartum check directly impacts national health statistics.

Linking Sector Reforms to Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)

Nigeria's national health sector policies do not exist in a vacuum; they align closely with global agendas, most notably Sustainable Development Goal 3, which aims to ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all ages. Specifically, Target 3.1 calls for reducing the global maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 per 100,000 live births. Aligning local health reforms with global SDGs helps attract international donor support, encourages intersectoral collaboration, and holds governments accountable to international standards. For maternity care providers, understanding the SDGs gives global context to the local struggle against postpartum hemorrhage and eclampsia.

Financing Mechanisms and Resource Allocation for Maternal Care

  • Out-of-Pocket Expenditure: Direct payments made by patients at the point of service, which often prevent poor pregnant women from seeking timely care.
  • National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA): A legislative framework designed to pool financial risks and provide subsidized or free maternal and child health services.
  • Basic Health Care Provision Fund (BHCPF): A statutory fund created to improve primary healthcare delivery, ensuring basic minimum package of health services for the vulnerable.
  • Performance-Based Financing: Allocating financial resources to health facilities based on verified outputs and the achievement of specific maternal health service targets.

The Midwife's Role in Implementing National Policy at the Facility Level

Policies formulated at national ministries only succeed if frontline healthcare workers put them into practice. Midwives translate policy into action by implementing evidence-based protocols, maintaining accurate health management information system (HMIS) records, and advocating for respectful maternity care. When the government introduces free maternal and child health services or rolls out new partograph guidelines, it is the midwife at the Primary Health Centre or general hospital who executes the directive. Midwives also act as educators, bridging the gap between community expectations and official health sector guidelines.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and Overcoming Barriers to Policy Success

Monitoring and evaluation (M&E) systems are essential to track whether health sector reforms are actually reducing maternal mortality and improving service delivery. Common barriers to policy success include corruption, inadequate infrastructure, unreliable power supply, and severe shortages of skilled health personnel in rural areas. Overcoming these obstacles requires transparent governance, community engagement, and consistent supportive supervision rather than punitive fault-finding. Midwives play a key role by providing honest feedback from the grassroots level to help managers refine and adjust failing strategies.
