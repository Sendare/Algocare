Concept of Quality Improvement in Maternity Care

Understanding Quality Improvement in Midwifery Practice

Quality improvement (QI) in maternity care refers to systematic, continuous efforts to better maternal and newborn health services and health system performance. Unlike traditional quality assurance, which often focuses on finding individual faults or punishing errors after a bad outcome, QI focuses on analyzing and improving the underlying processes of care. In a bustling Nigerian labor ward, this means looking beyond a single delayed C-section to examine the overall referral communication pathway, staff shift handovers, and equipment availability. QI is driven by the local healthcare team who identify everyday clinical bottlenecks and test small, manageable changes to solve them. It relies on data to guide decisions rather than guesswork or rigid tradition.

Why Maternity Care Demands Continuous Enhancement

Pregnancy and childbirth are dynamic physiological events that can transition from normal to life-threatening within minutes. Because maternal and neonatal mortality rates remain unacceptably high in many low-resource settings, standard maternity practices must never remain static. Clinical guidelines evolve as new global and local research emerges regarding postpartum hemorrhage management, pre-eclampsia treatment, and neonatal resuscitation. Midwives operate in environments with shifting patient volumes, variable supply chains, and mixed-skill teams. Continuous enhancement ensures that clinical protocols match current scientific evidence and adapt to local facility realities, safeguarding mothers and newborns from preventable complications.

Core Principles Driving Clinical Excellence

  • Patient-Centeredness: Respecting woman-led choices, cultural preferences, and ensuring dignified maternity care during labor and delivery.
  • Safety: Actively minimizing risks, medication errors, and hospital-acquired infections during antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal periods.
  • Effectiveness: Delivering evidence-based interventions that actually improve clinical outcomes, such as active management of the third stage of labor.
  • Timeliness: Reducing unnecessary delays in recognizing obstetric emergencies and providing prompt surgical or medical intervention.

Identifying Gaps: Standards Versus Current Practice

Quality improvement begins by measuring where a maternity unit currently stands against established clinical standards. Midwives cannot fix what they do not measure. A clinical audit might reveal that while national guidelines state every pre-eclampsia patient must receive magnesium sulfate promptly, only forty percent actually do due to stockouts or staff hesitation. Pinpointing this gap requires reviewing labor ward registers, interviewing nursing staff, and observing real-time shift workflows. Once the exact point of failure is exposed, the team can direct their improvement efforts specifically at that bottleneck rather than trying to fix everything at once.

The Plan-Do-Study-Act Cycle in Labor Wards

The Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle is a four-step model used to test changes on a small scale before adopting them broadly across the maternity unit.

  1. Plan: State the objective, predict the outcome, and plan the test (e.g., introducing a new checklist for checking emergency tray drugs).
  2. Do: Carry out the small-scale test on a single shift or in one specific antenatal ward for one week.
  3. Study: Analyze the data collected during the test and compare the results with your original predictions.
  4. Act: Refine the change based on what was learned, adopt it permanently, or abandon it if it caused unintended problems.

Fostering a Just Culture and Team Communication

Quality improvement cannot thrive in a blame-heavy environment where junior midwives are terrified to report near-misses or medication errors. A just culture balances individual accountability with the understanding that human error is often shaped by faulty systems, poor lighting, fatigue, or broken equipment. Effective communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) ensure clear information transfer during emergency obstetric handovers between midwives and doctors. When staff feel safe speaking up about a failing intravenous line or an unmonitored bleeding patient, preventable maternal deaths drop significantly.

Measuring Impact: Maternal and Neonatal Indicators

To know if quality improvement efforts are working, maternity units must track specific, measurable indicators. Outcome indicators look at end results, such as the institutional maternal mortality ratio, fresh stillbirth rates, and the incidence of birth asphyxia. Process indicators measure whether best practices are actually being performed, such as the percentage of postpartum mothers receiving oxytocin within one minute of delivery or the proportion of laboring women monitored with a completed partograph. Regular review of these indicators during perinatal mortality and morbidity meetings helps teams evaluate their progress and maintain accountability.

Sustaining Improvements in Resource-Constrained Settings

Implementing quality improvement in resource-constrained Nigerian health facilities presents unique challenges, including erratic power supply, stockouts of essential drugs like hydralazine or oxytocin, and severe staff shortages. Sustaining changes requires ingenuity rather than expensive technology. Midwives utilize local solutions, such as community-donated emergency transport systems, solar-powered lanterns for night deliveries, and peer-led mentorship programs instead of costly external consultants. Leadership engagement and routine staff recognition are vital to prevent burnout and keep the clinical team motivated to maintain high standards of care despite daily hardships.
