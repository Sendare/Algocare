Clinical Audit Processes and Setting Practice Standards

Introduction to Clinical Governance in Nigerian Midwifery

Clinical governance is the framework through which healthcare organizations, including Nigerian maternity units, are accountable for continuously improving the quality of their services and safeguarding high standards of care. In the context of midwifery, it means moving beyond individual clinical competence to systemic accountability. Midwives do not work in isolation; they rely on functional equipment, supportive teamwork, regular training, and reliable policies.

Within Nigerian healthcare facilities, implementing clinical governance involves several core pillars:

  • Risk Management: Identifying and minimizing hazards that could harm mothers or newborns during labor and delivery.
  • Education and Training: Ensuring birth attendants keep their emergency obstetric skills updated, such as managing postpartum hemorrhage or shoulder dystocia.
  • Clinical Audit: Systematically reviewing current maternity care practices against recognized standards to find gaps.
  • User Involvement: Listening to pregnant women and their families about their birth experiences and taking their feedback seriously.

Understanding the Clinical Audit Cycle

A clinical audit is not a punitive inspection or a research project; it is a quality improvement cycle. The fundamental goal is to compare actual clinical practice against a pre-determined, evidence-based standard to see where care can be bettered, make the necessary changes, and measure improvement later.

The audit process follows a continuous loop known as the audit cycle:

  1. Selecting the topic: Choosing an important area of maternity care, such as active management of the third stage of labor.
  2. Setting standards: Defining what best practice looks like based on reliable guidelines.
  3. Collecting data: Gathering information from patient case notes or registers to see if current practice matches the standard.
  4. Analyzing results: Comparing findings against the set benchmarks to identify any shortfalls.
  5. Implementing change: Introducing interventions to fix identified problems, such as staff retraining or updating ward charts.
  6. Re-auditing: Repeating the data collection after a period to confirm that care has actually improved.

Formulating Evidence-Based Midwifery Protocols

To set practice standards, midwives must rely on solid, current evidence rather than personal habits or old traditions. An evidence-based protocol translates global research, such as World Health Organization guidelines or Nigerian national health policies, into practical, step-by-step instructions for the labor ward or antenatal clinic.

When writing or updating a protocol, ensure it is clear, realistic, and tailored to the local resource setting. For instance, a protocol for managing pre-eclampsia must clearly state when to administer magnesium sulfate, how to monitor the patient for toxicity reflexes, and when to arrange emergency referral. Vague instructions lead to inconsistent care. Once formulated, these protocols serve as the gold standard benchmarks used during subsequent clinical audits.

Selecting Clinical Indicators and Measurable Criteria

A clinical indicator is a measurable item of care that reflects the quality of service provided. Selecting the right indicators is vital because you cannot audit everything at once. Indicators are generally divided into three categories based on Donabedian's model of quality care:

  • Structure Indicators: Assess the environment and resources, such as whether the delivery suite has a functional clock, running water, and stocked oxytocin.
  • Process Indicators: Evaluate what actions are actually performed, such as whether every woman admitted in labor has her blood pressure checked and recorded hourly.
  • Outcome Indicators: Measure the end result of care, such as the rate of perineal tear repair complications, birth asphyxia rates, or maternal survival.

Good indicators must be specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. Focus on high-volume, high-risk, or high-cost areas of midwifery practice where improvements will have the greatest impact.

Data Collection and Retrospective Chart Reviews in Maternity Units

Once standards and indicators are established, data collection begins. In many Nigerian hospitals, this relies heavily on retrospective chart reviews, where midwives examine completed case notes, delivery registers, and partographs from past weeks or months.

Data collection requires absolute accuracy and confidentiality. Auditors must use a standardized data collection tool or checklist to ensure they gather the exact same information from every file. A common challenge in retrospective reviews is missing documentation; if a clinical action was performed by a midwife but never written down in the register, the audit treats it as unperformed. This highlights a golden rule in midwifery documentation: if it wasn't charted, it wasn't done, at least as far as an audit can prove.

Analyzing Audit Findings Against Established Benchmarks

After gathering the data, the next step is analysis. The raw numbers must be calculated into percentages or rates to see how closely clinical practice matched the target standard. For example, if the standard states that 100% of women with postpartum hemorrhage must receive uterotonics within two minutes, data analysis might reveal that the facility only achieved 72%.

During analysis, midwives should look beyond the simple percentages to uncover the root causes of any gaps. Was the lower percentage due to a lack of drugs in the unit, a shortage of trained staff during night shifts, or simply a lack of awareness regarding the protocol? Presenting these findings clearly using simple tables or charts helps communicate the results effectively to the rest of the healthcare team during audit feedback meetings.

Implementing Change and Closing the Audit Loop

Finding problems through an audit is useless if no action is taken to fix them. The implementation phase translates audit findings into practical improvements on the ward. Change strategies can include organizing targeted clinical drills, posting reminder charts on delivery room walls, conducting peer reviews, or reorganizing staff shift handovers.

Crucially, closing the audit loop requires a follow-up re-audit after a reasonable interval, usually three to six months. This second round of data collection proves whether the introduced changes successfully bridged the quality gap or if further adjustments are required. Clinical audit is a permanent journey of professional growth rather than a one-off project.
