Ruptured Uterus

Understanding the Pathophysiology of a Ruptured Uterus

A ruptured uterus represents a catastrophic tearing of the uterine wall, extending through the myometrium and visceral peritoneum. When this occurs, the intrauterine contents may spill freely into the peritoneal cavity. The mechanism largely depends on whether the uterus has a prior scar. In a scarred uterus, dehiscence of a previous lower segment transverse incision can rapidly progress to complete rupture under the influence of strong, uncoordinated contractions. Conversely, an unscarred uterus usually ruptures due to severe mechanical obstruction, such as severe cephalopelvic disproportion, where the upper uterine segment actively thickens and retracts while the lower segment stretches dangerously thin, forming a retraction ring known as Bandl's ring.

Etiology and Predisposing Risk Factors in Nigerian Practice

In Nigerian clinical practice, uterine rupture remains a major contributor to maternal morbidity and mortality, driven by distinct socio-cultural and systemic challenges.

  • Previous Uterine Scar: The single greatest risk factor is a prior caesarean section or classical uterine incision, especially if labor is subsequently induced or augmented with oxytocin.
  • Obstructed Labor: Prolonged, neglected labor due to cephalopelvic disproportion, malpositions, or large fetal macrosomia is a leading cause among unbooked mothers.
  • Grand Multiparity: Weakened myometrial fibers from multiple previous pregnancies increase overall vulnerability.
  • Unsupervised Use of Oxytocics: Inappropriate administration of uterotonic drugs by unskilled birth attendants in peripheral settings often precipitates hyperstimulation and tears.
  • Instrumental Trauma or Manipulation: Aggressive internal podalic version or clumsy forceps delivery can breach the uterine wall.

Recognizing the Transition from Impending to Complete Rupture

Early identification of impending uterine rupture saves lives. Midwives must maintain high vigilance during prolonged labors.

  • Impending Rupture: Characterized by severe, continuous lower abdominal pain, extreme restlessness, tenderness over the lower uterine segment, and the formation of a rising Bandl's ring. The pulse rate begins to climb.
  • Complete Rupture: Marked by a sudden, sharp, tearing abdominal pain often described by the patient as a 'ripping' sensation. Contractions suddenly cease. The patient rapidly enters hypovolemic shock, and fetal parts become easily palpable beneath the abdominal wall because the fetus has drifted into the peritoneal cavity.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Challenges

The clinical picture of a ruptured uterus varies widely depending on whether the rupture is complete or incomplete. Classic signs include sudden abdominal pain, abnormal fetal heart traces progressing quickly to fetal demise, and signs of internal hemorrhage such as pallor, tachycardia, and hypotension. Vaginal bleeding may range from minimal to severe; surprisingly, internal bleeding into the peritoneal cavity is often much greater than what is seen externally. Diagnostic delays occur when symptoms mimic other acute abdominal conditions like severe placental abruption, acute appendicitis, or severe ovarian torsion, particularly in patients without a known obstetric history.

Emergency Midwifery Management and Resuscitation Protocols

Time is the single most critical factor when managing a ruptured uterus. Midwives must initiate emergency protocols simultaneously while calling for senior medical help.

  • Immediate Resuscitation: Secure two large-bore intravenous lines (16G or 18G) and commence rapid infusion of crystalloids or colloids while awaiting cross-matched blood.
  • Vital Sign Monitoring: Check blood pressure, pulse, and respiratory rate every 15 minutes. Insert an indwelling urinary catheter connected to a urometer to accurately monitor hourly urine output, targeting at least 30 mL/hour.
  • Avoid Uterotonic Infusions: Do not administer oxytocin or other uterotonics before surgical intervention, as stimulating a torn uterus worsens hemorrhage.
  • Urgent Theatre Preparation: Expedite transfer to the operating theater for emergency laparotomy.

Surgical Intervention and Intraoperative Care

Emergency laparotomy is the definitive treatment for a ruptured uterus. The surgical team aims to control hemorrhage and either repair the organ or remove it. The choice between a uterine repair (hystororrhaphy) and a hysterectomy depends on the patient's parity, stability, and the extent of the tear. A young woman with a clean, low transverse tear who desires future children may undergo repair, often with bilateral tubal ligation discussed postoperatively due to high recurrence risk. Conversely, if the tear is ragged, extends into the broad ligament or cervix, or if the patient is unstable and has completed her family, a total or subtotal hysterectomy is performed to save her life. Intraoperative blood loss replacement with fresh whole blood or packed cells is managed closely by the anesthesia team.

Maternal and Fetal Outcomes Associated with a Ruptured Uterus

The consequences of a ruptured uterus are severe for both mother and baby. Maternal complications include severe hypovolemic shock, bladder injury, broad ligament hematoma, postpartum sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and maternal death. Long-term physical and psychological sequelae include chronic pelvic pain, fear of subsequent pregnancies, and infertility if a hysterectomy was required. Fetal outcomes are notoriously poor due to abrupt asphyxia from placental detachment and extrusion into the peritoneal cavity. High rates of stillbirth and neonatal hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy are standard in unbooked cases presenting late to tertiary facilities.

Postoperative Nursing Care and Long-term Reproductive Rehabilitation

Postoperative recovery requires intensive multidisciplinary care. Midwives monitor vital signs, surgical wound integrity, and lochial discharge closely to detect secondary hemorrhage or infection early. Pain management, thromboprophylaxis with early ambulation, and nutritional support facilitate healing. Because psychological trauma following a near-miss obstetric event is profound, compassionate counseling is essential for the mother and her family. Long-term reproductive rehabilitation involves counseling on future contraception, emphasizing the extreme risks of subsequent pregnancies, and planning pre-conception care if a uterine repair was successful and future childbearing is attempted under strict hospital supervision.
