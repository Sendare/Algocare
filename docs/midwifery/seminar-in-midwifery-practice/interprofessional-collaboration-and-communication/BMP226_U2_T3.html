Managing Workplace Conflict and Professional Boundaries in Clinical Practice

Understanding Professional Boundaries in Clinical Practice

Professional boundaries define the limits of the relationship between a healthcare provider and a patient, or among colleagues, ensuring a safe and therapeutic environment. In Nigerian midwifery practice, these boundaries protect both the vulnerable woman in labor and the clinician from emotional or ethical compromise. Crossing these lines can lead to a loss of objectivity, favoritism, or even exploitation.

  • Therapeutic relationship: Maintaining a focus entirely on the patient's health needs without involving personal matters.
  • Dual relationships: Wearing two hats, such as treating a close family member or a business associate, which clouds clinical judgment.
  • Self-disclosure: Sharing personal stories only when it directly comforts or benefits the patient, rather than seeking emotional support from them.

Sources and Triggers of Interprofessional Friction in Maternity Units

Maternity units are high-stress environments where life-and-death decisions happen daily. This pressure often breeds tension between midwives, resident doctors, consultants, and ward orderlies. Common triggers include unclear role definitions, fatigue from staff shortages, and competition for scarce resources like oxygen cylinders or functional delivery couches.

When a postpartum hemorrhage or fetal distress occurs, communication can quickly break down into finger-pointing. Midwives often feel undervalued by medical colleagues who may issue orders without consulting the nursing team that has spent hours monitoring the patient. Recognizing these underlying stressors is the first step toward preventing open hostility in the labor ward.

Navigating Power Dynamics and Hierarchies

Traditional medical hierarchies often place doctors at the top, leaving midwives and nurses struggling to have their clinical assessments taken seriously. In many Nigerian hospitals, questioning a medical prescription is culturally viewed as disrespect rather than a vital safety check.

Overcoming these imbalances requires shifting the focus from ego to patient outcomes. Midwives must build a reputation for thorough, evidence-based documentation so that their verbal reports command respect. Conversely, senior clinicians must cultivate an open-door culture where junior staff feel safe voicing concerns about a deteriorating patient without fear of public humiliation.

Assertive Communication and De-escalation Techniques

Communication breakdowns in the clinic often stem from passive or aggressive responses. Assertiveness sits in the healthy middle, allowing midwives to stand up for their clinical opinions while respecting others.

When a colleague or a frustrated relative becomes verbally aggressive, de-escalation is vital:

  1. Stay calm: Keep your voice steady and lower your volume to counter their anger.
  2. Listen actively: Let the person vent without immediate interruption to show you acknowledge their frustration.
  3. Use "I" statements: Frame concerns around patient safety rather than attacking the other person's character (e.g., "I am worried about this blood loss" instead of "You are ignoring this patient").

Maintaining Boundaries Under High-Stress Conditions

Burnout, fatigue, and severe staff shortages make it easy for midwives to slip into unprofessional habits. When managing four active first stages of labor simultaneously, patience wears thin, leading to harsh tone of voice or perceived mistreatment of women seeking care.

Maintaining boundaries under pressure means recognizing your own fatigue limits and asking for help before snapping. It also means refusing to engage in workplace gossip or petty departmental politics during handover sessions. Protecting your mental wellbeing ensures you can continue treating patients with dignity, even when the shift feels overwhelming.

Institutional Policies and Reporting Grievances

When conflicts escalate beyond daily peer communication—such as persistent bullying, harassment, or unsafe medical practices—informal talks often fail. Nigerian healthcare facilities typically have established grievance procedures that outline how staff should report issues.

Midwives must familiarize themselves with their hospital's human resources guidelines, nursing and midwifery council regulations, and chain of command. Documenting dates, times, and specific behaviors is essential before filing a formal complaint. Relying on structured institutional channels protects the whistleblower and ensures that disputes are resolved fairly without compromising patient care.
