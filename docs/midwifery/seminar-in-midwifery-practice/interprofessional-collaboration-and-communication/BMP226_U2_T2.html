SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) Communication Framework

Why Standardized Communication Matters in Nigerian Midwifery Practice

Clear communication is a matter of life and death in maternity units across Nigeria. Midwives juggle multiple patients, emergency referrals, and busy shifts where vital details can easily slip through the cracks. When healthcare workers use casual or disorganized talk during handovers, critical information gets missed. Standardized tools like SBAR create a shared mental picture among doctors, nurses, and community health extension workers. This structure cuts down misunderstandings, speeds up emergency response times, and protects mothers and babies from preventable harm during transitions of care.

Overview of the SBAR Communication Framework

SBAR stands for Situation, Background, Assessment, and Recommendation. Originally developed in the military and aviation sectors, this framework migrated into healthcare to give professionals a predictable, concise way to talk about patient status. It answers four basic questions: What is happening right now? How did we get here? What do I think is going on? What do I need you to do? By following these four steps in order, midwives deliver targeted updates that busy doctors or senior colleagues can grasp immediately.

Situation: What is Happening Right Now?

The Situation is the opening line of your communication. It should take no more than ten to fifteen seconds. State your name, your facility or ward, the patient's name, and the immediate problem prompting the call or handover.

  • Patient Identity: State full name and hospital number.
  • Current Issue: Briefly describe the exact alarm or symptom happening right now.
  • Urgency: Indicate how fast help is needed.

Keep this section brief so the listener instantly understands the core reason for your interaction.

Background: Clinical Context and Patient History

Once the immediate problem is clear, provide the Background. This gives the listener the necessary context without dragging out the story. Mention relevant details from the patient's booking and current pregnancy history.

  • Gravida and Para: State parity and estimated gestational age.
  • Previous Complications: Mention past surgeries, medical conditions, or previous difficult labors.
  • Current Admission Details: Note medications given, lab results, or treatments started since admission.

Avoid reciting the entire maternity case note from the first antenatal visit. Share only what connects directly to the current situation.

Assessment: Current Findings and Vital Signs

The Assessment is your professional evaluation of the patient's current state. Here, you put your clinical findings together. What are the current vital signs? Is the blood pressure dropping? Is the fetal heart rate abnormal? Do not just list raw numbers; interpret what they mean. If you are unsure of the exact diagnosis, state what you suspect is going wrong, such as signs of impending eclampsia, fetal distress, or hypovolemic shock. This demonstrates clinical reasoning and guides the senior colleague toward the right response.

Recommendation: What Action is Needed Next?

The final step is the Recommendation. Never leave the listener guessing what you want. Clearly state what action you need taken and by when.

  • Direct Request: Ask for a specific intervention, such as an immediate review, specific lab tests, or emergency medications.
  • Timeframe: Specify how urgently the action is required.
  • Confirmation: Repeat back any orders given to ensure absolute clarity.

Taking charge of this step prevents dropped balls in emergency care and ensures accountability.

Applying SBAR in Urgent Obstetric Handover Scenarios

Obstetric emergencies move at lightning speed. When a patient crashes from postpartum hemorrhage or eclamptic seizures, panic can disrupt teamwork. Using SBAR during emergency calls or shift changes keeps everyone calm and focused. For instance, when transferring a bleeding patient from a primary health clinic to a general hospital, the referring midwife can rapidly transmit the SBAR summary over the phone or in writing. The receiving tertiary team can then prep the operating theatre and blood bank before the ambulance even arrives.
