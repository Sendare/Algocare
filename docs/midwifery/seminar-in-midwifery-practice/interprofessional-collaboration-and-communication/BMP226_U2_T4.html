Role of Midwives in Emergency Response Teams

Why Midwives Belong on Emergency Response Teams

When disasters, conflicts, or sudden disease outbreaks strike, pregnant women and newborns face disproportionately high risks. Midwives possess specialized life-saving skills that extend far beyond routine antenatal care, making them indispensable members of any emergency health response team. During crises, transport systems collapse and hospitals become overwhelmed, meaning births happen in community shelters, transit camps, or even road transport. A midwife brings expert hands to manage postpartum haemorrhage, breech deliveries, and birth asphyxia right at the point of need.

Beyond technical delivery skills, midwives understand the community networks that keep maternal services functioning when formal infrastructure fails. They bridge the gap between institutional medical protocols and local cultural practices, ensuring emergency interventions are accepted by displaced populations. Deploying midwives into rapid response frameworks directly saves the lives of mothers and babies who would otherwise slip through the cracks of a generalized disaster response.

Core Responsibilities During Obstetric and Neonatal Crises

Inside chaotic field environments, the midwife must rapidly stabilize life-threatening complications. The responsibilities shift from preventative guidance to acute emergency intervention.

  • Postpartum Haemorrhage Management: Administering uterotonics, performing bimanual uterine compression, and monitoring blood loss without reliable electricity.
  • Neonatal Resuscitation: Clearing airways, providing bag-mask ventilation, and keeping the newborn warm using skin-to-skin contact or improvised thermal care.
  • Obstructed Labor Identification: Recognizing early signs of cephalopelvic disproportion and coordinating immediate emergency evacuation before uterine rupture occurs.
  • Emergency Childbirth Support: Conducting rapid, sterile-as-possible deliveries in non-clinical environments like tents or community halls.

Integrating Maternal Care into Broader Disaster Management

Disaster management plans often focus heavily on trauma surgery and infectious disease outbreaks while completely overlooking reproductive health needs. Midwives must actively advocate for maternal health supplies within the broader logistics chain of disaster response agencies. This integration means ensuring that clean delivery kits, reproductive health inter-agency emergency kits (RH Kits), and emergency contraception are included in the very first wave of relief supplies dispatched to a disaster zone.

Furthermore, setting up safe spaces for pregnant and lactating women within displacement camps prevents gender-based violence and provides a centralized hub where midwives can monitor high-risk pregnancies. Disaster coordinators must recognize that births do not stop because a crisis has occurred; therefore, maternal health must be treated as a primary emergency service rather than an afterthought.

Interprofessional Communication in High-Stressor Environments

Crises breed chaos, noise, and confusion, which easily destroy clear communication between medical professionals. When a pregnant woman arrives at a triage station with an eclamptic seizure, seconds matter. Midwives must utilize structured communication tools such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to pass critical patient data to general physicians, paramedics, and logistics officers without ambiguity.

Communication breakdowns often stem from professional hierarchy and fatigue. Midwives must assert their clinical expertise firmly yet respectfully when advocating for a laboring mother's transfer. Establishing closed-loop communication—where the receiver repeats back an instruction given by the midwife—ensures that vital tasks like drug dosages or emergency transport requests are executed correctly under pressure.

Resource Allocation and Triage in Low-Resource Settings

When emergencies create a massive influx of casualties alongside ongoing obstetric emergencies, resources become severely restricted. Midwives face the agonizing reality of triage—deciding whose care must be prioritized when there are not enough blood bags, medications, or transport vehicles for everyone.

Effective triage in maternal health requires swift identification of conditions carrying immediate mortality risks, such as severe pre-eclampsia, ruptured uterus, or massive hemorrhage, placing them at the highest priority level. Midwives must remain calm, objective, and transparent while rationing supplies, ensuring that limited resources go to patients with a realistic chance of survival through prompt intervention. This process requires continuous reassessment, as a stable pregnant patient can deteriorate into a critical emergency within minutes.

Psychological First Aid for Affected Mothers and Families

The trauma of surviving a disaster while carrying a pregnancy or caring for a newborn leaves deep psychological scars. Midwives are often the primary healthcare providers available to offer Psychological First Aid (PFA). PFA is not formal psychotherapy; rather, it involves humane, supportive, and practical assistance that focuses on listening without forcing people to talk, assessing urgent needs, and protecting survivors from further harm.

For a mother who has lost her home, her belongings, or even her partner during a crisis, high anxiety can disrupt milk production and complicate the postpartum recovery process. Midwives provide a calming presence, validate the mother's emotional pain, and connect grieving families with community support networks. By addressing the psychological well-being of the mother, the midwife protects the delicate emotional bond between mother and newborn, fostering resilience in the face of overwhelming adversity.
