Leadership, Governance, and Professional Advocacy in Maternity Services

Understanding Leadership, Governance, and Professional Advocacy in Maternity Services

Maternity care in Nigeria demands more than clinical competence. Leadership in this context means guiding teams through high-pressure deliveries, resource shortages, and emergency obstetric scenarios with a steady hand. Midwives are often the first line of leadership in primary health centers where doctors may not be present.

Governance refers to the systems, policies, and accountability structures that ensure safe care delivery. Without proper governance, even well-trained clinicians struggle against systemic failures. Professional Advocacy is the active defense of the rights of mothers and newborns, ensuring they receive respectful, evidence-based care free from coercion or neglect.

Consider a busy maternity ward in a rural general hospital. A midwife coordinating shift handovers, auditing maternal mortality records, and standing up against harmful traditional practices is simultaneously practicing leadership, governance, and advocacy.

The Midwife as a Leader: Bridging Clinical Practice and Institutional Management

Midwives often find themselves caught between the harsh realities of the labor ward and the administrative demands of hospital management. Bridging this gap requires translating clinical needs into language that hospital administrators understand. When requesting new vacuum extraction kits or continuous oxygen supply, a clinical leader must present data on maternal outcomes rather than mere complaints.

Effective mid-level leadership involves:

  • Mentorship: Guiding junior student midwives through complex perineal repairs and emotional labor.
  • Resource Allocation: Prioritizing scarce consumables like oxytocin and magnesium sulfate during stockouts.
  • Conflict Resolution: Mediating tensions between overworked staff and distressed relatives.

Leadership is not about holding a title; it is about influence and creating a culture where safety supersedes hierarchy.

Clinical Governance Frameworks in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Clinical governance provides the structural backbone for quality assurance in Nigerian health institutions. It encompasses continuous audit of clinical outcomes, evidence-based protocols, risk management, and lifelong professional learning.

In maternity units, clinical governance manifests through maternal and perinatal death surveillance and response (MPDSR) meetings. These sessions review adverse outcomes not to apportion blame, but to identify systemic gaps—such as delayed blood transfusion or lack of functional theater space. Implementing national standard treatment guidelines ensures that a woman receives the same high standard of care whether she delivers in a tertiary teaching hospital in Lagos or a comprehensive health center in Zamfara.

Risk Management, Patient Safety, and Quality Improvement in Labor Wards

Labor wards are high-risk, fast-paced environments where the status of two patients can change within minutes. Risk management involves identifying hazards—such as misplaced emergency drugs, broken cardiotocography machines, or fatigued staff—before they cause harm.

Communication breakdowns during shift changes are a leading cause of adverse obstetric events. Utilizing structured handover tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) drastically minimizes missed details during patient transfers.

Professional Advocacy: Championing the Rights of Mothers and Newborns

Midwives are the ultimate guardians of physiological birth and maternal dignity. Professional advocacy requires speaking up against disrespectful maternity care, unnecessary medical interventions, and systemic barriers that deny vulnerable women access to essential services.

Advocacy takes many forms:

  • Individual Level: Ensuring a laboring woman gives informed consent before any episiotomy or vaginal examination is performed.
  • Community Level: Educating traditional rulers and community leaders against harmful practices such as female genital mutilation or abdominal massage during pregnancy.
  • Policy Level: Engaging with health ministries to ensure free maternal and child health policies are adequately funded and implemented.

When midwives remain silent in the face of abuse or neglect, they become passive participants in poor health outcomes.

Navigating Policy, Health Reform, and Midwifery Legislation in Nigeria

The practice of midwifery in Nigeria is governed by statutory laws, primarily the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) Act. Understanding these legal boundaries protects both the practitioner and the public. Midwives must remain aware of their scope of practice, mandatory continuing professional development (MCPD) requirements, and license renewal processes.

Health reforms, such as the Basic Health Care Provision Fund (BHCPF), aim to revitalize primary healthcare and reduce out-of-pocket expenses for pregnant women. However, policy on paper often differs from reality on the ward. Midwives must understand these health policies so they can guide patients to access entitled subsidies, free commodities, and emergency transport schemes without exploitation.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration and Resolving Ethical Dilemmas in Maternity Care

Obstetric emergencies do not respect professional boundaries. Saving a mother with severe pre-eclampsia or postpartum hemorrhage requires seamless teamwork between midwives, obstetricians, anaesthetists, pharmacists, and laboratory scientists. Effective multidisciplinary collaboration demands mutual respect, clear role definition, and open communication channels.

Ethical dilemmas frequently arise in the labor ward. Consider a case where a patient refuses a life-saving blood transfusion due to religious convictions, or a minor presents in obstructed labor whose parents refuse consent for emergency cesarean section. Midwives must navigate these conflicts by balancing the ethical principles of autonomy, beneficence, and non-maleficence while remaining compliant with legal precedents and institutional policies.
