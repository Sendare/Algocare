Midwife-Led Continuity Models of Care

Understanding Midwife-Led Continuity Models of Care

A midwife-led continuity model is an arrangement where a known midwife, or a small group of known midwives, provides care to a woman throughout her pregnancy, labor, birth, and the postnatal period. Unlike fragmented care where a pregnant woman sees multiple different doctors and nurses at every clinic visit, continuity models focus on building a trusting relationship with one primary healthcare provider.

In these models, the midwife acts as the lead professional responsible for assessing needs, coordinating specialized referrals if complications arise, and providing evidence-based health education. This approach shifts maternity services from a purely medicalized view of birth toward recognizing pregnancy as a normal physiological life event. By fostering familiarity and trust, midwives can better understand individual social contexts, cultural preferences, and personal anxieties, tailoring their clinical support accordingly.

Core Principles and Philosophy of Continuous Midwifery Support

The philosophy of continuous midwifery support rests on the belief in the normalcy of childbearing and the inherent strength of women's bodies. Key tenets include:

  • Woman-centred care: Placing the woman's personal choices, values, and autonomy at the center of all clinical decisions.
  • Partnership: Working alongside the woman and her family rather than acting as an authoritarian director of care.
  • Empowerment: Providing clear, evidence-based information so women can actively participate in managing their own health.
  • Safeguarding normality: Protecting the physiological process of birth by minimizing unnecessary medical interventions while remaining vigilant for complications.

Different Structures of Midwife-Led Care Services

Midwife-led care can be organized in several operational structures depending on the health system layout:

  • Caseload midwifery: A primary midwife carries a defined caseload of women, providing their antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care, backed by a small backup partner.
  • Team midwifery: A group of 4 to 8 midwives shares the care of a larger group of women, ensuring that the woman usually meets someone she knows when she arrives at the facility.
  • Birth centre models: Standalone or alongside units managed primarily by midwives for women with low-risk pregnancies, emphasizing a homelike environment.

Clinical Evidence: Maternal and Neonatal Outcomes

Robust international research, including Cochrane systematic reviews, consistently demonstrates that midwife-led continuity models offer significant clinical advantages for women with low-risk pregnancies. Mothers who experience continuous support are less likely to undergo regional analgesia (such as epidurals), fewer instrumental vaginal births, and lower rates of episiotomy.

Importantly, these models show a reduction in fetal loss before 24 weeks and fewer preterm births, with no increase in adverse outcomes. Women are also more likely to report positive experiences of care and feel satisfied with their birth preparation. However, these benefits rely heavily on having a well-functioning referral pathway when complications do develop, ensuring high-risk cases receive timely obstetric intervention.

Implementing Continuity Models in the Nigerian Healthcare System

Adapting midwife-led continuity models within Nigeria requires navigating a multi-tiered healthcare structure spanning primary health care (PHC) centres, general hospitals, and tertiary teaching centers. Primary health care centres, which form the bedrock of grassroots maternity service delivery in Nigeria, are ideally positioned to host community-based midwife continuity schemes.

Successful implementation demands task-shifting clarity, supportive supervision, and integration with local community leaders. Midwives working in rural or urban primary clinics must collaborate closely with Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs) to encourage hospital-based skilled attendance. By engaging community structures, midwives can build trust, address cultural barriers, and champion continuous care within existing resource limits.

Barriers, Challenges, and Solutions in Resource-Constrained Settings

Implementing continuity of care in low-resource environments like many parts of Nigeria faces distinct hurdles:

  • Staff shortages: Acute shortages of qualified midwives mean single practitioners often carry unmanageable workloads, leading to severe burnout.
  • Infrastructure gaps: Frequent power outages, lack of clean running water, and poor road networks delay emergency transport and hinder clinical care.
  • Financial constraints: Out-of-pocket spending for maternity supplies can prevent women from accessing continuous care.

Solutions include introducing flexible shift-sharing rosters to protect midwives from exhaustion, utilizing task-shifting frameworks where appropriate, and leveraging mobile health (mHealth) technology for antenatal appointment reminders and follow-up communication.

The Evolving Role and Autonomy of the Midwife

Professional autonomy is vital for the success of midwife-led models. An autonomous midwife possesses the legal, professional, and clinical authority to make independent decisions regarding screening, diagnostics, normal birth management, and postpartum follow-up.

In many Nigerian health settings, the scope of midwifery practice has historically been restricted by hierarchical medical structures that treat midwives merely as assistants to doctors. Expanding midwifery autonomy requires updating nursing and midwifery council guidelines, strengthening pre-service clinical training, and building interprofessional respect between midwives and obstetricians. When midwives are empowered to practice to their full professional scope, maternal health services become more efficient, responsive, and humane.
