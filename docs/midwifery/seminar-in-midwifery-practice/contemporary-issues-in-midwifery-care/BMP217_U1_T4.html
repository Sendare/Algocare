Addressing Maternal Mortality and Morbidity Metrics in Developing Nations

Global Landscape and Core Metrics of Maternal Health

Maternal health disparities remain one of the most striking divides in global public health. While women in wealthy regions face very low risks during childbirth, mothers in developing nations—particularly in sub-Saharan Africa—carry the heaviest burden of preventable death and disability. To understand and track this crisis, public health experts rely on specific standardized numbers:

  • Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR): The number of maternal deaths during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days of termination per 100,000 live births. This reflects the overall risk a woman faces each time she becomes pregnant.
  • Maternal Mortality Rate: The number of maternal deaths in a given population divided by the total number of women of reproductive age (15 to 49 years). This captures both the risk of pregnancy and how often women become pregnant.
  • Severe Acute Maternal Morbidity (SAMM): Often called near-miss events, these are instances where a woman nearly dies but survives a severe complication during pregnancy, childbirth, or postpartum. Studying near-miss cases provides vital clues because survivors share many clinical characteristics with women who unfortunately pass away.

The Three Delays Model in Obstetric Emergencies

When a pregnant woman experiences a life-threatening complication like postpartum hemorrhage or eclampsia, tragedy often results from a chain of delays. Thaddeus and Maine conceptualized the Three Delays Model to explain why mothers die despite medical knowledge existing to save them:

  1. First Delay: Deciding to seek care. This is influenced by the family's socioeconomic status, cultural beliefs about pregnancy complications, the status of women in the household, and a failure to recognize danger signs early.
  2. Second Delay: Reaching a care facility. Poor road infrastructure, geographical isolation, lack of reliable transport, and high costs of travel prevent women from getting to a clinic or hospital in time.
  3. Third Delay: Receiving adequate treatment. Once the patient arrives at a health facility, shortages of trained staff, lack of essential drugs like oxytocin or magnesium sulfate, broken equipment, and delayed emergency surgeries seal a fatal outcome.

Midwifery-Led Interventions and Community-Based Care

Strengthening primary healthcare through community-based midwifery is one of the most effective strategies to lower maternal mortality in resource-constrained settings. Midwives based directly in rural communities provide continuous, culturally grounded care that bridges the gap between traditional practices and modern medicine.

Community midwives conduct regular antenatal visits, screen for high-risk conditions such as gestational hypertension and severe anemia, and distribute preventive commodities like iron supplements and insecticide-treated nets. Beyond clinical duties, they build trust with local leaders and traditional birth attendants. This collaboration encourages early birth preparedness, prompt identification of danger signs, and timely referrals before complications overwhelm home-based options.

Strengthening Health Systems and Emergency Obstetric Care

Community education and primary prevention alone cannot stop all maternal deaths because unpredictable emergencies like uterine rupture or eclamptic fits will always occur. Addressing these requires a robust health system capable of delivering Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmOC).

Health facilities are categorized into Basic EmOC (BEmOC) and Comprehensive EmOC (CEmOC) centers based on the life-saving signals functions they perform:

  • BEmOC Facilities: Handle administration of parenteral antibiotics, uterotonic drugs, and anticonvulsants; manual removal of the placenta; removal of retained products; and assisted vaginal delivery.
  • CEmOC Facilities: Provide all BEmOC functions plus the vital additions of blood transfusion services and caesarean section surgery.

For an EmOC network to function effectively, facilities must maintain a 24-hour roster of skilled staff, a reliable supply chain for essential medicines, and functioning equipment without power interruptions.

Data Quality, Surveillance, and the Path Forward

Accurate measurement is the foundation of effective policy, yet many developing nations struggle with weak vital registration systems. Millions of births and deaths occur outside formal health facilities, resulting in underreporting and misclassification of maternal deaths. When governments do not know the exact scale or true causes of mortality, resource allocation remains a guessing game.

To bridge this gap, modern public health uses Maternal Death Surveillance and Response (MDSR) systems. MDSR makes maternal death a nationally notifiable event, requiring local review committees to investigate every single case. By identifying the exact breakdown—whether clinical errors, transport failures, or drug stockouts—health teams can implement corrective local actions rather than placing blame, steadily improving the quality of maternal care over time.
