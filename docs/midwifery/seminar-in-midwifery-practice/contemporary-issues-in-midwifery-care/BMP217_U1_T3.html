Respectful Maternity Care (RMC) and Universal Rights of Childbearing Women

Introduction to Respectful Maternity Care (RMC)

Respectful Maternity Care, commonly known as RMC, is a fundamental approach that hinges on the principle that every childbearing woman has the absolute right to the highest attainable standard of health, which includes the right to a dignified and respectful healthcare experience. In many Nigerian health facilities, maternity care has historically been viewed strictly through a clinical lens, focusing only on preventing maternal mortality while neglecting the emotional and psychological well-being of the woman. RMC bridges this gap by ensuring that care is organized around the needs, values, and preferences of the mother.

At its core, RMC transforms the maternity ward from a place of fear and clinical coldness into a safe space where women feel supported. It acknowledges that birth is not merely a medical event requiring intervention, but a profound life transition. When midwives practice RMC, they validate the dignity of the woman, recognizing her autonomy to make decisions concerning her own body and baby. This approach actively dismantles paternalistic attitudes in healthcare settings, fostering a collaborative partnership between the pregnant woman and her care team.

The Universal Rights of Childbearing Women

The Universal Rights of Childbearing Women charter outlines specific human rights that apply to every woman during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. These rights are derived from international human rights standards and adapted to the realities of maternity wards. Midwives must understand that these are not mere professional privileges granted by a benevolent hospital, but inherent human rights.

  • Freedom from harm and ill-treatment: Protection against physical, verbal, and psychological abuse during care.
  • Right to information and informed consent: Receiving clear explanations about procedures, risks, and alternatives before anything is done.
  • Right to privacy and confidentiality: Maintaining the personal dignity of the woman regarding her health status and body.
  • Right to dignity and respect: Being treated with kindness, empathy, and recognition of personal worth.
  • Right to equality and freedom from discrimination: Receiving care regardless of tribe, religion, financial status, or HIV status.
  • Right to timely healthcare: Accessing life-saving interventions without delay or extortion.

Understanding Obstetric Violence and Disrespect in Facility Settings

Obstetric violence remains a silent crisis in many maternity units, manifesting in ways that are often normalized by healthcare workers. It encompasses physical abuse, profound disrespect, humiliation, coercion, and the withholding of necessary treatments. When a midwife shouts at a laboring woman for crying out in pain, or performs an episiotomy without anesthesia or consent, that constitutes a severe violation of professional ethics and human rights.

Institutional routines often breed structural disrespect. For instance, mandatory restriction of movement, routine use of fundal pressure, and unnecessary enemas without clinical indication strip women of bodily integrity. Midwives must critically examine unit practices to identify where clinical efficiency crosses the line into abuse. Recognizing that disrespect is systemic helps practitioners move away from blaming individual overworked staff toward transforming the entire institutional culture.

Core Pillars of Human Rights in Midwifery Practice

Midwifery practice is anchored on several human rights pillars that guide daily clinical decisions. Foremost among these is autonomy, which requires midwives to respect that the pregnant woman is the primary decision-maker regarding her health. Informed consent is the practical tool of autonomy; it is an ongoing dialogue, not a static form signed under duress in the admission office.

Another vital pillar is beneficence paired with non-maleficence, ensuring that care truly benefits the woman while doing no avoidable harm through rough handling or harsh words. Equity and justice form the final pillar, demanding that poor, rural, or marginalized women receive the exact same high standard of polite, attentive care as wealthy or well-connected patients.

The Impact of Disrespectful Care on Maternal and Neonatal Outcomes

The consequences of disrespectful maternity care extend far beyond bruised feelings or temporary emotional distress; they directly influence clinical outcomes and maternal mortality statistics. When women experience abuse, humiliation, or neglect in health facilities, deep-seated fear and distrust are created within the community. Consequently, many pregnant women choose to stay away from hospitals, opting instead for unregulated traditional birth homes or unattended home births.

When these women finally arrive at the hospital, it is often too late, presenting with severe, life-threatening complications like ruptured uterus, severe postpartum hemorrhage, or eclampsia. Furthermore, high-stress environments and harsh treatment during labor elevate maternal cortisol levels, which can negatively affect uterine contractions and fetal oxygenation. Cultivating a respectful environment is therefore a vital, life-saving clinical intervention, not just a matter of good manners.

Translating RMC Principles into Daily Clinical Routines

Translating RMC from a theoretical concept into everyday ward reality requires intentional, practical habits from every midwife. It begins with simple communication: introducing yourself to the laboring woman, calling her by her preferred name, and explaining every single examination before touching her body. Midwives should actively encourage birth companionship, allowing a chosen family member or trusted friend to stay with the woman throughout labor for continuous emotional and physical support.

Labor positions should also reflect RMC principles. Instead of forcing women to lie flat on their backs—a position that increases pain and reduces placental blood flow—midwives should support upright, mobile postures that respect the woman's comfort. Pain relief must be offered and discussed openly, validating that labor pain is real and deserves compassionate management.

Advocacy and Systemic Change for Sustainable Maternity Care

Individual commitment by compassionate midwives is vital, but true, sustainable transformation requires systemic institutional change. Hospitals often perpetuate disrespect because staff are severely overworked, underpaid, and burnt out, operating in environments lacking basic supplies like gloves, clean water, and functional equipment. Advocacy means speaking up not only against individual bad behavior among colleagues, but also demanding better working conditions from hospital management and health ministries.

Midwives can lead quality improvement committees, mentor junior staff, and establish clear, confidential feedback mechanisms where women can report mistreatment without fear of victimization. True advocacy bridges the gap between policy documents and ward realities, ensuring that human rights are protected for every mother, every single day.
