Child Abuse and Neglect

Introduction to Child Rights and Vulnerability in Nigeria

Child protection in Nigeria operates under a framework designed to safeguard minors from harm, exploitation, and neglect. The Child Rights Act, enacted to domesticate the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, outlines the fundamental entitlements of every Nigerian child to survival, development, protection, and participation. Despite these legal provisions, children remain vulnerable due to systemic poverty, cultural practices, and economic pressures that often normalize child labor or informal fostering arrangements. Midwives and healthcare providers occupy a frontline position within this environment. Because maternity and pediatric clinics form points of regular contact with families, healthcare workers often observe the early domestic strains that threaten child welfare long before catastrophic harm occurs.

Recognizing Physical Abuse in Pediatric Patients

Physical abuse involves non-accidental trauma inflicted upon a child by a caregiver or guardian. When assessing pediatric patients, midwives must distinguish ordinary accidental childhood injuries from suspicious trauma patterns. Accidental bruises typically occur over bony prominences like shins, elbows, and foreheads during active play. Suspicious injuries, conversely, often appear on protected body parts such as the cheeks, ears, neck, upper thighs, and buttocks.

  • Patterned Bruises: Marks that bear the distinct shape of an implement, such as a belt buckle, cord, or switch.
  • Burns: Immersion burns with clear lines of demarcation, or cigarette burns characterized by uniform circular lesions.
  • Fractures: Multiple bone breaks at various stages of healing, particularly spiral fractures in non-ambulatory infants.

Injuries attributed to implausible mechanisms—such as an infant rolling off a high bed and sustaining complex bilateral skull fractures—warrant immediate clinical escalation.

Identifying Emotional and Psychological Abuse

Emotional and psychological abuse involves sustained acts by a caregiver that severely damage a child's sense of self-worth, emotional health, and social development. Unlike physical injuries, psychological maltreatment leaves no visible scars, making detection dependent on behavioral observation. Manifestations include constant belittling, terrorizing, exploiting, or withholding basic emotional responsiveness and affection.

Midwives should note behavioral red flags during pediatric consultations. A toddler or older child who displays extreme withdrawal, hypervigilance, developmental regression, or indiscriminate attachment to strangers may be experiencing chronic emotional deprivation at home. Parents who chronically demean, ignore, or display hostile detachment toward their infant during clinical interactions also provide vital behavioral clues.

Understanding Sexual Abuse and Exploitation

Child sexual abuse involves the engagement of a dependent, developmentally immature child in sexual activities they cannot fully comprehend, are unprepared for, and to which they cannot give informed consent. Perpetrators are frequently individuals known and trusted by the child and family. Clinical presentation can be direct or indirect.

Many victims show no physical signs during external examination. However, physical indicators that warrant urgent medical review include unexplained genital or anal bleeding, recurrent urinary tract infections, sexually transmitted infections in prepubescent children, and difficulty walking or sitting. Behavioral changes such as age-inappropriate sexualized language, extreme fear of specific individuals, or sudden onset sleep disorders also demand professional attention.

Child Neglect and Environmental Deprivation

Neglect is the failure of a parent or primary caregiver to provide for the basic developmental needs of a child, including food, clothing, shelter, medical care, and adequate supervision. It represents the most frequently reported form of child maltreatment.

  • Physical Neglect: Chronic failure to provide adequate nutrition, resulting in severe failure to thrive, persistent inappropriate clothing for weather conditions, and untreated medical or dental conditions.
  • Educational Neglect: Failure to enroll a child of school age in basic education or permitting chronic, unexcused truancy.
  • Emotional Neglect: Failure to provide psychological support, attention, and a nurturing home environment.
  • Supervisory Neglect: Leaving young, vulnerable children alone or in the care of immature siblings for extended periods, exposing them to avoidable hazards.

The Role of the Midwife in Mandatory Reporting and Protection

Midwives hold both an ethical and professional duty to protect vulnerable neonates, infants, and children. When clinical findings point toward maltreatment, documentation must be meticulous, objective, and non-judgmental. Midwives should record exact statements in the parents' or child's own words, alongside precise anatomical descriptions and measurements of any injuries.

In many jurisdictions and institutional settings, healthcare workers are bound by mandatory reporting guidelines. Suspected abuse must be escalated to hospital social work departments, child protection agencies, or law enforcement officers without delay. Midwives must never attempt to investigate the allegations themselves or confront abusive caregivers in an aggressive manner, as this can endanger both the child and the clinician. The primary clinical objective remains ensuring immediate patient safety and securing specialized multidisciplinary intervention.
