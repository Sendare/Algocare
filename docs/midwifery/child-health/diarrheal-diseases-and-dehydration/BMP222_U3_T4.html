IV Rehydration Protocols and Nursing Care of Dehydrated Child

Pathophysiology and Degree Assessment of Pediatric Dehydration

Dehydration in infants and children occurs rapidly due to a high body surface area, higher basal metabolic rate, and a greater percentage of total body water compared to adults. When fluid losses from vomiting, diarrhea, or fever exceed intake, the child shifts from euhydration into deficit states. Midwives and nurses must accurately classify the degree of dehydration to guide immediate fluid resuscitation.

  • Mild Dehydration: Estimated fluid deficit of 3 to 5% in infants (or 3% in older children). Clinical signs are subtle: normal vital signs, slightly dry mucous membranes, normal skin turgor, and adequate urine output.
  • Moderate Dehydration: Estimated fluid deficit of 6 to 9% in infants (or 5 to 6% in older children). Characterized by mild tachycardia, sunken anterior fontanelle, reduced skin turgor, dry mucous membranes, deep sighing respirations, and oliguria.
  • Severe Dehydration: Estimated fluid deficit of 10% or more in infants (or 9% in older children). Manifests as signs of impending shock: severe tachycardia, weak or thready pulses, cool and mottled skin, sunken eyes, absent tears, extreme lethargy or unconsciousness, and anuria.

A common mistake in clinical practice is relying on a single sign, such as skin turgor alone, which can be misleading in malnourished children. Always assess the overall clinical picture, including capillary refill time and mental status.

Emergency IV Rehydration Protocols (Plan C)

When a child presents with severe dehydration or is unable to tolerate oral rehydration therapy, emergency intravenous access must be secured immediately. This follows the WHO Plan C protocol for severe dehydration.

  1. Initial Fluid Resuscitation: Infuse 20 mL/kg of an isotonic crystalloid solution, such as Normal Saline or Ringer's Lactate, rapidly over 30 minutes.
  2. Reassessment: Check vital signs, peripheral perfusion, and mental status after the first bolus. If pulses remain weak and perfusion is poor, repeat the 20 mL/kg bolus.
  3. Switch to Maintenance and Deficit Phase: Once the child's radial pulses normalize, consciousness returns, and perfusion improves, transition to calculated maintenance plus deficit replacement fluids.
  4. Intraosseous Access: If peripheral veins cannot be cannulated within 90 seconds in an emergency shock scenario, insert an intraosseous (IO) needle without delay.

Do not use hypotonic fluids like 0.18% Saline with 5% Dextrose for rapid emergency boluses, as they cause a rapid drop in serum osmolality and risk cerebral edema.

Selecting IV Fluids and Calculating Rates

Selecting the correct intravenous fluid requires understanding the electrolyte composition of the solution relative to the patient's serum status. Isotonic solutions like Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl) or Ringer's Lactate match extracellular fluid osmolality and are reserved for volume expansion.

For ongoing maintenance and deficit repair after shock is corrected, solutions containing dextrose and sodium chloride (such as 0.45% Saline with 5% Dextrose) are commonly used to prevent hypoglycemia and provide baseline electrolytes.

To calculate maintenance fluid rates, midwives use the standard Holliday-Segar method (the 4-2-1 rule for hourly rates):

  • First 10 kg of body weight: 4 mL/kg/hour
  • Next 10 kg of body weight (11-20 kg): 2 mL/kg/hour
  • Each kg above 20 kg: 1 mL/kg/hour

Total daily fluid requirements are calculated by adding the remaining deficit volume (divided over 24 to 48 hours) to the calculated maintenance requirement.

Comprehensive Nursing Care of the Dehydrated Child

Nursing management bridges medical resuscitation with comfort, safety, and family-centered support.

  • Accurate Fluid Balance Tracking: Weigh all wet diapers on a gram scale (1 gram equals approximately 1 mL of urine) to measure exact output. Document every drop of IV fluid infused and all oral intake.
  • IV Site Monitoring: Check the cannula insertion site every hour. Infiltration of IV fluids into subcutaneous tissues causes pain, swelling, and potential tissue necrosis, especially if irritating drugs or hypertonic solutions leak.
  • Skin and Mucoral Care: Apply barrier creams to the buttocks to protect against excoriation from frequent watery stools. Lubricate dry, cracked lips with sterile water or petroleum jelly.
  • Thermal Regulation: Dehydrated children often experience temperature instability. Monitor core body temperature frequently and use tepid sponging or appropriate clothing adjustments as needed.

Monitoring Electrolyte Imbalances and Complications

Rapid rehydration and ongoing diarrheal losses put the child at risk for severe electrolyte disturbances. Midwives must remain vigilant for signs of these complications.

  • Hypokalemia: Low serum potassium results from ongoing gastrointestinal losses and renal excretion. Signs include muscle weakness, abdominal distension, decreased bowel sounds, and cardiac arrhythmias (such as flattened T waves).
  • Hyponatremia and Hypernatremia: Sodium imbalances affect neurological function. Rapid correction of chronic hypernatremia can cause water to shift into brain cells, leading to cerebral edema and seizures.
  • Metabolic Acidosis: Bicarbonate loss in diarrheal stool causes deep, rapid breathing (Kussmaul breathing) as the body attempts to blow off carbon dioxide to compensate.

Always check laboratory electrolyte panels when available, and report sudden changes in the child's level of consciousness immediately.

Transition to Oral Rehydration and Discharge Planning

As soon as the child's clinical condition stabilizes, vital signs normalize, and vomiting subsides, intravenous fluids should be tapered and replaced by oral rehydration therapy (ORT). Co-administration of oral zinc supplements for 10 to 14 days reduces the duration and severity of the diarrheal episode and prevents recurrence.

Before discharge, midwives must provide comprehensive education to the mother or caregiver:

  • Preparation of ORS: Teach how to mix one packet of ORS in the exact specified volume of clean, safe water.
  • Continued Feeding: Emphasize that breastfeeding or regular food intake should continue without interruption during and after the illness.
  • Danger Signs: Clearly outline when to return to the health facility immediately, such as blood in the stool, inability to drink, worsening fever, sunken eyes, or lethargy.
