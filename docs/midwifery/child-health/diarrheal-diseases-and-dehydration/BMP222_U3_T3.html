Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) and Zinc Supplementation

Understanding Childhood Diarrhea and Dehydration in Nigerian Contexts

Childhood diarrhea remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among under-five children across Nigeria. In local communities, frequent watery stools lead to rapid depletion of water and essential electrolytes, primarily sodium and potassium. When fluid losses outpace intake, the child slips into dehydration. Midwives working in primary health care centers frequently encounter mothers arriving late because symptoms were initially managed with local concoctions or herbs rather than active fluid replacement. Recognizing the early physiological shifts prevents mild loose stools from escalating into severe, life-threatening hypovolemic shock.

Dehydration is clinically categorized into distinct plans based on the child's physical presentation:

  • No Dehydration: The child is alert, eyes are normal, skin pinch goes back immediately, and drinks eagerly.
  • Some Dehydration: The child is restless or irritable, eyes are sunken, skin pinch goes back slowly (between two seconds), and drinks eagerly with thirst.
  • Severe Dehydration: The child is lethargic or unconscious, eyes are very sunken and dry, skin pinch goes back very slowly (longer than two seconds), and the child drinks poorly or is unable to drink.

Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)

Oral Rehydration Therapy is a simple, highly effective medical intervention designed to replace lost fluids and correct electrolyte imbalances using a precise drinkable solution. The fundamental science behind Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) relies on the sodium-glucose cotransporter mechanism in the small intestine. Glucose molecules actively pull sodium ions—and consequently water—across the mucosal cell membrane into the bloodstream, even when the gut is actively shedding water due to bacterial or viral infections.

Traditional high-osmolality formulas historically worsened stool output by drawing extra water into the intestinal lumen through osmotic pressure. Modern global health standards mandate the use of low-osmolality ORS, which contains reduced concentrations of glucose and salt. This optimized formulation decreases the need for intravenous fluids, shortens the duration of the diarrheal episode, and drastically cuts down hospital admission rates. Midwives must champion this therapy as the gold standard of care during outbreaks in rural primary health clinics.

Zinc Supplementation

Zinc is an indispensable micronutrient that plays a critical role in cellular repair, immune function, and intestinal mucosal regeneration. Clinical trials show that administering daily zinc supplements during acute childhood diarrhea significantly reduces the duration and severity of the current episode while protecting the child against future bouts for up to three months. Zinc restores the integrity of the intestinal lining, boosts brush-border enzyme activity, and enhances the antibody response of the mucosal immune system.

For infants under six months of age, the standard therapeutic dose is 10 mg per day, while children aged six months to five years require 20 mg per day for a total duration of 10 to 14 consecutive days. Midwives must emphasize finishing the entire multi-day course even after diarrhea stops, ensuring full restoration of the child's mucosal gut architecture and sustained immune protection.

Step-by-Step Preparation of Low-Osmolality ORS

Proper preparation of ORS is just as important as prescribing it. In many Nigerian homes, measuring tools are scarce, so midwives must teach mothers exact, foolproof mixing techniques using safe local containers. A single standard sachet of low-osmolality ORS is formulated to be dissolved in exactly one litre of clean, safe drinking water.

  1. Hand Hygiene: Wash hands thoroughly with soap and clean running water before touching any mixing utensils.
  2. Water Selection: Measure one clean litre of water into a wide-mouthed jug or bottle. If the safety of the tap or well water is doubtful, it must be boiled first and allowed to cool.
  3. Content Addition: Empty the entire contents of one ORS sachet completely into the measured water.
  4. Mixing: Stir the mixture thoroughly with a clean spoon until all the powder is completely dissolved and no granules remain visible.
  5. Storage and Safety: Cover the container and keep it away from flies. Any unused solution must be discarded after 24 hours to prevent bacterial contamination.

Administering ORT and Zinc in Clinical and Community Settings

Administering ORT requires patience, especially when treating an irritable infant who is vomiting. Midwives should teach mothers to give small, frequent sips using a clean cup or spoon rather than forcing a large volume all at once, which often triggers reflex vomiting. If the child vomits, the caregiver should wait about ten minutes and then resume feeding at a slower pace.

In primary health clinics, the first dose of ORS and zinc is often demonstrated right at the consultation desk. The midwife observes the mother feed the child to ensure she grasps the technique. For infants exclusively breastfeeding, breast milk should continue on demand between ORS doses. Zinc dispersible tablets dissolve easily in a small spoonful of breast milk, water, or expressed milk, making administration straightforward for young babies.

Counseling Mothers and Caregivers on Continued Feeding

A common cultural misconception during childhood diarrhea is that the gut needs to "rest," leading many mothers to starve the child or dilute food severely. Midwives must actively dispel this myth. Restricting food worsens malnutrition, damages the intestinal mucosa further, and prolongs recovery.

Caregivers should be counseled to continue frequent breastfeeding on demand for infants and young children. For older children who have started family foods, offer soft, nutrient-dense meals such as well-mashed maize pap enriched with groundnut paste, beans, or mashed yam. Encourage small, frequent meals at least six times a day. Additionally, offering an extra meal after the diarrheal episode resolves helps the child catch up on lost weight and nutritional stores.

Recognizing Danger Signs and When to Refer

While most cases of mild to moderate dehydration resolve successfully at home or in primary health clinics with ORT and zinc, midwives must teach caregivers how to spot warning signs that demand urgent hospital transfer. A delay in recognizing these clinical red flags can prove fatal.

Caregivers must bring the child back immediately if they notice any of the following danger signs:

  • Inability to Drink or Breastfeed: The child is too weak to suck or swallow anything.
  • Worsening Lethargy or Unconsciousness: The child does not wake up when stimulated or is abnormally limp.
  • Persistent Vomiting: Everything the child swallows comes right back up.
  • Blood in Stool: Dysentery indicates invasive bacterial infection requiring antibiotics alongside ORS.
  • Fever with Stiffness: High body temperature accompanied by abnormal neck stiffness or convulsions.
