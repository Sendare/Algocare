Tetanus

Introduction to Tetanus in Pediatric Practice

Tetanus is a severe, often fatal infectious disease caused by the potent neurotoxin produced by the bacterium Clostridium tetani. In the context of Nigerian pediatric and midwifery practice, healthcare providers primarily encounter two forms: neonatal tetanus, which affects newborns delivered in unhygienic conditions, and tetanus in older infants and children due to contaminated skin wounds. The spores of *Clostridium tetani* thrive in soil, dust, and animal manure, entering the body through breaks in the skin. Neonatal tetanus typically arises from unsterile cutting of the umbilical cord or applying contaminated substances to the stump. Despite being entirely vaccine-preventable, it remains a major public health concern in resource-limited settings where routine immunization coverage and clean delivery practices are sub-optimal.

Pathophysiology and Toxin Action

Once *Clostridium tetani* spores enter an anaerobic wound environment, they germinate and produce two toxins: tetanolysin and tetanospasmin. Tetanospasmin is the primary neurotoxin responsible for the clinical syndrome. After production, the toxin travels retrograde along the axons of peripheral motor nerves toward the central nervous system. Once inside the spinal cord and brainstem, it binds irreversibly to nerve synapses and blocks the release of inhibitory neurotransmitters, specifically GABA and glycine. Without these inhibitory signals, motor neurons fire continuously and uncontrollably. This widespread disinhibition leads to the classic clinical picture of intense, painful muscle spasms and rigidity.

Clinical Manifestations

The clinical presentation of tetanus is distinct and easily recognized once fully developed. Symptoms generally appear after an incubation period ranging from 3 to 21 days.

  • Trismus (Lockjaw): Difficulty opening the mouth due to spasm of the masseter muscles, often the very first sign.
  • Risus Sardonicus: A characteristic fixed, grimacing smile caused by facial muscle contractions.
  • Opisthotonos: Severe arching of the back due to powerful spasms of the spinal extensors.
  • Autonomic Instability: Fluctuations in blood pressure, rapid heart rate, heavy sweating, and high fever resulting from autonomic nervous system involvement.
  • Reflex Spasms: Painful, sudden muscle contractions triggered by minor stimuli like bright lights, loud noises, or a gentle touch.

Medical and Nursing Management

Managing a child with tetanus requires urgent, multidisciplinary intervention in a quiet, low-stimulation environment. Treatment revolves around neutralizing unbound toxin, eradicating the bacteria, controlling muscle spasms, and providing aggressive supportive care.

  1. Neutralization: Administer human tetanus immune globulin (HTIG) to bind circulating toxin and prevent it from reaching the nervous system.
  2. Eradication: Clean and debride the wound thoroughly, followed by antibiotic therapy such as metronidazole or penicillin to kill remaining bacteria.
  3. Spasm Control: Use sedatives and muscle relaxants like diazepam or magnesium sulfate to reduce rigidity and stop reflex spasms.
  4. Supportive Care: Maintain a patent airway, provide mechanical ventilation if respiratory muscles fail, ensure adequate nutrition via nasogastric feeding, and manage fluid balance carefully.

Prevention Strategies

Prevention remains the cornerstone of reducing tetanus morbidity and mortality globally. Midwives play a central role in delivering these preventive measures within communities and healthcare facilities. Routine childhood immunization using the pentavalent vaccine provides active immunity starting in early infancy. Furthermore, ensuring pregnant women receive the recommended doses of tetanus toxoid protects both the mother and newborn through passive transfer of antibodies. Promoting clean delivery practices—including clean cord care with chlorhexidine gel and avoiding harmful traditional substances on the umbilical stump—virtually eliminates neonatal tetanus. Finally, prompt and proper wound cleansing alongside appropriate booster doses following injuries safeguards older children and adults.
