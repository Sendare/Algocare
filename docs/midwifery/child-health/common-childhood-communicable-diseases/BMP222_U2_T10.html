Management of Acute Respiratory Infections (ARI) and Pneumonia in Children

Understanding Acute Respiratory Infections and Pneumonia in Pediatric Populations

Acute respiratory infections represent a major health burden for children across Nigeria, often starting simply before progressing rapidly. Acute respiratory infections (ARIs) involve any infection of the respiratory tract, categorized broadly by whether they affect structures above or below the vocal cords. Pneumonia specifically is a lower respiratory infection where the alveoli fill with pus and fluid, making gas exchange difficult. Young infants are particularly vulnerable because their airways are narrow, their chest muscles are weak, and their immune systems are still developing. Viral pathogens cause the majority of these episodes, but bacterial invaders like Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae frequently cause severe, life-threatening complications. Recognizing these dynamics early allows midwives to intervene before simple coughs spiral into fatal respiratory failure.

Clinical Recognition and Triage of Respiratory Distress

Spotting a struggling child quickly in a busy clinic can save a life. Midwives must look beyond the general appearance and assess specific physical signs of increased work of breathing.

  • Tachypnea: Fast breathing is often the earliest warning sign. Count the breaths for a full minute while the child is calm.
  • Chest indrawing: The lower chest wall pulls in noticeably when the child breathes in.
  • Stridor: A harsh, high-pitched noise heard when the child breathes in, pointing to upper airway blockage.
  • Wheezing: A whistling sound heard during exhalation, more common in lower airway narrowing.
  • Cyanosis: A bluish tint around the lips or tongue, signaling dangerously low oxygen in the blood.

Keep in mind that an agitated or crying baby will have an artificially high respiratory rate. Always try to settle the child before counting.

Differentiating Upper and Lower Respiratory Tract Infections

A clear clinical division separates upper and lower tract infections, guiding whether simple home care or urgent medical intervention is required. Upper infections involve the nose, sinuses, pharynx, and larynx, manifesting as common colds, pharyngitis, or croup. These typically resolve with supportive care alone and rarely cause low blood oxygen. Lower infections encompass bronchitis, bronchiolitis, and pneumonia, penetrating past the larynx into the lung parenchyma. These conditions feature deep coughs, fever, labored breathing, and abnormal lung sounds like crackles or wheezes. Midwives must listen carefully with a stethoscope and observe chest movement to accurately separate a routine cold from a dangerous chest infection.

Comprehensive Management and Treatment Protocols

Managing pediatric pneumonia follows standardized global guidelines adapted for local health facilities. When a child presents with fast breathing and chest indrawing, prompt classification determines the treatment setting. Outpatient management suits children with uncomplicated fast breathing, relying on oral antibiotics like amoxicillin, clear instructions for mothers, and scheduled follow-ups. Inpatient care becomes mandatory when danger signs appear, such as inability to feed, lethargy, convulsions, severe respiratory distress, or central cyanosis. Midwives play a central role in organizing this workflow, ensuring that severe cases bypass delays and receive immediate stabilization.

Indications for Hospital Admission and Oxygen Therapy

Oxygen is a vital medicine when managing severe pneumonia, and withholding it can lead to irreversible organ damage or death. Midwives must recognize the physical triggers that demand immediate oxygen delivery.

Clinical ParameterMild / Moderate (Outpatient)Severe / Critical (Inpatient Admission)
Respiratory RateElevated, no severe distressMarkedly elevated with chest indrawing
AlertnessCalm, alert, interactiveLethargic, drowsy, or unconscious
Feeding AbilityAble to breastfeed or drink fluidsUnable to feed, recurrent vomiting
Oxygen StatusNormal saturation on room airCyanosis or low pulse oximetry readings

When a child demonstrates central cyanosis, grunting, or severe chest indrawing, they need immediate admission and supplemental oxygen via nasal prongs or a mask.

Antimicrobial Therapy and Supportive Care in Low-Resource Settings

Providing effective care in resource-limited clinics requires a blend of clinical judgment and efficient use of available supplies. When injectable antibiotics are necessary for severe pneumonia, second-line options like ampicillin plus gentamicin are standard until the child stabilizes and can switch to oral medications. Supportive care remains just as crucial as drugs. Clearing blocked nostrils with gentle saline drops helps infants feed more easily. Keeping the child well-hydrated thins respiratory secretions, while managing fever with paracetamol reduces metabolic stress. Midwives must educate caregivers on how to provide these simple comforts at home alongside prescribed medicines.

Monitoring, Complications, and Recovery

A child undergoing treatment for pneumonia requires careful, continuous observation to catch worsening symptoms early. Midwives should check vital signs regularly, noting whether the respiratory rate is slowing down and whether chest indrawing is fading. Watch out for dangerous complications such as empyema (pus collection around the lung), lung abscesses, or respiratory failure. Recovery is marked by returning appetite, improving mood, and a normal breathing rate. If a child fails to improve after 48 hours of starting antibiotics, the midwife must reassess the diagnosis, look for treatment failure, or consider a resistant bacterial strain.

Preventive Strategies and Maternal-Child Education

Preventing respiratory infections is far more effective than treating advanced disease. Midwives occupy an ideal position to educate families on key protective measures during antenatal and postnatal visits. Promoting exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months provides vital immune protection. Ensuring children receive routine immunizations, including the pentavalent vaccine, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), and measles vaccine, shields them from major bacterial pneumonia drivers. Additionally, teaching mothers to reduce indoor air pollution by using clean cookstoves and keeping living spaces well-ventilated drastically lowers the daily irritation of young airways.
