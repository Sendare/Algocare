Mumps

Introduction and Transmission of Mumps in Children

Mumps is an acute viral illness caused by a paramyxovirus that primarily targets the salivary glands, particularly the parotid glands. It is highly contagious and spreads easily among school-aged children and adolescents living in close quarters, such as boarding schools or crowded classrooms across Nigeria. Transmission occurs mainly through airborne droplets from the respiratory tract when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or talks. Direct contact with saliva through shared utensils or fomites also facilitates spread. The virus can be transmitted from a few days before the parotid swelling appears until several days after it subsides, making early identification vital for infection control.

Pathophysiology and Incubation Period

Following inhalation or direct contact, the mumps virus enters the upper respiratory tract and multiplies in the local epithelial cells and regional lymph nodes. A brief viremia then disseminates the virus to various target organs, with a marked tropism for the salivary glands, central nervous system, and gonads.

  • Incubation Period: Typically ranges from 12 to 25 days, averaging around 16 to 18 days before clinical symptoms emerge.
  • Period of Communicability: Spans from approximately 1 to 2 days before parotid gland swelling begins up to 5 days after the swelling manifests.

Clinical Manifestations

The clinical picture of mumps often begins with non-specific prodromal symptoms such as low-grade fever, headache, myalgia, anorexia, and malaise. Within a day or two, the classic hallmark develops: painful swelling of one or both parotid salivary glands. This swelling can push the earlobe upward and outward, causing considerable discomfort with chewing and swallowing, especially when consuming acidic foods like citrus fruits or drinks. Some children experience submandibular or sublingual gland involvement instead of, or in addition to, parotid swelling. Approximately one-third of infections may present with mild or purely respiratory symptoms without prominent gland enlargement.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis

Assessing a child with suspected mumps involves a thorough history regarding immunization status, known contact with infected individuals, and travel history. Midwives examine the head and neck region, carefully palpating the parotid and submandibular areas to note the degree of tenderness, warmth, and swelling. Documenting temperature curves and monitoring fluid intake are crucial since painful chewing often leads to dehydration. While diagnosis is primarily clinical based on characteristic parotitis, confirmation can be achieved through laboratory tests such as real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) of buccal swabs or serological testing for mumps-specific IgM antibodies.

Nursing Management and Supportive Care

Because mumps is a viral infection, treatment is entirely supportive since antibiotics are ineffective. Midwives focus on relieving symptoms, preventing complications, and ensuring adequate hydration and nutrition.

  • Pain and Fever Management: Administer prescribed analgesics and antipyretics such as paracetamol.
  • Dietary Adjustments: Offer soft, bland foods that require minimal chewing, and encourage frequent small sips of water. Avoid citrus juices and sour foods that trigger painful salivary secretions.
  • Comfort Measures: Apply warm or cool compresses to the swollen parotid glands depending on what brings the child relief.
  • Isolation: Keep the child isolated at home from school or public gatherings for at least five days after the onset of parotid swelling to curb transmission.

Potential Complications

While most children recover fully without lasting health problems, mumps can occasionally lead to serious complications, especially when infection occurs post-puberty or in unimmunized populations. Aseptic meningitis is a known neurological complication that presents with headache, neck stiffness, and vomiting, though it usually resolves without permanent damage. Orchitis, which is inflammation of the testicles, can occur in post-pubertal males and raises anxiety about future fertility, though bilateral involvement causing sterility is rare. Other less common complications include oophoritis in females, pancreatitis, sensorineural hearing loss, and encephalitis.

Prevention and Immunization

The most effective defense against mumps is active immunization through the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine. Midwives play a crucial role in community health by educating parents on the importance of routine childhood vaccination schedules and debunking local myths surrounding vaccine safety. The first dose is typically administered at 12 to 15 months of age, followed by a second booster dose before school entry. Maintaining high routine vaccination coverage rates creates herd immunity, protecting vulnerable infants and immuno-compromised children who cannot receive live vaccines.
