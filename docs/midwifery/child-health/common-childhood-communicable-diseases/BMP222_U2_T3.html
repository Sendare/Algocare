Rubella

Understanding Rubella: Overview and Transmission in Children

Rubella, commonly known as German measles, is a viral illness caused by a togavirus. While it is typically mild in children, understanding its transmission is vital because of the devastating risks it poses to developing fetuses if pregnant women are exposed. The virus spreads primarily through airborne droplets when an infected person coughs or sneezes. Children can also transmit the virus through direct contact with nasopharyngeal secretions. A child with rubella is most contagious from one week before the rash appears until about seven days after it fades. In many Nigerian communities where children live in close quarters, schools and family compounds can facilitate rapid viral spread if immunization coverage drops.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

Once the rubella virus enters the respiratory mucosa, it replicates locally and spreads to the regional lymph nodes. A viremia follows, allowing the virus to seed into various tissues, including the skin and joints.

  • Prodromal phase: Often mild or absent in young children, but older children and adolescents may experience a low-grade fever, malaise, sore throat, and noticeable posterior cervical lymphadenopathy.
  • Rash presentation: A maculopapular rash typically starts on the face and neck, then rapidly spreads downward to the trunk and limbs within 24 hours.
  • Duration: The rash is usually fading by the third day, which earned rubella the traditional name of 'three-day measles'.
  • Joint involvement: Arthralgia and arthritis can occur, though they are much more common in adolescent girls and adult women than in young children.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis

Assessing a child with suspected rubella requires a careful review of immunization history and a physical examination focusing on characteristic signs. Midwives should check for the classic descending maculopapular rash and palpate for post-auricular and posterior cervical lymphadenopathy. Because clinical features of rubella overlap heavily with other viral exanthems like measles and roseola, laboratory confirmation is necessary for definitive diagnosis, especially in pregnant contacts. Serological testing detects specific rubella IgM antibodies, indicating a recent infection, or a rising IgG titer in paired sera. Documenting travel history and local outbreak data also helps midwives build a clear epidemiological picture.

Supportive Management and Complications

Postnatal rubella in children is generally a benign, self-limiting disease requiring no specific antiviral treatment. Management focuses purely on supportive care to ease discomfort.

  1. Rest: Encourage adequate bed rest during the febrile phase.
  2. Hydration: Ensure liberal fluid intake to prevent dehydration from fever.
  3. Symptom relief: Administer paracetamol for fever and body aches, avoiding aspirin in children due to Reye's syndrome risks.

While postnatal complications are rare, midwives must remain vigilant for transient arthritis, thrombocytopenia, or rare instances of encephalitis. The true medical emergency lies in accidental exposure of pregnant women to an infected child, which can cause congenital rubella syndrome.

Prevention through Immunization

The cornerstone of rubella control is active immunization using the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine. Midwives play a frontline role in educating parents and administering vaccines during routine child welfare clinic visits in primary healthcare settings. Herd immunity relies on maintaining high routine coverage rates in early childhood. Additionally, protecting women of childbearing age is critical to prevent congenital rubella syndrome. Routine screening of antenatal clients and postpartum vaccination for non-immune mothers—while counseling them to delay subsequent pregnancy for at least one month—form essential public health safeguards in Nigerian maternal and child health programs.
