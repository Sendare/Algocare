Poliomyelitis

Introduction and Transmission of Poliovirus in Children

Poliomyelitis is a highly contagious viral disease caused by the poliovirus, an enterovirus that primarily affects young children under five years of age. The virus invades the nervous system and can cause total paralysis in a matter of hours. Transmission occurs mainly through the fecal-oral route, typically via contaminated water or food, or through direct contact with an infected person's infectious secretions. Once inside the body, the virus multiplies in the intestine, from where it can enter the blood stream and invade the central nervous system. Poor sanitation and overcrowding greatly accelerate its spread in communities, making environmental hygiene a primary concern for midwives educating mothers during antenatal and immunization clinics.

Pathophysiology and Nervous System Invasion

After entering through the mouth, the poliovirus replicates in the pharynx and intestinal mucosa, invading local lymphoid tissue. From there, it enters the bloodstream, resulting in a transient viremia. In a small percentage of cases, the virus crosses the blood-brain barrier and targets the anterior horn cells of the spinal cord and the motor nuclei of the brainstem. As the virus replicates within these motor neurons, it causes inflammation, cellular destruction, and eventual lysis. This selective destruction of motor neurons interrupts nerve impulses to the skeletal muscles, producing the characteristic flaccid paralysis seen in the disease.

Clinical Manifestations

The clinical presentation of poliomyelitis varies widely depending on the extent of viral invasion.

  • Inapparent or Asymptomatic Infection: Up to 95 percent of all polio infections show no symptoms at all, though the child still sheds the virus.
  • Abortive Poliomyelitis: A minor illness causing fever, fatigue, headache, nausea, and sore throat that resolves within a few days.
  • Non-Paralytic Aseptic Meningitis: Features signs of central nervous system involvement, including stiffness of the neck and back, alongside the minor illness symptoms.
  • Paralytic Poliomyelitis: The most severe form, characterized by rapid onset of asymmetrical, flaccid muscle weakness, loss of deep tendon reflexes, and sometimes respiratory muscle paralysis.

Clinical Management and Supportive Care

There is no known cure for poliomyelitis once paralysis has set in. Medical management is entirely supportive, aimed at relieving symptoms, preventing complications, and supporting respiratory function. Pain relief, adequate nutrition, and proper positioning help prevent joint deformities during the acute phase. Physical therapy is critical once the acute inflammation subsides to maintain muscle stretch and prevent permanent contractures. In cases where bulbar muscles or respiratory muscles are compromised, mechanical ventilation and intensive monitoring in a hospital setting become life-saving interventions.

Prevention through Vaccination and Public Health

Prevention is the cornerstone of dealing with poliomyelitis, as the disease is entirely vaccine-preventable. Two main types of vaccines are used globally: the Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV) and the Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV). OPV creates strong mucosal immunity in the intestine, making it vital for stopping community transmission, while IPV provides excellent systemic immunity. Routine immunization schedules, mass vaccination campaigns, and robust surveillance systems for Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP) are essential strategies employed by health workers to eradicate polio permanently.
