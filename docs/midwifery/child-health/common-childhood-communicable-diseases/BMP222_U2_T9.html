Integrative Management of Childhood Illnesses (IMCI) Protocols

Introduction and Purpose of the IMCI Strategy

The Integrative Management of Childhood Illnesses is a vital strategy developed by the World Health Organization and UNICEF to reduce child mortality, morbidity, and frequent illnesses in developing countries like Nigeria. Midwives frequently encounter mothers bringing in sick babies and young children with overlapping symptoms. Instead of looking at a single disease in isolation, this protocol looks at the whole child. It combines preventive and curative measures to address the major causes of under-five deaths, which include malaria, pneumonia, diarrhea, measles, and malnutrition.

The Three Main Pillars of the IMCI Framework

The framework rests on three distinct pillars that work together to improve child health outcomes at the community and facility levels.

  • Case Management Skills: Upgrading the clinical skills of healthcare workers to properly assess, classify, and treat sick children using standard guidelines.
  • Health System Strengthening: Improving the overall organization of health facilities, drug availability, and supervision of health workers.
  • Family and Community Practices: Promoting key family behaviors in home care, nutrition, breastfeeding, and timely seeking of care.

The Three-Step Case Management Process

When a mother brings an under-five child to the clinic, the midwife follows a systematic three-step process: Assess, Classify, and Treat.

  1. Assess: Check for general danger signs, ask about main symptoms like fever or cough, and check nutritional and immunization status.
  2. Classify: Use a color-coded symptom chart to categorize the illness severity (Pink for urgent referral, Yellow for outpatient treatment, Green for home management).
  3. Treat: Provide the correct pre-referral dose, prescribe oral medications, teach the mother how to give treatments at home, and counsel on feeding.

Identifying General Danger Signs

General danger signs indicate a severe illness that requires urgent referral to a hospital. A midwife must check every sick child for these signs immediately upon arrival.

  • Inability to drink or breastfeed: The child cannot suck or swallow anything.
  • Vomiting everything: The child brings up everything they consume.
  • Convulsions: The child is having fits during the current illness or right now.
  • Lethargy or unconsciousness: The child is unusually sleepy, drowsy, or unresponsive to touch and sound.

Managing Major Symptoms: Cough, Diarrhea, Fever, and Ear Problems

After checking for danger signs, the midwife evaluates specific major symptoms using standardized inquiry and physical checks.

Checking for Malnutrition and Anemia

Nutritional deficiencies heavily worsen childhood infections. Midwives must systematically check every child for wasting, edema, and pallor.

Look for visible severe wasting, where the child appears extremely thin with prominent ribs and muscle loss. Check both feet for bilateral pitting edema, which is a hallmark of severe acute malnutrition (kwashiorkor). Examine the palms for severe palmar pallor to detect anemia. If severe malnutrition or severe anemia is present, the child requires urgent referral to a stabilization center or outpatient therapeutic feeding program.

Assessing Immunization and Feeding Practices

Prevention is just as important as curative treatment in the IMCI protocol. The midwife must check the child's immunization card against the national schedule to catch up on missed vaccines such as measles, pentavalent, and polio drops.

In addition, feeding assessment is crucial, especially for infants and toddlers. The midwife should ask about exclusive breastfeeding for babies under six months and the introduction of diverse, nutrient-dense complementary foods alongside continued breastfeeding for older infants. Identifying feeding problems early prevents chronic malnutrition and stunting.

Midwife Counseling and Community Strengthening

A successful consultation ends with clear, practical communication. Midwives must teach mothers how to give oral medications at home, explaining dosage, frequency, and duration clearly. Mothers must also be taught danger signs that require an immediate return to the clinic.

Beyond the clinic walls, midwives play a vital role in community outreach. They train community health volunteers to recognize danger signs, promote clean water usage, and support exclusive breastfeeding. Strengthening this bridge between the household and the health facility ensures that children receive timely interventions before minor health issues escalate into emergencies.
