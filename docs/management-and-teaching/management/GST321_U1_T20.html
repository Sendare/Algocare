Establishment of Standards

Introduction to Controlling and Standards in Nursing Management

Controlling is a fundamental administrative function in nursing management. It involves monitoring performance, comparing actual results against predetermined goals, and taking corrective action when deviations occur. Without a robust control mechanism, even the best-planned healthcare delivery systems can drift off course, compromising patient safety and operational efficiency.

At the heart of the control process lies the establishment of standards. A standard serves as an agreed-upon level of excellence or a baseline measurement against which actual nursing practice is evaluated. Establishing these benchmarks allows nurse managers to maintain consistency, ensure accountability, and protect both patients and staff from substandard care.

Types of Standards: Structure, Process, and Outcome

Nursing standards are generally categorized into three distinct types based on the Donabedian model, which evaluates healthcare quality. Understanding these categories helps nurse leaders evaluate care comprehensively rather than focusing on a single metric.

  • Structure Standards: Focus on the environment and resources available for patient care. This includes staffing ratios, equipment availability, facility safety, and staff qualifications.
  • Process Standards: Describe the actual activities and delivery of care performed by nurses. Examples include correct medication administration steps, proper hand hygiene protocols, and accurate wound dressing techniques.
  • Outcome Standards: Measure the end result of care or the final health status of the patient. Examples include patient recovery rates, incidence of hospital-acquired pressure ulcers, and patient satisfaction scores.

Characteristics of Effective Nursing Standards

For a standard to genuinely guide clinical practice rather than sit forgotten in a manual, it must possess specific qualities. Vague or unrealistic expectations frustrate clinical staff and fail to improve patient outcomes.

  • Measurable: The standard must be quantifiable or observable so performance can be objectively assessed.
  • Achievable: It must reflect realistic expectations given the available resources, staffing, and technology.
  • Relevant: It should directly address a priority clinical need or safety concern.
  • Clear and Concise: Ambiguous wording leads to inconsistent interpretation among nursing shifts.

The Process of Establishing Standards in Clinical Settings

Creating practical clinical standards requires a systematic, step-by-step approach involving key stakeholders. Rushing this process often results in policies that staff reject or ignore.

  1. Review Evidence: Examine current clinical guidelines, research, and best practices.
  2. Consult Stakeholders: Involve bedside nurses, charge nurses, and doctors who will actually use or be affected by the standard.
  3. Draft the Standard: Write clear, precise statements detailing the expected level of practice.
  4. Approve and Communicate: Obtain administrative sign-off and educate all nursing personnel on the new expectations.
  5. Review and Update: Periodically evaluate the standard against emerging evidence and clinical feedback.

Role of Regulatory Bodies and Professional Guidelines

Nursing standards do not exist in a vacuum. They are heavily shaped by external authorities that protect the public and uphold professional integrity. In Nigeria, bodies such as the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) set the foundational legal and ethical boundaries for practice.

Additionally, federal health ministries and international organizations provide baseline protocols for disease management, infection control, and patient safety. Nurse managers must align their internal hospital standards with these overarching regulatory requirements to ensure legal compliance and professional accountability.

Challenges in Implementing Standards in Healthcare Facilities

Translating written standards into everyday nursing actions often encounters significant roadblocks, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Recognizing these barriers helps managers plan effective interventions.

  • Resource Shortages: Lack of basic supplies like PPE, functional infusion pumps, or medications makes adherence nearly impossible.
  • Staff Resistance: Resistance to change or a general fatigue regarding 'new rules' can breed non-compliance.
  • Heavy Workload: High patient-to-nurse ratios leave clinical staff with little time to follow exhaustive procedural protocols.
  • Poor Communication: New standards failing to reach frontline night or weekend shifts due to weak communication channels.

Monitoring and Evaluating Performance Against Established Standards

Establishing standards is only the first phase; continuous monitoring ensures they translate into actual improvements in patient care. Nurse managers use various tools to track performance, including direct observation audits, incident report reviews, patient feedback surveys, and chart audits.

When evaluations reveal gaps between the expected standard and actual practice, the manager must investigate the root cause. Often, the failure points to a lack of training or missing resources rather than individual staff negligence. Constructive feedback and targeted re-education close the loop and foster a culture of safety.
