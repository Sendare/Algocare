Organising

Introduction to Organising in Nursing Management

Organising is the second phase of the management process, coming right after planning. Once a nurse manager sets goals and outlines a plan, organising turns those ideas into a workable reality. It involves arranging human and material resources in a logical structure to achieve the objectives of the healthcare unit. Without proper organising, even the best nursing care plan will fail due to confusion, overlapping duties, and wasted supplies. Nurse managers use this function to establish clear reporting lines, assign specific duties, and ensure that every staff member knows what is expected of them during their shift.

Organizational Structures in Healthcare Settings

An organizational structure is the visual framework that shows how people, jobs, and communication channels are arranged within a hospital or clinic. In most healthcare settings, you will find different types of structures:

  • Bureaucratic/Hierarchical Structure: Features a tall, top-down chain of command where decisions flow slowly from upper management to bedside nurses.
  • Flat Structure: Minimises middle management levels, allowing staff nurses to communicate more directly with top hospital directors.
  • Matrix Structure: Employees report to two different managers, such as a nursing director and a special project coordinator, which improves collaboration but can sometimes cause confusion.

Division of Labour, Specialisation, and Coordination

Division of labour breaks down a massive workload into smaller, manageable tasks assigned to different people based on their skills. In a busy surgical ward, one nurse might handle medication administration while another focuses on wound dressings and patient hygiene. This task specialisation makes staff very efficient at their specific duties. However, high specialisation can make workers isolated and fragmented in their thinking. Therefore, coordination is required to bring all individual efforts together into unified, patient-centred care through regular handovers, team meetings, and clear communication.

Authority, Responsibility, and Delegation in Nursing

Authority is the formal power given to a position to make decisions, give orders, and use resources. Responsibility is the personal obligation to perform assigned duties well. When a nurse manager delegates a task to a staff nurse, authority for that specific duty is transferred, but ultimate accountability always stays with the manager. A common misconception is that delegation means dumping unwanted chores; true delegation involves matching the task to the competency of the subordinate while providing necessary support and oversight.

Span of Control and Chain of Command

Span of control refers to the number of subordinates who directly report to a single manager. A wide span means one manager oversees many workers, which can stretch supervision thin. A narrow span means fewer workers per manager, allowing close monitoring but creating tall hierarchies. The chain of command is the unbroken line of authority running from the highest executive down to the entry-level nurse. Following this chain is crucial for maintaining discipline and ensuring communication flows without bypassing immediate supervisors.

In nursing, a decentralised approach empowers ward managers and senior staff nurses to make quick decisions regarding patient care and unit supplies without waiting for clearance from the chief medical director.

Designing Effective Rosters and Workflow Systems

Creating a fair and balanced duty roster is one of the most practical organising tasks for a ward manager. A good workflow system matches patient acuity levels with the right mix of experienced and junior nursing staff across morning, afternoon, and night shifts. Poor roster design leads to staff fatigue, burnout, absenteeism, and compromised patient safety. Managers must consider leave requests, skill mix, and labor laws when scheduling to ensure continuous, high-quality care without overworking the team.

Overcoming Barriers to Effective Organising in Nigerian Healthcare

Organising nursing services in resource-constrained environments like Nigeria comes with unique hurdles. Chronic staff shortages, erratic power supply, obsolete equipment, and bureaucratic bottlenecks frequently disrupt workflow. Additionally, interprofessional rivalries and unclear job descriptions can cause friction among healthcare team members. To overcome these barriers, nurse leaders must cultivate strong resource management skills, advocate for policy changes, foster open communication, and maximise available local materials to deliver safe patient care.
