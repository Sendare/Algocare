Organising

Introduction to Organising in Nursing Management

Organising is the second phase of the classic administrative process, following planning and preceding leading and controlling. In a nursing context, it involves arranging human, material, and financial resources in a systematic way to achieve set patient care objectives. Once a nurse manager sets goals during planning, organising turns those blueprints into an operational reality. This function establishes formal relationships among people and resources, creating a framework where clinical workflows can run smoothly without constant guesswork or administrative chaos. Effective organising prevents duplication of duties, clarifies who reports to whom, and ensures that the right equipment and personnel are available at the patient's bedside when needed.

Principles and Theories of Organising

Classical management theories provide the structural backbone for organising healthcare institutions. Max Weber's bureaucratic theory emphasizes a clear hierarchy, written rules, and professional specialization, which helps maintain order in large teaching hospitals. Henry Fayol introduced principles such as unity of command, scalar chain, and division of work, arguing that administrative efficiency depends on orderly arrangement. In modern Nigerian clinical settings, applying these principles prevents role confusion. When staff members understand structural expectations, patient safety improves because accountability is unambiguous. Bureaucracy, while sometimes slow moving, provides the protective standard operating procedures necessary for medication administration and sterile procedures.

Organizational Structures in Healthcare Settings

- **Functional Structure**: Groups employees by specialized roles, such as placing all theatre nurses in one department and all paediatric nurses in another, promoting deep clinical expertise. - **Service-Line Structure**: Organizes units around specific patient populations or disease states, like an oncology center housing outpatient chemotherapy, inpatient beds, and supportive care together. - **Matrix Structure**: Blends functional and project or service lines, where a nurse might report to both a clinical nursing director and a specialized programme coordinator.

Division of Labour, Specialisation, and Coordination

Division of labour breaks down comprehensive patient care into distinct, manageable tasks assigned to different workers based on their training and licensure. In a busy surgical ward, this means registered nurses handle complex wound assessments and IV medications, auxiliary nurses assist with basic hygiene and mobility, and ward clerks manage admissions and chart filing. While specialisation increases efficiency and mastery of specific skills, it creates fragmentation. If coordination fails, patient care becomes disconnected. Nurse managers act as the connective tissue, using multidisciplinary rounds, handover meetings, and shared care plans to harmonize specialized efforts into a unified healing experience.

Authority, Responsibility, and Delegation in Nursing

Authority is the formal, legitimate power granted to a position to make decisions, issue orders, and allocate resources. Responsibility is the obligation of an individual to perform assigned duties satisfactorily. Accountability, distinct from both, is the willingness to answer for the outcomes of those actions. In nursing management, authority can be delegated to subordinates, but ultimate responsibility and accountability for patient safety cannot be offloaded. When a ward manager assigns task execution to a junior nurse or community health extension worker, the manager remains accountable for ensuring the subordinate is competent and supervised. Effective delegation empowers staff while maintaining uncompromising standards of care.

Span of Control and Chain of Command

Span of control refers to the number of subordinates reporting directly to a single manager. A wide span involves many workers under one supervisor, which works well in highly routine, predictable environments but can overwhelm leaders in chaotic intensive care units. A narrow span creates tall organizational charts with many supervisory layers, slowing down communication but allowing close oversight. The chain of command establishes an unbroken line of authority running from the top executive down to the frontline ward nurse. Bypassing this chain during clinical disputes creates organizational friction, whereas respecting it ensures orderly conflict resolution and clear operational directives.

Centralisation versus Decentralisation in Nursing Services

| Feature | Centralised Nursing Services | Decentralised Nursing Services | |---|---|---| | **Decision Making** | Concentrated at top executive levels | Distributed to frontline unit managers | | **Responsiveness** | Slower due to bureaucratic approval chains | Rapid and adaptable to local ward needs | | **Standardisation** | High uniformity across all hospital units | Variable practices tailored to specific units | | **Control** | Tight executive control over budgets and policies | Greater autonomy for clinical nurse leaders |

Designing Effective Rosters and Workflow Systems

Creating staff rosters is a critical organising task that directly influences patient safety and nurse retention. A poorly designed shift schedule leads to fatigue, absenteeism, and clinical errors during medication administration. Effective rostering balances patient acuity census with staff competency mix across morning, afternoon, and night shifts. Nurse managers must account for leave entitlements, equity among staff, and peak admission times. Workflow systems should integrate smoothly with these rosters, ensuring that handovers are structured using tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to maintain continuity of care across shift changes.

Overcoming Barriers to Effective Organising in Nigerian Healthcare

Organising nursing services within the Nigerian healthcare system presents unique administrative hurdles. Chronic staffing shortages, brain drain to international countries, and irregular power supply strain daily ward operations. Resource scarcity means nurse managers often have to improvise with equipment while maintaining sterile techniques. Bureaucratic bottlenecks in public health institutions can delay procurement of basic consumables like syringes and gloves. To overcome these barriers, nurse managers must embrace flexible resource pooling, cultivate strong interpersonal advocacy with hospital boards, and foster a culture of resilience and creative problem-solving among nursing teams.
