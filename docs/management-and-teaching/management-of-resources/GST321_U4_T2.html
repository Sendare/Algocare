Management of Drugs and Supplies

Introduction to Healthcare Resource Management

Healthcare resource management involves planning, procuring, storing, and distributing drugs, equipment, and medical supplies within a clinical facility. In Nigerian hospitals and primary health centers, effective resource management directly prevents stockouts of life-saving commodities like oxytocin or antimalarials. Nurses often act as the final gatekeepers of these supplies at the ward level, balancing patient needs against limited institutional budgets. Waste, pilferage, and poor tracking compromise patient outcomes and strain fragile health systems. Understanding the operational cycle of medical supplies ensures that clinics maintain a steady, reliable inventory for daily clinical demands.

Classification and Identification of Drugs and Medical Supplies

Medical inventory must be clearly categorized to prevent administration errors and streamline storage. Drugs are typically classified by their pharmacological action, statutory schedule (such as controlled substances), or essential medicines list status. Consumables and surgical supplies are grouped by their single-use nature or durability.

  • Consumables: Single-use items like syringes, cotton wool, and gauze dressings.
  • Durable Equipment: Reusable items like stethoscope heads, blood pressure cuffs, and nebulizers.
  • Controlled Drugs: Highly regulated narcotics requiring dual-signature logs, such as pethidine and morphine.
  • Emergency Medicines: Rapidly accessible life-saving drugs kept together in a designated trolley.

Inventory Control Systems and Stock Management

Maintaining the right quantity of stock prevents both dangerous shortages and costly wastage from expired items. Two primary systems govern this process: the First-In, First-Out (FIFO) rule and the First-Expiry, First-Out (FEFO) principle. FEFO dictates that items with the nearest expiration date must be dispensed first, regardless of when they arrived.

Stock levels are monitored using specific thresholds:

  • Minimum Stock Level: The safety threshold that triggers a reorder before supplies run out.
  • Maximum Stock Level: The upper ceiling to prevent overstocking and storage clutter.
  • Lead Time: The duration between placing an order and receiving the actual delivery.

Storage, Handling, and Maintenance Guidelines

Environmental factors such as heat, humidity, and direct sunlight degrade pharmaceutical compounds rapidly, which is a major concern in tropical climates like Nigeria. Cold chain management is vital for items like vaccines, insulin, and certain oxytocics, requiring reliable refrigerators maintained between 2°C and 8°C with daily temperature chart logs. Tablets and capsules must be kept in their original packaging to protect against moisture. Flammable liquids and concentrated electrolytes need separate, secure storage to prevent accidental misuse. Routine cleaning of shelves and checking for pest infestations protect the physical integrity of all stored items.

Dispensing and Administration Protocols

The transition of a drug from the ward stock to the patient involves strict verification protocols. Nurses must adhere to the standard rights of medication administration to prevent clinical errors. Before dispensing or administering any drug, checking the label against the prescription chart three times is standard practice. High-alert medications, such as concentrated potassium chloride or heparin, demand independent double-checks by a second nurse. Unlabeled or defaced medication packages must never be used, as identity confirmation becomes impossible.

Management of Expired, Damaged, and Contaminated Supplies

Expired, broken, or compromised items pose severe biological and chemical hazards within a healthcare setting. These items must never be mixed with regular domestic waste or left on active shelves. Expired drugs require segregation in a clearly labeled, locked quarantine box until official disposal according to institutional or national environmental agency guidelines. Sharps containers must be closed and replaced when they reach the three-quarters full mark to prevent needle-stick injuries. Documenting write-offs maintains financial accountability and prevents fraudulent stock diversion.

Auditing, Accountability, and Record Keeping

Transparent record keeping safeguards against drug theft, loss, and discrepancies in inventory counts. Stock cards, electronic ledgers, and patient-specific utilization sheets form the paper trail of healthcare resources. Regular physical stock counts—often conducted weekly or monthly—reconcile actual physical quantities with recorded balances. Discrepancies require immediate investigation to identify whether errors stem from documentation omissions, wastage, or pilferage. Accountability ensures that resources funded by government budgets or donor agencies reach the intended patients.
