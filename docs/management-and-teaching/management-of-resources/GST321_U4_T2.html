Management of Drugs and Supplies

Introduction to Healthcare Resource Management

Healthcare resource management encompasses the systematic oversight of medications, surgical instruments, consumable items, and physical infrastructure within a clinical setting. Effective management ensures that clinical teams have uninterrupted access to necessary tools while minimizing financial waste through spoilage, pilferage, or overstocking. In many Nigerian health facilities, resource scarcity makes efficient utilization a direct determinant of patient survival rates. When essential items are poorly tracked, critical delays occur during emergencies, compromising patient safety and straining institutional budgets.

Classification and Identification of Drugs and Medical Supplies

Clinical supplies are broadly divided into pharmaceuticals and non-pharmaceutical medical consumables. Pharmaceuticals include tablets, capsules, injectables, and syrups, which require strict regulatory control. Non-pharmaceutical supplies encompass syringes, gauze, surgical sutures, catheters, and personal protective equipment.

  • Consumables: Single-use items designed for direct patient care that are depleted during procedures.
  • Durable Equipment: Reusable clinical assets such as blood pressure cuffs, stethoscopes, and nebulizers that require periodic maintenance.
  • Controlled Substances: Medications with high potential for abuse or dependence, necessitating double-lock storage and rigorous documentation.

Inventory Control Systems and Stock Management

Maintaining appropriate stock levels prevents both dangerous shortages and costly surpluses. A cornerstone of inventory control is the FIFO principle (First-In, First-Out), which dictates that older stock must be utilized before newly delivered items to prevent expiration on shelves. Regular stock rotation and physical counts help reconcile ledger entries with actual quantities present in the dispensary or ward store.

Another vital concept is establishing reorder levels—a predetermined threshold that, when reached, triggers an immediate requisition for replenishment before a total stockout occurs.

Storage, Handling, and Maintenance Guidelines

Environmental factors significantly impact the stability and efficacy of drugs and medical supplies. Heat, humidity, direct sunlight, and dust can degrade active chemical ingredients and compromise sterile packaging. Thermolabile drugs, such as vaccines and certain insulins, demand strict cold chain maintenance between 2°C and 8°C. Regular temperature monitoring of refrigerators and storerooms is a non-negotiable daily nursing duty. Furthermore, medical equipment must be cleaned, serviced, and stored according to manufacturer instructions to ensure readiness during clinical emergencies.

Dispensing and Administration Protocols

The transition of a drug from the pharmacy shelf to the patient requires adherence to safety checks to prevent medication errors. Nurses serve as the final barrier against administration mistakes. The fundamental practice involves applying the rights of medication administration—right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, and right documentation. Supplies must be inspected for package integrity immediately before use; a compromised sterile barrier on a syringe or catheter renders it unusable, regardless of its expiration date.

Infection Control and Management of Expired or Damaged Supplies

Expired or compromised items pose severe health hazards if retained in active clinical areas. Expired medications may lose potency or degrade into toxic chemical compounds, while damaged sterile supplies invite healthcare-associated infections. Facilities must implement clear segregation protocols where expired drugs and unusable consumables are moved immediately to designated quarantine holding areas. Under no circumstances should expired items be administered to patients. Disposal must follow institutional and environmental safety guidelines, typically involving incineration or return to central medical stores.

Auditing, Accountability, and Record Keeping

Accurate record-keeping provides the audit trail necessary for financial transparency and clinical safety. Every movement of drugs and supplies—from receipt at the central store to administration at the bedside—must be documented in stock cards, registers, or electronic health systems. Regular stock audits help detect discrepancies caused by loss, wastage, or undocumented usage. Accountability ensures that healthcare personnel handle institutional resources with professional diligence, protecting both patients and the organization from liability.
