Nurses' Legal Status and Hospital Liabilities

Legal Status of the Nurse: Employee, Agent, and Practitioner

A nurse working in a hospital or clinic generally holds the legal status of an employee. In this capacity, the nurse enters into a contract of employment that defines duties, working hours, and remuneration. However, depending on the setting, a nurse can also act as an agent of the hospital, meaning actions performed within the scope of employment legally bind the hospital as the principal.

Beyond hospital employment, the nurse is a registered professional accountable directly to the regulatory body and the law. While doctors traditionally diagnose and prescribe, nurses exercise independent professional judgment in direct patient care, assessment, and the execution of the nursing process. Understanding this tripartite status helps clarify who bears responsibility when clinical errors occur in daily ward routines.

Scope of Practice and NMCN Regulations

The Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) is the statutory body established by law to regulate the education, training, and practice of nursing and midwifery in Nigeria. The Council defines the legal boundaries of what a nurse is trained and authorized to do, commonly known as the scope of practice.

Operating outside this defined scope or performing procedures without valid registration is illegal and attracts severe disciplinary actions, including the withdrawal of the practicing license. Nurses must ensure their annual practicing license is current and never perform specialized tasks—such as advanced prescribing or complex surgical assists—without proper training and institutional authorization.

Civil Liability: Negligence and Malpractice in Nursing

Civil liability in nursing primarily revolves around negligence and malpractice, which occur when a nurse fails to provide the standard of care expected of a reasonably prudent professional, resulting in patient injury.

To successfully prove negligence in a court of law, four core elements must be established:

  1. Duty of Care: The nurse had a legal obligation to care for the patient.
  2. Breach of Duty: The nurse failed to meet the standard of care.
  3. Causation: The breach directly caused the patient's injury.
  4. Damages: The patient suffered actual physical, financial, or psychological harm.

Criminal Liability and Gross Negligence

While civil liability focuses on compensation for damages, criminal liability involves offenses against the state, punishable by fines or imprisonment. A nurse faces criminal charges when conduct is so reckless, careless, or wanton that it shows a total disregard for human life and safety.

Examples include administering a lethal dose of medication due to sheer recklessness, falsifying official records to cover up a fatal medication error, or engaging in illegal acts such as patient abuse or unlawful termination of pregnancy. In such cases, professional indemnity insurance does not protect the nurse from criminal prosecution and imprisonment.

Vicarious Liability: Respondeat Superior

The Latin legal maxim respondeat superior, meaning "let the master answer," forms the basis of vicarious liability. Under this doctrine, an employer—such as a hospital management board or private clinic owner—can be held legally and financially responsible for the negligent acts of employees committed during the course of their employment.

For vicarious liability to apply, the nurse must have been acting within the ordinary scope of official duties at the time of the incident. If a nurse performs unauthorized tasks completely outside employment boundaries, the hospital may successfully disclaim liability, leaving the nurse solely responsible.

Corporate Negligence and Institutional Liability

Corporate negligence holds the hospital directly liable for its own independent failures as an institution, rather than just being vicariously responsible for employee mistakes. This doctrine imposes a direct duty of care on the hospital to ensure patient safety through proper administrative systems.

Institutional duties include:

  • Hiring qualified and licensed professional staff.
  • Maintaining safe, functional medical equipment.
  • Implementing effective policies to monitor patient care.
  • Maintaining safe and sanitary hospital premises.

If a hospital fails in these direct duties—such as employing an unlicensed nurse or keeping broken resuscitation machines—it is guilty of corporate negligence.

Consent, Confidentiality, and Patient Rights

Patient autonomy is a fundamental legal and ethical principle. Informed consent requires that a patient be fully informed about the risks, benefits, and alternatives of a proposed procedure before giving voluntary permission. While doctors usually obtain surgical consent, nurses frequently witness consent forms and must ensure the patient is competent and acting voluntarily.

Confidentiality is another legal duty protecting patient information. Nurses must not disclose health details to unauthorized persons. Breaching confidentiality without legal justification—such as statutory reporting of gunshot wounds or infectious diseases—can lead to professional disciplinary action and civil lawsuits for invasion of privacy.

Risk Management and Legal Protection Strategies for Nurses

Risk management involves proactive strategies to identify, evaluate, and reduce the risk of patient injury and subsequent lawsuits. For nurses, documentation is the single most effective legal shield.

Key risk reduction practices include:

  • Maintaining accurate, contemporaneous, and legible nursing records.
  • Adhering strictly to hospital protocols and evidence-based guidelines.
  • Reporting clinical incidents and near-misses immediately through proper channels.
  • Securing professional indemnity insurance for personal legal defense.

The golden rule in nursing documentation remains: if it was not charted, it was not done.
