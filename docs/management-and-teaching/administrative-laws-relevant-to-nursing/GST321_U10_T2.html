Public Agencies and Corporations

Introduction to Public Agencies and Public Corporations

Public administration in healthcare operates through two main entities: public agencies and public corporations. A public agency is a direct governmental department funded through the national budget, tasked with implementing health policies, regulating standards, and providing essential services directly to the public without aiming for commercial profit. Examples include the Federal Ministry of Health and state health departments.

Conversely, a public corporation (sometimes called a statutory corporation) is established by an act of legislature to operate more like a business while remaining government-owned. They are expected to generate revenue through services or products, offering greater financial autonomy than standard ministries. Teaching hospitals and specialized federal medical centers often function with characteristics of public corporations, balancing social welfare mandates with commercial viability.

Key Regulatory Health Agencies in Nigeria

Healthcare delivery in Nigeria is guided by several statutory bodies established to protect public safety, regulate professional practice, and control medical products.

  • Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN): Controls the education, registration, and discipline of nurses and midwives, setting the legal scope of practice.
  • National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC): Regulates and controls the manufacture, importation, exportation, distribution, advertisement, and sale of food, drugs, and cosmetics.
  • Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria (MDCN): Regulates medical and dental education and practice.
  • National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA): Promotes, regulates, and integrates health insurance schemes to secure universal coverage.

Governance, Structure, and Accountability

Public health institutions require clear governance structures to ensure resources are used effectively. At the apex of a public corporation or teaching hospital is a Governing Board appointed by the government. This board sets broad policy directions, approves budgets, and oversees the chief executive officer, such as the Chief Medical Director or Director of Administration.

Accountability in these institutions flows in multiple directions. Administratively, leaders report upward to supervisory ministries. Financially, they answer to legislative public accounts committees and independent auditors to prevent corruption and mismanagement. Clinical accountability, however, rests directly on healthcare professionals who must answer to their respective regulatory councils for patient care standards.

Legal and Ethical Responsibilities of Nurse Administrators

Nurse administrators operating within public agencies and corporations carry heavy legal and ethical duties. They must ensure institutional policies do not violate national labor laws, the code of ethics for nurses, or fundamental human rights. A common misconception is that administrative orders override professional ethics; in practice, a nurse manager can never legally compel a subordinate to commit professional negligence or breach the scope of practice.

Administrators are also liable for maintaining safe staffing levels, providing adequate equipment, and reporting incompetent practitioners to the appropriate licensing boards. Failing to act on known safety hazards within a public facility can result in institutional liability and individual professional sanction.

Challenges in Managing Public Health Institutions

Managing public health agencies and corporations in developing settings involves navigating severe systemic hurdles. Bureaucratic red tape often delays procurement of critical drugs and repair of essential diagnostic equipment. Frequent changes in political leadership can disrupt long-term strategic health plans, leaving facilities with abandoned projects.

Additionally, public institutions frequently face severe brain drain, underfunding, and infrastructural decay. Nurse leaders must master resource allocation, advocacy, and crisis management to maintain operational continuity despite these structural deficits.
