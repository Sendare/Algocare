Assertive Behaviour

Introduction to Assertive Behaviour in Nursing

Assertiveness sits right in the middle of human communication styles. It is the clear and direct expression of your thoughts, feelings, and professional needs while showing complete respect for the rights and opinions of others. In nursing, this means you stand up for safe patient care and your own professional boundaries without stepping on colleagues or backing down silently. You communicate with confidence, maintain an even tone, and use direct eye contact. Unlike aggressive behavior that attacks the person, assertiveness focuses strictly on the issue at hand.

Assertiveness versus Passivity, Aggression, and Passive-Aggression

  • Passive behaviour: You swallow your feelings, avoid conflict at all costs, and let others make decisions for you, which often leads to burnout and unvoiced safety concerns.
  • Aggressive behaviour: You dominate conversations, use a harsh tone, and ignore the feelings of others to get your way, creating fear and poor teamwork.
  • Passive-aggressive behaviour: You hide your anger behind sarcastic remarks, delayed tasks, or silent treatment, which poisons team morale without solving anything.
  • Assertive behaviour: You address problems openly and honestly, fostering mutual trust and safe patient outcomes.

Psychological and Professional Benefits of Assertiveness

Practicing assertiveness protects your mental health and strengthens your career. When you speak up early about heavy workloads or unclear orders, you prevent stress and moral injury from building up inside. Professionally, colleagues and doctors learn to respect your clinical judgment because you present facts clearly. Patients benefit directly because an assertive nurse catches medication errors and questions unsafe discharge plans before harm occurs.

Barriers to Assertive Communication in Healthcare

Many nurses struggle to be assertive because of deeply ingrained workplace traditions. The traditional medical hierarchy often trains junior nurses to obey senior doctors and nursing leaders without question. Fear of intimidation, professional punishment, or being labeled as "difficult" stops many from raising alarms. Cultural factors that demand deep respect for seniority can also make junior staff hesitant to challenge an incorrect order.

Core Techniques for Practicing Assertive Behaviour

You can build your assertiveness using practical communication frameworks. The "Broken Record" technique lets you calmly repeat your safety concern without getting dragged into arguments. Using "I" statements—such as "I am concerned about this dosage"—focuses on your perspective rather than blaming the doctor. The DESC script—Describe the situation, Express your feeling, Specify the change, and state the Consequences—gives you a step-by-step tool for tough conversations.

Fostering Inter-Professional Collaboration Through Assertiveness

Assertiveness builds bridges between doctors, pharmacists, physiotherapists, and nurses. When every healthcare worker communicates clearly and respectfully, patient care runs smoothly. Instead of guessing what another department wants, assertive nurses ask direct questions and share vital patient updates without hesitation. This open exchange of information creates a culture of safety where teamwork thrives.

Managing Conflict and Resistance Professionally

Even when you are polite and clear, people may resist your assertiveness. A doctor might snap back or ignore your safety concern. When this happens, stay calm, keep your voice steady, and stick strictly to the clinical facts. Do not take resistance personally. If a colleague pushes back aggressively, use the fogging technique to acknowledge a tiny grain of truth in their statement while holding your ground on the core safety issue.
