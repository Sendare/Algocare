Definition of Conflict

Defining Conflict in Nursing

Conflict is a natural friction that arises when people with different values, goals, or needs interact. In nursing, it occurs whenever the interdependence of team members is disrupted by a clash of opinions or priorities. Think of it as a breakdown in communication or a genuine difference in how two people see the same clinical situation. It is neither inherently good nor bad, but simply a signal that the current state of affairs needs attention.

Intrapersonal and Interpersonal Conflict

Nurses experience conflict at different levels. Intrapersonal conflict happens inside an individual. A nurse might struggle with a personal moral dilemma, such as whether to report a trusted colleague's minor medication error. Interpersonal conflict occurs between two or more people. This is the classic argument between a nurse and a physician over a patient's pain management order, or a disagreement between two shift mates about workload distribution.

Group-Level Conflicts: Intragroup and Intergroup

  • Intragroup conflict: Happens among members of the same team. For example, the nursing staff on the surgical ward might split into factions over scheduling preferences.
  • Intergroup conflict: Occurs between two entirely different departments or groups. A classic healthcare example is tension between the emergency department and the intensive care unit regarding bed turnover rates.

Stages of the Conflict Process

Conflict does not appear out of nowhere; it follows a predictable pathway:

  1. Latent stage: Conditions are ripe for friction, though no actual disagreement has surfaced yet (e.g., severe understaffing).
  2. Perceived stage: Staff members recognize intellectually that a problem or potential trigger exists.
  3. Felt stage: Emotional involvement sets in; nurses feel frustration, anger, or anxiety.
  4. Manifest stage: Conflict is out in the open through active arguments, silent treatment, or uncooperative behavior.
  5. Aftermath stage: The situation resolves or blows over, leaving behind either stronger relationships or lingering resentment.

The Impact of Conflict on Patient Care

Unresolved conflict directly threatens patient safety. When healthcare workers do not communicate well due to tension, critical patient data gets missed during handovers. Medication errors increase, response times to call lights lengthen, and patients pick up on the hostile atmosphere, causing anxiety. Conversely, managed conflict where team members openly and respectfully debate care plans can actually drive innovation and improve safety standards.
