Antifungal Drugs

Introduction to Fungal Infections and Mycoses

Fungal infections, known as **mycoses**, present a unique challenge in clinical pharmacology because fungi are eukaryotic organisms. Unlike bacteria, fungal cells share biochemical and structural similarities with human host cells, particularly in their cell membranes and protein synthesis pathways. This biological kinship makes selective toxicity difficult to achieve, often leading to a narrower therapeutic window and more adverse effects compared to antibacterial agents. Mycoses are broadly categorized into superficial infections affecting the skin, hair, and nails, and systemic infections that invade deep tissues and visceral organs. In tropical environments like Nigeria, high ambient humidity and heat create an ideal microclimate for dermatophytes and opportunistic yeast proliferations, particularly **Candida** species. While superficial dermatophytosis is an everyday outpatient complaint, systemic mycoses such as cryptococcal meningitis or histoplasmosis are devastating, especially among immunocompromised populations. Recognizing whether an infection is localized or invasive dictates the entire pharmacological strategy, shifting the choice from topical creams to potent intravenous infusions.

Classification and Mechanisms of Action

Antifungal drugs are classified based on their chemical structures and the specific cellular targets they disrupt. Understanding these mechanisms clarifies why certain drugs treat specific pathogens while remaining useless against others. - **Polyenes**: Bind irreversibly to **ergosterol** within the fungal cell membrane, creating pores that cause intracellular leakage of potassium and vital ions, leading to cell death. - **Azoles**: Inhibit the fungal cytochrome P450 enzyme **lanosterol 14-alpha-demethylase**, halting the conversion of lanosterol to ergosterol and disrupting membrane fluidity. - **Echinocandins**: Inhibit the synthesis of **beta-1,3-D-glucan**, a critical polysaccharide necessary for maintaining structural rigidity in the fungal cell wall. - **Antimetabolites**: Interfere with fungal RNA and DNA synthesis by converting into 5-fluorouracil inside the fungal cell.

Systemic Antifungal Agents

Systemic mycoses require powerful agents capable of reaching deep-seated tissue infections. **Amphotericin B**, a polyene, remains the gold standard for severe, life-threatening systemic fungal infections despite its notorious toxicity profile. Often nicknamed "amphoterrible" by clinicians, it causes severe infusion reactions and dose-dependent nephrotoxicity. Liposomal formulations have been developed to target the drug to infected tissues while sparing the kidneys, though cost often limits their availability in resource-constrained settings. The **azole** family provides both oral and intravenous options for systemic therapy. **Fluconazole** achieves high concentrations in cerebrospinal fluid, making it the frontline therapy for cryptococcal meningitis in HIV-positive patients. **Itraconazole** and **voriconazole** cover broader spectra, including *Aspergillus* species, but require careful therapeutic drug monitoring because they inhibit human hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, triggering numerous dangerous drug interactions.

Superficial and Topical Antifungal Drugs

Superficial infections of the skin, hair, and nails are managed primarily with topical agents, though stubborn or extensive dermatophytosis requires oral therapy. **Terbinafine**, an allylamine, acts by inhibiting the enzyme **squalene epoxidase**, causing a toxic accumulation of squalene within the fungal cell and subsequent cell death. Terbinafine is fungicidal against dermatophytes and is heavily concentrated in the nail bed, making it the preferred oral drug for onychomycosis. Topical polyenes such as **nystatin** are used extensively for mucosal candidiasis, including oral thrush in infants or immunocompromised adults. Because nystatin is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, oral suspension is swished and swallowed or spit, exerting a purely local effect. Topical azoles like clotrimazole and ketoconazole creams are widely available over the counter for tinea corporis, tinea cruris, and cutaneous candidiasis.

Adverse Effects, Contraindications, and Drug Interactions

Antifungal pharmacotherapy demands vigilance regarding adverse reactions and metabolic interference. Systemic azoles are potent inhibitors of the hepatic cytochrome P450 enzyme system. When administered alongside drugs with narrow therapeutic indices—such as warfarin, phenytoin, or certain antiretrovirals—azoles can drastically increase serum concentrations of these co-administered drugs, leading to unexpected toxicity. For instance, concurrent use of ketoconazole with statins can precipitate severe rhabdomyolysis. Infusion-related reactions are classic with intravenous Amphotericin B, manifesting as high fever, chills, rigors, and hypotension. Pre-medication with antipyretics, antihistamines, or corticosteroids is standard clinical practice to mitigate these symptoms. Furthermore, baseline and ongoing renal function tests, serum electrolytes, and liver enzymes are mandatory when managing patients on long-term systemic antifungal regimens.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Safe administration of antifungal medications requires meticulous nursing oversight. For intravenous infusions, nurses must monitor infusion sites closely, as extravasation can cause severe tissue necrosis. Electrolyte imbalances, particularly hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia induced by polyenes, demand vigilant lab tracking and prompt replacement therapy to prevent lethal cardiac arrhythmias. Patient education is vital for treatment success, especially given the lengthy courses required for fungal eradication. Patients taking oral therapies for dermatophytosis or onychomycosis must understand that treatment takes months, and premature discontinuation leads to immediate relapse. For topical creams, instructions must emphasize cleaning and drying the affected area thoroughly before application and continuing the treatment for at least a week after visible lesions disappear to eradicate hidden spores.
