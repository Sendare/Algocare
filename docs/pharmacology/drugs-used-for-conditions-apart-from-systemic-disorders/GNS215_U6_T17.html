Contraceptives

Introduction to Contraception and Pharmacological Principles

Contraception refers to intentional methods used to prevent pregnancy by interfering with the normal process of ovulation, fertilization, or implantation. In clinical practice, understanding the pharmacological basis of these methods helps nurses explain how different products protect against unwanted pregnancies.

  • Mechanism of Action: How a specific drug stops conception, usually by altering hormones or creating a physical blockade.
  • Efficacy Rate: The statistical likelihood of a contraceptive method failing during typical use versus perfect use.
  • Reversibility: The ability of the reproductive system to return to normal fertility after discontinuing the drug or device.

Pharmacological contraceptives mainly rely on synthetic sex hormones, namely estrogens and progestins, which mimic natural feedback loops in the body to halt the release of eggs from the ovaries.

Hormonal Contraceptives: Mechanisms and Classifications

Hormonal contraceptives are broadly divided based on the specific hormones they contain and how those chemicals interact with the female endocrine system. Synthetic progestin is present in almost all hormonal methods, sometimes combined with a synthetic estrogen like ethinyl estradiol.

These drugs prevent pregnancy through three primary pharmacological actions:

  1. Suppression of Ovulation: High baseline levels of progestin and estrogen send a negative feedback message to the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, blocking the surge of luteinizing hormone (LH).
  2. Cervical Mucus Thickening: Progestin alters cervical secretions, making them thick and hostile to sperm entry.
  3. Endometrial Atrophy: The lining of the uterus becomes thin and inactive, which prevents the successful implantation of a fertilized egg.

Nurses must know these mechanisms to reassure patients that protection occurs through multiple safety barriers, not just one single action.

Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs) and Injectables

Combined Oral Contraceptives contain both estrogen and progestin, usually taken in 21-day or 28-day pill packs. The daily steady dose keeps hormone levels consistent to prevent the ovarian cycle from starting. Injectable options, such as depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA), deliver a repository dose of progestin that slowly releases into the bloodstream over three months.

Injectables offer high discretion and require less daily adherence than oral pills, making them popular in many community health settings. However, injectables can sometimes cause a delay in the return of normal fertility after the drug wears off, a clinical point worth noting for patients who want to conceive soon after stopping.

Progestin-Only Contraceptives and Emergency Contraception

Progestin-only options, often called the mini-pill, suit patients who cannot tolerate estrogen—such as breastfeeding mothers or those with a history of blood clots. They require strict adherence because their primary mechanism relies heavily on thickening cervical mucus rather than complete ovulation suppression.

Emergency contraception pills (ECPs) contain a high dose of progestin (like levonorgestrel) or a selective progesterone receptor modulator (like ulipristal acetate). They work by delaying or preventing ovulation if taken shortly after unprotected intercourse. They do not disrupt an established pregnancy, nor do they protect against future acts of intercourse.

Long-Acting Reversible Contraception (LARC) and Barrier Methods

Long-Acting Reversible Contraception includes intrauterine devices (IUDs) and subdermal implants. They provide continuous, highly effective pregnancy prevention for several years without requiring daily or monthly user action.

Pharmacological Side Effects and Adverse Reactions

Hormone-based contraceptives frequently cause mild side effects as the body adjusts to altered chemical levels. These can include irregular spotting, breast tenderness, mood shifts, and mild weight changes. While usually harmless, these symptoms can cause patients to stop taking their medication if not properly counselled.

Severe adverse reactions, though rare, demand immediate clinical attention. Nurses must teach patients to recognize warning signs associated with estrogen-containing drugs, often remembered by the acronym ACHES:

  • A: Abdominal pain (severe liver or blood vessel issues)
  • C: Chest pain or shortness of breath (pulmonary embolism or heart issue)
  • H: Headaches that are severe and sudden (stroke risk)
  • E: Eye problems like blurred vision or loss of sight (blood clot in the eye)
  • S: Severe leg pain or swelling (deep vein thrombosis)

Contraindications and Drug Interactions

Prescribing contraceptives requires a careful health history review because certain medical conditions make specific drugs unsafe. For example, estrogen-containing methods are strictly contraindicated in patients with a history of deep vein thrombosis, active breast cancer, severe liver disease, or migraines with aura.

Furthermore, liver enzyme-inducing drugs—such as certain anti-seizure medications and rifampicin for tuberculosis—can accelerate the breakdown of hormonal contraceptives in the liver. This interaction reduces contraceptive efficacy, requiring nurses to advise patients to use backup barrier methods or non-hormonal alternatives.

Nursing Assessment, Patient Education, and Counseling in Nigeria

Effective contraceptive nursing care in Nigeria requires cultural sensitivity, confidentiality, and clear communication. Nurses must assess a patient's reproductive goals, medical history, lifestyle preferences, and financial capability before recommending a specific method. Many clients face societal myths, stigma, or pressure from family members regarding family planning.

Education must be tailored to the local context, using simple language free of complex medical jargon. Nurses should verify understanding by asking the patient to repeat instructions in their own words, ensuring they know what to do if a pill is missed or if unusual side effects occur.
