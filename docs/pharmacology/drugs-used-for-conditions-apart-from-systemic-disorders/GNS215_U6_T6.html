Drugs Used in Newborn and Childhood Diseases (IMNCI)

Introduction to IMNCI and Essential Pharmacology in Pediatrics

Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMNCI) is a major strategy jointly developed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and UNICEF to reduce mortality, morbidity, and severity of illness among children under five years of age. Essential pharmacology within the context of IMNCI focuses on the standardized, evidence-based use of medicines in resource-limited settings, particularly in primary health care facilities across Nigeria. Nursing students must understand that childhood diseases rarely occur in isolation; therefore, IMNCI guidelines provide syndromic approaches to diagnosis and treatment. This means nurses treat clinical signs and symptoms rather than waiting for definitive laboratory confirmation, which is often unavailable in remote primary health centers. Essential pediatric pharmacology under IMNCI emphasizes oral formulations, pre-referral rectal or injectable treatments, and precise weight- or age-based dosing regimens to prevent toxicity and treatment failure. Nurses play a frontline role in triaging, administering medications, educating mothers and caregivers, and monitoring therapeutic responses. Understanding these protocols ensures that nurses can effectively manage common killer diseases like pneumonia, diarrhea, malaria, measles, and malnutrition. Furthermore, adherence to these standardized drug regimens prevents antimicrobial resistance and promotes optimal public health outcomes for vulnerable pediatric populations.

Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics in Neonates and Children

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics in neonates and children differ significantly from adults due to ongoing physiological maturation. Neonates have a higher percentage of total body water and extracellular fluid, which increases the volume of distribution for water-soluble drugs like aminoglycosides, requiring higher weight-based loading doses to achieve therapeutic serum levels. Conversely, lower levels of plasma proteins, particularly albumin, result in decreased drug binding, leaving a higher fraction of free, active drug in circulation and increasing the risk of toxicity, such as kernicterus with sulfonamides. Hepatic metabolism is immature in newborns due to low concentrations of cytochrome P450 enzymes and conjugating enzymes, leading to prolonged drug half-lives. Renal elimination is also reduced in neonates due to lower glomerular filtration rates and tubular secretion, meaning drugs excreted by the kidneys can easily accumulate if dosage intervals are not appropriately extended. Pharmacodynamically, receptor sensitivity and maturation vary widely by age. For example, blood-brain barrier permeability is much higher in neonates, making the central nervous system more vulnerable to toxicity from centrally acting agents. Nursing students must recognize these unique physiological properties to administer medications safely and prevent adverse drug reactions in pediatric and neonatal patients.

Antibiotics for Childhood Infections: Pneumonia, Diarrhea, and Sepsis

Antibiotic selection for childhood infections under IMNCI guidelines targets the most common pathogens responsible for pneumonia, diarrhea, and neonatal sepsis. For community-acquired pneumonia characterized by fast breathing and chest indrawing, oral amoxicillin is the first-line outpatient antibiotic of choice. Severe pneumonia requires referral to a hospital where injectable ampicillin or penicillin, combined with gentamicin, is administered parenterally. For acute watery diarrhea, antibiotics are generally not indicated unless cholera is suspected or dysentery (bloody diarrhea) is diagnosed; oral ciprofloxacin is recommended for shigellosis-associated dysentery. Neonatal sepsis presents subtle and non-specific signs such as lethargy, poor feeding, and temperature instability. Managing suspected neonatal sepsis in primary healthcare settings involves administering pre-referral doses of intramuscular or intravenous ampicillin and gentamicin before urgent transfer to a secondary or tertiary facility. Nursing responsibilities include verifying accurate weight-based dosage calculations, observing for signs of immediate hypersensitivity reactions, educating caregivers on completing the full course of antibiotics even if the child improves, and monitoring for adverse effects like antibiotic-associated diarrhea or superinfections.

Antimalarial Drugs and Management of Fever in Malaria-Endemic Regions

Malaria remains a leading cause of childhood morbidity and mortality in malaria-endemic regions like Nigeria. IMNCI guidelines mandate that any child presenting with fever or a history of fever in a malaria-endemic area must be evaluated for malaria using a Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT) or blood smear microscopy, where available, before treatment begins. Uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria is treated with weight-based courses of Artemisinin-based Combination Therapies (ACTs), such as artemether-lumefantrine, which provide rapid parasite clearance and prevent resistance. Severe malaria, characterized by danger signs such as prostration, impaired consciousness, multiple convulsions, or severe anemia, is a medical emergency requiring parenteral treatment. Intravenous or intramuscular artesunate is the preferred first-line medication for severe malaria, with quinine serving as an alternative if artesunate is unavailable. Once the child can tolerate oral medications, parenteral therapy must be transitioned to a full 3-day course of an oral ACT. Nurses play a crucial role in monitoring vital signs, assessing neurological status using scales like the Blantyre Coma Scale, monitoring for hypoglycemia (a common complication of severe malaria and quinine therapy), and educating parents on insecticide-treated net usage and environmental vector control.

Micronutrient Supplementation: Vitamin A, Zinc, and Iron

Micronutrient supplementation is a critical component of IMNCI aimed at reducing childhood morbidity, supporting immune function, and preventing long-term developmental deficits. Vitamin A supplementation is administered to infants and young children at specified age intervals to boost immunity, reduce mortality from measles and severe diarrhea, and prevent xerophthalmia and blindness. Therapeutic high-dose Vitamin A is also given immediately to children diagnosed with measles or severe acute malnutrition. Zinc supplementation is strongly recommended for all children with acute diarrhea, given daily for 10 to 14 days alongside oral rehydration therapy. Zinc reduces the duration and severity of the diarrheal episode and decreases the likelihood of subsequent diarrheal episodes over the next two to three months. Iron supplementation is vital for preventing and treating iron deficiency anemia, which impairs cognitive development and physical growth; however, iron should not be routinely given during acute systemic infections or in populations with high malaria transmission unless integrated with malaria control programs. Nurses are responsible for correctly calculating and administering these supplements, teaching mothers about dietary diversification using local foods, and documenting all administered micronutrient doses on child health cards.

Oral Rehydration Therapy and Fluid Management

Dehydration secondary to acute gastroenteritis is a major killer of children under five, making fluid management a cornerstone of IMNCI. Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT), utilizing low-osmolarity Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) solution, is the gold standard for treating mild to moderate dehydration. Low-osmolarity ORS reduces stool output, vomiting, and the need for intravenous infusions. Children with no signs of dehydration receive Plan A home management, which includes increased fluid intake, continued feeding, and zinc supplementation. Children with some dehydration receive Plan B, where a calculated volume of ORS is administered over a 4-hour period under supervision in the health facility, while nurses teach mothers how to recognize dehydration signs. Children with severe dehydration require emergency referral and Plan C fluid management, which involves rapid intravenous infusion of Ringer's Lactate solution or normal saline, followed by oral ORS as soon as the child can drink. Nurses must monitor the rate of intravenous infusion closely to prevent fluid overload and pulmonary edema, assess skin turgor, check mucous membranes and sunken fontanelles, and educate caregivers on hygienic practices and safe drinking water.

Antipyretics, Analgesics, and Anti-inflammatory Drugs in Pediatrics

Fever and pain are common presenting symptoms in pediatric patients, requiring careful administration of antipyretics, analgesics, and anti-inflammatory drugs. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) is the antipyretic and analgesic of choice for children under IMNCI guidelines due to its favorable safety profile and efficacy in lowering body temperature and relieving mild to moderate pain. It is administered orally based on the child's weight or age, with strict attention to dosing intervals (every 4 to 6 hours) and maximum daily limits to prevent hepatotoxicity. Ibuprofen is an alternative NSAID used for children older than six months for fever and inflammation, but it must be avoided in children with dehydration, renal impairment, or suspected dengue fever due to the risk of renal injury and bleeding complications. Aspirin is strictly contraindicated in pediatric practice for viral illnesses due to its well-established association with Reye's syndrome, a rare and often fatal condition characterized by encephalopathy and fatty liver failure. Nursing responsibilities include checking accurate weight-based calculations, educating parents on safe dosing intervals, discouraging alternating between paracetamol and ibuprofen without professional guidance, and emphasizing non-pharmacological comfort measures such as tepid sponging and lightweight clothing.

Dosage Calculation, Administration Safety, and Nursing Responsibilities

Safe medication administration in pediatric and neonatal nursing requires meticulous dosage calculation, verification, and adherence to safety protocols. Because children are not simply 'miniature adults,' drug doses must be calculated based on accurate body weight in kilograms, or body surface area when necessary, rather than age alone, to prevent underdosing or fatal toxicity. Nurses must perform double-checks for high-alert medications such as injectable electrolytes, antimicrobials, and sedatives. The 'Five Rights' of medication administration—right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, and right time—must be strictly applied, along with verifying expiration dates and dilution instructions. Pediatric formulations often require oral syringes rather than household spoons to ensure accurate measurement of liquid suspensions. Administration safety also involves assessing the child's developmental stage; toddlers may need gentle restraint and honest reassurance, while infants require secure positioning during oral or parenteral drug delivery. Nursing responsibilities extend to educating parents on how to administer medications at home, demonstrating the use of oral syringes, advising on storage away from children, and monitoring for adverse drug reactions or allergic manifestations throughout the treatment course.
