Drugs Used in Newborn and Childhood Diseases (IMNCI)

Introduction to IMNCI and Essential Pharmacology in Pediatrics

Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMNCI) is a strategy developed by the World Health Organization and UNICEF to reduce mortality and morbidity among children under five years old. Pharmacology in this age group differs significantly from adult care because children are not just small adults; their organ systems are still maturing, which drastically alters how medications behave in their bodies.

Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics in Neonates and Children

Neonates and infants process drugs uniquely due to physiological immaturity.

  • Absorption: Neonates have reduced gastric acid secretion, delayed gastric emptying, and erratic intramuscular absorption due to low muscle mass.
  • Distribution: Total body water is much higher in neonates, meaning water-soluble drugs require larger doses, while lipid-soluble drugs can lead to unexpected toxicity.
  • Metabolism: Hepatic enzyme systems are underdeveloped at birth, prolonging the half-life of many drugs.
  • Excretion: Glomerular filtration rate is low in newborns, making them prone to drug accumulation if renal clearance is not monitored.

Antibiotics for Childhood Infections: Pneumonia, Diarrhea, and Sepsis

Managing severe bacterial infections in children relies on standardized antibiotic protocols under IMNCI guidelines. Amoxicillin is the first-line oral treatment for uncomplicated community-acquired pneumonia. For severe pneumonia or suspected neonatal sepsis, injectable ampicillin and gentamycin are the standard choices. In cases of acute watery diarrhea, antibiotics are generally avoided unless cholera or dysentery is diagnosed, as most diarrheal episodes are viral.

Antimalarial Drugs and Fever Management in Malaria-Endemic Regions

Fever in endemic areas requires prompt assessment to rule out severe malaria. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), such as artemether-lumefantrine, serve as the frontline treatment for uncomplicated Plasmodium falciparum malaria. For severe malaria presenting with prostration or inability to drink, parenteral artesunate is the drug of choice, administered prior to urgent hospital referral.

Micronutrient Supplementation: Vitamin A, Zinc, and Iron

Micronutrients play a crucial role in supporting immune function and preventing chronic childhood morbidity. Vitamin A supplements boost epithelial integrity and reduce measles severity. Zinc supplementation reduces the duration and severity of acute diarrhea when given alongside oral rehydration salts. Iron is vital for preventing and treating iron-deficiency anemia, though it must be dosed carefully to avoid gastric irritation.

Oral Rehydration Therapy and Fluid Management

Dehydration from acute gastroenteritis remains a leading cause of pediatric mortality. Low-osmolarity Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) solution is the gold standard for treating mild to moderate dehydration by actively utilizing sodium-glucose cotransporters in the gut. Severely dehydrated children require rapid intravenous fluid resuscitation, using Ringer's Lactate or Normal Saline when oral routes fail.

Antipyretics, Analgesics, and Anti-inflammatory Drugs in Pediatrics

Fever management centers on improving the child's comfort rather than normalizing body temperature alone. Paracetamol is the safest and most widely used antipyretic for infants and children. Ibuprofen is an alternative for children older than six months, but it must be avoided in children with dehydration or suspected dengue due to renal and bleeding risks. Aspirin is strictly contraindicated in children due to its association with Reye's syndrome.

Dosage Calculation, Administration Safety, and Nursing Responsibilities

Safe medication administration in children requires meticulous calculation based on weight, expressed in milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg). Errors can easily lead to fatal toxicity due to small body margins. Nurses must double-check all calculations with a colleague, use appropriate oral measuring devices rather than household spoons, and educate caregivers on correct administration techniques before discharge.
