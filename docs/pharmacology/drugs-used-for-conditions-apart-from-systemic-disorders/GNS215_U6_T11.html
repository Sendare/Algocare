Infusions and Transfusions

Introduction and Fluid Dynamics in Nursing Practice

Intravenous therapy forms the backbone of acute and routine clinical care, requiring precision from every nurse at the bedside. At its core, intravenous therapy involves the direct administration of fluids, medications, or blood products into the venous system. Understanding fluid dynamics is vital for managing this therapy safely. Body fluid is divided into two primary compartments: intracellular and extracellular, with the extracellular space further split into intravascular and interstitial spaces. When a nurse hangs a bag of fluid, the goal is to manipulate these compartments to restore volume, correct electrolyte imbalances, or maintain hemodynamic stability. In Nigerian clinical settings, where patients may present late with severe dehydration from gastroenteritis or malaria, rapid mastery of intravenous access and fluid replacement can be lifesaving.

Categorization of IV Fluids and Blood Products

Intravenous solutions and hemotherapeutic agents are grouped based on their molecular properties and clinical purposes.

  • Crystalloids: Aqueous solutions of mineral salts and small molecules that easily pass semipermeable membranes, used widely for volume resuscitation and maintenance.
  • Colloids: Solutions containing large, insoluble molecules like proteins or starches that remain in the intravascular space longer due to high oncotic pressure.
  • Blood Products: Therapeutic components derived from human blood donations, including whole blood, packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets.

Each category serves distinct physiological needs, and selecting the wrong type can exacerbate tissue edema or fail to restore oxygen-carrying capacity.

Composition and Physiological Effects of Solutions

The specific chemical makeup of an infusion determines how fluid moves across cellular membranes via osmosis.

IsotonicEqualExpands extracellular volume without fluid shifting
HypotonicLowerMoves fluid from blood vessels into cells
HypertonicHigherDraws fluid out of cells into blood vessels

Normal Saline (0.9% Sodium Chloride) and Hartmann's solution (Ringer's Lactate) are standard isotonic fluids. 0.45% Sodium Chloride is a typical hypotonic choice, helpful for treating cellular dehydration. 3% Sodium Chloride is a potent hypertonic solution reserved for severe hyponatremia, requiring strict monitoring to prevent rapid fluid overload and central pontine myelinolysis.

Preparation, Crossmatching, and Safety Protocols

Safety protocols form an absolute shield against fatal errors during intravenous and blood therapy. Before initiating any transfusion, strict patient identification matching must occur at the bedside with two qualified healthcare professionals verifying the patient's wristband against the blood bag label, compatibility form, and hospital number. For blood products, pre-medication with antihistamines or antipyretics may be ordered for patients with a history of mild allergic reactions. Nurses must inspect all fluid bags and blood units for leaks, unusual discoloration, clots, or expiration dates. In busy hospital wards, never rush the clerical verification step; misidentification of blood recipients remains a leading cause of fatal acute hemolytic transfusion reactions.

Administration Techniques and Patient Monitoring

Executing safe infusion and transfusion requires accurate calculations, appropriate equipment, and vigilant observation. Standard peripheral cannulation uses the largest possible vein in the forearm for routine fluids, whereas blood products require a large-bore cannula, typically 18 or 20 gauge, to prevent mechanical hemolysis of red blood cells. Blood administration sets must contain an integral mesh filter and be primed exclusively with 0.9% Sodium Chloride; dextrose solutions cause hemolysis and lactated Ringer's contains calcium that can trigger micro-clotting. Vital signs must be recorded baseline, fifteen minutes after starting a blood transfusion, hourly throughout, and upon completion. The nurse remains the primary sentinel, staying alert to subtle changes in temperature, respiratory rate, or patient comfort during the initial phase of infusion.

Complications and Emergency Management

Adverse reactions during infusions and transfusions demand immediate, decisive nursing intervention. Transfusion reactions can be acute or delayed, spanning allergic, febrile non-hemolytic, septic, and acute hemolytic reactions. An acute hemolytic reaction occurs due to ABO incompatibility, presenting with fever, chest pain, hypotension, and hemoglobinuria. Circulatory overload is another common complication, especially in elderly or cardiac-compromised patients receiving fluids too rapidly, marked by dyspnea, engorged neck veins, and crackles in the lung bases. When any serious reaction is suspected, the nurse must instantly stop the infusion, disconnect the tubing, notify the medical team, send the remaining blood bag and tubing back to the blood bank, and support vital organ functions.
