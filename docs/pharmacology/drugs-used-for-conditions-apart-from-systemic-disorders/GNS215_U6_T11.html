Infusions and Transfusions

Introduction to Intravenous Infusions and Blood Transfusions

Intravenous infusions and blood transfusions are everyday lifelines in clinical nursing. An infusion delivers fluids, electrolytes, or medications directly into a patient's vein, restoring hydration and stabilizing blood pressure. A transfusion, specifically, introduces whole blood or specific blood components—like packed red blood cells, platelets, or plasma—to replace lost components due to trauma, surgery, or disease.

Time is often critical. A nurse must master the calculation of flow rates, understand the distinct clinical purposes of various fluids, and remain fiercely vigilant for adverse reactions. While infusing fluids feels routine, every bag hung and every unit of blood spiked carries inherent risks. Respect for these procedures safeguards patient survival.

Understanding Crystalloids, Colloids, and Blood Products

Fluids and blood products are categorized by how they behave inside the human body.

  • Crystalloids: Small molecules that easily cross semipermeable membranes. They move freely between blood vessels and body tissues. Examples include Normal Saline (0.9% NaCl) and Ringer's Lactate, used heavily for general hydration and electrolyte replacement.
  • Colloids: Large molecules that stay inside the blood vessels because they are too big to cross the capillary membrane. They draw fluid into the bloodstream through high oncotic pressure. Examples include albumin.
  • Blood Components: Biological tissues derived from human donors tailored to treat specific deficits.
  • Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBCs): Increases oxygen-carrying capacity.
  • Platelets: Essential for stopping bleeding in patients with low platelet counts.
  • Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP): Supplies clotting factors for bleeding disorders.

Preparation and Safety Protocols for Blood Transfusions

Blood safety begins long before the bag reaches the bedside. A single clerical error can lead to a fatal incompatible transfusion.

  1. Prescreening and Matching: The blood bank performs type and crossmatching to ensure the donor red blood cells match the recipient's antigens and antibodies.
  2. Informed Consent: The patient or legal guardian must understand the risks, benefits, and alternatives, and sign a consent form.
  3. Vascular Access: Secure a reliable, patent intravenous line, ideally gauge 18 or 20, to prevent hemolysis of red blood cells.
  4. The Bedside Double-Check: Two qualified healthcare professionals must independently verify the patient's identity against the blood bag tag, hospital wristband, blood bank slip, and expiration date.
  5. Prime with Normal Saline: Blood products must only be infused with 0.9% Sodium Chloride. Dextrose solutions cause red blood cells to clump and burst, while lactated Ringer's contains calcium that can trigger clotting inside the tubing.

Administration Techniques and Patient Monitoring

Once safety checks are complete, proper administration technique ensures the patient remains safe. Always use specialized blood administration tubing equipped with a built-in filter to catch any micro-clots or cellular debris.

Begin the transfusion very slowly—usually no more than 2 milliliters per minute for the first 15 minutes. The nurse must stay at the bedside during this initial window, as most severe, life-threatening acute reactions happen quickly.

Monitor and record vital signs: baseline before starting, after 15 minutes, and then periodically according to hospital policy until the unit finishes. Most blood components must be infused within a strict four-hour window to prevent bacterial growth in the nutrient-rich environment at room temperature.

Recognizing and Managing Transfusion Reactions

Despite rigorous checks, transfusion reactions can still occur. Recognizing the signs early saves lives.

  • Acute Hemolytic Reaction: Caused by blood type incompatibility. Symptoms include fever, chills, low back pain, and dark urine. Stop the infusion immediately!
  • Allergic Reaction: Ranging from hives (urticaria) and itching to full anaphylaxis with wheezing and low blood pressure.
  • Febrile Non-Hemolytic Reaction: A sudden fever and chills without hemolysis, triggered by donor white blood cell antibodies.
  • Circulatory Overload (TACO): Fluid fills the lungs because blood was given too fast. Expect shortness of breath, high blood pressure, and crackles in the lungs.

Immediate Nursing Actions for any suspected reaction:

  1. Stop the transfusion immediately.
  2. Disconnect the blood tubing and replace it with fresh 0.9% saline attached to a new line to keep the vein open.
  3. Notify the physician and the blood bank.
  4. Save the blood bag and tubing for laboratory investigation.
