Oral Rehydration Salt and Oral Rehydration Therapy

Introduction to Oral Rehydration Therapy and Dehydration

Dehydration resulting from acute gastroenteritis remains a leading cause of childhood morbidity and mortality across resource-limited settings in Nigeria. Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) involves the simple administration of fluids by mouth to prevent or correct the electrolyte and water deficits that occur during diarrheal illnesses. Before the widespread adoption of ORT, severe dehydration required intravenous cannulation, which carried high costs, risks of sepsis, and logistical challenges in rural primary healthcare clinics. ORT relies on the gut's remaining absorptive capacity, proving that most acute diarrheal episodes can be managed safely outside the hospital ward through community health education and maternal empowerment.

Physiological Mechanisms and the Sodium-Glucose Linked Transporter

The efficacy of ORS depends entirely on the sodium-glucose linked transporter (SGLT1) located on the brush border of the enterocytes in the small intestine. During infectious diarrhea, secretory mechanisms cause massive fluid loss in the crypts of the intestine, but nutrient-coupled absorption in the villi remains largely intact. When glucose and sodium are present in the lumen simultaneously, SGLT1 actively transports one molecule of sodium and one molecule of glucose across the apical membrane together. This cotransport creates an osmotic gradient that drags water molecules along with the solutes from the gut lumen into the intercellular space and ultimately into the bloodstream, successfully rehydrating the patient even while diarrhea persists.

Composition and WHO Guidelines for Oral Rehydration Salts

The World Health Organization reformulated ORS to optimize both safety and clinical efficacy, reducing osmolarity to prevent hypernatremia and decrease stool output.

  • Sodium chloride: Provides sodium and chloride to correct extracellular electrolyte depletion.
  • Glucose anhydrous: Serves as the essential substrate to drive active cotransport through SGLT1.
  • Potassium chloride: Replaces intracellular potassium lost through profuse watery stools, preventing muscular weakness and cardiac arrhythmias.
  • Trisodium citrate dihydrate: Acts as a buffer to correct the metabolic acidosis caused by bicarbonate loss in stool.

ORS is indicated for patients classified with no dehydration (for prevention) or some dehydration (for active correction). Severe dehydration constitutes a medical emergency requiring rapid IV fluid boluses, though ORS can resume once the patient regains consciousness and can drink.

Administration Guidelines and Preparation Protocols for Nurses

Proper preparation of ORS is critical; errors in dilution can lead to severe hypernatremia or render the therapy ineffective. Nurses must instruct caregivers to dissolve one standard WHO-ORS sachet completely in exactly one litre of clean, safe drinking water kept in a clean container. If the water is from an unverified source, it must be boiled and cooled first. Once mixed, the solution must be used within 24 hours and then discarded to prevent bacterial contamination in tropical ambient temperatures. During active fluid loss, the nurse administers ORS frequently in small sips using a cup and spoon rather than a feeding bottle, which is difficult to sterilize and can introduce pathogens.

Contraindications, Precautions, and Adverse Effects

While ORT is remarkably safe, certain clinical scenarios require immediate cessation of oral routes or specialized hospital management. Absolute contraindications include prolonged, intractable vomiting where fluid retention is impossible, severe hypovolemic shock requiring immediate vascular access, and clinical evidence of paralytic ileus or intestinal obstruction. Nurses must monitor for signs of fluid overload, particularly periorbital edema, in infants with compromised renal or cardiac function. If a child develops excessive eyelid swelling while taking ORS, the nurse should temporarily pause the solution, assess hydration status, and consult the medical officer.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Education in ORT

Effective nursing management extends beyond mixing and passing packets; it requires empowering mothers and caregivers with actionable home-care knowledge. Nurses should teach the three rules of home management: give extra fluids, continue feeding, and return for reassessment if danger signs appear. Caregivers must understand that food should never be withheld during diarrhea; early reintroduction of nutrient-rich local foods shortens illness duration and maintains nutritional status. Furthermore, nurses should demonstrate how to recognize warning signs like blood in the stool, persistent vomiting, inability to drink, or fever, advising prompt return to the clinic if these occur.
