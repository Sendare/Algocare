Antiparasitic Drugs

Introduction to Parasitic Infections and Antiparasitic Therapy

Parasitic infections remain a major public health concern globally, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions where sanitation challenges and warm climates facilitate transmission. Unlike bacteria and viruses, parasites are eukaryotic organisms, meaning their cellular structure closely resembles human host cells. This biochemical similarity makes designing safe antiparasitic drugs uniquely difficult, because agents toxic to the parasite frequently produce adverse effects in the human patient. Parasites typically fall into two main categories: protozoa, which are single-celled microscopic organisms, and helminths, which are multicellular worms visible to the naked eye. Ectoparasites, such as lice and mites, live on the skin surface. Effective antiparasitic therapy relies on selective toxicity—exploiting biochemical pathways unique to the parasite, such as distinct metabolic enzymes or structural proteins, to destroy the invader while sparing the host from severe systemic harm.

Anthelmintics: Classification, Mechanism of Action, and Nursing Care

Anthelmintics are medications used to treat infections caused by parasitic worms, including roundworms, tapeworms, and flukes. These drugs work through diverse mechanisms to paralyze, starve, or disrupt the cellular metabolism of the worm so the host body can expel it.

  • Benzimidazoles: Examples include albendazole and mebendazole. They inhibit glucose uptake in worms, leading to glycogen depletion and eventual parasite death.
  • Macrocyclic Lactones: Ivermectin is the primary drug here, paralyzing the parasite by increasing chloride ion permeability, which disrupts nerve impulses.
  • Praziquantel: Highly effective against schistosomes and tapeworms, this drug increases cell membrane permeability to calcium, causing severe muscle spasms and paralysis of the worm.

When caring for patients receiving anthelmintics, nurses must anticipate gastrointestinal upset as dying worms detach and are eliminated from the bowel. Proper hygiene education is critical to prevent reinfection, especially in household contacts.

Antimalarial Agents: Types, Pharmacodynamics, and Patient Monitoring

Malaria is a life-threatening disease caused by Plasmodium parasites transmitted through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. Antimalarial drugs are categorized based on their specific phase in the parasite's life cycle and their clinical goal—prophylaxis, acute treatment, or prevention of relapse.

  • Chloroquine: Historically the cornerstone for uncomplicated malaria, though resistance is now widespread in many regions. It works by preventing the parasite from detoxifying heme, leading to toxic buildup within the organism.
  • Artemisinin-Based Combination Therapies (ACTs): Currently recommended as the gold standard for treating uncomplicated falciparum malaria, combining a fast-acting artemisinin derivative with a longer-acting partner drug to reduce resistance.
  • Primaquine: Essential for eradicating dormant liver-stage parasites (hypnozoites) found in *Plasmodium vivax* and *Plasmodium ovale* to prevent clinical relapses.

Nurses managing patients on antimalarials must monitor closely for adverse reactions. Chloroquine can cause visual disturbances and cardiac arrhythmias. Primaquine carries a high risk of acute hemolytic anemia in patients with Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, making pre-treatment screening mandatory.

Antiprotozoal Drugs: Treatment of Amebiasis, Giardiasis, and Trichomoniasis

Non-malarial protozoal infections like amebiasis, giardiasis, and trichomoniasis are frequently encountered in clinical practice, often spread through contaminated food and water or sexual contact. The frontline agent for these anaerobic protozoal infections is metronidazole (and its relative tinidazole).

Metronidazole enters the protozoal cell where it is chemically reduced by ferredoxin-like proteins unique to anaerobic organisms. This reduction creates reactive intermediate compounds that damage the parasite's DNA, halting replication and causing cell death.

A critical nursing consideration for patients taking metronidazole is the severe disulfiram-like reaction that occurs if alcohol is consumed during therapy and for at least three days afterward. Patients must be explicitly warned that ingestion of alcohol will trigger flushing, severe nausea, vomiting, tachycardia, and headache. Metallic taste and dark urine are common, benign side effects that should be explained beforehand to prevent needless panic.

Ectoparasiticides: Management of Scabies and Pediculosis

Ectoparasites reside on the external surfaces of the body, primarily infesting the skin and hair. Pediculosis refers to lice infestations on the head, body, or pubic region, whereas scabies is caused by tiny mites that burrow beneath the stratum corneum of the skin.

Treatment involves topical applications such as permethrin cream, malathion, or oral agents like ivermectin. Permethrin works by disrupting the sodium channel current in the parasite's nerve cell membranes, leading to delayed repolarization, paralysis, and death of the lice or mite.

Successful management requires strict adherence to application guidelines. For scabies, cream must be applied over the entire body from the neck down, left on for the prescribed duration (often 8 to 14 hours), and washed off thoroughly. Household contacts and sexual partners must be treated simultaneously, and bedding, clothing, and towels must be washed in hot water and dried on high heat to prevent reinfection.

General Adverse Effects and Drug Interactions

Antiparasitic medications are potent chemicals that frequently interact with other pharmaceutical agents and produce significant systemic side effects. Because many of these drugs are metabolized through hepatic cytochrome P450 enzyme systems, concurrent use of enzyme inducers or inhibitors can alter plasma concentrations dangerously. For instance, mebendazole and albendazole levels can be significantly affected by anti-seizure medications or antifungals.

Gastrointestinal intolerance—including nausea, abdominal cramps, and diarrhea—is a universal class effect for almost all oral antiparasitic drugs. Central nervous system symptoms such as dizziness, headache, and lethargy also occur frequently, particularly with agents crossing the blood-brain barrier like ivermectin or metronidazole. Nurses must review the patient's complete medication history, including over-the-counter products and herbal supplements, before administration to intercept adverse drug interactions before they harm the patient.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education for Safe Administration

Safe administration of antiparasitic therapy extends beyond simply handing the patient pills or applying topical creams. Nurses play a frontline role in confirming accurate diagnosis, ensuring correct weight-based dosing (especially in pediatric populations where under-dosing causes resistance and over-dosing causes toxicity), and monitoring laboratory values such as complete blood counts and liver function tests.

Patient and family education remains the cornerstone of long-term eradication. Nurses must teach proper hand hygiene after using the toilet and before handling food, advocate for drinking safe, boiled, or filtered water in endemic areas, and explain the absolute necessity of completing the full course of medication even after symptoms subside. Emphasizing environmental measures—such as washing linens in hot water and treating all family members simultaneously—breaks the transmission cycle and prevents recurring infections.
