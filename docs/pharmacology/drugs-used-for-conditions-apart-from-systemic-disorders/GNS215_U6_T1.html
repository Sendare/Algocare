Antiparasitic Drugs

Introduction to Parasitic Infections and Antiparasitic Therapy

Parasitic infections remain a significant global health burden, particularly in tropical and subtropical regions like sub-Saharan Africa. Organisms that cause these infections range from microscopic unicellular protozoa to complex multicellular helminths and ectoparasites. Unlike bacteria or viruses, parasites possess eukaryotic cellular machinery that closely resembles human host cells, making the development of selective toxicity a major pharmacological challenge. Antiparasitic drugs are specifically designed to exploit biochemical or physiological differences between the parasite and the human host, such as unique metabolic pathways or structural components. Treatment success relies heavily on understanding the life cycle of the invading organism and the pharmacokinetic profile of the administered agent. Many antiparasitic medications target specific developmental stages—such as eradicating tissue-dwelling cysts or paralyzing luminal adult worms—while leaving other stages unaffected. Consequently, combination therapy or repeated dosing regimens are frequently required to prevent relapse and achieve complete cure.

Anthelmintics: Classification, Mechanism of Action, and Nursing Care

Anthelmintics are pharmacological agents utilized to treat infections caused by parasitic worms, which are broadly divided into nematodes (roundworms), cestodes (tapeworms), and trematodes (flukes). - **Benzimidazoles**: Drugs like **albendazole** and **mebendazole** inhibit microtubule synthesis in the parasite, disrupting glucose uptake and depleting glycogen stores until the worm dies. - **Tetrahydropyrimidines**: **Pyrantel pamoate** acts as a depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent, causing persistent contraction of the worm's musculature, leading to spastic paralysis and expulsion via peristalsis. - **Macrocyclic Lactones**: **Ivermectin** intensifies inhibitory neurotransmission by binding to glutamate-gated chloride channels, resulting in hyperpolarization, paralysis, and death of microfilariae. When administering anthelmintics, nurses must monitor for gastrointestinal distress as dying worms detach and cause localized inflammation or mechanical obstruction.

Antimalarial Agents: Types, Pharmacodynamics, and Patient Monitoring

Malaria, caused by protozoan parasites of the genus *Plasmodium*, requires a strategic pharmacological approach depending on the infecting species and disease severity. 1. **Artemisinin-Based Combination Therapies (ACTs)**: Currently the gold standard for uncomplicated *Plasmodium falciparum* malaria, combining a fast-acting artemisinin derivative with a longer-acting partner drug to clear parasites quickly and prevent resistance. 2. **Quinoline Derivatives**: **Chloroquine** and **hydroxychloroquine** accumulate within the parasite's digestive vacuole, preventing the detoxification of heme into non-toxic hemozoin. The resulting buildup of free heme is toxic to the parasite. 3. **Folate Antagonists**: **Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine** inhibits sequential steps in parasite folate synthesis, vital for nucleic acid replication. Critical nursing monitoring includes assessing for hemolysis in patients with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency, observing for neuropsychiatric disturbances with mefloquine, and performing baseline and periodic visual acuity checks during prolonged quinoline therapy.

Antiprotozoal Drugs: Treatment of Amebiasis, Giardiasis, and Trichomoniasis

Infections caused by protozoan flagellates and amoebas—such as *Entamoeba histolytica*, *Giardia lamblia*, and *Trichomonas vaginalis*—are commonly managed with nitroimidazole derivatives, notably **metronidazole** and **tinidazole**. These drugs enter the protozoan cell by passive diffusion. Once inside, microbial nitroreductase enzymes reduce the drug's nitro group, forming reactive intermediate compounds that bind to DNA, disrupt helical structure, and inhibit nucleic acid synthesis. This mechanism is largely specific to anaerobic and microaerophilic pathogens, sparing aerobic human host cells under normal conditions. A major clinical counseling point for patients taking nitroimidazoles is strict avoidance of alcohol during treatment and for at least three days afterward. The medication inhibits aldehyde dehydrogenase, provoking an unpleasant disulfiram-like reaction characterized by flushing, severe headache, nausea, and vomiting.

Ectoparasiticides: Management of Scabies and Pediculosis

Ectoparasiticides are topical agents formulated to eradicate infestations of the skin and hair, primarily scabies mites (*Sarcoptes scabiei*) and lice (*Pediculus humanus*, *Phthirus pubis*). - **Permethrin**: A synthetic pyrethroid that disrupts the voltage-gated sodium channels in the parasite's nerve cell membranes, causing delayed repolarization, paralysis, and death. It is considered a first-line treatment due to its low mammalian toxicity. - **Ivermectin (Topical or Oral)**: Binds to glutamate-gated chloride channels, causing paralysis in mites and lice. - **Benzyl Benzoate and Malathion**: Alternative scabicides and pediculicides used when first-line therapies fail, though malathion is flammable and requires cautious handling. Successful eradication requires treating close contacts simultaneously and properly washing bed linens and clothing in hot water to prevent reinfection.

General Adverse Effects and Drug Interactions of Antiparasitic Medications

Because many antiparasitic agents share metabolic pathways with human hepatic enzymes, polypharmacy can lead to significant clinical complications. Drugs such as certain antimalarials and antiprotozoals frequently inhibit or induce cytochrome P450 enzymes, altering the serum concentrations of co-administered medications like anticoagulants or anticonvulsants. Gastrointestinal upset—including nausea, abdominal cramping, and diarrhea—is a nearly universal adverse effect across this drug class, often stemming from both direct mucosal irritation and the physiological reaction to dying parasites. Additionally, central nervous system toxicities, ranging from dizziness and headache to rare seizures and peripheral neuropathies, require vigilant assessment during prolonged therapy. Routine laboratory testing of liver and renal function parameters is vital, especially when managing chronic or systemic parasite infestations.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education for Safe Administration

Safe administration of antiparasitic therapy extends far beyond the hospital bedside, requiring comprehensive patient education to ensure full course completion and prevent reinfection. Nurses must verify baseline weight, particularly in pediatric populations, as many antiparasitic dosages are calculated strictly on a milligram-per-kilogram basis to avoid toxicity or treatment failure. Patients should be advised regarding the timing of administration—some agents require high-fat meals to enhance systemic absorption, while others should be taken on an empty stomach. Clear instructions regarding sanitation, personal hygiene, and environmental laundering must be reinforced before discharge. Finally, emphasizing the importance of finishing the entire prescribed course, even after acute symptoms subside, remains a cornerstone of nursing advocacy in combating drug resistance.
