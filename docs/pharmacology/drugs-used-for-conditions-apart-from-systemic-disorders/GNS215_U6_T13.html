Antiretroviral Drugs

Introduction to HIV Pathophysiology and the Mechanisms of Antiretroviral Action

Human Immunodeficiency Virus targets the immune system, specifically destroying CD4+ T-lymphocytes which coordinate cellular immunity. Viral entry begins when the HIV envelope glycoprotein binds to the CD4 receptor and chemokine co-receptors on the host cell membrane. Once fused, the virus releases its single-stranded RNA core into the cytoplasm. Through reverse transcriptase, the viral RNA is transcribed into double-stranded DNA. This newly formed DNA enters the host cell nucleus and integrates into the human genome using the viral integrase enzyme. The host machinery then transcribes viral genes, producing new viral proteins that are cleaved by protease into functional units, assembling into a mature virion capable of infecting adjacent cells. Antiretroviral therapy disrupts specific steps of this replication cycle, reducing viral load below detectable limits, preserving CD4 counts, and halting disease progression to AIDS.

Classes of Antiretroviral Drugs and Their Mechanisms of Action

Modern antiretroviral therapy relies on combining multiple pharmacological classes to suppress viral replication at different stages of the life cycle. - **Nucleoside and Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs)**: Act as faulty building blocks that prematurely terminate the viral DNA chain during reverse transcription. - **Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs)**: Bind directly and non-competitively to the reverse transcriptase enzyme, altering its shape and halting activity. - **Protease Inhibitors (PIs)**: Block the protease enzyme, preventing the cleavage of viral polyproteins and halting the production of mature, infectious virions. - **Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs)**: Prevent the insertion of viral DNA into the host cellular genome by blocking the integrase enzyme. - **Entry and Fusion Inhibitors**: Block viral attachment or fusion with the host cell membrane, preventing cellular entry altogether.

National Antiretroviral Treatment Guidelines in Nigeria

The Federal Ministry of Health in Nigeria, through the National AIDS and STIs Control Programme, outlines standardized treatment algorithms aligned with global health recommendations. First-line therapy for treatment-naive adults and adolescents typically comprises an integrase strand transfer inhibitor-based regimen, specifically tenofovir disoproxil fumarate combined with lamivudine and dolutegravir (TDF/3TC/DTG). This fixed-dose combination offers high genetic barriers to resistance, rapid viral suppression, and a favorable safety profile. Alternative first-line regimens may substitute tenofovir with abacavir or use an NNRTI-based regimen like efavirenz when dolutegravir is contraindicated. Second-line regimens are deployed following confirmed treatment failure and generally switch the backbone NRTIs alongside a boosted protease inhibitor, such as darunavir or atazanavir boosted with ritonavir, combined with a new anchor drug.

Pharmacokinetics, Administration, and Adherence Strategies

Achieving continuous viral suppression requires strict adherence to daily dosing schedules, often demanding adherence rates above 95 percent to prevent drug resistance. Fixed-dose combinations reduce pill burden, simplifying administration routines for patients. Pharmacokinetic profiles vary by drug class, with some formulations requiring administration with food to enhance absorption, while others can be taken regardless of meals. Healthcare providers in resource-limited settings must assess potential barriers to adherence, such as transportation costs, stigma, social disclosure fears, and memory lapses. Practical interventions include peer support groups, pill boxes, mobile phone alarm reminders, and community pharmacy refill integration to maintain consistent drug concentrations in the bloodstream.

Adverse Drug Reactions, Toxicity, and Safety Monitoring

Antiretroviral agents possess distinct toxicity profiles that necessitate routine laboratory monitoring and clinical evaluation. Tenofovir can cause proximal renal tubular dysfunction and a decrease in bone mineral density, requiring baseline and periodic serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate assessments. Efavirenz is frequently associated with central nervous system disturbances, including vivid dreams, dizziness, and depressive symptoms, which typically subside after the first few weeks of therapy. Zidovudine frequently induces bone marrow suppression, leading to macrocytic anemia and neutropenia. Protease inhibitors and dolutegravir may contribute to metabolic alterations, including dyslipidemia and weight gain. Routine monitoring includes baseline complete blood counts, renal function panels, liver enzymes, and viral load tracking.

Management of Drug Interactions and Resistance

Antiretroviral medications, particularly non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors and boosted protease inhibitors, are potent inducers or inhibitors of hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, creating significant potential for drug-drug interactions. Co-administration with rifampicin for tuberculosis treatment poses a major clinical challenge because rifampicin induces metabolism of many antiretroviral drugs, requiring dose adjustments or alternative agents such as dolutegravir administered twice daily. Furthermore, acquired drug resistance emerges when viral replication occurs in the presence of sub-therapeutic drug levels caused by poor adherence, erratic absorption, or drug-drug interactions. Genotypic resistance testing helps guide therapy switches when virological failure is confirmed.

Special Considerations: Pregnancy, Pediatric Care, and Co-infections

Managing human immunodeficiency virus across diverse patient groups requires tailored therapeutic approaches. Pregnant women require immediate initiation of effective antiretroviral therapy to achieve rapid viral suppression, thereby preventing mother-to-child transmission during gestation, labor, and breastfeeding. Pediatric patients require weight-based dosing, palatable liquid or dispersible formulations, and careful caregiver counseling. Co-infections such as tuberculosis and viral hepatitis complicate treatment regimens due to overlapping toxicities and complex drug interactions. In tuberculosis co-infection, integrating anti-tubercular therapy with optimized antiretroviral regimens requires precise scheduling to balance efficacy and safety while preventing immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome.

Nursing Responsibilities in Patient Education and Care

Nurses play a central role in the multidisciplinary management of individuals receiving antiretroviral therapy. Responsibilities extend beyond medication administration to include comprehensive baseline assessments, counseling on potential side effects, and evaluating readiness for lifelong treatment. Nurses assess adherence barriers at every clinic visit, offering empathetic, non-judgmental support to foster a trusting therapeutic relationship. Education sessions cover the importance of never missing doses, recognizing severe adverse reactions that require immediate medical attention, and practicing safe health habits. Additionally, nurses coordinate laboratory monitoring schedules and facilitate linkage to community support networks to ensure holistic, patient-centered care.
