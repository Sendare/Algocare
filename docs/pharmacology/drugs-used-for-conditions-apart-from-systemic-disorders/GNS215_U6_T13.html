Antiretroviral Drugs

Introduction to HIV Pathophysiology and the Role of Antiretroviral Therapy

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is a retrovirus that primarily targets the human immune system, specifically infecting cells bearing CD4 receptors such as helper T-lymphocytes, monocytes, and macrophages. Once HIV enters a host cell, it uses its viral enzyme, reverse transcriptase, to convert its single-stranded RNA into double-stranded DNA. This newly formed viral DNA is then integrated into the host cell's genome via the integrase enzyme, effectively commandeering the cellular machinery to replicate viral particles. The continuous destruction of CD4+ T-cells over time leads to profound immune system deterioration, culminating in Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS), characterized by severe opportunistic infections and malignancies. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) does not cure HIV infection, but it plays a crucial and life-saving role by suppressing viral replication to undetectable levels. By interrupting different stages of the viral life cycle, ART halts the destruction of CD4+ T-cells, allows the immune system to partially recover, significantly reduces morbidity and mortality, and prevents the transmission of the virus to sexual partners and vertically from mother to child.

Classes of Antiretroviral Drugs and Their Mechanisms of Action

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) consists of various classes of medications designed to disrupt the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) life cycle at distinct stages. Nucleoside and Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs) act as decoy substrates that prematurely terminate the viral DNA chain synthesized by reverse transcriptase. Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs) bind directly and non-competitively to the reverse transcriptase enzyme, inducing a conformational change that halts its enzymatic activity. Protease Inhibitors (PIs) inhibit the viral protease enzyme, which is responsible for cleaving newly synthesized polyproteins into functional units, thereby preventing the formation of mature, infectious virions. Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs) block the integrase enzyme, stopping the insertion of viral DNA into the host cell's genetic material. Finally, Entry and Fusion Inhibitors block the attachment or fusion of HIV to the host cell membrane, preventing viral entry altogether. By combining medications from at least two different classes, healthcare providers create a synergistic effect that robustly suppresses viral replication and minimizes the emergence of drug-resistant viral strains.

First-Line and Second-Line Antiretroviral Regimen Guidelines in Nigeria

National guidelines in Nigeria, aligned with World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations, outline structured protocols for initiating and switching antiretroviral therapy (ART). The standard first-line regimen for treatment-naive adults and adolescents typically consists of a combination of two Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs) paired with an Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitor (INSTI) or a non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor. Specifically, a regimen containing Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF) plus Lamivudine (3TC) or Emtricitabine (FTC), combined with Dolutegravir (DTG), is widely preferred due to its high genetic barrier to resistance, rapid viral suppression, and favorable safety profile. Alternative first-line regimens may utilize Efavirenz (EFV) when Dolutegravir is contraindicated. Second-line regimens are deployed when a patient experiences confirmed treatment failure, which is indicated by persistent viral load rebound despite good adherence. Second-line options generally transition the patient to a boosted Protease Inhibitor (such as Atazanavir/ritonavir or Lopinavir/ritonavir) combined with a new optimized backbone of two NRTIs. Regular viral load monitoring and adherence assessments are vital before determining that a regimen has truly failed and deciding to switch therapies.

Pharmacokinetics, Administration, and Patient Adherence Strategies

Understanding the pharmacokinetics of antiretroviral drugs is essential for optimizing therapeutic outcomes and ensuring patients maintain appropriate drug concentrations in the bloodstream. Most oral antiretroviral agents are absorbed through the gastrointestinal tract and metabolized primarily by the hepatic cytochrome P450 enzyme system, making liver function a critical factor in drug dosing. Administration guidelines vary significantly by drug class; some medications must be taken with food to enhance absorption or minimize gastrointestinal upset, while others require administration on an empty stomach. Strict patient adherence—typically defined as taking at least 95% of prescribed doses on time—is the single most important determinant of treatment success. Poor adherence not only leads to viral rebound and disease progression but is the primary driver behind the development of drug-resistant viral strains. Nursing strategies to promote adherence include assessing readiness before initiation, identifying potential barriers such as financial constraints or stigma, establishing daily pill-taking routines, utilizing reminder alarms or pillboxes, and providing continuous psychological support and counseling during clinic visits.

Adverse Drug Reactions, Toxicity, and Safety Monitoring

Antiretroviral drugs are potent medications capable of causing a wide array of adverse drug reactions (ADRs) that range from mild, transient symptoms to severe, life-threatening toxicities. Common short-term side effects include nausea, vomiting, fatigue, headache, and sleep disturbances, particularly associated with agents like Efavirenz or Dolutegravir. Long-term toxicities can be more insidious; for instance, Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate is associated with renal impairment and bone mineral density loss, while certain Protease Inhibitors and NRTIs can contribute to metabolic complications such as dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and lipodystrophy. Hypersensitivity reactions, such as severe rash or Stevens-Johnson syndrome, can occur with medications like Abacavir or Nevirapine, necessitating baseline genetic testing (HLA-B*5701 for abacavir) when feasible. Safety monitoring requires routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood counts, liver function tests, serum creatinine, estimated creatinine clearance, fasting lipid profiles, blood glucose, and regular viral load and CD4 count tracking. Nurses play a crucial role in educating patients on recognizing early warning signs of toxicity and reporting them promptly to prevent severe complications.

Management of Antiretroviral Drug Interactions and Resistance

Managing drug interactions and preventing drug resistance are critical components of long-term HIV care. Many antiretroviral agents—especially Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors, Protease Inhibitors, and pharmacokinetic enhancers like ritonavir or cobicistat—are substrates, inducers, or inhibitors of hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes. This metabolic profile creates significant potential for adverse drug interactions with concurrent medications, including tuberculosis treatments, oral contraceptives, antimalarials, and herbal supplements such as St. John's Wort, which can dangerously lower antiretroviral concentrations. Drug resistance occurs when mutations emerge in the viral genome during ongoing viral replication in the presence of suboptimal drug levels. These mutations can render specific drugs or entire classes ineffective, severely narrowing future treatment options. Preventing resistance requires reinforcing flawless adherence, avoiding monotherapy or unboosted suboptimal regimens, and performing genotype resistance testing when treatment failure is suspected to construct a fully active salvage regimen.

Special Considerations: Pregnancy, Pediatric Patients, and Co-infections

Special populations require tailored antiretroviral management strategies to balance efficacy, safety, and unique physiological demands. In pregnancy, achieving rapid and sustained viral suppression is paramount to prevent mother-to-child transmission (MTCT) of HIV during gestation, labor, and breastfeeding; regimens containing Dolutegravir or Efavirenz are generally utilized alongside an NRTI backbone based on current national guidelines. Pediatric patients require weight- or surface-area-based dosing adjustments, and care must be taken to provide palatable liquid formulations or dispersible tablets that children can easily swallow. Co-infections present complex challenges, particularly the syndemic relationship between HIV and Tuberculosis (TB) or Viral Hepatitis (B and C). Managing HIV-TB co-infection requires careful scheduling of anti-TB medications (such as rifampicin) and ART, often necessitating regimen adjustments like doubling doses or switching to INSTI- or PI-based regimens because rifampicin is a potent enzyme inducer that lowers antiretroviral concentrations. Nursing care must encompass holistic assessments, close multidisciplinary coordination, and strict vigilance regarding overlapping toxicities in these vulnerable groups.

Nursing Responsibilities in Patient Education and Care

Nurses occupy a front-line position in the delivery of comprehensive HIV care, acting as educators, advocates, and clinical monitors. Patient education must be empowering, non-judgmental, and tailored to the patient's literacy level, addressing how antiretroviral medications work, the critical importance of lifelong adherence, and what to expect regarding potential side effects. Nurses should actively debunk myths and provide practical guidance on managing minor adverse effects, such as taking food with certain medications to reduce nausea. Beyond education, nursing responsibilities include conducting baseline and ongoing clinical assessments, screening for depression and substance use, monitoring laboratory results for signs of toxicity or treatment failure, and linking patients with community support groups to combat stigma and isolation. By fostering a trusting nurse-patient relationship, healthcare professionals can enhance retention in care, promote self-management, and significantly improve the overall quality of life for individuals living with HIV in Nigeria.
