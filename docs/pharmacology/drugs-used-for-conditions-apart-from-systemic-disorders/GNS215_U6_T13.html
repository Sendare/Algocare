Antiretroviral Drugs

Introduction to HIV Pathophysiology and the Role of Antiretroviral Therapy

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) primarily targets the immune system, specifically attacking CD4+ T-lymphocytes, which are vital white blood cells responsible for coordinating the body's defense against infections. Once the virus enters a CD4 cell, it uses an enzyme called reverse transcriptase to convert its viral RNA into DNA. This viral DNA then integrates into the host cell's genetic material, forcing the cell to produce more copies of the virus. Over time, this relentless destruction leads to a severe drop in CD4 counts, plunging the patient into the advanced, highly vulnerable stage known as Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS).

Antiretroviral therapy, commonly abbreviated as ART, does not cure HIV. Instead, it interrupts the life cycle of the virus at different stages, drastically reducing viral replication. By lowering the amount of virus in the blood—known as the viral load—to undetectable levels, ART halts further damage to the immune system. This allows the CD4 count to rebound, keeping opportunistic infections at bay and enabling people living with HIV to lead long, healthy lives.

Classes of Antiretroviral Drugs and Their Mechanisms of Action

Antiretroviral drugs are grouped into different classes based on the specific step of the viral life cycle they block. Using a combination of drugs from different classes, known as combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), prevents the virus from easily developing resistance.

  • Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs): These act as faulty building blocks, tricking the viral reverse transcriptase enzyme so it cannot build viral DNA.
  • Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs): These bind directly to the reverse transcriptase enzyme, physically blocking its ability to convert RNA into DNA.
  • Protease Inhibitors (PIs): These block the protease enzyme, preventing newly made viral pieces from being cut into mature, infectious viral particles.
  • Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (Integrase Inhibitors): These stop the integrase enzyme from inserting the viral DNA into the host cell's own genetic material.
  • Entry and Fusion Inhibitors: These prevent HIV from attaching to or entering the surface of the healthy human host cell in the first place.

First-Line and Second-Line Antiretroviral Regimen Guidelines

National treatment guidelines dictate standard drug combinations to ensure optimal outcomes while preserving future treatment options. First-line regimens are the preferred initial treatment for newly diagnosed adults and adolescents because they offer high efficacy, a high genetic barrier to resistance, and minimal side effects.

A typical preferred first-line regimen in many resource-limited settings consists of an Integrase Inhibitor, most commonly Dolutegravir, combined with two NRTIs, such as Tenofovir and Lamivudine. This combination is usually provided as a convenient single-pill fixed-dose combination taken once daily.

When a first-line regimen fails—usually indicated by a persistently high viral load despite good adherence—the patient is switched to a second-line regimen. A failing non-integrase first-line regimen is typically switched to a boosted protease inhibitor combined with a fresh pair of NRTIs. Regular viral load monitoring is the most reliable tool for catching treatment failure early before clinical illness reappears.

Pharmacokinetics, Administration, and Patient Adherence Strategies

Antiretroviral drugs require strict consistency in timing and dosing to maintain therapeutic blood concentrations. If drug levels drop too low in the bloodstream, the virus replicates actively and can easily mutate, rendering the drug regimen ineffective.

Adherence—meaning taking the exact medications correctly, on time, every single day—must be at least 95% for therapy to succeed. Nurses play a crucial role in supporting adherence by assessing individual lifestyle barriers, linking patients to community adherence clubs, and encouraging the use of alarm clocks or pillboxes.

Adverse Drug Reactions, Toxicity, and Safety Monitoring

Like all powerful pharmaceuticals, antiretroviral drugs come with potential side effects and toxicities. Nurses must distinguish between mild initial side effects that resolve on their own and severe toxicities that require an immediate drug substitution.

  • Efavirenz: Can cause vivid dreams, dizziness, and central nervous system grogginess, usually settling after a few weeks.
  • Tenofovir: Can affect renal function and bone density over long-term use, requiring routine monitoring of serum creatinine and urine protein.
  • Zidovudine: Frequently associated with bone marrow suppression, leading to anemia and severe fatigue.

Routine laboratory safety monitoring, including full blood counts, liver function tests, and kidney markers, is mandatory to catch organ toxicity before it becomes irreversible.

Management of Antiretroviral Drug Interactions and Resistance

Drug-drug interactions represent a major clinical challenge in HIV care, especially because many patients take concurrent treatments for co-infections like tuberculosis or malaria. Certain antiretroviral classes, particularly protease inhibitors and NNRTIs, interact heavily with liver enzymes, altering the concentration of other medications.

For example, rifampicin, a staple tuberculosis drug, speeds up the breakdown of several antiretrovirals, potentially dropping their levels too low. Nurses must review all medications—including herbal remedies and over-the-counter supplements—to prevent dangerous interaction risks.

When resistant viral strains emerge, treatment options narrow. Acquired drug resistance develops when the virus mutates in the presence of inadequate drug levels. Managing resistance requires specialized resistance testing and expert consultation to build a salvage regimen utilizing brand-new drug classes.

Special Populations: Pregnancy, Pediatrics, and Co-infections

Special patient populations require tailored medication choices to ensure safety for both the mother, the developing fetus, and children. Pregnant women living with HIV are initiated on safe, effective regimens—such as Dolutegravir-based combinations—to prevent mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) during pregnancy, labor, and breastfeeding.

Pediatric patients require weight- or age-appropriate liquid formulations or dispersible tablets because standard adult pills cannot be swallowed safely. Furthermore, co-infections like hepatitis B and tuberculosis complicate management. When HIV and hepatitis B co-exist, treatment regimens must include drugs active against both viruses, such as Tenofovir and Lamivudine, to prevent severe liver damage if ART is abruptly stopped.

Nursing Responsibilities in Patient Education and Care

Nurses are the frontline educators and emotional pillars for individuals starting antiretroviral therapy. Comprehensive patient education demystifies the disease, dispels cultural myths, and prepares the patient for lifelong adherence. Nurses must explain how the drugs work in simple language, what common side effects to expect, and when to report danger signs.

Beyond dispensing pills, nursing care involves creating a judgment-free, confidential environment. Stigma remains a massive barrier keeping people away from clinics; therefore, protecting patient privacy is both an ethical duty and a practical clinical necessity. By fostering trust and offering continuous encouragement, nurses empower patients to take charge of their health journey.
