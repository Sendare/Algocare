Drugs Used in Conditions of Special Senses

Introduction to Pharmacology of Special Senses

The pharmacology of the special senses focuses primarily on the eye and the ear. Because these organs possess unique anatomical barriers like the cornea and the blood-labyrinth barrier, systemic medications often fail to reach therapeutic levels inside them. Therefore, nurses must understand topical delivery systems. Eye drops and ear drops require precise techniques to prevent rapid drainage through the nasolacrimal duct or the ear canal, which could otherwise lead to unwanted systemic absorption and side effects.

Pharmacotherapy of Glaucoma

Glaucoma is characterized by elevated intraocular pressure that damages the optic nerve. Pharmacotherapy aims to either reduce aqueous humor production or increase its outflow.

  • Prostaglandin analogues: Examples include latanoprost; they increase uveoscleral outflow and are usually taken once daily.
  • Beta-blockers: Timolol reduces the production of aqueous humor in the ciliary body.
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors: Dorzolamide decreases fluid secretion inside the eye.
  • Cholinergic agonists: Pilocarpine constricts the pupil, pulling the iris away from the drainage angle to improve outflow.

A common misconception is that patients will feel eye pain when pressure rises; chronic open-angle glaucoma is often painless until significant vision loss occurs.

Mydriatics, Cycloplegics, and Miotics

These agents manipulate the size of the pupil and the focusing muscles of the eye for diagnostic exams or specific surgeries.

  • Mydriatics: Drugs like phenylephrine stimulate the radial muscle of the iris to cause pupil dilation.
  • Cycloplegics: Agents like atropine block acetylcholine receptors in the ciliary muscle, paralyzing accommodation so the eye cannot focus.
  • Miotics: Drugs like pilocarpine constrict the pupil.

Cycloplegics and mydriatics blur vision and cause photophobia. Nurses must warn patients not to drive or attempt reading until the medication wears off completely.

Anti-infective and Anti-inflammatory Ophthalmic Agents

Infections and inflammation of the eye require prompt antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory therapy to preserve vision. Antibacterial drops or ointments treat bacterial conjunctivitis, while antiviral agents manage conditions like herpes simplex keratitis. Corticosteroid drops control severe inflammation following trauma or surgery. However, nurses must remember that long-term use of ocular corticosteroids can trigger secondary glaucoma, mask underlying fungal infections, or cause corneal thinning.

Local Anesthetics and Diagnostic Agents

Diagnostic procedures and minor surgical interventions on the eye rely heavily on local anesthetics and staining agents. Topical anesthetics like tetracaine temporarily numb the corneal surface by blocking nerve impulses. Fluorescein sodium is a diagnostic dye used alongside a cobalt blue light to highlight corneal abrasions, foreign bodies, or poorly fitting contact lenses. Nurses must instruct patients never to rub their numb eyes, as the cornea can easily be scratched without the person feeling the injury.

Pharmacological Management of Otic Disorders

Treating ear disorders involves addressing infections, inflammation, and impacted cerumen. Otic drops often combine antibiotics like ciprofloxacin with corticosteroids to reduce inflammation and pain in conditions like otitis externa. For excessive earwax, cerumenolytics such as carbamide peroxide soften the wax for easy removal. Nurses must ensure ear drops are warmed to room temperature before instilling them; cold drops can stimulate the vestibular system and cause severe vertigo, nausea, and dizziness.

Administration Techniques and Nursing Implications

Proper technique ensures medication reaches the target tissue while minimizing systemic toxicity. For eye drops, pull down the lower lid to create a conjunctival sac, instill the drop, and apply gentle pressure to the nasolacrimal duct for one minute to prevent systemic absorption. For ear drops in adults, gently pull the pinna up and back; for children under three, pull it down and back. Always check the expiration date and avoid touching the tip of the dropper to any surface to prevent contamination.

Patient Education and Safety Considerations

Patient education is crucial for adherence, especially in chronic conditions like glaucoma where treatment is lifelong. Nurses must teach patients how to administer their own drops correctly and emphasize that missing doses can lead to irreversible vision loss. In environments like Nigeria, counterfeit drugs and improper storage in high temperatures pose significant risks. Nurses must advise patients to purchase medications from reputable pharmacies and store drops away from direct heat to maintain therapeutic efficacy.
