Antihaemorrhagic Agents

Introduction to Haemostasis and Antihaemorrhagic Agents

Haemostasis is the physiological process that halts bleeding following vascular injury, protecting the body from catastrophic blood loss. This intricate cascade unfolds in three rapid phases: vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and the coagulation cascade, which ultimately converts soluble fibrinogen into a stable, insoluble fibrin mesh. When this natural clotting mechanism fails—whether due to hereditary disorders like haemophilia, trauma, or surgical interventions—medical intervention becomes essential. Antihaemorrhagic agents, often referred to as haemostatic agents, are pharmacological substances designed to promote blood coagulation or prevent the breakdown of existing blood clots. In Nigerian clinical settings, where timely surgical intervention and management of postpartum haemorrhage or traumatic injuries are critical, understanding these drugs is a core competency for safe practice. They act at various points along the clotting pathway to restore haemostatic balance and preserve hemodynamic stability.

Classification and Mechanisms of Action

Pharmacological agents that control bleeding are broadly categorized based on where and how they exert their effects. Systemic agents circulate through the bloodstream to influence the overall clotting factors, while topical agents are applied directly to a bleeding site on tissue surfaces. - **Systemic Haemostatics**: Drugs such as tranexamic acid inhibit fibrinolysis by blocking lysine binding sites on plasminogen, thereby preventing the premature dissolution of blood clots. - **Replacement Factors**: Concentrates of specific clotting factors, such as Factor VIII or IX, directly supply missing proteins required to sustain the intrinsic coagulation pathway. - **Topical Adherents**: Agents like thrombin or gelatin sponges accelerate local clot formation independently of the systemic circulation by creating a mechanical matrix or directly converting fibrinogen. - **Vitamin K Analogues**: Phytomenadione promotes the hepatic synthesis of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors, specifically factors II, VII, IX, and X.

Systemic Haemostatic Agents

Systemic antihaemorrhagic drugs are administered orally, intravenously, or intramuscularly to manage widespread or internal bleeding disorders. Aminocaproic acid and tranexamic acid are potent antifibrinolytics frequently utilized during major surgeries, dental extractions in patients with bleeding disorders, and to curb heavy menstrual bleeding or postpartum haemorrhage. Desmopressin acetate, a synthetic analogue of vasopressin, is another valuable systemic agent. It stimulates the release of stored von Willebrand factor and Factor VIII from endothelial cells, offering a non-blood-product option for patients with mild haemophilia A or von Willebrand disease. Administration requires careful fluid balance monitoring, as systemic agents can occasionally precipitate unwanted microvascular thrombosis if dosage guidelines are exceeded.

Topical and Local Haemostatic Agents

When surgical bleeding occurs in localized areas that are difficult to reach with sutures, topical haemostatic agents provide rapid, localized control. These agents are categorized into mechanical agents, active agents, and flowable matrices. - **Mechanical Agents**: Microfibrillar collagen, oxidized regenerated cellulose, and gelatin sponges absorb blood and provide a physical matrix that promotes platelet adhesion and activation. - **Active Agents**: Topical thrombin preparations interact directly with endogenous fibrinogen to rapidly form a stable fibrin clot at the bleeding site. - **Fibrin Sealants**: Combinations of human thrombin and fibrinogen mimic the final step of the natural coagulation cascade, creating an adhesive biological glue. Care must be taken to remove excess non-absorbable topical materials from neural or spinal tissues where swelling could cause dangerous compression.

Clinical Indications in Nursing Practice

Antihaemorrhagic agents are indicated across diverse medical and surgical specialties. In obstetrics, uterotonics are paired with antifibrinolytics like tranexamic acid to manage postpartum haemorrhage, a leading cause of maternal mortality in Nigeria. Surgical departments rely on these medications to reduce intraoperative blood loss during orthopaedic procedures, cardiac surgeries, and extensive resections. Furthermore, these agents manage acute bleeding episodes in patients with inherited coagulopathies, such as von Willebrand disease or haemophilia. Emergency departments administer them following severe traumatic injuries to mitigate the effects of trauma-induced coagulopathy. Recognizing the specific clinical indication guides the nurse in anticipating the correct dosage, route, and expected therapeutic response.

Contraindications and Precautions

While antihaemorrhagic agents save lives, their inappropriate administration can provoke dangerous thromboembolic complications. They are generally contraindicated in patients with active intravascular clotting, severe renal impairment (for renally excreted agents like tranexamic acid), or known hypersensitivity to the specific drug components. Special precautions are warranted when administering systemic antifibrinolytics to individuals with a history of thromboembolic events, such as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or recent myocardial infarction. In patients with upper urinary tract bleeding, systemic antifibrinolytics can cause ureteral obstruction by forming insoluble clots within the renal pelvis. Thorough history-taking regarding prior clotting disorders is a mandatory nursing safeguard prior to therapy initiation.

Adverse Effects and Complications

The therapeutic benefit of antihaemorrhagic agents must be carefully weighed against their potential adverse effect profile. Systemic agents frequently cause gastrointestinal disturbances, including nausea, vomiting, and diarrhoea, particularly when administered orally. More serious systemic complications stem from excessive coagulation, manifesting as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or cerebral infarction. Rapid intravenous administration of certain agents, such as tranexamic acid, can provoke transient hypotension, dizziness, and visual disturbances. Topical agents, although localized, can occasionally trigger hypersensitivity reactions or foreign body granuloma formation if residual material is left behind in closed surgical spaces. Continuous vigilance for signs of abnormal clotting is paramount throughout the treatment course.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care involves rigorous baseline assessments, precise administration, and ongoing patient monitoring. Before administering any antihaemorrhagic therapy, the nurse reviews the patient's coagulation profile, baseline vital signs, and medication history to identify potential drug interactions or contraindications. During and after administration, the nurse tracks vital signs closely, noting any sudden changes in blood pressure or pulse that might signal cardiovascular strain. Observation for signs of bleeding cessation or, conversely, early indicators of thrombosis—such as unilateral calf pain, swelling, chest pain, or dyspnoea—is critical. Accurate input and output charting is maintained, especially in surgical or obstetric patients receiving combination therapies, ensuring safe and effective recovery.
