Antihaemorrhagic Agents

Introduction to Haemostasis and Antihaemorrhagic Agents

Haemostasis is the body's natural physiological process designed to stop bleeding following a vascular injury. This intricate protective mechanism occurs in three rapid, overlapping phases: vascular spasm to reduce blood flow, platelet plug formation through cellular adhesion and aggregation, and the coagulation cascade. The coagulation cascade culminates in the conversion of soluble fibrinogen into a stable, insoluble fibrin meshwork that firmly seals the damaged vessel wall.

When this delicate balance fails or is overwhelmed by trauma, surgery, or underlying bleeding disorders, pharmacological intervention becomes essential. Antihaemorrhagic agents, also widely referred to as hemostatic drugs, are pharmacological substances specifically administered to promote rapid blood clotting and effectively control abnormal or excessive haemorrhage. Understanding how these agents support the body's native clotting pathways enables nurses to anticipate clinical needs and manage bleeding emergencies safely.

Classification and Mechanisms of Action

Antihaemorrhagic drugs are broadly categorized based on their site of action and how they influence the clotting process. Systemic agents circulate through the bloodstream to support or replace clotting factors, whereas topical agents are applied directly to a bleeding surface to form a mechanical or chemical matrix.

  • Systemic antifibrinolytics: Drugs like tranexamic acid block the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, thereby preventing the premature breakdown of established blood clots.
  • Coagulation factor concentrates: Purified human factors, such as Factor VIII or IX, directly replace missing proteins in patients with inherited bleeding disorders like hemophilia.
  • Topical hemostatics: Agents such as thrombin or gelatin sponges accelerate clot formation right at the wound site independent of the systemic circulation.
  • Vitamin K: Acts as an essential cofactor enabling the liver to synthesize functional clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X.

Systemic Haemostatic Agents and Clinical Uses

Systemic antihaemorrhagic agents are administered orally, intravenously, or subcutaneously to manage generalized bleeding or prepare patients for high-risk surgical procedures. Desmopressin (DDAVP) is frequently used in mild hemophilia A and von Willebrand disease because it stimulates the release of stored endothelial Factor VIII and von Willebrand factor into the circulation.

Another vital systemic group includes replacement therapies used during severe haemorrhage or massive transfusion protocols. Fresh frozen plasma and specific prothrombin complex concentrates supply deficient factors immediately. Furthermore, systemic agents are crucial in reversing the effects of accidental anticoagulant overdoses, such as giving Vitamin K to counteract excessive warfarin toxicity.

Topical and Local Haemostatic Agents

When bleeding is localized to an accessible surgical site, wound, or mucosal surface, topical haemostatic agents provide rapid control without needing systemic absorption. These agents create a physical barrier or activate local clotting components instantly.

  • Absorbable gelatin sponges: Provide a porous physical matrix that absorbs blood and encourages platelet aggregation at the surgical bed.
  • Fibrin sealants: Combine human fibrinogen and thrombin right on the wound to mimic the final physiological step of the clotting cascade.
  • Oxidized regenerated cellulose: Forms a gelatinous mass when soaked with blood, lowering local pH and facilitating rapid clot formation.
  • Topical thrombin: Applied directly to oozing capillaries where standard pressure or ligatures are ineffective.

Contraindications and Precautions

Administering antihaemorrhagic agents requires careful patient evaluation because promoting clotting in the wrong clinical scenario can be life-threatening. The most critical contraindication across almost all pro-clotting medications is the presence of active thromboembolic disease, such as deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or recent acute myocardial infarction.

If a patient has an existing blood clot or is prone to unwanted clotting, giving an antihaemorrhagic agent can trigger widespread vessel occlusion, leading to stroke or tissue death. Furthermore, severe hypersensitivity reactions to animal-derived or human plasma products require extreme caution or complete avoidance. Nurses must review the patient's complete medical and medication history before administering any clotting promoter.

Adverse Effects and Complications

While antihaemorrhagic drugs are lifesaving in hemorrhage, they carry significant potential adverse effects that demand vigilant nursing surveillance. The most severe complication is thromboembolism, where excessive clot formation blocks normal arterial or venous blood flow. Patients may develop chest pain, shortness of breath, sudden neurological deficits, or painful, swollen limbs.

Systemic administration of plasma-derived products or factor concentrates also carries a risk of allergic or hypersensitivity reactions, ranging from mild hives to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Additionally, rapid intravenous administration of certain agents can provoke sudden hypotension or flushing. Recognizing these complications early allows for prompt medical intervention and prevents permanent patient harm.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care involves baseline assessments, rigorous intra-treatment monitoring, and patient education. Before administration, the nurse must check vital signs, review coagulation laboratory profiles such as PT, INR, and aPTT, and confirm the patient's allergy history.

During and after therapy, continuous assessment is vital. The nurse monitors the surgical site or wound for ongoing oozing, checks peripheral pulses, and watches closely for early signs of abnormal clotting or allergic reactions. Accurate documentation of fluid balance, blood loss, and drug administration times ensures safe continuity of care across multidisciplinary healthcare teams.
