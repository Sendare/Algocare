Drugs for Allergic Reactions

Pathophysiology of Allergic Reactions and Pharmacological Targets

An allergic reaction represents an inappropriate, exaggerated immune response to a normally harmless substance known as an allergen. When a susceptible individual first encounters an allergen, sensitization occurs. B-lymphocytes produce large quantities of Immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies, which bind tightly to the surface of mast cells and basophils residing in mucosal and connective tissues.

Upon re-exposure, the allergen cross-links these surface-bound IgE molecules, triggering rapid cellular activation. The mast cell undergoes degranulation, releasing pre-formed chemical mediators directly into the surrounding tissue and circulation. The primary mediator, histamine, immediately causes capillary vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and smooth muscle contraction. Secondary mediators such as leukotrienes and prostaglandins sustain the inflammatory cascade, recruiting additional inflammatory cells and prolonging mucosal edema and bronchospasm.

Pharmacotherapy for allergic disorders aims to interrupt this cascade at various points. Interventions either block the released mediators from binding to target receptors, stabilize the cell membranes to prevent degranulation, or suppress the underlying inflammatory response altogether.

Antihistamines: Classification and Mechanism of Action

Antihistamines function as competitive antagonists at histamine H1-receptors, preventing histamine from binding and exerting its typical effects on blood vessels and sensory nerve endings. They are broadly divided into two major generations based on chemical properties and central nervous system penetration.

  • First-generation antihistamines: Agents such as chlorpheniramine and promethazine readily cross the blood-brain barrier. This central nervous system activity produces significant sedation, drowsiness, and pronounced anticholinergic side effects including dry mouth, blurred vision, and urinary retention.
  • Second-generation antihistamines: Agents like cetirizine, loratadine, and fexofenadine are peripherally selective. They do not readily cross the blood-brain barrier, providing effective symptom relief for allergic rhinitis and urticaria without causing marked sedation.

Corticosteroids in Allergy Management

Corticosteroids are potent anti-inflammatory agents utilized when acute allergic reactions involve significant mucosal edema, delayed-phase responses, or chronic conditions like severe asthma and allergic rhinitis. Rather than blocking a single mediator, corticosteroids modify transcription in target cells, upregulating anti-inflammatory proteins while suppressing the expression of multiple inflammatory cytokines, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes.

Systemic administration—such as oral prednisolone or intravenous hydrocortisone—is required for severe systemic reactions, contact dermatitis, or asthma exacerbations. Topical and intranasal preparations, including fluticasone and mometasone, deliver high local concentrations to affected tissues while minimizing systemic absorption and associated adverse effects. Clinical improvement with corticosteroids is often delayed compared to antihistamines, as altering gene expression and protein synthesis requires hours to manifest fully.

Epinephrine and Emergency Treatment of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening, multi-system systemic allergic reaction characterized by rapid airway compromise, hypotension, and circulatory collapse. Epinephrine is the absolute first-line, life-saving treatment and must be administered immediately at the onset of severe systemic symptoms without waiting for diagnostic confirmation.

Epinephrine acts as a non-selective agonist across alpha-1, beta-1, and beta-2 adrenergic receptors:

  • Alpha-1 activation: Causes intense peripheral vasoconstriction, which reverses hypotension, restores blood pressure, and reduces mucosal edema in the upper airway.
  • Beta-1 activation: Increases inotropy and chronotropy, improving cardiac output.
  • Beta-2 activation: Relaxes bronchial smooth muscle, reversing bronchospasm and wheezing while inhibiting further inflammatory mediator release from mast cells.

The preferred route for emergency administration is intramuscular injection into the anterolateral aspect of the middle third of the thigh, ensuring rapid systemic absorption compared to subcutaneous delivery.

Mast Cell Stabilizers and Leukotriene Modulators

When daily control of chronic allergic conditions like asthma or allergic conjunctivitis is required, adjunctive medications such as mast cell stabilizers and leukotriene receptor antagonists are often prescribed.

  • Mast cell stabilizers: Agents such as sodium cromoglicate stabilize the cell membrane of mast cells, preventing them from degranulating and releasing histamine and other mediators when exposed to an allergen. They are prophylactic agents requiring regular, scheduled dosing rather than acute rescue use.
  • Leukotriene receptor antagonists: Medications like montelukast block cysteinyl leukotriene receptors in the airways. By inhibiting these potent bronchoconstrictors, they reduce airway edema and smooth muscle constriction, offering effective oral maintenance therapy for chronic asthma and allergic rhinitis.

Nursing Assessments and Administration Considerations

Safe administration of anti-allergic pharmacotherapy demands rigorous pre- and post-administration nursing assessments. Nurses must evaluate baseline respiratory status, blood pressure, heart rate, and the extent of cutaneous involvement before administering any allergy medication.

When managing emergency epinephrine, clinicians must verify dosage accuracy, inspect the solution for discoloration or particulates, and monitor the patient continuously for tachycardia, palpitations, and tremors following injection. For patients receiving systemic corticosteroids, nurses should assess blood glucose levels, particularly in individuals with diabetes, as these agents frequently induce hyperglycemia. Monitoring for signs of secondary infection and gastrointestinal irritation is also critical during prolonged courses of anti-inflammatory therapy. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, ensuring proper patient positioning during anaphylaxis—such as lying flat with legs elevated unless respiratory distress is severe—remains a vital supportive nursing intervention.

Patient Education and Prevention of Allergic Triggers

Pharmacotherapy alone is insufficient for long-term allergy management; empowering patients with practical self-management skills is essential. Education begins with identifying and avoiding known triggers, whether through environmental controls, dietary restrictions, or occupational modifications.

Patients prescribed self-injectable epinephrine devices must receive hands-on instruction using trainer devices. They must learn to check expiration dates regularly, protect the medication from extreme heat or direct sunlight, and understand that any symptom recurrence following initial recovery warrants immediate emergency medical evaluation, as biphasic reactions can occur hours later. Emphasizing strict adherence to maintenance therapies, such as nasal sprays or oral antihistamines, ensures sustained symptom control and prevents escalating hypersensitivity episodes.
