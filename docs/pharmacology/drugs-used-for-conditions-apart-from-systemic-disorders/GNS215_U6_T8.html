Drugs for Allergic Reactions

Introduction to Allergic Reactions and Pharmacology

An allergy is an overreaction of the immune system to a substance that is normally harmless, such as pollen, dust, or certain foods. When a person is exposed to an allergen for the first time, their body produces IgE antibodies. These antibodies attach to mast cells and basophils, priming the immune system. During a second exposure, the allergen binds to these IgE antibodies, causing the cells to burst open and release inflammatory chemicals like histamine. This release triggers classic allergic signs such as itching, swelling, runny nose, and hives. Pharmacology intervenes at various points in this immune cascade to block these chemicals, reduce inflammation, or reverse life-threatening symptoms.

Antihistamines: Classification and Mechanism of Action

Antihistamines work by competing with histamine for H1-receptor sites on tissues, preventing histamine from binding and causing its typical effects. They are broadly split into two generations:

  • First-generation antihistamines (e.g., diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine): These cross the blood-brain barrier easily, which makes them very effective for allergies but causes significant drowsiness and dry mouth.
  • Second-generation antihistamines (e.g., cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine): These do not cross the blood-brain barrier as easily, offering effective relief without heavy sedation.

First-generation drugs are often used for acute allergic reactions or insomnia due to their sedative side effect, while second-generation agents are preferred for daily daytime management of allergic rhinitis.

Corticosteroids in Allergy Management

Corticosteroids are powerful anti-inflammatory agents used when antihistamines alone cannot control an allergic response. They suppress the immune response by inhibiting multiple inflammatory cytokines, reducing swelling, redness, and mucus production.

  • Intranasal sprays (e.g., fluticasone): The gold standard for controlling chronic allergic rhinitis with minimal systemic absorption.
  • Oral or intravenous systemic steroids (e.g., prednisolone, hydrocortisone): Used for severe allergic conditions such as asthma exacerbations, contact dermatitis, or systemic allergic flare-ups.

A common clinical observation is that corticosteroids do not provide instant relief; they can take several hours to days to show maximum effectiveness because they alter protein synthesis and gene expression inside immune cells.

Epinephrine and Emergency Treatment of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening systemic allergic reaction characterized by airway swelling, severe drop in blood pressure, and widespread hives. Epinephrine is the absolute first-line, life-saving drug for anaphylaxis and has no absolute contraindications in an emergency.

  • Alpha-1 adrenergic effects: Constricts blood vessels, which quickly raises blood pressure and reduces swelling in the throat and face.
  • Beta-2 adrenergic effects: Relaxes smooth muscle around the airways, opening up bronchial tubes to restore breathing.
  • Beta-1 adrenergic effects: Increases heart rate and contraction strength to support circulation.

Epinephrine is given via intramuscular injection into the anterolateral thigh for fastest absorption. A second dose may be required if symptoms do not improve or return within five to fifteen minutes.

Mast Cell Stabilizers and Leukotriene Modulators

When antihistamines and steroids are not enough for long-term control of conditions like asthma and chronic allergic conjunctivitis, other specialized medications are utilized.

  • Mast cell stabilizers (e.g., cromolyn sodium): Prevent mast cells from rupturing and releasing histamine in the first place. They must be taken preventatively before exposure to an allergen because they cannot stop histamine once it is already released.
  • Leukotriene receptor antagonists (e.g., montelukast): Block leukotrienes, which are potent inflammatory chemicals that cause airway constriction and mucus production, particularly in allergic asthma.

Nursing Assessments and Administration Considerations

Safe medication administration for allergic reactions requires careful baseline assessments and ongoing monitoring. Nurses must check respiratory status, lung sounds, blood pressure, and heart rate before and after giving allergy drugs, especially when administering systemic steroids or emergency epinephrine.

When managing patients on antihistamines, nurses should monitor for excessive sedation, dizziness, and urinary retention, particularly in older adults. For patients prescribed nasal sprays, proper technique instruction prevents local irritation and nosebleeds. Nurses should always verify previous allergy history and ensure emergency resuscitation equipment is nearby when high-risk patients are exposed to known allergens during testing or treatment.

Patient Education and Prevention of Allergic Triggers

Pharmacology alone cannot protect a patient from severe allergic episodes; lifestyle modifications and trigger avoidance are essential. Nurses must teach patients how to identify environmental triggers such as specific foods, pollens, animal danders, and insect venoms.

Patients with severe allergies must carry an epinephrine auto-injector at all times and know how to demonstrate its use to family members. Education should include checking expiration dates regularly, storing the device at room temperature away from direct sunlight, and seeking immediate emergency medical care in a hospital even if symptoms resolve after using epinephrine, due to the risk of a delayed secondary reaction.
