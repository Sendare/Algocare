Topical Applications

Introduction to Topical Drug Administration

Topical drug administration involves applying medications directly to body surfaces, such as the skin, mucous membranes, eyes, ears, and nose. This route is primarily chosen for localized effects, minimizing systemic exposure and reducing widespread side effects. However, certain topical agents are specifically formulated to deliver systemic therapy, such as transdermal patches used for pain management or hormone replacement. Nursing responsibilities include understanding the pharmacokinetics of local versus systemic absorption, selecting appropriate application sites, and ensuring patient comfort.

Skin Barrier Function and Percutaneous Absorption

The stratum corneum serves as the outermost layer of the epidermis, acting as the primary barrier against chemical and microbial invasion. Drug molecules must cross this lipid-rich matrix to enter viable tissue and systemic circulation. Several physiological factors influence this absorption rate:

  • Hydration status: Moist skin absorbs medications much faster than dry or thickened skin.
  • Skin integrity: Open wounds, abrasions, or inflammatory conditions drastically increase drug penetration.
  • Anatomic site: Areas with thinner skin, like the face or scrotum, absorb drugs rapidly compared to the thick soles of the feet.

Types of Topical Formulations and Vehicles

Topical medications combine an active pharmaceutical ingredient with a vehicle, which dictates how the drug spreads, adheres, and penetrates the skin.

Choosing the correct vehicle ensures optimal drug delivery and prevents skin maceration.

Specialized Mucosal Applications: Ophthalmic, Otic, and Nasal

Administering drugs to the eyes, ears, and nose requires distinct techniques due to sensitive mucosal membranes and unique anatomical structures.

  • Ophthalmic: Medications must be sterile drops, ointments, or inserts placed into the lower conjunctival sac. Avoid touching the dropper to the eye to prevent contamination.
  • Otic: Ear drops require straightening the ear canal—pulling the pinna upward and back for adults, or downward and back for young children.
  • Nasal: Drops or sprays target nasal mucosa for local congestion relief or systemic absorption. Patients should blow their nose gently before administration.

Safe Administration Techniques and Nursing Procedures

Safe topical administration prevents cross-contamination, ensures correct dosage delivery, and protects the healthcare provider. Nurses must perform thorough hand hygiene and wear gloves, especially when applying potent drugs like topical nitroglycerin or hormonal creams to avoid accidental occupational absorption. The application site must be cleansed gently to remove prior residue, crusts, or exudate before applying a fresh layer. Medications should be applied in smooth, even strokes following the direction of hair growth to prevent follicular irritation and folliculitis.

Local and Systemic Adverse Effects

While generally safer than oral or intravenous routes, topical medications can still trigger adverse reactions. Local reactions include contact dermatitis, skin irritation, burning, stinging, and allergic sensitization to active ingredients or preservatives. Systemic toxicity may occur if potent drugs are applied over large surface areas, broken skin, or under occlusive dressings that enhance absorption. For example, excessive use of topical corticosteroids can lead to skin atrophy, striae, and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis suppression.
