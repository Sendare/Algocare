Vaccines

Introduction to Vaccines and Immunology Basics

Vaccines are critical biological preparations designed to establish active acquired immunity to particular infectious diseases. To understand how vaccines work, nursing students must first grasp the basics of immunology, specifically the roles of antigens, antibodies, and lymphocytes. When a pathogen invades the human body, its surface carries specific molecules called antigens. The immune system identifies these foreign antigens as non-self, triggering a defense mechanism. B-lymphocytes produce specialized proteins known as antibodies that bind to and neutralize the invading pathogens, while T-lymphocytes help destroy infected cells and regulate the overall immune response. Vaccines introduce weakened, killed, or fragmented pieces of these pathogens—or genetic blueprints like mRNA—into the body. This mimics a natural infection without causing the actual disease. Consequently, the immune system mounts a primary response, producing specific antibodies and establishing immunological memory via memory B and T cells. If the vaccinated individual is exposed to the real pathogen in the future, their immune system mounts a rapid, robust secondary response, neutralizing the threat before severe symptoms can develop. This foundational immunological concept underpins all vaccination strategies used globally and nationally to protect vulnerable populations, eradicate deadly diseases, and achieve herd immunity within communities.

Types and Classifications of Vaccines

Vaccines are categorized into several distinct types based on their manufacturing technology and composition, each presenting unique properties relevant to nursing practice. Live-attenuated vaccines use a weakened form of the live virus or bacterium. Because they closely mimic natural infection, they typically evoke strong, long-lasting cellular and humoral immunity, often with only one or two doses. However, they must be used cautiously in immunocompromised patients due to the risk of uncontrolled replication. In contrast, inactivated vaccines use pathogens that have been killed or treated with heat, chemicals, or radiation. They cannot replicate, making them safer for vulnerable populations, though they usually require booster doses to maintain protective immunity over time. Subunit, recombinant, polysaccharide, and conjugate vaccines use only specific pieces of the target germ, such as its protein, sugar, or capsid, minimizing adverse reactions. For instance, conjugate vaccines link weak polysaccharide antigens to carrier proteins to help infants develop strong immune responses against encapsulated bacteria like Streptococcus pneumoniae. Finally, toxoid vaccines target bacterial toxins rather than the whole organism by using inactivated toxins called toxoids, as seen in tetanus and diphtheria vaccines. Understanding these classifications enables nurses to explain vaccine safety profiles, schedule requirements, and suitability for various patient cohorts accurately.

Mechanisms of Action and Immune Response

The mechanism of action of vaccines revolves around triggering both the humoral and cell-mediated branches of the adaptive immune system. When a vaccine is administered, specialized antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as macrophages and dendritic cells, engulf the vaccine components and display the processed antigens on their surfaces using major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules. These APCs travel to local lymph nodes where they present the antigen to naive T-helper cells. Activated T-helper cells then stimulate B-cells to undergo clonal expansion and differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete large quantities of pathogen-specific antibodies into the bloodstream and mucosal surfaces. Concurrently, cytotoxic T-cells are activated to destroy cells that display intracellular foreign markers. A crucial subset of these lymphocytes becomes memory cells—both memory B-cells and memory T-cells—which remain dormant in the lymphatic tissues for years or even decades. Upon subsequent exposure to the actual wild-type pathogen, these memory cells recognize the familiar antigen instantly. They bypass the slower primary lag phase, proliferating and producing high-affinity antibodies within hours. This swift, magnified secondary immune response clears the pathogen before clinical disease symptoms can manifest, providing durable protection to the individual and reducing transmission within the broader community.

National Immunization Schedule in Nigeria

The National Immunization Schedule in Nigeria provides a structured framework for delivering life-saving vaccines to infants and children through the National Programme on Immunization (NPI) and primary healthcare systems. Nigeria's schedule aligns with World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations while addressing local epidemiological priorities, particularly the burden of vaccine-preventable diseases like poliomyelitis, tuberculosis, tetanus, measles, and yellow fever. The schedule begins at birth with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) for tuberculosis protection, the oral polio vaccine (OPV zero dose), and Hepatitis B vaccination. As the infant ages at 6, 10, and 14 weeks, doses of pentavalent vaccine (Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b), oral and inactivated polio vaccines (IPV), pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), and rotavirus vaccine are administered. Toward the end of the first year of life, measles and yellow fever vaccines are given, usually around 9 months of age, followed by second-dose measles and meningococcal or other booster formulations as recommended by national guidelines. Nurses play an indispensable role in educating mothers and caregivers on the importance of adhering strictly to these timelines, ensuring completion of all doses, and tracking defaulters within the community to maintain high herd immunity and prevent outbreaks.

Administration Routes, Handling, and Cold Chain Maintenance

Proper administration routes, rigorous handling, and cold chain maintenance are fundamental competencies for nurses managing vaccines. The cold chain refers to the temperature-controlled supply chain—spanning from the point of manufacture down to the point of administration—that ensures vaccines remain potent and safe. Most vaccines must be stored within specific temperature ranges, typically +2°C to +8°C for refrigerators and -15°C to -25°C for freezers, depending on the specific product. Vaccines such as the oral polio vaccine and measles are sensitive to heat and require strict temperature monitoring, while others like frozen diluents or certain liquid formulations can be damaged by freezing. Nurses must regularly check and document refrigerator temperatures, use vaccine carriers correctly during outreach sessions, and follow the 'First Expiry, First Out' (FEFO) principle. In addition to storage, administering vaccines via the correct anatomical route and needle size is crucial for efficacy and safety. BCG is given via intradermal injection, measles and yellow fever subcutaneously, and pentavalent, PCV, and tetanus toxoid intramuscularly in the anterolateral thigh for infants or the deltoid muscle for older children and adults. Incorrect administration routes can lead to reduced immune response or increased local adverse reactions such as sterile abscesses.

Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI) and Pharmacovigilance

Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI) refer to any untoward medical occurrence that happens after a person has received a vaccine, which may or may not be causally related to the vaccine product. AEFIs are classified into five major categories: vaccine product-related reactions, vaccine quality defect-related reactions, immunization error-related reactions, immunization anxiety-related reactions, and coincidental events. Most AEFIs are mild, expected, and self-limiting, such as low-grade fever, mild soreness, redness, or swelling at the injection site. However, rare severe reactions like anaphylaxis, febrile seizures, or hypotensive-hyporesponsive episodes can occur. Pharmacovigilance is the science and practice relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other vaccine-related problems. Nurses play a frontline role in pharmacovigilance by monitoring patients for a minimum of 15 to 30 minutes post-vaccination, treating minor symptoms, managing acute anaphylactic reactions with epinephrine and emergency protocols, and promptly reporting all serious or unexpected AEFIs to national regulatory authorities such as NAFDAC in Nigeria. Accurate documentation and transparent reporting help maintain public trust, ensure vaccine safety, and guide public health policy adjustments.

Contraindications, Precautions, and Special Populations

While vaccines are safe and effective for the vast majority of the population, nurses must evaluate every patient for contraindications and precautions before administration. A contraindication is a condition that greatly increases the chance of a serious adverse reaction, meaning the vaccine should definitely not be given. A classic permanent contraindication to subsequent doses of a vaccine containing pertussis is the occurrence of encephalopathy within 7 days of a previous dose. Severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) to a prior vaccine dose or vaccine component are also strict contraindications. Conversely, a precaution is a condition that might cause diagnostic confusion or increase the risk of a serious adverse reaction, or that might compromise the vaccine's ability to produce immunity. Moderate or severe acute illness with or without fever is generally a precaution, and vaccination is deferred until the patient recovers. Special populations—such as pregnant women, premature infants, immunocompromised individuals, and persons with chronic medical conditions—require specialized clinical judgment. For instance, live-attenuated vaccines are generally contraindicated in pregnancy and severe immunosuppression, whereas inactivated vaccines are often strongly recommended for pregnant women to protect both mother and infant against diseases like influenza and tetanus. Nurses must consult national guidelines carefully when navigating these clinical nuances.

The Role of the Nurse in Vaccine Advocacy and Patient Education

Nurses occupy a vital position in vaccine advocacy, community mobilization, and patient education. Vaccine hesitancy—the reluctance or refusal to vaccinate despite the availability of vaccination services—poses a major threat to global and national public health. Nurses must combat misinformation, myths, and cultural concerns by engaging communities through empathetic, culturally sensitive, and evidence-based communication. Patient education should address common fears about vaccine safety, explain how vaccines protect against debilitating diseases, and clearly outline expected mild side effects versus warning signs that require medical attention. Effective advocacy also involves collaborating with community leaders, religious figures, and local health committees to build trust and increase immunization uptake in hard-to-reach areas. Furthermore, nurses must advocate for robust health policies, consistent vaccine supply chains, and equitable distribution of immunization services to marginalized populations. By combining clinical expertise with strong interpersonal communication skills, nursing professionals build confidence in immunization programs, safeguard public health, and drive progress toward the eradication of vaccine-preventable diseases in Nigeria and beyond.
