Vaccines

Immunology Basics and Vaccine Principles

Vaccines introduce weakened, inactivated, or fractionated parts of a pathogen into the human body to trigger an adaptive immune response without causing severe disease. When a vaccine is administered, specialized antigen-presenting cells engulf the foreign material and display its markers to helper T-cells. This signaling activates B-lymphocytes, which differentiate into plasma cells that produce specific antibodies against the target antigen. Crucially, a subset of these lymphocytes transforms into memory cells that persist in lymphoid tissues for years. If the individual encounters the actual wild-type pathogen later, these memory cells recognize it instantly and mount a rapid, high-affinity neutralizing response, preventing clinical illness.

Classification and Types of Vaccines

Biological products used for immunization fall into distinct categories based on their manufacturing technology and biological properties: - **Live-attenuated vaccines**: Contain a weakened form of the live microorganism. They replicate in the host, inducing a robust cellular and humoral response, often conferring lifelong immunity with fewer doses (e.g., BCG, Oral Polio Vaccine). - **Inactivated vaccines**: Comprise killed microorganisms produced with heat, chemicals, or radiation. Because they cannot replicate, they generally require adjuvants and multiple booster doses (e.g., Inactivated Polio Vaccine). - **Subunit, recombinant, or polysaccharide vaccines**: Use specific pieces of the germ, such as its protein, sugar, or capsid, producing a very targeted immune response with minimal side effects. - **Toxoid vaccines**: Contain toxin derivatives produced by bacteria rather than the microorganism itself, training the immune system to fight the toxin rather than the whole bacterium (e.g., Tetanus toxoid).

The National Immunization Schedule in Nigeria

The National Programme on Immunization (NPI), integrated into the Primary Health Care delivery system in Nigeria, dictates the precise timing and spacing of routine immunizations to protect infants against major childhood scourges. Vaccination begins at birth with the BCG vaccine for tuberculosis protection and the first dose of the Oral Polio Vaccine. As the infant grows, scheduled visits at 6, 10, and 14 weeks introduce pentavalent vaccine (diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b), pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, rotavirus vaccine, and inactivated polio vaccine. Measles and yellow fever vaccines are administered at 9 and 12 months respectively. Adhering strictly to these intervals ensures optimal seroconversion, though catch-up schedules must be calculated when children present late to clinics.

Cold Chain Maintenance and Vaccine Administration

Vaccines are temperature-sensitive biological substances that lose potency or degrade completely if exposed to extreme heat or freezing temperatures. The cold chain is a continuous system of transport and storage refrigerators, vaccine carriers, and cold boxes maintaining temperatures between +2°C and +8°C for most routine vaccines, while oral polio requires sub-zero storage at central levels. In Nigerian primary health facilities, daily temperature logging of vaccine refrigerators is a mandatory shift duty. Routes of administration vary by formulation and dictate absorption rates: - **Intramuscular (IM)**: Injected into the vastus lateralis muscle in infants or deltoid in older children and adults (e.g., Pentavalent, TT). - **Subcutaneous (SC)**: Administered into the fatty tissue layer (e.g., Measles, Yellow Fever). - **Intradermal (ID)**: Delivered into the epidermal layer, raising a distinct bleb (e.g., BCG). - **Oral**: Dropped directly into the mouth (e.g., OPV, Rotavirus).

Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI) and Pharmacovigilance

Monitoring vaccine safety is an ongoing clinical responsibility. An Adverse Event Following Immunization (AEFI) is any untoward medical occurrence that follows vaccination, which does not necessarily have a causal relationship with the vaccine usage. AEFIs are categorized into program errors, vaccine product-related reactions, coincidence, injection reactions, or idiopathic events. Minor reactions like local pain, low-grade fever, and mild irritability are common, self-limiting, and require only supportive reassurance. Severe systemic reactions, such as anaphylaxis or febrile seizures, require immediate intervention and formal reporting to national health authorities. Robust pharmacovigilance protects public trust and identifies rare manufacturing anomalies quickly.

Contraindications, Precautions, and Special Populations

Screening clients before vaccination prevents avoidable complications. A true permanent contraindication to live-attenuated vaccines is severe immunodeficiency (such as symptomatic HIV infection or undergoing active chemotherapy) and a history of a severe anaphylactic reaction to a previous dose of the same vaccine. Temporary precautions, including moderate or severe acute illness with or without fever, warrant delaying the vaccination until the client recovers to prevent confounding symptoms and ensure the immune system can respond effectively. Pregnant women generally avoid live-attenuated formulations due to theoretical risks to the fetus, though inactivated vaccines like tetanus toxoid are safely given during antenatal care to prevent neonatal tetanus.

Nurse-Led Advocacy and Vaccine Confidence

Community engagement and myth-busting form the bedrock of successful immunization campaigns. Nurses encounter vaccine hesitancy rooted in cultural rumors, religious beliefs, or misinformation spread via social media. Addressing these concerns requires empathetic listening, cultural sensitivity, and clear, evidence-based communication rather than dismissive lecturing. Nurses collaborate with community leaders, traditional birth attendants, and local gatekeepers in Nigeria to build trust and increase uptake. Providing accurate explanations about herd immunity helps communities understand that high vaccination coverage protects vulnerable members—such as newborns and immunocompromised individuals—who cannot receive certain vaccines themselves.
