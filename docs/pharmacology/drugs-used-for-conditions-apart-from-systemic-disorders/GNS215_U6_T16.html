Vaccines

Introduction to Vaccines and Immunity Basics

Vaccination is one of the most cost-effective public health interventions available. At its core, a vaccine introduces a weakened, killed, or partial piece of a pathogen into the human body to stimulate an immune response without causing actual disease. This process relies on the remarkable adaptability of the human immune system, specifically our ability to recognize foreign invaders—known as antigens—and manufacture specialized proteins called antibodies to destroy them.

When a person receives a vaccine, primary lymphoid organs process the antigen, and B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes swing into action. B-lymphocytes produce antibodies tailored specifically to that antigen, while T-lymphocytes help coordinate the attack and destroy infected cells. Crucially, this initial encounter creates immunological memory. If the vaccinated individual encounters the actual, live disease-causing organism years later, their immune system mounts a rapid, powerful secondary response that neutralizes the threat before severe symptoms can develop.

Types and Classifications of Vaccines

Vaccines are categorized based on the technology used to manufacture them and how the body interacts with the component. Understanding these categories helps nurses explain safety profiles to patients and anticipate storage requirements.

  • Live-attenuated vaccines: Made from a weakened form of the live germ. They produce a strong, lifelong immunity with few doses, but can sometimes cause disease in immunocompromised people (e.g., Oral Polio Vaccine, Measles).
  • Inactivated vaccines: Made from a killed version of the germ. They are safer for vulnerable populations, but usually require multiple doses or booster shots to maintain protection (e.g., Inactivated Polio Vaccine).
  • Subunit, recombinant, or conjugate vaccines: Use only specific pieces of the germ, like its protein, sugar, or casing, giving a very targeted immune response with minimal side effects (e.g., Hepatitis B, HPV).
  • Toxoid vaccines: Made from toxins produced by the bacteria rather than the germ itself. The immune system learns to fight off the toxin rather than the whole organism (e.g., Tetanus toxoid).

Administration Routes, Handling, and Cold Chain Maintenance

A potent vaccine can become completely useless if exposed to improper temperatures. The cold chain is a temperature-controlled supply chain that ensures vaccines remain safe and effective from the manufacturer all the way to the point of administration. In Nigeria, the National Primary Health Care Development Agency enforces strict cold chain guidelines using solar-powered refrigerators, vaccine carriers, and ice packs.

Nurses must inspect the Vaccine Vial Monitor (VVM)—a small dot on the label that changes color with heat exposure—before using any vial. If the inner square is darker than the outer circle, the vaccine must be discarded. Furthermore, nurses must adhere to correct administration routes:

  1. Intramuscular (IM): Given in the anterolateral thigh or deltoid muscle at a 90-degree angle (e.g., Pentavalent vaccine).
  2. Subcutaneous (SC): Given into the fatty tissue at a 45-degree angle (e.g., Measles vaccine).
  3. Intradermal (ID): Given just beneath the epidermis forming a visible bleb (e.g., BCG vaccine).
  4. Oral: Dropped directly into the mouth (e.g., Oral Polio, Rotavirus).

Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI) and Pharmacovigilance

An Adverse Event Following Immunization (AEFI) is any untoward medical occurrence that happens after a person receives a vaccine, whether it is directly caused by the vaccine or purely coincidental. Robust pharmacovigilance systems rely heavily on frontline nurses to identify, manage, and report these occurrences.

AEFI cases are generally grouped into five categories:

  • Vaccine product-related reaction: Caused by an inherent property of the vaccine (e.g., mild fever, soreness at the injection site).
  • Vaccine quality defect-related reaction: Caused by a manufacturing flaw in the product or its cold chain equipment.
  • Immunization error-related reaction: Caused by inappropriate handling, prescribing, or administration (e.g., unsterile needle reuse or wrong dosage).
  • Immunization anxiety-related reaction: Triggered by fear or pain associated with the injection (e.g., vasovagal syncope or fainting in older children).
  • Coincidental event: An underlying illness or medical condition that happened to occur after vaccination by pure chance, unrelated to the product.

Nurses must educate parents on normal mild reactions while remaining vigilant for rare, severe systemic reactions like anaphylaxis.

Contraindications, Precautions, and Special Populations

Before administering any vaccine, nurses must perform a thorough health screening to verify safety. A contraindication is a condition that greatly increases the chance of a serious adverse reaction, making the vaccine unsafe to give. For example, severe allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) to a previous dose of a vaccine or its components is a permanent contraindication to subsequent doses. Live-attenuated vaccines must never be given to severely immunocompromised individuals, such as untreated HIV patients or those undergoing chemotherapy, because the weakened germ can multiply out of control.

A precautions condition, on the other hand, is a situation where the vaccine might cause a poor immune response, or where the side effect could mask an underlying illness. Moderate or severe acute illness with or without fever is a universal precaution; nurses usually defer vaccination until the child recovers to avoid confusing acute disease symptoms with vaccine reactions.

The Role of the Nurse in Vaccine Advocacy and Patient Education

Nurses are the most trusted source of health information for local communities. Beyond technical skills like cold chain management and injections, nurses must actively fight vaccine hesitancy through empathetic communication and health education. Misinformation spreads rapidly via social media and word-of-mouth, often leading parents to refuse essential immunizations for their children.

Effective nurse-led advocacy involves listening respectfully to parental fears without judgment, correcting myths with clear, simple facts, and highlighting the community benefits of herd immunity. Nurses also collaborate with community leaders, religious figures, and village health workers to mobilize populations during mass vaccination campaigns. By building genuine trust and addressing local concerns directly, nurses ensure high immunization coverage and protect vulnerable populations from preventable outbreaks.
