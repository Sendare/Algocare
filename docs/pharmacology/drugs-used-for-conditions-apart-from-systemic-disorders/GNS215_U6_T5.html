Drugs Used in Pregnancy Labour and Puerperium

Introduction to Pharmacotherapy in Obstetrics

Pharmacotherapy in obstetrics is a critical area of nursing care that requires a deep understanding of how medications impact both the pregnant woman and the developing fetus. When administering drugs during pregnancy, labour, and the puerperium, the nurse must always consider that most pharmacological agents cross the placenta and can directly or indirectly affect the fetus. The primary goal of obstetric pharmacology is to effectively manage maternal medical conditions or pregnancy complications while minimizing any potential harm to the foetus or neonate. Nurses working in Nigerian healthcare settings frequently encounter situations where pregnant women present with pre-existing conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and infections like malaria, as well as pregnancy-specific complications like pre-eclampsia and gestational diabetes. Safe medication administration requires a comprehensive assessment of gestational age, maternal organ function, and the therapeutic risk-benefit ratio. Because physiological changes alter drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion, standard non-pregnant dosages are rarely appropriate. Furthermore, nurses serve as the last line of defense against medication errors by verifying drug orders, understanding mechanisms of action, and monitoring both maternal vital signs and fetal heart rate patterns. Education also plays a vital role, as pregnant women often harbor fears about taking medications. By providing evidence-based explanations, the nurse helps alleviate anxiety and ensures greater medication adherence, ultimately improving both maternal and neonatal health outcomes across diverse clinical environments in Nigeria.

Physiological Changes in Pregnancy Affecting Pharmacokinetics

The physiological adaptations that occur during pregnancy significantly alter pharmacokinetic parameters, impacting how drugs behave in the maternal body. Absorption is affected by decreased gastrointestinal motility and increased gastric pH, which can delay or alter the peak concentration of orally administered medications. Furthermore, nausea and vomiting in early pregnancy can erraticize oral absorption. Once drugs enter the systemic circulation, distribution is profoundly influenced by an expansion of maternal plasma volume by approximately 45%, leading to a larger volume of distribution for water-soluble drugs. Concurrently, serum albumin concentrations decrease, resulting in a lower percentage of protein-bound drugs and an increased concentration of active, free drug in the plasma. Metabolism is also altered, as hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes can be induced or inhibited by pregnancy hormones, changing the rate at which certain medications are cleared. Excretion is perhaps most dramatically affected through the renal system. Renal plasma flow and the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) increase by up to 50% by the second trimester. This leads to a significantly faster renal clearance of renally excreted drugs, such as certain antibiotics and low-molecular-weight heparins, meaning therapeutic drug levels may drop rapidly if dosages are not appropriately adjusted. Nursing students must recognize that these pharmacokinetic shifts require vigilant monitoring and individualized dosing schedules to maintain therapeutic efficacy and prevent maternal toxicity or sub-therapeutic treatment failures.

Teratogenicity and FDA Risk Categories in Pregnancy

Teratogenicity refers to the capacity of a substance to cause structural or functional abnormalities in the developing embryo or fetus. The vulnerability of the fetus to teratogens is heavily time-dependent based on the stage of gestation. The pre-implantation period (first two weeks after conception) often follows an 'all-or-none' rule, where an exposure either causes fetal death or the embryo survives without structural defects. The period of organogenesis, spanning from week 3 through week 8 of gestation, is the window of highest susceptibility to major congenital malformations because major organ systems are actively forming. Exposure during the fetal period (from week 9 to term) is more likely to result in growth restriction, functional impairment, or toxicity rather than gross structural malformations. Historically, the FDA utilized letter categories (A, B, C, D, and X) to classify the risk of medications during pregnancy, where category A represented safe agents and category X denoted proven human fetal abnormalities outweighing any potential benefit. Although the FDA has transitioned to the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule (PLLR) to provide detailed narrative summaries of risks and clinical considerations, many textbooks and clinical settings still reference the historical categories for quick risk estimation. Nurses must be well-versed in teratology, routinely check up-to-date pharmaceutical references before administering any medication, and counsel pregnant patients to avoid unprescribed herbal remedies or over-the-counter drugs that lack safety data.

Drugs Used During Pregnancy for Common Conditions and Complications

Pregnant women frequently require pharmacotherapy to manage common physiological complaints, pre-existing chronic conditions, or acute pregnancy-induced complications. For minor ailments like nausea and vomiting, first-line treatments often include pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) and doxylamine, while severe cases of hyperemesis gravidarum may require antiemetics such as promethazine or ondansetron. Nutritional supplementation is universally important; folic acid is prescribed preconceptionally and during early pregnancy to prevent neural tube defects, and iron supplements are routinely provided to prevent or treat iron-deficiency anemia, which is highly prevalent among pregnant women in Nigeria. For chronic conditions like hypertension, safe antihypertensive agents such as methyldopa, labetalol, and nifedipine are utilized to prevent maternal stroke and end-organ damage while avoiding uteroplacental hypoperfusion. ACE inhibitors and ARBs are strictly contraindicated as they cause severe fetal renal failure and oligohydramnios. Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is managed initially with medical nutrition therapy, but when glycemic targets are not met, insulin is the gold standard because it does not cross the placenta in significant amounts; certain oral agents like metformin are also increasingly used based on clinical guidelines. In regions endemic to malaria, intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) using sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) is a standard protocol to prevent malaria-related maternal illness, low birth weight, and congenital transmission. Nurses must educate patients on the strict adherence to these regimens and monitor for therapeutic response and adverse drug reactions.

Pharmacology of Uterotonics and Tocolytics in Labour

The management of labour relies heavily on two contrasting pharmacological classes: uterotonics and tocolytics. Uterotonics are medications used to stimulate uterine contractions, playing a pivotal role in the induction of labour, augmentation of dysfunctional labour, and the active management of the third stage of labour to prevent postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). Oxytocin is the primary endogenous uterotonic used for induction and PPH prevention; it acts on specific myometrial receptors to produce rhythmic uterine contractions. Other important uterotonics include ergometrine (and its derivative methylergometrine) and prostaglandin analogues like misoprostol and dinoprostone. Ergometrine produces sustained tetanic contractions and is highly effective for PPH, but it is strictly contraindicated in women with hypertension or pre-eclampsia because it can cause profound vasoconstriction and dangerously high blood pressure spikes. Misoprostol, a prostaglandin E1 analogue, is widely used for cervical ripening and PPH management, particularly in low-resource settings due to its low cost and thermal stability. Conversely, tocolytics are medications used to suppress uterine contractions and delay preterm labour, affording time for the administration of antenatal corticosteroids to accelerate fetal lung maturity. Common tocolytic agents include nifedipine (a calcium channel blocker), magnesium sulfate (which also provides fetal neuroprotection against cerebral palsy), and beta-adrenergic agonists like ritodrine or terbutaline. The nurse must carefully differentiate between these drug classes, monitor uterine activity and fetal well-being continuously, and understand contraindications to prevent catastrophic complications such as uterine rupture or acute pulmonary edema.

Pain Management and Anesthesia During Labour

Managing pain during labour is a core component of intrapartum nursing care, promoting maternal coping, reducing stress-induced catecholamine release, and preserving uteroplacental blood flow. Pain relief options range from non-pharmacological comfort measures to systemic pharmacological agents and regional anesthesia. Systemic medications frequently include opioids such as pethidine (meperidine) or fentanyl, administered via intramuscular or intravenous routes. While opioids provide pain relief, the nurse must monitor for maternal sedation, nausea, and respiratory depression, as well as neonatal respiratory depression if delivery occurs shortly after administration due to placental transfer. Regional anesthesia techniques, particularly epidural and spinal blocks, offer superior pain relief with minimal systemic sedation. An epidural involves placing a local anesthetic (such as bupivacaine or ropivacaine) combined with an opioid into the epidural space, blocking sensory nerve impulses while allowing the mother to remain awake and participate in pushing. A primary complication of regional anesthesia is maternal hypotension resulting from sympathetic blockade, which can lead to uteroplacental hypoperfusion and fetal bradycardia; thus, pre-hydration with IV crystalloids and continuous blood pressure monitoring are mandatory nursing interventions. General anesthesia is rarely used for routine vaginal delivery but may be indicated for emergency cesarean sections when regional anesthesia is contraindicated or time does not permit. The nurse plays an essential role in advocating for the parturient's pain management preferences while safeguarding both maternal and fetal physiological stability.

Medications Used in the Puerperium and Breastfeeding Safety

The puerperium (postpartum period) presents unique pharmacological needs focused on recovery, lactation support, and contraception. Postpartum pain management typically begins with non-opioid analgesics such as paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen, which are generally safe during breastfeeding and effectively manage afterpains and perineal discomfort. If opioids are needed for severe pain following a cesarean section, short-acting agents are prescribed with careful monitoring of maternal sedation. Postpartum hemorrhage prophylaxis or treatment continues with oxytocin infusions. For women who choose not to breastfeed or need lactation suppression due to medical reasons, cabergoline (a dopamine receptor agonist) is sometimes utilized, though non-pharmacological measures like tight-fitting bras and ice packs are preferred first-line approaches. When considering medication safety during breastfeeding, nurses must evaluate the drug's pharmacokinetic properties. Most drugs pass into breast milk via passive diffusion, and factors such as high lipid solubility, low molecular weight, high maternal plasma concentration, and low protein binding increase the concentration of the drug in breast milk. Medications with short half-lives, high molecular weight, or poor oral bioavailability in the infant are generally considered safe. Additionally, nurses educate postpartum mothers on contraceptive choices, emphasizing that estrogen-containing combined oral contraceptives can reduce breast milk supply and are typically avoided during early lactation, making progestin-only pills, injectables (like medroxyprogesterone acetate), or barrier methods much safer choices.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education in Obstetric Pharmacology

Nurses carry profound professional responsibilities when managing obstetric pharmacotherapy, acting as a critical safeguard between medication orders and the pregnant or postpartum patient. The nursing process in obstetric pharmacology involves comprehensive baseline assessments—including an accurate medication history, screening for herbal or traditional remedies common in local cultures, verifying gestational age, and assessing renal and hepatic function. The nurse must strictly adhere to the 'rights' of medication administration, paying meticulous attention to dosage accuracy, as small calculation errors can lead to fetal distress or maternal toxicity. Continuous monitoring is essential; for example, when oxytocin is infused, the nurse must monitor uterine contraction frequency, duration, and resting tone alongside fetal heart rate tracings to prevent uterine hyperstimulation and fetal hypoxia. Patient education is equally paramount. Pregnant and lactating women often experience anxiety regarding drug safety, making clear, empathetic communication vital. The nurse must explain the indication, potential side effects, and warning signs of adverse reactions for every prescribed medication, while also warning against self-medication with over-the-counter or traditional concoctions. Documentation must be accurate, detailing the time of administration, patient response, and any untoward effects. By combining rigorous clinical vigilance with compassionate education, the nurse ensures safe and effective pharmacological care across pregnancy, labour, and the puerperium.
