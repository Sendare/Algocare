Drugs Used in Pregnancy Labour and Puerperium

Physiological Changes in Pregnancy Affecting Pharmacokinetics

Pregnancy alters almost every maternal organ system, directly changing how drugs are absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted. **Increased plasma volume** and total body water expand the volume of distribution for water-soluble drugs, often requiring higher loading doses to achieve therapeutic blood levels. Conversely, **decreased serum albumin concentration** reduces protein binding, leaving a higher fraction of free, active drug circulating in the bloodstream. Renal hemodynamics shift dramatically. **Glomerular filtration rate increases** by up to fifty percent early in the first trimester, accelerating the renal clearance of renally eliminated drugs like lithium or certain antibiotics. Hepatic enzyme activity also changes; some cytochrome P450 enzymes are induced while others are inhibited, altering the clearance rate of metabolic substrates. These combined shifts mean standard non-pregnant dosing regimens frequently result in subtherapeutic levels for cleared medications or toxicity for protein-bound agents.

Teratogenicity and Drug Risk Classification

Exposing a developing fetus to medications requires strict risk-benefit analysis, particularly during **organogenesis** in the first trimester when structural anomalies are most easily induced. Drugs cross the placenta primarily via simple diffusion, driven by concentration gradients, lipid solubility, and molecular weight. While the historical FDA letter categories (A, B, C, D, X) are being phased out in favor of descriptive narrative summaries under the Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule, clinicians still rely heavily on the underlying principle of fetal vulnerability. - **Category A**: Adequate, well-controlled human studies show no fetal risk. - **Category B**: Animal studies show no risk, or animal studies showed toxicity but human studies failed to confirm. - **Category C**: Animal studies show adverse effects, and no controlled human studies exist, or no studies are available at all. - **Category D**: Positive evidence of human fetal risk exists, but potential benefits may outweigh potential risks in life-threatening situations. - **Category X**: Studies demonstrate definitive fetal abnormalities, and the risk clearly outweighs any possible benefit.

Pharmacology of Uterotonics and Tocolytics

Managing the myometrium requires balancing agents that stimulate contractions against those that quiet uterine activity. **Uterotonics** are deployed to induce or augment labor and to prevent or treat postpartum hemorrhage, whereas **tocolytics** attempt to delay preterm birth. | Drug Class / Agent | Primary Clinical Indication | Key Mechanism of Action | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Oxytocin** | Labor induction, postpartum hemorrhage | Binds to specific myometrial receptors, increasing intracellular calcium | | **Misoprostol** | Cervical ripening, postpartum hemorrhage | Prostaglandin E1 analog that softens the cervix and stimulates contractions | | **Nifedipine** | Preterm labor suppression | Calcium channel blocker that relaxes smooth muscle by reducing intracellular calcium | | **Magnesium Sulfate** | Fetal neuroprotection, pre-eclampsia seizure prophylaxis | Competes with calcium ions at the neuromuscular junction and myometrium | Selecting the correct agent prevents catastrophic complications such as uterine rupture from hyperstimulation or acute pulmonary edema during tocolytic therapy.

Pain Management and Anesthesia During Labour

Labor pain arises from visceral uterine distension and cervical dilation during the first stage, shifting to somatic pain from perineal stretching during the second stage. Pharmacological management ranges from systemic opioids to regional neuraxial blocks. Systemic analgesics like **pethidine** or **fentanyl** provide moderate relief but readily cross the placenta, potentially causing neonatal respiratory depression if delivery occurs at peak drug concentration. **Naloxone** serves as the specific reversal agent for opioid-induced respiratory depression, though it should be used cautiously in opioid-dependent mothers. Regional techniques, including **epidural** and **spinal anesthesia**, block nerve transmission locally. Epidurals provide continuous pain relief by infusing local anesthetics combined with opioids into the epidural space. While highly effective, they can cause **maternal hypotension** due to sympathetic blockade, which decreases uteroplacental perfusion if fluid preload and vasopressors like ephedrine or phenylephrine are not promptly managed.

Medications in the Puerperium and Breastfeeding Safety

The postpartum period requires careful pharmacotherapy, particularly for mothers who choose to breastfeed. Most drugs transfer into breast milk via passive diffusion, governed by maternal plasma concentration, lipid solubility, molecular weight, and protein binding. Medications with high lipid solubility and low molecular weight enter breast milk more readily. Analgesics such as **paracetamol** and **ibuprofen** are generally considered safe for postpartum pain management while breastfeeding. Conversely, drugs like **tetracyclines**, **lithium**, and certain psychotropics require caution or complete avoidance due to infant toxicity risks. Additionally, postpartum hemorrhage management often continues in the ward with oxytocin infusions or secondary uterotonics like **methylergometrine**, which can suppress lactation due to its strong dopaminergic activity.

Nursing Responsibilities in Obstetric Pharmacology

Administering medications across pregnancy, labor, and the puerperium demands rigorous clinical vigilance. Nurses must verify baseline vital signs, assess fetal heart rate patterns before and after drug administration, and recognize adverse drug reactions early. For instance, when infusing oxytocin, continuous monitoring of uterine activity is mandatory to prevent **tachysystole**, which deprives the fetus of oxygen. Patient education remains a core duty, addressing the risks of self-medication with over-the-counter herbal or orthodox remedies. Clear communication empowers mothers to report unusual symptoms like visual disturbances in pre-eclampsia or heavy bleeding postpartum, bridging the gap between clinical safety and patient empowerment.
