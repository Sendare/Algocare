Nurses' Responsibility in Drug Administration

Introduction to the Nurse's Role in Drug Administration

Drug administration is a core daily duty for nurses in Nigerian health institutions, bridging the gap between medical prescription and patient recovery. The nurse acts as the final safety barrier before a pharmaceutical agent enters a patient's body. This responsibility requires absolute diligence, a solid grasp of pharmacokinetics, and a clear understanding of professional accountability. A prescription written by a doctor is not an automatic command to execute blindly; the nurse must independently verify its safety, appropriateness, and accuracy for the specific individual under their care.

The Rights of Medication Administration

Adhering to the standard rights of medication administration is non-negotiable for preventing clinical errors. While traditionally counted as five, modern practice often expands these to ensure foolproof safety.

  • Right Patient: Verify identity using two unique identifiers, such as hospital number and full name, checking the wristband.
  • Right Drug: Compare the medication label against the prescription order at least three times.
  • Right Dose: Calculate dosages meticulously and have a colleague double-check high-alert drugs like insulin.
  • Right Route: Confirm the prescribed pathway matches the anatomical site and drug formulation.
  • Right Time: Administer drugs within the approved window to maintain therapeutic blood levels.
  • Right Documentation: Record the administration immediately after delivery, never before.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before touching a drug trolley, the nurse must evaluate the clinical status of the patient. This involves checking vital signs, reviewing recent laboratory results, and screening for known allergies. For instance, giving an antihypertensive drug without checking the current blood pressure can precipitate dangerous hypotension. Patient preparation also involves educating the individual on what medication they are receiving, its expected benefits, and potential side effects to secure informed cooperation.

Medication Preparation and Calculation Safety

Mathematical errors in drug calculations can have fatal consequences. Nurses must master formula applications, conversion factors between metric units, and pediatric weight-based dosing. Preparation should occur in a quiet, well-lit environment free from distractions. Never leave drawn-up medications unattended on a tray or preparation surface. A common misconception is that liquid medications can be measured using ordinary household spoons; nurses must always use calibrated oral syringes or medicine cups.

Administration Techniques Across Routes

Drugs enter the body through various routes, each demanding specific clinical techniques to ensure efficacy and patient safety.

  • Oral (PO): Ensure the patient is fully alert and upright to prevent aspiration.
  • Intramuscular (IM): Select appropriate anatomical landmarks like the ventrogluteal site and use the Z-track method for irritating substances.
  • Intravenous (IV): Inspect the cannula site for patency, swelling, or signs of phlebitis before pushing or infusing drugs.
  • Topical and Sublingual: Apply gloves for topical creams; instruct patients not to chew or swallow sublingual tablets.

Post-Administration Monitoring and Documentation

The nursing process does not end when the medication is swallowed or infused. The nurse must return to evaluate the therapeutic outcome and screen for unexpected adverse reactions. For instance, pain medications require a follow-up assessment within thirty to sixty minutes to check if discomfort has subsided. All actions, including the exact time, dosage, and patient response, must be recorded clearly in the medical record without leaving blank spaces.

Reporting and Managing Medication Errors

When a medication error occurs—whether a wrong dose, wrong route, or omitted drug—patient safety takes immediate priority. The nurse must first assess the patient's clinical condition and notify the attending physician. Following stabilization, an incident report must be completed objectively. The focus of error management in modern healthcare is systemic learning rather than victimization, aiming to identify root causes in workplace processes.

Legal and Ethical Responsibilities in Pharmacology

Nurses function under a distinct legal scope of practice governed by professional regulatory bodies such as the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria. Administering drugs without a valid, legible prescription or performing tasks outside one's legal training constitutes negligence. Ethically, nurses advocate for patients who may feel unable to question a doctor's order. Upholding autonomy, beneficence, and non-maleficence ensures that patient well-being remains the central focus of all pharmacological interventions.
