Handling and Storage of Drugs

Introduction to Safe Drug Handling

Proper drug handling and storage are foundational responsibilities for every nurse. Medications are powerful chemical agents that lose their therapeutic value or turn dangerous if kept in the wrong conditions. When drugs degrade, patients might receive a weaker dose than prescribed, leading to treatment failure. Conversely, altered chemical structures can sometimes produce toxic breakdown products. Nurses handle medications at multiple points—from receiving supplies at the pharmacy or ward to administering them at the bedside. Maintaining drug integrity protects both the patient's health and the hospital's resources.

Environmental Factors Affecting Drug Stability

Several environmental elements actively break down active pharmaceutical ingredients.

  • Temperature: Most drugs require room temperature control, typically between 15°C and 25°C. Excessive heat accelerates chemical reactions, destroying the medication.
  • Light: Ultraviolet and bright fluorescent light cause photo-degradation. Many sensitive drugs come packed in amber bottles or foil wraps for this exact reason.
  • Humidity: Moisture promotes physical and chemical breakdown, especially for loose tablets, powders, and soft capsules. Bathrooms and utility rooms with high steam levels make poor storage spaces.

Cold Chain Management and Vaccines

The cold chain is an uninterrupted system of storage and transport that keeps temperature-sensitive products within a specific low-range, usually 2°C to 8°C for refrigerators. Vaccines, insulin, and certain biological products suffer irreversible damage if allowed to freeze or warm up. A common misconception is that household food refrigerators are acceptable for vaccines; however, they experience wild temperature swings and lack precise monitoring. Nurses must check and record refrigerator temperatures twice daily, keeping items away from internal walls and doors to ensure proper air circulation.

Storage of Controlled Substances and High-Alert Medications

Controlled substances, such as strong opioids, carry a high risk of abuse, addiction, and diversion. Facilities must store these medications inside a double-locked, secure metal box or cabinet. Access must remain strictly limited to authorized nursing and pharmacy staff, with every dose accounted for through a sign-out log. High-alert medications like concentrated electrolytes, insulin, and anticoagulants also demand special care. Even though they may not be narcotics, storing them with clear warning labels prevents catastrophic administration errors in busy clinical wards.

Stock Rotation and Expiry Management

Effective inventory control prevents drug waste and ensures patients receive fresh supplies. The standard practice in healthcare is the FEFO principle: First Expire, First Out. This means items with the shortest remaining shelf life are placed at the front of the shelf for immediate use, regardless of which batch arrived last. Nurses should regularly check expiry dates during routine ward stock audits. Expired drugs must be immediately removed from active stock to prevent accidental administration.

Disposal of Expired and Damaged Medications

Disposing of pharmaceutical waste requires strict adherence to safety and environmental guidelines. Throwing expired drugs into regular household trash or flushing them down sinks and toilets pollutes local water systems and soil. Liquid medications, broken ampoules, and outdated pills must go into designated pharmaceutical waste bins or return systems sent back to the central pharmacy. Nurses must document all disposed items according to local hospital policy to maintain an accurate audit trail of drug destruction.
