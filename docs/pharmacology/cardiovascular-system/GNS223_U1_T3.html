Antiarrhythmic Drugs

Introduction to Cardiac Electrophysiology and Arrhythmias

Normal cardiac rhythm relies on coordinated electrical impulse generation and conduction through the heart. The process begins in the sinoatrial node, spreading across the atria, pausing at the atrioventricular node, and rapidly conducting through the bundle of His and Purkinje fibers. An arrhythmia arises when this orderly sequence is disrupted by abnormal impulse formation, abnormal impulse conduction, or a combination of both.

Action potentials in cardiac cells depend on ion fluxes across cell membranes. Depolarization is primarily driven by rapid sodium influx, while the plateau phase is maintained by calcium entry. Repolarization relies on potassium efflux. Disruptions in these ionic movements alter the refractory period and automaticity, paving the way for tachyarrhythmias or bradyarrhythmias. Recognizing these foundational mechanisms helps clinical staff understand why specific pharmacological agents are selected to restore normal sinus rhythm.

Classification of Antiarrhythmic Drugs (Vaughan Williams Classification)

The Vaughan Williams classification system categorizes antiarrhythmic drugs based on their primary electrophysiological mechanism of action on the cardiac cell membrane. This framework organizes agents into four main groups, with a few notable individual drugs placed outside these classes.

  • Class I: Sodium channel blockers, which slow the rate of depolarization.
  • Class II: Beta-blockers, which reduce sympathetic activity and slow nodal conduction.
  • Class III: Potassium channel blockers, which prolong repolarization and effective refractory periods.
  • Class IV: Calcium channel blockers, which depress calcium-dependent depolarization in the sinoatrial and atrioventricular nodes.

While clinically useful, this classification has limitations. Many drugs exhibit overlapping actions across multiple classes, and their electrophysiological profiles can change depending on heart rate and the presence of ischemic tissue.

Class I Antiarrhythmic Drugs: Sodium Channel Blockers

Sodium channel blockers interrupt the fast inward sodium current during Phase 0 of the cardiac action potential. This action depresses the upstroke velocity of the action potential and slows conduction throughout working myocardial tissue. Class I is further subdivided into three groups based on their effects on action potential duration and repolarization kinetics:

  1. Class IA: Moderate sodium channel blockade, which also prolongs repolarization and action potential duration. Examples include quinidine and disopyramide.
  2. Class IB: Mild sodium channel blockade that actually shortens repolarization. Lidocaine is a classic representative, frequently used for ventricular arrhythmias.
  3. Class IC: Marked sodium channel blockade with little effect on repolarization, causing a profound slowing of conduction velocity. Flecainide and propafenone belong to this subgroup.

Class II Antiarrhythmic Drugs: Beta-Adrenergic Blockers

Class II agents are beta-adrenergic receptor antagonists that suppress the arrhythmogenic effects of catecholamines on the heart. By competitively blocking beta-1 receptors, these drugs decrease the slope of Phase 4 depolarization in pacemaker cells, reducing automaticity. They also prolong atrioventricular nodal conduction time and increase the refractory period of the node.

Drugs such as metoprolol, propranolol, and esmolol are integral in managing supraventricular tachycardias, controlling ventricular rate during atrial fibrillation, and preventing recurrent ventricular arrhythmias following myocardial infarction. Because of their negative chronotropic and inotropic properties, careful assessment for bradycardia and worsening heart failure is required before and during administration.

Class III Antiarrhythmic Drugs: Potassium Channel Blockers

Potassium channel blockers prolong the repolarization phase (Phase 3) of the cardiac action potential. By delaying the efflux of potassium, these agents widen the effective refractory period in both atrial and ventricular muscle fibers, preventing re-entrant circuits that sustain many dangerous tachyarrhythmias.

Amiodarone is the most widely utilized agent in this class, possessing complex electrophysiological properties that also involve sodium, calcium, and beta-adrenergic blocking capabilities. Other agents include sotalol (which also has beta-blocking properties) and dofetilide. Amiodarone is exceptionally effective for both supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias, but its cumulative tissue toxicity profile—affecting the lungs, thyroid, liver, and skin—demands rigorous long-term monitoring.

Class IV Antiarrhythmic Drugs: Calcium Channel Blockers

Class IV antiarrhythmics are non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers that inhibit the influx of calcium ions through L-type channels during the plateau phase of the action potential. This action is most prominent in nodal tissues, where calcium currents drive impulse generation and conduction.

Verapamil and diltiazem slow atrioventricular nodal conduction and increase the nodal refractory period, making them highly effective for terminating or controlling the ventricular rate in re-entrant supraventricular tachycardias, such as AV nodal re-entrant tachycardia. They are generally ineffective against ventricular arrhythmias and should be avoided in patients with systolic heart failure due to their strong negative inotropic effects.

Miscellaneous Antiarrhythmic Agents

Several important agents with distinct mechanisms do not fit neatly into the four Vaughan Williams categories but play critical roles in managing cardiac dysrhythmias:

  • Adenosine: A naturally occurring nucleoside that transiently blocks conduction through the atrioventricular node. It has an extremely short half-life of a few seconds and is administered via rapid intravenous bolus to terminate paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia.
  • Digoxin: A cardiac glycoside that enhances vagal tone while inhibiting the sodium-potassium ATPase pump. It slows atrioventricular nodal conduction and controls ventricular rates in atrial fibrillation, though its narrow therapeutic index requires careful monitoring for toxicity.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

Administering antiarrhythmic therapy demands rigorous clinical surveillance because these medications can paradoxically induce new arrhythmias or worsen existing ones. Before giving any dose, nurses must evaluate baseline vital signs, check apical pulse rates, review recent electrocardiogram strips for rhythm and interval changes, and assess renal and hepatic function test results.

Continuous cardiac telemetry is standard practice during intravenous loading of antiarrhythmic agents, with particular attention paid to PR, QRS, and QT interval measurements. Prolongation of the QT interval, for instance, warns of impending torsades de pointes in patients receiving Class IA or Class III drugs. Additionally, patient education regarding symptom recognition—such as dizziness, palpitations, visual disturbances, or shortness of breath—ensures timely intervention before minor adverse effects escalate into life-threatening emergencies.
