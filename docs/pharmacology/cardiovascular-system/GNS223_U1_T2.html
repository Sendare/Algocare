Drugs Used in the Treatment of Hypertension

Introduction to Hypertension and Antihypertensive Therapy

Hypertension remains a major public health challenge globally, characterized by persistently elevated systemic arterial pressure. In clinical settings across Nigeria, it frequently underpins cardiovascular morbidity, contributing significantly to stroke, renal failure, and heart failure presentations. Blood pressure is determined by cardiac output multiplied by systemic vascular resistance. Antihypertensive therapy targets these exact variables to lower pressure and reduce end-organ damage.

Treatment strategies typically begin with lifestyle modifications, but pharmacotherapy becomes essential when blood pressure targets are not met or when comorbid conditions are present. Monotherapy can sometimes suffice, yet many patients require a combination of agents with complementary mechanisms to achieve optimal control. Long-term adherence is a persistent hurdle in resource-limited settings due to medication costs and asymptomatic disease progression, making effective nursing support indispensable.

Diuretics: Mechanisms, Types, and Nursing Considerations

Diuretics reduce blood pressure primarily by depleting body sodium and reducing extracellular fluid volume. Over time, persistent sodium depletion also decreases vascular resistance.

  • Thiazide Diuretics: Agents like hydrochlorothiazide act at the distal convoluted tubule to inhibit sodium and chloride reabsorption. They are standard first-line therapies for uncomplicated hypertension.
  • Loop Diuretics: Medications such as furosemide act on the ascending limb of the loop of Henle. They produce potent diuresis and are reserved for patients with severe hypertension combined with heart failure or chronic kidney disease.
  • Potassium-Sparing Diuretics: Spironolactone acts as an aldosterone antagonist in the collecting ducts, promoting sodium excretion while retaining potassium.

Electrolyte imbalances are a primary concern with diuretic therapy. Hypokalemia is common with thiazides and loop diuretics, predisposing patients to cardiac dysrhythmias, whereas potassium-sparing agents risk hyperkalemia. Daily morning dosing is typically scheduled to prevent nocturnal diuresis and disrupted sleep.

Sympatholytics: Beta-Blockers, Alpha-Blockers, and Central Acting Agents

Sympatholytics lower blood pressure by inhibiting the sympathetic nervous system, which normally drives heart rate and vascular tone upward.

  • Beta-Blockers: Drugs like atenolol and metoprolol block beta-1 receptors on the heart, reducing heart rate and myocardial contractility. Non-selective agents also affect bronchial beta-2 receptors and must be used cautiously in asthmatic patients.
  • Alpha-1 Blockers: Medications such as prazosin dilate both arterioles and veins by blocking postsynaptic alpha-1 receptors, though they can trigger first-dose orthostatic hypotension.
  • Central Acting Agents: Methyldopa and clonidine stimulate alpha-2 receptors in the brainstem, reducing sympathetic outflow from the central nervous system. Methyldopa remains a preferred agent for managing gestational hypertension in Nigerian obstetric wards.

Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)

Calcium channel blockers inhibit the influx of calcium ions into vascular smooth muscle cells and myocardial cells. This cellular restriction prevents muscle contraction, leading to peripheral vasodilation and decreased vascular resistance.

  1. Dihydropyridines: Agents ending in '-ipine', such as amlodipine and nifedipine, possess high vascular selectivity. They dilate peripheral arteries effectively but can occasionally cause reflex tachycardia and peripheral ankle edema.
  2. Non-Dihydropyridines: Verapamil and diltiazem exert greater effects on cardiac tissue, slowing heart rate and decreasing AV nodal conduction alongside their vasodilatory properties.

Nursing assessments must include monitoring heart rate and blood pressure prior to administration, particularly with non-dihydropyridines. Patients should also be educated that peripheral edema caused by dihydropyridines is a vascular pooling effect, not a sign of heart failure.

Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS) Inhibitors

The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is a critical hormonal regulator of blood pressure and fluid balance. When renal perfusion drops, renin is released, converting angiotensinogen to angiotensin I. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) then transforms angiotensin I into angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor that also stimulates aldosterone secretion.

Interventions target specific steps in this pathway:

  • ACE Inhibitors: Drugs ending in '-pril' (e.g., enalapril, lisinopril) block the conversion enzyme. A characteristic side effect is a persistent, dry cough caused by bradykinin accumulation.
  • Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs): Drugs ending in '-sartan' (e.g., losartan, valsartan) block angiotensin II from binding directly to its receptors, avoiding the cough side effect.
  • Direct Renin Inhibitors: Aliskiren binds directly to renin, halting the cascade at its inception.

A major clinical pitfall with all RAAS inhibitors is the risk of hyperkalemia, especially when combined with potassium supplements or potassium-sparing diuretics. They are also strictly contraindicated during pregnancy due to fetal toxicity.

Vasodilators: Direct-Acting Smooth Muscle Relaxants

Direct-acting vasodilators relax vascular smooth muscle directly, predominantly affecting arterioles to reduce systemic vascular resistance. Because blood pressure drops rapidly, reflex sympathetic activation frequently occurs, causing tachycardia and increased myocardial oxygen demand. Consequently, these agents are rarely used as monotherapy and are typically combined with a beta-blocker and a diuretic.

Hydralazine is a prominent agent in this class, frequently utilized in managing severe hypertension of pregnancy and hypertensive emergencies. Minoxidil is an even more potent vasodilator reserved for severe, treatment-resistant hypertension, notable for causing hypertrichosis (excessive hair growth) as a side effect. Nursing monitoring requires continuous blood pressure surveillance during initial parenteral administration to prevent precipitous hypotension.

Management of Hypertensive Emergencies and Urgencies

Hypertensive crises are divided into two distinct clinical categories requiring different management urgencies.

FeatureHypertensive UrgencyHypertensive Emergency
Blood PressureSeverely elevated (usually >180/120 mmHg)Severely elevated (usually >180/120 mmHg)
Target Organ DamageAbsentPresent (e.g., encephalopathy, stroke, MI, dissection)
Reduction GoalGradual reduction over 24 to 48 hoursRapid reduction within minutes to hours
Route of AdministrationOral medicationsParenteral infusions in intensive care

Dropping blood pressure too quickly in emergency settings can precipitate cerebral ischemia or myocardial infarction because autoregulatory vessels have adapted to chronic high pressure. Intravenous agents such as sodium nitroprusside, labetalol, or nicardipine require precise titration using infusion pumps with continuous arterial pressure monitoring.

Comprehensive Nursing Care and Patient Education for Antihypertensive Drugs

Administering antihypertensive medications safely demands rigorous nursing assessment, including baseline vital signs, orthostatic blood pressure checks, and renal function laboratory values. Nurses must educate patients that antihypertensive therapy is typically lifelong. Stopping medications abruptly upon feeling well can trigger dangerous rebound hypertension.

Practical guidance should cover lifestyle adjustments such as sodium restriction, weight management, and moderation of alcohol intake. Patients should be instructed on recognizing signs of hypotension, such as dizziness upon standing, and advised to rise slowly from sitting or lying positions. In Nigerian clinical practice, engaging family members and utilizing community health volunteers can significantly improve medication adherence and follow-up appointment attendance.
