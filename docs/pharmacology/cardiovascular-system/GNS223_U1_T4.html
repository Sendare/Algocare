Antianginal Drugs

Introduction to Angina Pectoris and Myocardial Oxygen Demand

Angina pectoris presents as a gripping, tight, or squeezing chest pain resulting from myocardial ischemia. This ischemic state occurs when the coronary blood supply fails to meet the metabolic demands of the heart muscle. The fundamental goal of antianginal pharmacotherapy is to restore the delicate balance between myocardial oxygen supply and myocardial oxygen demand.

Myocardial oxygen demand is primarily driven by three hemodynamic factors:

  • Heart rate: Faster rates shorten diastole, reducing coronary perfusion time while increasing myocardial work.
  • Preload: The ventricular volume at the end of diastole, which determines the stretch of the muscle fibers.
  • Afterload: The resistance the ventricles must overcome to eject blood into the systemic circulation.

Conversely, myocardial oxygen supply depends on coronary blood flow, arterial blood pressure, and oxygen-carrying capacity. Pathological narrowing of the coronary arteries by atherosclerotic plaques restricts the maximum achievable blood flow, triggering anginal episodes during physical exertion or emotional stress.

Classification and Mechanisms of Antianginal Agents

Pharmacological management of angina relies on drug classes that either decrease myocardial oxygen demand or increase oxygen supply to the ischemic myocardium.

By targeting these distinct physiological parameters, antianginal regimens are often combined to provide comprehensive protection against ischemic episodes, particularly when monotherapy fails to control symptoms during daily activities.

Nitrates and Nitrites: Pharmacology and Administration

Nitroglycerin remains the cornerstone for terminating acute anginal attacks. These agents are converted to nitric oxide within vascular smooth muscle, activating cyclic GMP and leading to potent vasodilation. Because veins are more sensitive to nitrates than arteries, venous pooling predominates, lowering preload and ventricular wall tension.

Administering sublingual nitroglycerin requires specific clinical protocols. The patient places the tablet under the tongue and lets it dissolve, avoiding swallowing, which would cause high first-pass hepatic metabolism and render the drug ineffective. A burning sensation under the tongue often indicates the tablets are fresh and potent.

A major limitation of continuous nitrate therapy is tolerance, where the blood vessels lose their responsiveness. To prevent this, patients require a daily nitrate-free interval, typically 10 to 12 hours overnight, achieved by removing patches or adjusting dosing schedules.

Beta-Adrenergic Blockers in Angina Management

Beta-blockers suppress the adverse cardiac effects of catecholamines by competitively blocking beta-1 adrenergic receptors in the heart. This action slows the heart rate, reduces myocardial contractility, and decreases AV nodal conduction velocity. The net result is a substantial reduction in myocardial oxygen demand during exertion and rest.

Cardioselective agents like metoprolol and atenolol target beta-1 receptors primarily, making them safer for patients with mild pulmonary conditions. Non-selective agents like propranolol also block beta-2 receptors in bronchial smooth muscle, which can precipitate dangerous bronchospasm in individuals with asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Sudden cessation of beta-blocker therapy is dangerous. Abrupt withdrawal can cause rebound tachycardia, severe hypertension, or a myocardial infarction due to upregulated receptor sensitivity.

Calcium Channel Blockers: Indications and Action

Calcium channel blockers are vital when beta-blockers are contraindicated or when treating vasospastic (Prinzmetal's) angina. By blocking L-type calcium channels, these drugs relax vascular smooth muscle and cause coronary artery dilation, increasing myocardial oxygen supply.

These agents fall into two major categories based on their primary site of action:

  • Dihydropyridines: (e.g., amlodipine, nifedipine) act predominantly on vascular smooth muscle to produce profound vasodilation and lower blood pressure.
  • Non-dihydropyridines: (e.g., verapamil, diltiazem) have significant direct depressant effects on the myocardium and the AV node, lowering heart rate and contractility.

Common adverse effects include peripheral edema, flushing, dizziness, and constipation, particularly with verapamil due to its slowing effect on gut motility.

Adjuvant and Novel Antianginal Drugs

When traditional first-line therapies—nitrates, beta-blockers, and calcium channel blockers—fail to control refractory angina, adjuvant agents offer alternative mechanisms of action.

  • Ranolazine: Inhibits late sodium current in myocardial cells, preventing intracellular calcium overload and reducing ventricular wall tension without altering heart rate or blood pressure.
  • Ivabradine: Selectively inhibits the 'funny' (If) ion channels in the sinoatrial node, slowing heart rate exclusively without affecting myocardial contractility or vascular tone.
  • Nicorandil: Acts as both a potassium channel opener and a nitrate donor, providing dual vasodilation of both large and small coronary arteries.

These medications are typically prescribed by cardiologists for chronic stable angina when patients continue to experience debilitating symptoms despite optimized conventional medical therapy.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive clinical assessment is vital before and after administering antianginal medications. The nurse must systematically evaluate the character, duration, radiation, and precipitating factors of chest pain using the PQRST mnemonic.

Vital sign parameters dictate medication safety. Blood pressure must be monitored closely; administering nitrates or calcium channel blockers when systolic pressure is below 90 mmHg can induce profound, life-threatening hypotension and reflex tachycardia. Similarly, pulse rates must be checked before giving beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers; if the apical pulse falls below 60 beats per minute, the dose should be held and the physician notified.

In Nigerian clinical settings, where electronic monitoring equipment may occasionally be limited, manual palpation of radial and apical pulses combined with frequent auscultation for adventitious breath sounds and fluid overload remains an essential nursing skill.

Patient Education and Safety Considerations

Empowering patients with clear, actionable safety instructions is crucial for managing angina safely at home. Patients must understand how to use sublingual nitroglycerin correctly during an acute attack: stop current activity, sit down to prevent falls from sudden hypotension, and take one tablet. If pain persists after five minutes, they should take a second tablet and call emergency services or prepare for hospital transport. A third tablet may be taken after another five minutes if help has not arrived, but exceeding three doses within 15 minutes without medical oversight is dangerous.

Storage of nitroglycerin is another key teaching point. The medication degrades rapidly when exposed to heat, moisture, and light. It must be kept in its original dark glass container with the cap tightly closed, and cotton fillers should be removed as they absorb moisture. Tablets must be replaced every three to six months.

Patients should also avoid sudden changes in posture to prevent orthostatic hypotension, limit alcohol consumption, and recognize that concurrent use of phosphodiesterase inhibitors for erectile dysfunction can cause catastrophic, refractory hypotension.
