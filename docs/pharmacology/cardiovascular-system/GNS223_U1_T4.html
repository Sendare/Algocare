Antianginal Drugs

Understanding Angina Pectoris and Myocardial Oxygen Demand

Angina pectoris is chest pain that happens when your heart muscle does not get enough oxygen-rich blood. This imbalance usually occurs because fatty plaques have narrowed the coronary arteries, limiting blood flow. When physical exertion or emotional stress increases the workload of the heart, the oxygen demand goes up, but the narrowed vessels cannot deliver. The classic symptom is a heavy, squeezing pressure behind the breastbone that may spread to the left arm, jaw, or neck.

Myocardial oxygen demand is determined primarily by three factors: heart rate, contractility, and wall tension. Antianginal medications work by targeting these specific factors. If a drug slows the heart rate or reduces the force of contraction, the heart uses less oxygen. If a drug relaxes blood vessels to decrease the pressure the heart must pump against, oxygen demand drops. Recognizing these underlying mechanics helps nurses understand why certain drugs are ordered for chest pain.

Nitrates: Pharmacology and Administration

Nitrates, such as nitroglycerin, are the cornerstone of acute angina relief. They work by releasing nitric oxide, which relaxes smooth muscle in blood vessels. This relaxation causes both veins and arteries to dilate. Venodilation reduces the amount of blood returning to the heart, which lowers ventricular volume and wall tension, drastically cutting down oxygen demand.

Administration routes vary depending on the clinical goal:

  • Sublingual tablets or spray: Used for acute attacks, bypassing the liver for rapid absorption under the tongue.
  • Transdermal patches: Worn daily to provide a steady release of medication for prevention, but must be removed for 10 to 12 hours daily to prevent drug tolerance.
  • Intravenous infusion: Reserved for severe, unstable angina or acute heart failure in monitored hospital settings.

A common side effect is a throbbing headache caused by rapid blood vessel dilation in the head. Nurses must also monitor for sudden drops in blood pressure, which can cause dizziness.

Beta-Adrenergic Blockers in Angina Management

Beta-blockers prevent angina by blocking the action of catecholamines like adrenaline on beta-1 receptors in the heart. By blocking these receptors, beta-blockers decrease heart rate and reduce the force of myocardial contraction. This dual action significantly lowers the heart's oxygen requirements during exertion.

Unlike nitrates, beta-blockers are used for long-term prevention rather than stopping an acute attack. They improve survival rates after a heart attack and help control resting heart rate. However, nurses must watch out for adverse effects such as resting bradycardia, fatigue, and worsening asthma symptoms due to unintended blocking of beta-2 receptors in the lungs. Stopping these drugs abruptly can cause a dangerous rebound increase in chest pain or heart attacks.

Calcium Channel Blockers: Indications and Action

Calcium channel blockers inhibit the movement of calcium ions across the membranes of heart muscle cells and vascular smooth muscle cells. Calcium is necessary for muscle contraction, so blocking it leads to relaxed blood vessels and decreased myocardial contractility.

Specific agents like amlodipine mainly dilate peripheral blood vessels, lowering blood pressure and afterload. Others, like diltiazem and verapamil, also slow down conduction through the heart and reduce heart rate. These drugs are especially useful for variant or vasospastic angina, where coronary arteries suddenly spasm and restrict blood flow. Common side effects include peripheral edema, constipation, and dizziness.

Adjuvant and Novel Antianginal Medications

When standard therapy with nitrates, beta-blockers, and calcium channel blockers fails to control symptoms, secondary antianginal agents are introduced.

  • Ranolazine: Modates sodium currents inside myocardial cells, reducing intracellular sodium and subsequent calcium overload. This improves the metabolic efficiency of the heart without drastically changing heart rate or blood pressure.
  • Ivabradine: Specifically targets the sinus node to reduce heart rate without affecting contractility or blood pressure.

These drugs offer alternative pathways for managing chronic stable angina, particularly in patients who cannot tolerate the blood pressure-lowering effects of traditional agents.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Safety Considerations

Safe administration of antianginal medications requires careful baseline and ongoing nursing assessments. Before giving any antianginal drug, the nurse must check blood pressure and heart rate to prevent dangerous hypotension or bradycardia. Nurses should evaluate the characteristics of chest pain using standard criteria: location, duration, precipitating factors, and relieving measures.

Patient safety is paramount when dealing with vasodilating drugs. Dizziness and orthostatic hypotension are common, putting patients at risk for falls, especially older adults. Nurses must educate patients to change positions slowly. Furthermore, combining nitrates with erectile dysfunction medications like sildenafil can cause a life-threatening drop in blood pressure and is strictly contraindicated.

Patient Education and Safe Administration

Effective teaching ensures that patients manage their angina safely at home. Patients taking sublingual nitroglycerin must know how to store their medication properly in a dark, airtight glass container away from moisture, as heat and light degrade the tablets quickly.

Patients should follow a clear action plan during an angina attack: stop current activity, sit down, and take one sublingual tablet. If pain persists after five minutes, they should take a second tablet and call emergency services if relief does not occur after a third dose. Nurses should ensure patients understand that a tingling sensation under the tongue usually indicates the sublingual tablet is fresh and potent.
