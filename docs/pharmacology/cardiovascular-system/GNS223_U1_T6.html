Haematinics

Introduction to Haematinics and Erythropoiesis

Haematinics are nutritional supplements required for the formation of blood cells, a process medically termed erythropoiesis. When the body lacks essential raw materials—specifically iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid—the bone marrow fails to produce adequate, functional erythrocytes. This shortfall results in various forms of anemia, notably iron deficiency anemia and megaloblastic anemia. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, managing these deficiencies remains a cornerstone of care, particularly given the high prevalence among pregnant women and young children battling malaria or parasitic infestations. Understanding these agents requires looking beyond simple replacement; it involves recognizing how raw minerals and vitamins translate into cellular oxygen-carrying capacity.

Iron Therapy: Oral and Parenteral Administration

Iron is the foundational building block of hemoglobin. Clinical administration relies on two primary routes depending on severity, patient tolerance, and urgency:

  • Oral Iron: Ferrous sulfate, ferrous gluconate, and ferrous fumarate are common agents. Best absorbed on an empty stomach, though food can reduce gastrointestinal upset if tolerance is poor. Vitamin C co-administration enhances absorption.
  • Parenteral Iron: Iron dextran or iron sucrose administered via intramuscular or intravenous routes. Reserved for patients with severe malabsorption, chronic kidney disease, or severe iron intolerance.

A notorious hazard of liquid oral iron preparations is the staining of dental enamel; patients must drink these formulations through a straw and rinse their mouths afterward. When administering intramuscular iron, the Z-track method is mandatory to prevent permanent skin discoloration and leakage into subcutaneous tissue.

Vitamin B12 Physiology and Deficiency

Vitamin B12, or cobalamin, is crucial for DNA synthesis and neurological function. Dietary B12 binds to intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein secreted by the parietal cells of the gastric mucosa. This complex travels to the terminal ileum, where active absorption takes place. A deficiency disrupts myelin synthesis, leading to irreversible neurological deficits if left untreated.

  • Pernicious Anemia: An autoimmune condition destroying parietal cells, causing a complete lack of intrinsic factor.
  • Gastrectomy: Surgical removal of the stomach eliminates intrinsic factor production.
  • Strict Vegan Diets: Plant-based diets lack natural sources of B12, requiring long-term supplementation.

Unlike iron and folate, the human liver stores large quantities of vitamin B12, meaning deficiency symptoms can take years to manifest after dietary cessation.

Folic Acid and Megaloblastic Anemia

Folic acid (vitamin B9) works in tandem with vitamin B12 to synthesize nucleic acids for cell division. Rapidly dividing cells, such as bone marrow erythroid precursors and the gastrointestinal lining, are exceptionally vulnerable to folate deficiency. A lack of folate produces megaloblastic anemia, characterized by large, immature, dysfunctional red blood cells known as megaloblasts.

In clinical practice, distinguishing between B12 and folate deficiency is critical. Administering folic acid to a patient with an undiagnosed B12 deficiency will correct the blood picture temporarily but allows neurological damage to progress silently and destructively. Pregnant women receive routine prophylactic folic acid to prevent neural tube defects such as spina bifida in the developing fetus.

Adverse Effects and Toxicity Management

While haematinics save lives, they carry notable side effects that challenge patient adherence. Oral iron frequently causes gastrointestinal distress, including nausea, epigastric pain, constipation, and dark, tarry stools—a harmless finding that often alarms patients if unaddressed.

Parenteral iron carries risks of severe hypersensitivity reactions, ranging from localized phlebitis to life-threatening anaphylaxis. A test dose is frequently administered before full infusion where protocol dictates. Acute iron poisoning, particularly accidental ingestion by toddlers attracted by colorful tablets, is a medical emergency managed using deferoxamine, a specific iron-chelating agent that binds excess iron for renal excretion.

Patient Assessment, Monitoring, and Nursing Responsibilities

Comprehensive nursing care involves baseline assessments, continuous monitoring, and targeted health education. Before initiating therapy, nurses must check complete blood count parameters, specifically hemoglobin levels, hematocrit, and red cell indices.

  • Monitor reticulocyte count as an early indicator of bone marrow response to treatment.
  • Assess dietary habits and screen for conditions that hinder absorption, such as chronic diarrhea or antacid overuse.
  • Emphasize adherence, explaining that therapy often continues for months after hemoglobin normalizes to replenish ferritin stores.

Evaluating the therapeutic response confirms whether the bone marrow is successfully utilizing the provided supplements to restore oxygen-carrying capacity.
