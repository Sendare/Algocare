Haematinics

Introduction to Haematinics and Erythropoiesis

Haematinics are nutritional supplements required for the formation of blood cells, a process known as erythropoiesis. When the body lacks these vital substances, red blood cell production drops, leading to different forms of anemia. The three primary haematinics essential for clinical practice are iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid.

  • Iron: The core building block of hemoglobin, the protein responsible for oxygen transport.
  • Vitamin B12: Required for DNA synthesis and proper neurological function alongside red cell maturation.
  • Folic Acid: Works hand-in-hand with vitamin B12 to ensure rapid cell division in bone marrow.

Without adequate supplies of these nutrients, the bone marrow produces abnormally large, immature red blood cells or microcytic, pale cells that cannot carry oxygen efficiently. Nurses encounter patients needing haematinics across nearly all hospital wards, from maternity units caring for pregnant women to surgical wards managing post-operative blood loss.

Iron Therapy: Preparations, Administration, and Pharmacokinetics

Iron is absorbed primarily in the duodenum and upper jejunum. Dietary iron exists in two forms: heme iron, which is easily absorbed from animal products, and non-heme iron, found in plant sources, which requires an acidic stomach environment for optimal absorption.

Oral iron preparations, such as ferrous sulfate, ferrous fumarate, and ferrous gluconate, are the first-line treatment for iron-deficiency anemia. Nurses must instruct patients to take oral iron between meals to maximize absorption, though taking it with food may reduce gastrointestinal upset if tolerance is poor. Vitamin C co-administration enhances iron absorption by maintaining an acidic environment.

When oral therapy fails, is poorly tolerated, or when rapid replacement is needed—such as in severe chronic kidney disease—parenteral iron (e.g., iron sucrose or ferric carboxymaltose) is administered via the intravenous route. Intramuscular injection of iron is largely avoided today because it causes painful staining of the skin and localized tissue irritation.

Vitamin B12 (Cobalamin): Physiology and Deficiency

Vitamin B12, or cobalamin, is vital for DNA replication and maintaining the myelin sheath protecting nerve fibers. Unlike iron, the body stores large amounts of vitamin B12 in the liver, meaning deficiencies can take years to manifest after dietary intake stops or absorption fails.

Absorption of dietary B12 requires intrinsic factor, a glycoprotein secreted by the parietal cells of the gastric mucosa. Without intrinsic factor—as seen in pernicious anemia or following a total gastrectomy—oral vitamin B12 cannot be absorbed in the terminal ileum, regardless of how much is consumed in the diet.

Clinical manifestations of B12 deficiency include megaloblastic anemia alongside progressive neurological symptoms such as peripheral numbness, tingling, and balance difficulties. Because neurological damage can become irreversible if left untreated, patients with B12 deficiency typically require lifetime intramuscular injections of hydroxocobalamin or cyanocobalamin.

Folic Acid and Megaloblastic Anemia Management

Folic acid, or vitamin B9, is a water-soluble vitamin required for nucleotide synthesis and cell division. Rapidly dividing tissues, such as bone marrow and the developing fetus, have a high demand for folate. Deficiency leads to impaired DNA synthesis, resulting in megaloblastic anemia, characterized by large, dysfunctional red blood cells.

Dietary folate is found in green leafy vegetables, legumes, and fortified grains. However, cooking and heat easily destroy dietary folates, making deficiency common in individuals with poor diets, alcoholism, or malabsorption syndromes like celiac disease.

A critical clinical rule governs folate supplementation: never treat vitamin B12 deficiency with folic acid alone. While folic acid will correct the blood count abnormalities of B12 deficiency, it does nothing to stop the progressive and permanent neurological damage caused by lack of cobalamin. Nurses must verify the exact cause of megaloblastic anemia before initiating single-agent therapy.

Adverse Effects and Toxicity Management

Haematinics are generally safe when used correctly, but they carry notable side effects that challenge patient compliance. Oral iron preparations frequently cause gastrointestinal disturbances, including constipation, nausea, epigastric discomfort, and dark or black stools. Patients must be reassured that dark stools are a harmless side effect of unabsorbed iron excretion and do not indicate gastrointestinal bleeding.

Accidental ingestion of iron tablets by children is a medical emergency that can lead to severe systemic iron toxicity, shock, and metabolic acidosis. Management involves supportive care and the use of deferoxamine, a specific iron-chelating agent that binds excess iron for renal excretion.

Parenteral iron therapy carries a risk of severe hypersensitivity reactions, ranging from mild rashes to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Because of this risk, emergency resuscitation equipment and epinephrine must be readily available whenever intravenous iron is infused.

Patient Assessment, Monitoring, and Nursing Responsibilities

Comprehensive nursing care for patients receiving haematinic therapy involves careful baseline assessment, ongoing laboratory monitoring, and patient education. Nurses should assess dietary habits, history of gastrointestinal surgeries, and symptoms like fatigue, pallor, shortness of breath, and glossitis (a smooth, sore tongue).

Laboratory monitoring focuses on hemoglobin levels, hematocrit, serum ferritin, and red blood cell indices to track treatment effectiveness. An upward trend in hemoglobin confirms successful therapy, though treatment typically continues for several months after blood counts normalize to replenish body iron stores in the liver and bone marrow.

Patient education remains vital for compliance. Nurses must explain how to manage mild side effects without stopping treatment abruptly. For liquid oral iron preparations, nurses should teach patients to use a straw and brush their teeth afterward to prevent permanent dental staining.
