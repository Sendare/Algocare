Hypoglycaemic Drugs

Introduction to Blood Glucose Regulation and Antidiabetic Drugs

Blood glucose regulation is a delicate balance managed primarily by the hormones insulin and glucagon, both produced by the pancreas. When blood sugar rises after a meal, the beta cells of the pancreas release insulin. Insulin acts like a key, unlocking body cells so glucose can enter and be used for energy or stored for later use. In diabetes mellitus, this system fails either because the body cannot produce enough insulin or because the cells stop responding to the insulin that is present.

Antidiabetic medications are pharmacological agents designed to lower blood glucose levels and restore this metabolic balance. They do not cure diabetes, but they manage blood sugar to prevent both short-term emergencies and long-term organ damage. As a nurse, understanding how these drugs work helps you anticipate therapeutic effects, recognize warning signs of danger, and educate patients effectively.

Classification of Anti-Diabetic Medications

Antidiabetic drugs are broadly divided into two main categories: insulin formulations and oral or injectable non-insulin agents. Choosing the right class depends on whether the patient has type 1 diabetes, where insulin is completely absent, or type 2 diabetes, where insulin resistance and relative deficiency are the core problems.

  • Insulin: Given parenterally because digestive enzymes destroy it in the gut; essential for all type 1 and advanced type 2 cases.
  • Oral Hypoglycaemics: Pills taken by mouth to lower blood sugar through various mechanisms in type 2 diabetes.
  • Injectable Non-Insulin Agents: Medications like GLP-1 receptor agonists administered subcutaneously to aid glycemic control and weight management.

Insulin Therapy: Types and Regimens

Insulin preparations are classified by how fast they start working, when they peak, and how long their effects last. Knowing these timelines is vital for preventing life-threatening drops in blood sugar.

Basal-bolus regimens mimic normal pancreatic function by combining a long-acting background insulin with rapid-acting insulin doses given before meals.

Oral Hypoglycaemic Agents: Biguanides, Sulfonylureas, and Meglitinides

Oral medications are the cornerstone of pharmacological management for type 2 diabetes when diet and exercise are not enough. Each class works through a distinct physiological pathway.

  1. Biguanides (Metformin): Decreases glucose production in the liver and improves insulin sensitivity in muscle tissue. It does not cause weight gain or low blood sugar on its own, making it the first-line drug.
  2. Sulfonylureas (Gliclazide, Glimepiride): Stimulate the pancreas to release more insulin. Because they push insulin out regardless of blood sugar levels, they carry a significant risk of hypoglycaemia and weight gain.
  3. Meglitinides (Repaglinide): Act similarly to sulfonylureas by stimulating insulin release, but they work much faster and have a shorter duration. Patients take them right before meals to control post-meal blood sugar spikes.

Incretin-Based Therapies and SGLT2 Inhibitors

Modern diabetes management includes newer drug classes that target different organ systems to control glucose without stimulating the pancreas directly.

  • SGLT2 Inhibitors (Empagliflozin, Dapagliflozin): These drugs work in the kidneys. They block the reabsorption of filtered glucose, causing the body to excrete excess sugar through the urine. A common clinical observation is mild weight loss and lower blood pressure, but patients must maintain good hygiene to prevent genital fungal infections due to sugar-rich urine.
  • DPP-4 Inhibitors and GLP-1 Receptor Agonists: These target the incretin system. They stimulate insulin secretion, slow stomach emptying, and suppress appetite after eating.

Adverse Effects and Management of Hypoglycaemia

Hypoglycaemia, defined as a blood glucose level below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL), is the most common and dangerous acute complication of glucose-lowering therapy. Symptoms are divided into autonomic warnings and neuroglycopenic signs.

  • Autonomic Symptoms: Sweating, trembling, tachycardia, palpitations, and intense hunger as the body releases adrenaline.
  • Neuroglycopenic Symptoms: Confusion, dizziness, blurred vision, slurred speech, and loss of consciousness as the brain is starved of glucose.

Management follows the Rule of 15: give 15 grams of fast-acting carbohydrates (such as half a glass of fruit juice or glucose tablets), wait 15 minutes, and recheck the blood sugar. Repeat if the level remains low.

Nursing Considerations and Patient Education in Nigeria

Managing diabetes in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria presents unique nursing challenges. Patients often navigate the co-use of orthodox medications and traditional herbal remedies, inconsistent electricity supply for storing insulin, and cultural beliefs regarding fasting and diet.

Nurses must provide practical education tailored to the local environment. Teach patients how to store insulin safely at room temperature using a clay pot filled with water (pot-in-pot cooler) if a refrigerator is unavailable. Emphasize the importance of consistent carbohydrate intake alongside medications like sulfonylureas to prevent sudden drops in blood sugar. Always assess for herbal concoctions that may dangerously interact with prescribed hypoglycaemic drugs.
