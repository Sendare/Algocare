Hypoglycaemic Drugs

Introduction to Hypoglycaemic Drugs and Blood Glucose Regulation

Blood glucose homeostasis relies on the delicate balance between insulin and glucagon, two pancreatic hormones secreted by the islets of Langerhans. Insulin, produced by beta cells, promotes glucose uptake into peripheral tissues and suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis. When this regulatory system fails, chronic hyperglycemia ensues, defining diabetes mellitus. Hypoglycaemic drugs are pharmacological agents designed to restore normoglycemia, preventing both acute metabolic emergencies and long-term microvascular or macrovascular complications. Understanding normal physiology is the foundation for managing any endocrine disorder.

Classification of Anti-Diabetic Medications

Anti-diabetic medications are broadly categorized based on their chemical structure, primary site of action, and whether they are administered orally or parenterally.

  • Insulins: Replacement therapy mimicking endogenous hormone profiles.
  • Oral Hypoglycaemic Agents (OHAs): Pills that stimulate insulin release, improve tissue sensitivity, or slow carbohydrate digestion.
  • Non-Insulin Injectables: Medications like GLP-1 receptor agonists that enhance incretin activity and promote satiety.

Insulin Therapy: Types, Regimen, and Administration

Insulin preparations are classified by their onset, peak action, and duration. Clinical practice utilizes rapid-acting, short-acting, intermediate-acting, and long-acting analogues. Basal-bolus regimens closely mimic physiological pancreatic secretion by combining a long-acting background insulin with rapid-acting doses before meals. Subcutaneous injection sites—abdomen, thighs, and arms—must be rotated systematically to prevent lipodystrophy. Nurses must verify the specific insulin type before administration, as mixing errors can precipitate life-threatening glycaemic fluctuations.

Oral Hypoglycaemic Agents: Biguanides, Sulfonylureas, and Meglitinides

  • Biguanides (Metformin): Decreases hepatic glucose production and increases insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues without causing direct hypoglycemia.
  • Sulfonylureas (Glibenclamide, Gliclazide): Stimulate insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells by closing ATP-sensitive potassium channels, carrying a notable risk of weight gain and hypoglycaemia.
  • Meglitinides (Repaglinide): Act similarly to sulfonylureas by stimulating rapid, short-acting insulin release, and are taken specifically before meals to control postprandial spikes.

Incretin-Based Therapies and SGLT2 Inhibitors

Modern anti-diabetic pharmacology leverages gut hormones and renal physiology. DPP-4 inhibitors prolong the action of endogenous incretins, while GLP-1 receptor agonists mimic them, enhancing glucose-dependent insulin secretion and delaying gastric emptying. Meanwhile, Sodium-Glucose Co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors—such as Empagliflozin—block renal glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubules, promoting glucosuria. This unique renal mechanism provides added cardiovascular and renal protection independent of insulin pathways.

Adverse Effects and Management of Hypoglycaemia

Hypoglycaemia, defined as a blood glucose level below 3.9 mmol/L, is a medical emergency. Autonomic symptoms include tremors, sweating, tachycardia, and anxiety, while neuroglycopenic symptoms involve confusion, blurred vision, and seizures. Conscious patients should receive fast-acting oral carbohydrates using the rule of fifteen: consume 15 grams of glucose and recheck after 15 minutes. Unconscious patients require parenteral interventions such as intravenous 50% dextrose or intramuscular glucagon.

Nursing Considerations and Patient Education in Nigeria

Clinical management of diabetes in resource-limited settings like Nigeria demands culturally tailored nursing interventions. Nurses must assess affordability, access to refrigeration for insulin, and concurrent use of herbal remedies. Patient education must emphasize proper glucometer use, recognition of hypoglycemia, and foot care to prevent diabetic ulcers. Addressing traditional misconceptions regarding orthodox medicine ensures adherence and improves long-term health outcomes for patients living with diabetes.
