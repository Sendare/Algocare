Drugs Used in the Treatment of Hyperthyroidism

Introduction to Hyperthyroidism and Antithyroid Therapy Goals

Hyperthyroidism occurs when the thyroid gland becomes overactive, pumping excessive amounts of thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) into the bloodstream. This metabolic overdrive accelerates every organ system in the body. Patients present with heat intolerance, weight loss despite increased appetite, tachycardia, tremors, and severe anxiety.

Antithyroid therapy aims to restore a normal metabolic state, known as euthyroidism, without permanently destroying the gland unless radioactive iodine or surgery is specifically chosen. Nurses must understand that treatment is not a quick fix; normalizing thyroid hormone levels can take several weeks because the gland stores large amounts of preformed hormone.

Thioamides: Mechanism of Action and Pharmacokinetics

Thioamides are the cornerstone of medical management for hyperthyroidism. The two primary agents used in clinical practice are propylthiouracil (PTU) and methimazole.

  • Mechanism of Action: Both drugs inhibit the enzyme thyroid peroxidase, which prevents the oxidation of iodide and the coupling of iodotyrosines. This stops the synthesis of new thyroid hormones. Additionally, PTU has a unique extra action: it inhibits the peripheral conversion of T4 into the more active T3 hormone.
  • Pharmacokinetics: Methimazole has a longer half-life, allowing for convenient once-daily dosing, whereas PTU requires multiple daily doses due to its shorter half-life. Methimazole is generally preferred for long-term therapy because it is more potent and carries a lower risk of severe liver injury.

Clinical Uses and Administration of PTU and Methimazole

Choosing between PTU and methimazole depends heavily on the patient's physiological status and specific life stages.

Methimazole is the frontline agent for most patients due to its once-daily dosing schedule and safer liver profile. However, propylthiouracil (PTU) is strictly preferred during the first trimester of pregnancy. Methimazole is linked to a rare birth defect called aplasia cutis when taken in early pregnancy. PTU is also the drug of choice in managing thyroid storm because of its added benefit of stopping peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion.

Nurses must teach patients to take their medication at the same time every day with or without food, and to report missed doses immediately without doubling up.

Radioactive Iodine (Iodine-131) Therapy

Radioactive iodine (I-131) is a definitive, non-surgical treatment option for hyperthyroidism, especially in Graves' disease or toxic multinodular goiter. Administered orally as a single capsule or liquid dose, the isotope concentrates inside the thyroid gland because the thyroid actively takes up iodine. Once inside, the emitted radiation destroys overactive thyroid cells locally, shrinking the gland and reducing hormone production over two to three months.

This therapy is strictly contraindicated in pregnancy because it crosses the placenta and destroys the fetal thyroid gland. A baseline pregnancy test is mandatory for all women of childbearing potential before administration. A common long-term outcome of I-131 therapy is permanent hypothyroidism, requiring lifelong thyroid hormone replacement therapy.

Adjuvant Therapy: Role of Beta-Blockers in Symptom Control

While thioamides and radioactive iodine address the root cause of excess hormone production, they do nothing for immediate physical discomfort. Beta-blockers (such as propranolol or atenolol) act as vital adjuvant medications to manage adrenergic symptoms like tachycardia, tremors, sweating, anxiety, and palpitations.

Beta-blockers do not lower thyroid hormone levels in the blood; instead, they block the action of excess hormones on target organs. Propranolol has an added advantage at higher doses because it weakly inhibits the peripheral conversion of T4 to T3. Nurses must monitor pulse and blood pressure closely before administering these drugs, holding the dose if the heart rate drops below established parameters.

Iodine Solutions and Their Acute Preoperative Applications

High doses of iodide solutions, such as Lugol's solution or saturated solution of potassium iodide (SSKI), have a paradoxical effect on the thyroid gland. Instead of feeding hormone production, large amounts temporarily inhibit the release of thyroid hormones into the bloodstream through the Wolff-Chaikoff effect, and reduce the vascularity and size of the gland.

This rapid action makes iodine solutions ideal for acute preoperative preparation before a thyroidectomy. Shrinking the gland and reducing blood supply minimizes the dangerous risk of severe intraoperative hemorrhage. Because this inhibitory effect is temporary and typically wears off after 10 to 14 days, these solutions are rarely used for long-term maintenance.

Adverse Effects, Toxicity, and Nursing Monitoring Parameters

Antithyroid medications carry serious potential adverse effects that require diligent nursing oversight.

  • Agranulocytosis: A severe, life-threatening drop in white blood cells (neutrophils) that leaves the patient vulnerable to overwhelming infections. Patients must be taught to report fever and sore throat immediately.
  • Hepatotoxicity: PTU carries a black-box warning for severe liver injury and acute liver failure, whereas methimazole is more frequently linked to cholestatic jaundice.
  • Monitoring: Baseline and periodic complete blood counts (CBC) and liver function tests (LFTs) are mandatory. Nurses also assess for signs of hypothyroidism—such as lethargy, cold intolerance, and weight gain—which indicate the medication dosage is too high.

Patient Education and Special Considerations in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Providing care in diverse healthcare settings across Nigeria requires nurses to address unique socioeconomic and logistical realities. Many patients present late with advanced goiters and severe thyrotoxicosis due to delays in reaching tertiary care facilities, financial constraints, or initial reliance on traditional herbal remedies.

Nurses must provide culturally sensitive education, emphasizing the importance of adherence to daily medication schedules despite improving symptoms. Educational discussions should cover dietary sources of iodine, the necessity of keeping follow-up clinic appointments for blood tests, and recognizing red-flag symptoms like extreme fatigue, yellowing eyes, or high fever. Bridging the gap between orthodox medicine and local health practices ensures safer therapeutic outcomes.
