Drugs Used in the Treatment of Hypothyroidism

Introduction to Hypothyroidism and Thyroid Hormone Physiology

Hypothyroidism is a clinical state resulting from inadequate production of thyroid hormones by the thyroid gland, or rarely, from peripheral resistance to these hormones. In a healthy body, the hypothalamus releases thyrotropin-releasing hormone, which stimulates the anterior pituitary to secrete thyroid-stimulating hormone. Thyroid-stimulating hormone then signals the thyroid gland to synthesize and release two main hormones: thyroxine and triiodothyronine. These hormones regulate basal metabolic rate, oxygen consumption, cellular growth, and the metabolism of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.

When hormone levels drop, the body attempts to compensate by increasing thyroid-stimulating hormone output. Therefore, an elevated serum thyroid-stimulating hormone level serves as the primary laboratory marker for primary hypothyroidism. Patients typically experience symptoms reflecting a slowed metabolic rate, such as fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, and sluggish heart rate. Recognizing this physiological background is the first step in understanding why hormone replacement is necessary.

Classification and Types of Thyroid Hormone Replacement Drugs

Pharmacological management of hypothyroidism relies on replacing deficient hormones with synthetic or natural preparations. These agents restore normal metabolic function and relieve deficiency symptoms.

  • Levothyroxine: A synthetic form of thyroxine. It is the gold standard and drug of choice for long-term hormone replacement because of its predictable potency and smooth onset of action.
  • Liothyronine: A synthetic form of triiodothyronine. It has a rapid onset and short duration of action, making it useful in acute settings like myxedema coma, but less ideal for daily maintenance due to cardiac side effect risks.
  • Desiccated Thyroid: Derived from dried and powdered animal thyroid glands, usually porcine. It contains both thyroxine and triiodothyronine in a fixed ratio, though it is less frequently prescribed today due to batch-to-batch potency variations.

Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics of Levothyroxine

Levothyroxine is absorbed primarily in the small intestine, particularly the duodenum and jejunum. Oral absorption is variable, ranging from 40% to 80%, and is significantly reduced by food, fiber, and certain medications. Once absorbed, more than 99% of the drug binds tightly to plasma proteins, leaving only a tiny fraction free to enter target cells and exert metabolic effects.

Inside the body's peripheral tissues, levothyroxine acts as a prohormone. Enzymes convert it into its active form, triiodothyronine, by removing an iodine atom. Triiodothyronine then binds to nuclear receptors inside cells, altering gene expression to boost metabolic activity. Because of its long half-life of approximately seven days, levothyroxine takes about four to six weeks to reach a steady state in the bloodstream. This means dose adjustments should never be made too frequently.

Clinical Indications, Dosing Principles, and Administration Guidelines

Levothyroxine is indicated for primary, secondary, and tertiary hypothyroidism, as well as congenital hypothyroidism and certain types of thyroid cancer. Dosing follows a strict individualization principle. For healthy young adults, therapy can begin at full replacement doses. However, for elderly patients or individuals with pre-existing cardiovascular disease, clinicians start with a low dose and titrate slowly upward. A sudden increase in metabolic demand can trigger angina, arrhythmias, or myocardial infarction in a weakened heart.

Administration guidelines require taking the medication consistently every morning, typically 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, with a full glass of water. Maintaining consistency in the brand or manufacturer is also vital, as minor variations in bioequivalence between different generic formulations can alter therapeutic blood levels.

Adverse Effects, Toxicity, and Drug Interactions

When thyroid hormone replacement is properly dosed, adverse effects are rare because the body is simply returning to a normal physiological state. Toxicity symptoms mimic hyperthyroidism, resulting from over-replacement. Patients may present with palpitations, tachycardia, nervousness, tremors, heat intolerance, weight loss, and insomnia. Chronic over-replacement can also lead to accelerated bone loss and osteoporosis over time.

Several medications interact with levothyroxine by altering its absorption or metabolism. Calcium carbonate, ferrous sulfate, magnesium-containing antacids, and bile acid sequestrants bind to the drug in the gut, reducing absorption. Conversely, drugs that induce liver enzymes, such as rifampin or certain anticonvulsants, speed up the breakdown of thyroid hormones. Nurses must coordinate medication schedules to separate these interacting substances by at least four hours.

Nursing Assessment, Monitoring, and Patient Education in Nigeria

Providing care for hypothyroid patients in resource-constrained environments requires a strong focus on patient education and clinical follow-up. Nurses must assess baseline vital signs, weight, energy levels, and bowel habits before therapy begins. Laboratory monitoring of thyroid-stimulating hormone levels guides ongoing dosage adjustments, typically performed every six to eight weeks until a stable maintenance dose is reached, and annually thereafter.

Patient education must address the chronic nature of the condition. Many patients in local communities may feel better within weeks and mistakenly assume they are cured, leading to poor adherence. Nurses should emphasize that therapy is usually lifelong. Furthermore, teaching patients how to monitor their own pulse and recognizing signs of both under-treatment and over-treatment empowers them to manage their health safely.
