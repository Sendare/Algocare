Drugs Used in the Treatment of Hypothyroidism

Introduction to Hypothyroidism and Thyroid Hormone Physiology

The thyroid gland relies on a delicate feedback loop involving the hypothalamus and the anterior pituitary gland to regulate basal metabolic rate, growth, and development. When the hypothalamus detects low circulating thyroid hormones, it secretes thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH). This stimulates the pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which directs the thyroid gland to synthesize and release thyroxine ($T_4$) and triiodothyronine ($T_3$). In hypothyroidism, this axis malfunctions. The thyroid gland fails to produce adequate hormone levels, resulting in a systemic slowing of metabolic processes. Common clinical manifestations include fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, constipation, bradycardia, and dry skin. In severe, prolonged cases, myxedema coma can develop, representing a life-threatening medical emergency characterized by hypothermia, hypoventilation, and progressive alteration of consciousness.

Classification and Pharmacology of Thyroid Hormone Replacements

Therapeutic management of hypothyroidism requires exogenous hormone replacement to restore euthyroid status. The available pharmacologic agents mimic endogenous hormones and differ primarily in their chemical structure, potency, and onset of action. - **Levothyroxine ($T_4$)**: A synthetic replica of the primary hormone produced by the thyroid gland, serving as the gold standard for long-term management. - **Liothyronine ($T_3$)**: A synthetic preparation of the biologically active triiodothyronine, characterized by rapid onset and short duration of action. - **Liotrix**: A fixed-ratio combination of synthetic $T_4$ and $T_3$ in a 4:1 ratio, though rarely prescribed today due to lack of clinical advantage over levothyroxine alone. - **Desiccated Thyroid Extract**: Derived from animal thyroid glands (usually porcine), containing natural mixtures of both $T_4$ and $T_3$, though standardization of hormone content remains a historical concern.

Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics of Levothyroxine

Levothyroxine shares the exact pharmacokinetic profile of endogenous thyroxine. Following oral administration, absorption occurs primarily in the small intestine, specifically the jejunum and ileum. Food intake, fiber, calcium, and iron supplements significantly impair this absorption process. Once absorbed, over 99% of circulating levothyroxine binds tightly to plasma proteins, primarily thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG). Only the unbound, free fraction is pharmacologically active. In peripheral tissues, cellular enzymes known as deiodinases convert the inactive prohormone $T_4$ into the active hormone $T_3$. Because levothyroxine possesses a prolonged plasma half-life of approximately seven days, it takes about four to six weeks of daily dosing to achieve a steady-state serum concentration. This pharmacokinetic reality explains why dosage adjustments should never be made more frequently than every six weeks.

Clinical Indications, Dosing Principles, and Administration Guidelines

Levothyroxine is indicated for primary, secondary, and tertiary hypothyroidism, as well as congenital hypothyroidism and thyroid-stimulating hormone suppression therapy in thyroid cancer management. Dosing must be individualized based on age, body weight, and cardiovascular status. Young, healthy patients can often start on full replacement doses. Conversely, elderly patients or individuals with pre-existing ischemic heart disease require a cautious "start low and go slow" approach, typically beginning with microgram-level fractions to prevent precipitating angina, myocardial infarction, or severe arrhythmias. Optimal administration requires taking the medication orally on an empty stomach with a full glass of water, usually 30 to 60 minutes before breakfast, to maximize consistent gastrointestinal absorption.

Adverse Effects, Toxicity, and Drug Interactions

Adverse effects of thyroid hormone replacement typically mimic the clinical presentation of hyperthyroidism and stem from iatrogenic over-replacement or excessive dosing. Manifestations include tachycardia, palpitations, chest pain, tremors, nervousness, insomnia, weight loss, heat intolerance, and excessive sweating. Chronic overtreatment can also accelerate bone resorption, increasing the long-term risk of osteoporosis, particularly in postmenopausal women. Numerous pharmacological interactions alter thyroid hormone efficacy. Drugs that bind or chelate levothyroxine in the gastrointestinal tract—such as calcium carbonate, ferrous sulfate, and aluminum-containing antacids—reduce absorption and must be separated by at least four hours. Conversely, hepatic enzyme inducers like rifampin, carbamazepine, and phenytoin accelerate the metabolic clearance of thyroid hormones, potentially necessitating a dosage increase.

Nursing Assessment, Monitoring, and Patient Education in Nigeria

Comprehensive nursing care requires systematic assessment of baseline metabolic status, cardiovascular parameters, and weight trends. Nurses play a vital role in monitoring laboratory markers, particularly serum thyroid-stimulating hormone levels, which serve as the primary guide for therapeutic efficacy. Follow-up blood draws are scheduled regularly until the target range is stably maintained. In clinical settings across Nigeria, patient education must address local dietary patterns and concurrent herbal or traditional remedies. Nurses should explicitly counsel patients to avoid taking their morning levothyroxine dose with calcium-rich local foods or herbal preparations that might bind the drug. Emphasizing lifelong adherence is critical, as abrupt discontinuation leads to a return of debilitating symptoms and potential cardiovascular instability.
