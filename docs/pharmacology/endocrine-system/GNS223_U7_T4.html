Steroids

Introduction to Corticosteroids and Endocrine Physiology

Corticosteroids are powerful hormones naturally produced by the adrenal cortex. The body secretes two main types: glucocorticoids, such as cortisol, which regulate metabolism and immune response, and mineralocorticoids, such as aldosterone, which control electrolyte balance and blood pressure. Regulation of these hormones relies on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone, triggering the anterior pituitary to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone, which then stimulates the adrenal glands to release cortisol. Synthetic corticosteroids mimic these natural hormones to reduce inflammation and suppress immune activity in various disease states.

Classification and Mechanisms of Action

Corticosteroids act by crossing cell membranes and binding to intracellular glucocorticoid receptors. This hormone-receptor complex enters the cell nucleus, where it alters gene transcription. Specifically, these drugs inhibit pro-inflammatory enzymes like phospholipase A2 and suppress inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes. Clinically, they are categorized by their potency and duration of action. Short-acting agents include hydrocortisone, intermediate-acting agents include prednisolone and methylprednisolone, and long-acting agents include dexamethasone.

Clinical Indications in Nursing Practice

Nurses administer corticosteroids across diverse clinical specialties due to their potent anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties.

  • Respiratory disorders: Managing acute exacerbations of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
  • Autoimmune conditions: Controlling flare-ups of rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus.
  • Allergic reactions: Treating severe anaphylaxis, contact dermatitis, and drug hypersensitivities.
  • Neoplasms: Reducing cerebral edema associated with brain tumors and supporting chemotherapy regimens.

Adverse Effects of Short-Term and Long-Term Therapy

Short-term therapy often brings mild, manageable side effects, whereas prolonged use causes widespread systemic changes.

  • Short-term effects: Hyperglycemia, increased appetite, mood changes, insomnia, and fluid retention.
  • Long-term effects: Osteoporosis, muscle wasting, delayed wound healing, increased susceptibility to infection, and fat redistribution.

A common misconception is that short-term bursts of oral steroids are entirely harmless; however, even brief courses can cause significant blood glucose spikes in vulnerable patients.

Patient Assessment and Monitoring Parameters

Nurses must systematically monitor patients receiving systemic corticosteroids to catch adverse changes early. Regular assessments include tracking daily weights for fluid retention, measuring blood pressure for hypertension, and checking blood glucose levels, especially in patients with diabetes. Nurses also monitor wound sites for signs of delayed healing and assess for subtle signs of infection, such as low-grade fever, since classic inflammatory signs may be masked by the medication.

Cushingoid Features and Adrenal Suppression

Prolonged exposure to excess corticosteroids leads to iatrogenic Cushing's syndrome, characterized by specific physical changes. These include a moon face, buffalo hump, central obesity, and purple abdominal striae. Simultaneously, exogenous steroid intake suppresses the HPA axis through negative feedback. The adrenal cortex shrinks from lack of stimulation, leaving the body unable to produce its own cortisol if the drug is stopped suddenly.

Safe Tapering and Patient Education

Abruptly discontinuing corticosteroids after prolonged use can trigger acute adrenal crisis, characterized by severe hypotension, hypoglycemia, vomiting, and circulatory collapse. To prevent this, nurses must educate patients never to stop their medication suddenly. Instead, doses are gradually reduced over weeks or months to allow the HPA axis time to resume natural cortisol production. Nurses should also instruct patients to carry an emergency identification card indicating their steroid use.
