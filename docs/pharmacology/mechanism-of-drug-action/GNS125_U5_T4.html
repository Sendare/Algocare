Drug Excretion

Introduction and Major Routes of Drug Excretion

Drug excretion is the irreversible removal of drugs and their metabolites from the body. While the kidneys do most of the heavy lifting, the body uses several other exit routes depending on the chemical properties of the substance. Understanding these pathways helps nurses anticipate how long a drug stays in a patient's system.

  • Renal excretion: The primary route for water-soluble drugs and small molecules.
  • Biliary excretion: Eliminates larger, lipid-soluble compounds through the liver and bile into the feces.
  • Pulmonary excretion: Exhales volatile gases and anesthetics from the lungs.
  • Glandular excretion: Secretes small amounts through sweat, saliva, tears, and breast milk.

Renal Excretion Mechanisms

The kidney handles drug elimination through three distinct processes occurring within the nephron. First, glomerular filtration pushes unbound, small-molecule drugs out of the blood plasma and into the renal tubules. Next, tubular secretion uses active transport pumps in the proximal tubule to actively shove remaining drug molecules from the blood capillaries directly into the urine filtrate. Finally, tubular reabsorption can pull lipid-soluble drugs back out of the tubule and into the bloodstream, slowing down their exit until the liver modifies them to be more water-soluble.

Factors Affecting Renal Excretion

A patient's ability to clear drugs through their kidneys changes based on physiological variables. Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) sets the baseline pace; if kidney function drops, drug clearance plummets. Urine pH also plays a major role through ion trapping. If a nurse administers a weak base drug, making the urine more acidic causes the drug to become ionized, trapping it in the urine so it cannot diffuse back into the blood. Additionally, plasma protein binding keeps large molecules confined to the circulation, preventing them from slipping through the glomerulus.

Biliary and Fecal Excretion

Not all medications leave through urine. The liver pulls certain large or fat-soluble compounds out of the blood and dumps them into bile. Once bile empties into the small intestine, these drugs pass out of the body in the feces.

Some drugs undergo enterohepatic recirculation. After entering the intestine via bile, friendly gut bacteria can strip away chemical tags, turning the drug back into a fat-soluble form that gets reabsorbed through the intestinal wall back into the bloodstream. This recycling loop delays final elimination and keeps the drug active in the body much longer than expected.

Alternative Routes of Excretion

Beyond urine and feces, the body uses minor pathways to clear medications. The lungs eliminate alcohol and volatile general anesthetics, which is why a patient's breath smells of alcohol after drinking. Mammary excretion is critical because many drugs pass from maternal blood into breast milk. Nursing mothers must be cautious because lipid-soluble drugs easily enter milk and can affect the nursing infant. Sweat and saliva also eliminate tiny amounts of certain drugs, occasionally causing skin rashes or metallic tastes in the mouth.

Clearance and Half-Life

Two key pharmacokinetic values measure how fast a drug leaves the body. Clearance describes the volume of blood cleared of a drug per unit of time, usually measured in milliliters per minute. Half-life ($t_{1/2}$) is the time required for the plasma concentration of a drug to drop by half.

Drugs with short half-lives leave quickly and need frequent dosing, while drugs with long half-lives take days to clear and carry a higher risk of accumulation. It generally takes about four to five half-lives for a drug to be almost completely eliminated from the body after stopping administration.

Clinical Implications in Nursing Practice

Nurses play a direct role in monitoring drug excretion to prevent toxicity. In older adults, normal aging reduces kidney and liver function, which lowers drug clearance and extends half-life. Standard adult doses can easily cause toxicity in elderly patients unless adjusted. Nurses must monitor laboratory values like serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) to catch declining renal function early. Adjusting dosing schedules or holding medications when excretion organs fail is a core safety responsibility.
