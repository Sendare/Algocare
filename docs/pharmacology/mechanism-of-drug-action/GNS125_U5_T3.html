Drug Metabolism

Introduction to Drug Metabolism and Biotransformation

Drug metabolism, also known as biotransformation, is the biochemical alteration of a pharmaceutical substance within the living body. When a lipophilic drug enters the systemic circulation, the body typically treats it as a foreign xenobiotic, setting off enzymatic processes designed to transform active, fat-soluble compounds into inactive, water-soluble metabolites. These hydrophilic derivatives can then be readily excreted through the kidneys via urine or through the biliary tract into feces.

Without this vital conversion, many lipid-soluble medications would persist in fatty tissues indefinitely, leading to prolonged pharmacological action and severe toxicity. Biotransformation generally converts pharmacologically active drugs into inactive forms, though notable exceptions exist. Some substances are administered as inactive prodrugs that rely entirely on metabolic conversion within the body to generate their active therapeutic moiety.

Primary Sites of Drug Metabolism in the Human Body

While virtually every tissue possesses some capacity to metabolize xenobiotics, certain organs contain exceptionally high concentrations of drug-metabolizing enzymes. The liver stands unquestionably as the primary and most robust site of drug metabolism, equipped with a massive complement of smooth endoplasmic reticulum and cytosolic enzymes.

Other significant extra-hepatic sites include:

  • Gastrointestinal tract: Intestinal wall enterocytes express notable metabolizing enzymes and transporter proteins, contributing significantly to pre-systemic drug extraction.
  • Kidneys: Renal parenchyma handles both drug filtration and select enzymatic transformations before urinary elimination.
  • Lungs: Pulmonary endothelial cells process circulating gaseous agents and endogenous amines through localized enzymatic activity.
  • Skin: Cutaneous tissues possess limited metabolic capacity, primarily relevant for topically applied dermatological formulations.

Phase I Reactions: Oxidation, Reduction, and Hydrolysis

Phase I reactions modify chemical structures by introducing or unmasking a polar functional group, such as hydroxyl (-OH), amino (-NH2), or sulfhydryl (-SH). This slight structural alteration often results in a minor increase in water solubility and, crucially, prepares the molecule for subsequent Phase II conjugation.

  1. Oxidation: The addition of oxygen atoms or removal of hydrogen atoms, predominantly catalyzed by the cytochrome P450 enzyme superfamily.
  2. Reduction: The addition of hydrogen atoms or removal of oxygen atoms, frequently occurring in low-oxygen environments such as the gut lumen or hepatic interior.
  3. Hydrolysis: The chemical breakdown of a compound through the incorporation of a water molecule, splitting ester, amide, or peptide bonds via specialized enzymes like esterases.

The Cytochrome P450 Enzyme System

The Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) system represents a massive superfamily of heme-containing membrane proteins located primarily in the hepatic endoplasmic reticulum. These enzymes serve as the workhorses of Phase I oxidation, responsible for the biotransformation of the vast majority of clinically prescribed medications as well as endogenous steroids and environmental toxins.

Nomenclature uses family numbers, subfamilies designated by letters, and individual gene numbers (e.g., CYP3A4). Among all variants, CYP3A4 metabolizes the greatest diversity of foreign drugs. Genetic polymorphisms within this system explain why identical doses of standard antihypertensives or antimalarials can produce therapeutic failure in one person and profound toxicity in another.

Phase II Reactions: Conjugation Pathways

If a Phase I metabolite is still not sufficiently polar to be excreted, it enters Phase II. These reactions involve the attachment of a small, highly polar, endogenous molecule to the drug or its Phase I metabolite. This process is known as conjugation.

Common conjugation pathways include:

  • Glucuronidation: Attachment of glucuronic acid via UDP-glucuronosyltransferases, making the compound very water-soluble.
  • Sulfation: Addition of a sulfate group, prominent in the metabolism of phenols and alcohols.
  • Acetylation: Transfer of an acetyl group from acetyl-CoA, notable for drugs like isoniazid.
  • Glutathione conjugation: Attachment of glutathione to neutralize reactive, potentially toxic electrophilic intermediates.

Phase II products are almost universally pharmacologically inactive and readily cleared by renal or biliary elimination.

Enzyme Induction and Inhibition

Metabolic enzyme activity is dynamic and susceptible to external chemical influences. Certain medications and environmental agents can accelerate or suppress the function of CYP450 enzymes, triggering dangerous drug interactions.

Enzyme induction occurs when a substance stimulates transcription of CYP genes, leading to increased enzyme synthesis over several days. This accelerates the metabolism of co-administered drugs, often dropping their plasma concentrations below therapeutic thresholds. Conversely, enzyme inhibition happens when a drug blocks the active site of a metabolic enzyme or destroys it. This decelerates clearance, causing parent drug accumulation, elevated serum levels, and heightened toxicity risks.

First-Pass Effect and Bioavailability

When an oral medication is swallowed, it dissolves in the gastrointestinal lumen, passes through the intestinal wall, and travels via the mesenteric vessels straight into the portal vein system directly to the liver. Before ever reaching the systemic arterial circulation, a substantial fraction of the drug may be enzymatically degraded by hepatic and intestinal enzymes. This phenomenon is known as the first-pass effect (or pre-systemic metabolism).

Because of this hepatic clearance, the percentage of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged—known as bioavailability—is significantly reduced for oral formulations compared to intravenous administration. Drugs with exceptionally high first-pass metabolism require much higher oral doses than parenteral doses to achieve equivalent therapeutic plasma concentrations.

Factors Affecting Drug Metabolism in Nigerian Populations

Pharmacokinetics is profoundly influenced by genetic, environmental, and nutritional variables unique to specific geographical regions. In Nigerian and broader West African populations, genetic polymorphisms in enzymes such as CYP2C19 and CYP2B6 (vital for processing common antiretrovirals and antimalarials) display distinct phenotypic distributions.

Furthermore, environmental exposures, endemic infections like malaria, dietary factors involving local staples, and the concurrent use of herbal remedies can alter hepatic enzyme expression. Herbal preparations commonly consumed alongside orthodox medicines can act as potent enzyme inducers or inhibitors. Recognizing these population-specific and individual variables ensures safer prescribing practices and minimizes adverse drug reactions in local clinical settings.
