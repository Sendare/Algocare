Drug Metabolism

Introduction to Drug Metabolism and Biotransformation

Drug metabolism, also known as biotransformation, is the chemical alteration of foreign substances, or xenobiotics, inside the living body. When a person swallows a tablet or receives an injection, the body recognizes that foreign chemical as something that does not belong there. The main goal of metabolism is to change these active, lipid-soluble drugs into inactive, water-soluble compounds so the kidneys can easily filter and flush them out in urine. Without this process, many fat-soluble medicines would stay trapped inside fatty tissues and build up to toxic levels.

Most medications start out active and become inactive after passing through metabolic pathways. However, a special group of drugs called prodrugs are given in an inactive form on purpose. These prodrugs rely entirely on normal body metabolism to convert them into the active medicine that heals the patient. Understanding how the body changes these molecules helps nurses predict how long a drug will stay active and when it will leave the body.

Primary Sites of Drug Metabolism in the Human Body

While almost every living tissue has some capacity to break down chemicals, the liver is the undisputed powerhouse of drug metabolism. Liver cells contain the highest concentration and widest variety of drug-altering enzymes in the human body.

Beyond the liver, several other organs contribute significantly to biotransformation:

  • Gastrointestinal tract: The wall of the stomach and intestines contains enzymes and bacteria that begin breaking down oral drugs before they even reach the portal vein.
  • Kidneys: While their main job is excretion, renal tissues also metabolize certain circulating compounds.
  • Lungs: Pulmonary tissues can process circulating anesthetics and other volatile substances.
  • Plasma: Blood contains floating enzymes that rapidly chop up specific drug molecules, such as certain muscle relaxants.

Phase I Reactions: Oxidation, Reduction, and Hydrolysis

Phase I reactions are often called the functionalization steps. These processes either introduce a new polar, water-soluble group onto a drug molecule or uncover an existing one, such as a hydroxyl or amine group. This slight structural change usually reduces the pharmacological activity of the drug, though some molecules remain active.

  1. Oxidation: The addition of oxygen atoms or removal of hydrogen atoms, mostly carried out by liver enzymes.
  2. Reduction: The addition of hydrogen atoms or removal of oxygen atoms, lowering the oxidation state of the drug.
  3. Hydrolysis: The breakdown of a drug molecule by adding a water molecule, which splits chemical bonds like esters or amides.

Sometimes, a Phase I reaction does not make the drug ready for excretion yet. Instead, it creates an intermediate metabolite that must pass through a second chemical pathway before the body can safely get rid of it.

The Cytochrome P450 Enzyme System

The Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) system is a massive family of hemoproteins located primarily in the smooth endoplasmic reticulum of liver cells. These enzymes handle the vast majority of Phase I drug oxidation reactions. While there are many CYP enzymes, a few specific families—notably CYP3A4, CYP2D6, and CYP2C9—metabolize the large majority of all clinical medications prescribed today.

Because so many different drugs rely on these exact same enzymes, they often have to compete for processing time. If two drugs use the exact same CYP pathway, one drug may slow down the breakdown of the other, leading to an accidental drug overdose even when the patient took the correct dose. Nurses must always check for potential drug interactions when a patient is prescribed multiple medications processed by the CYP450 system.

Phase II Reactions: Conjugation Pathways

If a drug molecule remains too fat-soluble after a Phase I reaction, it enters Phase II conjugation. These pathways attach a large, highly polar, endogenous chemical group to the drug molecule. This process is like slapping a large neon shipping label on the drug so the body easily recognizes it as waste.

  • Glucuronidation: Attaches glucuronic acid to the drug, making it very water-soluble.
  • Sulfation: Adds a sulfate group, common in processing hormones and certain drugs.
  • Acetylation and Methylation: Adds acetyl or methyl groups to alter drug activity and prepare them for removal.

Unlike Phase I reactions which can sometimes yield active intermediates, Phase II conjugation almost always produces inactive, highly polar compounds that are ready for rapid excretion through bile or urine.

Enzyme Induction and Inhibition

The liver is remarkably adaptable. Its metabolic enzymes can increase or decrease their working speed based on chemical signals from outside substances.

Enzyme induction happens when a drug or substance (an inducer) stimulates the liver to produce more CYP450 enzyme molecules. Because there are now extra workers in the liver factory, any drug processed by that system gets broken down much faster. This drops the drug concentration in the blood, often causing the medication to fail.

Enzyme inhibition occurs when a substance blocks or slows down enzyme activity. Without working enzymes, the target drug stays in the blood much longer than planned, which can easily trigger dangerous toxicity. A classic everyday example is grapefruit juice, which inhibits gut enzymes and causes certain blood pressure medications to flood the bloodstream at dangerously high levels.

First-Pass Effect and Bioavailability

When a patient swallows a pill, it dissolves in the stomach and travels through the intestinal wall into the portal vein, heading straight to the liver before reaching the rest of the body. As the blood flows through the liver for the very first time, a large portion of the drug may be destroyed or metabolized before it ever reaches systemic circulation. This phenomenon is known as the first-pass effect.

Because of this initial liver breakdown, oral doses of certain medications must be much higher than intravenous doses to achieve the same therapeutic effect. Bioavailability measures the exact fraction of an administered drug that actually reaches the systemic circulation in an active form. Intravenous medications have a bioavailability of 100% because they skip the digestive tract and liver entirely, entering the bloodstream directly.

Factors Affecting Drug Metabolism in Nigerian Populations

Drug metabolism is not identical in every human being. Genetic makeup, nutritional status, age, liver health, and environmental exposures all shape how quickly a person processes medications.

Pharmacogenetics reveals that people inherit different versions of CYP450 genes, categorizing them as poor, intermediate, extensive, or ultrarapid metabolizers. In diverse West African populations, genetic variations in enzymes like CYP2B6 influence how patients process essential medicines, such as antiretroviral therapies used in HIV management. Additionally, dietary factors, herbal supplements like garlic or bitter leaf preparations, and local infections that cause inflammation can alter liver enzyme activity. Nurses must remain aware that standard textbook dosages may need individualized adjustments based on patient genetics and local clinical contexts to ensure safe and effective therapy.
