Drug Actions

Introduction and Mechanism of Drug Action

Drugs do not magically create brand-new functions inside the human body. Instead, they interact with existing biological processes to either speed them up or slow them down. When a drug enters the body, it travels through the bloodstream until it reaches its target site, where it produces a specific biochemical change. This desired biological response is what we call the drug action. Understanding how medicines work at the cellular level helps nurses anticipate both the healing effects and the unwanted side effects of treatments administered in the ward.

Receptor Theory and Drug-Receptor Interactions

Most drugs work by binding to specialized protein structures located on cell membranes or inside cells, known as receptors. Think of this relationship like a lock and a key; only a specific drug key will fit securely into a designated receptor lock. When the right drug attaches to its receptor, it triggers a chemical change inside the cell.

  • Affinity: The strength of the attraction between a drug and its specific receptor.
  • Intrinsic activity: The ability of a drug to activate the receptor and produce a biological response after binding.

A drug with high affinity will bind tightly to the receptor even when present in very small amounts.

Agonists, Antagonists, and Partial Agonists

Once a drug binds to a receptor, it can take on different roles depending on what kind of response it triggers.

  • Agonist: A drug that binds to a receptor and mimics the body's natural chemical messengers, producing a full cellular response.
  • Antagonist: A drug that blocks a receptor, stopping natural substances or other drugs from attaching and creating an effect.
  • Partial agonist: A drug that binds to a receptor but produces a weaker, less intense response than a full agonist, even when all receptors are occupied.

Clinical observation shows that giving an antagonist to a patient who has overdosed on a strong agonist can quickly reverse the dangerous respiratory depression.

Non-Receptor Mediated Drug Actions

Not every medicine relies on cell receptors to produce therapeutic effects. Some drugs work through straightforward physical or chemical interactions in the body. Antacids neutralize excess stomach acid through a basic chemical neutralization reaction, completely independent of cellular receptors. Osmotic laxatives draw water into the bowel through simple physical osmosis to relieve constipation. Other examples include chelating agents that bind directly to toxic heavy metals in the bloodstream so the kidneys can excrete them safely.

Dose-Response Relationships and Potency

The relationship between the amount of a drug given and the resulting bodily effect is known as the dose-response relationship. As a nurse administers higher doses, the therapeutic effect generally increases until all available receptors are completely saturated.

Potency refers strictly to the amount of drug needed to produce a specific degree of effect. A highly potent drug requires only a tiny milligram dose to achieve results, whereas a less potent drug needs much larger doses. A common student misconception is equating potency with maximum effectiveness; a highly potent drug is not necessarily more effective overall—it just requires less quantity.

Efficacy and Therapeutic Index

Efficacy represents the maximum intensity of response that a drug can produce, regardless of how much extra medicine you give. It is often the most important clinical consideration when choosing treatment for a severe illness.

The therapeutic index is a safety ratio that compares the dose of a drug that produces a toxic effect to the dose that produces a therapeutic effect. A narrow therapeutic index means the safe dose is very close to the dangerous or lethal dose. Drugs with narrow safety margins require regular laboratory blood monitoring in the ward to prevent accidental toxicity.

Factors Modifying Drug Action

No two patients respond to medications in the exact same way. Many physiological and environmental variables can alter how a drug behaves in the body:

  • Age: Infants and elderly adults often have altered metabolism and excretion, making them sensitive to standard doses.
  • Body weight: Heavier individuals may require larger doses to achieve the same tissue concentration as smaller patients.
  • Genetics: Inherited enzyme differences can cause some people to break down drugs rapidly while others process them dangerously slowly.
  • Pathology: Liver or kidney disease delays drug clearance, leading to dangerous accumulation in the bloodstream.

Adverse Drug Reactions and Toxicity

Even when administered correctly, medications can produce unintended, harmful responses known as adverse drug reactions (ADRs). These range from mild rashes to life-threatening allergic reactions like anaphylaxis. Toxicity occurs when drug levels in the blood become excessively high, often due to impaired metabolism, improper dosing, or organ failure. Nurses play a crucial frontline safety role by monitoring vital signs, checking laboratory results, and observing patients closely for early warning signs of adverse drug events.
