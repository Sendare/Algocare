Drug Absorption

Introduction to Pharmacokinetics and Drug Absorption

Pharmacokinetics is how the body handles a drug from the moment it enters the system until it is completely cleared. The entire process follows four main steps often remembered by the acronym ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. Drug absorption specifically refers to how a medication moves from its administration site into the bloodstream.

Before a drug can reach its target receptor in tissues, it must cross several biological membranes. For oral medications, this journey usually starts in the gastrointestinal tract, where the drug dissolves and passes through the intestinal wall. The speed and completeness of this movement determine how much active medication finally reaches the systemic circulation to produce a therapeutic effect.

Mechanisms of Membrane Transport

To enter the bloodstream, drug molecules must cross cellular membranes, which are primarily made of lipid bilayers. Medications use different pathways to cross these barriers depending on their chemical properties:

  • Passive diffusion: The most common mechanism. Drugs move naturally from an area of higher concentration to an area of lower concentration without requiring energy.
  • Facilitated diffusion: Carrier proteins help move larger or less lipid-soluble molecules across the membrane down a concentration gradient, still without using energy.
  • Active transport: Specific carrier proteins pump drug molecules across the membrane against a concentration gradient, which requires cellular energy (ATP).
  • Endocytosis and exocytosis: Cells engulf very large molecules by wrapping the cell membrane around them to bring them inside, which is rare for most standard drugs.

Routes of Drug Administration and Absorption Profiles

The route chosen to give a medication directly dictates how fast and how completely it is absorbed.

Intravenous administration bypasses absorption entirely by placing the drug directly into the bloodstream, yielding an immediate and 100% absorption profile. In contrast, oral medications face a winding path. They must dissolve in stomach or intestinal fluids, survive digestive enzymes, and cross the gut wall before entering portal circulation.

Topical medications applied to the skin or mucous membranes generally offer slow, sustained absorption, though some mucous membranes like the sublingual area absorb drugs very rapidly due to rich blood supplies. Subcutaneous and intramuscular injections rely on local blood flow at the injection site; drugs injected into muscles usually absorb faster than those placed under the skin due to greater vascularity.

Physicochemical Factors Affecting Absorption

A drug's chemical and physical makeup strongly influences how well it passes through body tissues.

  • Lipid solubility: Cell membranes are fatty, so lipophilic (fat-soluble) drugs cross them easily. Hydrophilic (water-soluble) drugs struggle to pass through lipid cell walls without special channels.
  • Molecular size: Smaller molecules slip through membrane pores faster than large, bulky compounds.
  • Ionization state: Drugs exist in charged (ionized) or uncharged (unionized) forms depending on the pH of their environment. Only unionized, neutral drug molecules pass easily through lipid membranes because charged molecules are repelled.
  • Formulation: Coatings, fillers, and binders in pills can speed up or slow down how fast a tablet dissolves, which alters overall absorption.

Physiological and Anatomical Factors at the Absorption Site

The condition of the patient's body directly changes how well a drug is absorbed.

Surface area is a major determinant. The small intestine has an enormous surface area covered in villi and microvilli, making it the primary site for drug absorption in the digestive tract. Blood flow also matters enormously; areas with rich capillary networks absorb drugs quickly, while poorly perfused tissues slow absorption down.

Gastric emptying time affects oral drugs. If food or illness delays stomach emptying, the drug stays trapped in the stomach longer, delaying its arrival at the absorptive surfaces of the intestine. Conversely, certain foods or digestive conditions can bind to drugs in the gut lumen, preventing absorption altogether.

First-Pass Metabolism and Bioavailability

When a drug is swallowed, it absorbs through the gut wall into the portal vein, which carries blood straight to the liver before it goes anywhere else in the body. The liver contains many enzymes that can chemically alter or break down a portion of the drug before it ever reaches the systemic circulation. This process is called first-pass metabolism.

Because of first-pass metabolism, much of an oral dose can be inactivated before it helps the patient. This leads to the concept of bioavailability, which describes the percentage of the administered drug that actually reaches the systemic circulation in an active form. Intravenous drugs have a bioavailability of 100%, whereas oral drugs often have significantly lower and more variable bioavailability values.

Clinical Implications for Nursing Practice

Nurses play a critical role in managing drug absorption by understanding how patient variables and medication forms interact. Crushing pills, giving drugs with or without food, and monitoring GI motility can all alter therapeutic outcomes.

For instance, enteric-coated tablets are designed to resist stomach acid and dissolve only in the intestines. Crushing such a pill destroys this coating, leading to premature breakdown in the stomach or irritation of the gastric mucosa. Similarly, nurses must check whether a drug should be taken on an empty stomach to avoid food binding that prevents proper absorption.
