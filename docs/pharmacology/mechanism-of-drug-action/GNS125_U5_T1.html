Drug Absorption

Introduction to Pharmacokinetics and Drug Absorption

Pharmacokinetics describes how the body handles a medication through four distinct phases: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. Often remembered by the acronym ADME, this process dictates the onset, intensity, and duration of a drug's therapeutic effect. Drug absorption specifically refers to the movement of a drug from its site of administration into the central blood circulation. Except for intravenous administration where drugs enter the bloodstream directly, every other route requires the medication to cross biological membranes. Understanding this foundational step helps nurses anticipate why different drugs take varying amounts of time to work in emergency or routine clinical wards.

Mechanisms of Membrane Transport

For a drug to reach the bloodstream, it must cross cellular lipid bilayers. Medications utilize different transport pathways depending on their chemical properties:

  • Passive diffusion: The most common mechanism where lipid-soluble drugs move freely from an area of high concentration to low concentration without expending energy.
  • Facilitated diffusion: Carrier proteins assist water-soluble or larger molecules down a concentration gradient without using cellular energy.
  • Active transport: Specialized carrier proteins move molecules against a concentration gradient, a process requiring cellular energy (ATP).
  • Endocytosis and exocytosis: Cells engulf large or peptide-based drug molecules by enclosing them in vesicles to transport them across the membrane.

Physicochemical Factors Affecting Absorption

A drug's chemical makeup strongly influences how well it is absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract. Most drugs are weak acids or weak bases. In bodily fluids, they exist in two forms: ionized and non-ionized. Only the non-ionized form is lipophilic enough to cross cell membranes easily.

Particle size and formulation also matter greatly. Micronized powders dissolve faster than large crystals. Additionally, excipients and coatings added by manufacturers control whether a tablet dissolves rapidly in the stomach or resists acid to release its active ingredient slowly in the intestines.

Physiological and Anatomical Factors at the Absorption Site

The biological environment where a drug is absorbed dictates the success of the process. The small intestine serves as the primary site for oral drug absorption due to its massive surface area created by folds, villi, and microvilli, alongside a rich capillary network.

Gastric emptying time alters absorption rates. Conditions that delay stomach emptying, such as high-fat meals or certain disease states, keep medications away from the absorptive surface of the small intestine longer. Conversely, diseases causing severe diarrhea decrease contact time, leading to incomplete drug absorption.

First-Pass Metabolism and Bioavailability

When drugs are absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, they enter the mesenteric veins, which drain directly into the hepatic portal system. Consequently, oral medications pass through the liver before reaching the rest of the body.

Liver enzymes can metabolize a significant portion of the drug during this initial transit, a phenomenon known as first-pass metabolism or presystemic elimination. This process reduces the concentration of active medication entering systemic circulation.

Bioavailability measures the fraction of an unchanged drug that successfully reaches the systemic circulation. An intravenous dose has a bioavailability of 100%, whereas oral medications often have lower and variable bioavailability due to first-pass metabolism in the gut wall and liver.

Clinical Implications for Nursing Practice

Nurses play a critical role in managing drug absorption during medication administration. Clinical assessments must include checking for factors that hinder absorption, such as altered bowel motility, concurrent administration of conflicting foods, or impaired blood flow in shock states.

Crucial nursing actions include verifying whether a tablet can be crushed. Crushing an enteric-coated or sustained-release tablet destroys its formulation, causing a dangerous dump of the entire drug dose into the bloodstream at once. Nurses must also instruct patients regarding proper fluid intake and timing with meals to optimize therapeutic outcomes.
