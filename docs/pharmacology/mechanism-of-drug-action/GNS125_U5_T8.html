Adverse Drug Reactions

Understanding Adverse Drug Reactions

An Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR) is an unwanted, uncomfortable, or dangerous effect that happens when someone takes a normal dose of a medication. Unlike an allergic reaction, which involves the immune system, ADRs can happen through various chemical pathways in the body. Nurses encounter these reactions daily in medical-surgical wards, emergency units, and community health clinics across Nigeria. Recognizing the early signs of an ADR can prevent minor discomfort from turning into a life-threatening hospital emergency. It is vital to separate a true ADR from a medication error or an intentional overdose, as ADRs occur even when the prescription, dose, and administration route are entirely correct.

Classification of Adverse Drug Reactions

Pharmacologists divide adverse reactions into distinct groups to help clinicians predict and manage them. The most practical system uses the Rawlins-Thompson classification, which breaks reactions into two main types:

  • Type A (Augmented): These are dose-dependent, predictable, and make up the majority of all drug reactions. For example, a patient taking a standard blood pressure pill might experience severe dizziness because their blood pressure drops too low.
  • Type B (Bizarre): These are unpredictable, unrelated to the dose, and often involve unusual immune or genetic responses. A classic example is sudden anaphylaxis or severe skin peeling from a medication.

Type A reactions usually resolve simply by lowering the dose, whereas Type B reactions require immediate discontinuation of the drug and strict avoidance in the future.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Presentation

Adverse drug reactions can mimic almost any disease state, making them a challenge to diagnose in busy clinical settings. Nurses must look out for sudden changes in vital signs, skin appearance, gastrointestinal comfort, and mental status.

Common presentation patterns include:

  • Gastrointestinal: Nausea, vomiting, severe diarrhea, or silent constipation.
  • Dermatological: Itching, hives, generalized rashes, or blistering.
  • Neurological: Drowsiness, confusion, tremors, or unexpected restlessness.
  • Hematological: Unusual bruising or bleeding caused by low platelets or altered clotting.

A common clinical misconception is assuming every new symptom in an admitted patient is part of their original disease, when it might actually be a side effect of the newly started medications.

Assessment, Monitoring, and Reporting

Nurses play the front-line role in catching adverse reactions before they cause permanent harm. Assessment begins with a thorough medication history, asking patients specifically about past bad reactions to drugs, herbs, or local concoctions. During hospitalization, continuous monitoring of vital signs and laboratory parameters—such as kidney and liver function tests—helps catch organ stress early.

When an ADR is confirmed or strongly suspected, documentation in the patient chart is mandatory. Beyond the hospital chart, reporting the event to national regulatory bodies helps protect the wider public. In Nigeria, nurses should report suspected adverse reactions to the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) using their yellow reporting forms or digital reporting channels.

Patient Management and Treatment Strategies

Managing an adverse drug reaction requires swift clinical action focused on stopping the trigger and stabilizing the patient.

  1. Discontinuation: Stop the offending medication immediately, unless the drug is life-saving and the reaction is very mild.
  2. Supportive Care: Provide breathing support, intravenous fluids, or vasopressors if blood pressure crashes.
  3. Antidotes and Reversal: Administer specific antagonists when available, such as naloxone for opioid overdoses or specific rescue inhalers for bronchospasm.
  4. Documentation: Clearly flag the patient's chart and inform them of the specific drug name so they can avoid it in future healthcare encounters.

Prevention and Nursing Responsibilities in Nigeria

Preventing adverse drug reactions is a core professional duty for every registered nurse. Prevention starts with applying the five rights of medication administration every single time: right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, and right time. Nurses must also screen patients for renal or hepatic impairment, because drugs clear much slower when these organs are weak, leading to accidental toxicity.

In the Nigerian context, patient education remains a powerful tool. Many patients combine orthodox prescription drugs with herbal remedies, local concotions, or over-the-counter supplements without telling their healthcare providers. Nurses must take time during health talks and bedside nursing care to ask about these herbal mixtures, as they frequently cause dangerous drug-herb interactions that mimic severe ADRs.
