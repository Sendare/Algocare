Adverse Drug Reactions

Introduction to Adverse Drug Reactions

An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is an appreciably harmful or unpleasant reaction resulting from an intervention related to the use of a medicinal product. Unlike side effects, which are often predictable and sometimes tolerable pharmacological extensions of a drug's main action, ADRs are typically noxious, unintended, and occur at doses normally used in humans for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, distinguishing between a true ADR, a medication error, and a disease progression remains a daily diagnostic challenge. Every practicing healthcare professional must recognize that ADRs contribute significantly to morbidity, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs. They can affect anyone, though vulnerable populations such as the elderly, neonates, and patients with impaired hepatic or renal clearance face a substantially higher baseline risk.

Classification and Types of Adverse Drug Reactions

ADRs are broadly categorized using the Rawlins-Thompson classification system, which splits them into augmented and bizarre reactions:

  • Type A (Augmented): Dose-dependent, predictable based on the drug's known pharmacology, and generally carries a low mortality rate. Examples include excessive bleeding from warfarin or hypoglycemia caused by insulin.
  • Type B (Bizarre): Dose-independent, unpredictable, uncommon, and often severe or life-threatening. These are mediated by immunological or idiosyncratic mechanisms, such as anaphylaxis to penicillin or severe skin detachment from sulfonamides.
  • Type C (Chronic): Associated with long-term, cumulative drug therapy, such as analgesic nephropathy from chronic NSAID abuse.
  • Type D (Delayed): Manifest months or years after treatment cessation, including secondary malignancies or teratogenicity.
  • Type E (End-of-use): Occur immediately upon drug withdrawal, representing a classic withdrawal syndrome.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Presentation

The clinical presentation of an ADR varies widely, mimicking common pathological disease states, which frequently leads to diagnostic confusion. Cutaneous manifestations like pruritus, maculopapular rashes, and urticaria represent the most easily visualized signs. Systemically, patients might present with gastrointestinal distress, acute kidney injury evidenced by declining urinary output, or profound hemodynamic instability such as drug-induced hypotension. Severe cutaneous adverse reactions, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome, require immediate recognition of blistering lesions on mucosal surfaces. In neuropsychiatric presentations, confusion, extrapyramidal symptoms, or acute delirium can suddenly appear, particularly in older adults receiving multiple neuroactive agents.

Assessment, Monitoring, and Reporting

Proactive surveillance forms the bedrock of patient safety. Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion, routinely reviewing medication charts against laboratory values such as liver enzymes, serum electrolytes, and renal panels. When an adverse event occurs, immediate clinical stabilization takes priority over administrative tasks. Once the patient is stable, documentation must be precise, detailing the exact time of onset, description of symptoms, concurrent drug therapy, and immediate interventions taken.

In Nigeria, official reporting of suspected adverse drug reactions is coordinated through the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC). Utilizing the yellow form provided by NAFDAC allows for post-marketing surveillance, helping regulators detect rare safety signals that clinical trials failed to uncover.

Patient Management and Treatment Strategies

Managing an established adverse reaction requires a swift, multi-faceted approach aimed at halting exposure and reversing pathology. The immediate cessation or dose reduction of the offending drug remains the most critical intervention. Supportive therapy depends entirely on the system affected: maintaining a patent airway and administering epinephrine for anaphylaxis, providing intravenous fluids for hypotension, or utilizing antihistamines and corticosteroids for moderate allergic events. In cases of intentional or severe accidental toxicity, specific antidotes—such as naloxone for opioid overdose or phytomenadione for warfarin toxicity—must be readily accessible in clinical care areas.

Prevention and Nursing Responsibilities in Nigeria

Preventing adverse drug reactions requires meticulous adherence to safe medication administration principles. Nurses must diligently practice the core safety checks, screen clients for known drug allergies prior to administration, and reconcile medication lists upon hospital admission, transfer, and discharge. In the Nigerian healthcare context, nurses must also educate patients against self-medication, herbal-concoction mixing with orthodox drugs, and the purchase of medications from unregistered open-air medicine vendors. Effective communication bridges the gap between clinical safety and community health outcomes, ensuring patients recognize early warning signs before minor reactions escalate into life-threatening emergencies.
