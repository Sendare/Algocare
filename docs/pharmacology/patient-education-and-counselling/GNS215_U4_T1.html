Self-Medication

Definition and Scope of Self-Medication

Self-medication is the selection and use of medicines by individuals to treat self-recognized illnesses or symptoms. This practice involves obtaining drugs without a prescription, reusing old prescriptions, sharing medications with family members, or purchasing leftover drugs from local chemists and open drug markets. While responsible self-care can alleviate minor ailments, unchecked self-medication often leads to drug misuse and delayed medical diagnoses.

Common Drivers and Socio-Economic Factors

Several practical pressures drive people toward unsupervised drug use, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria. Long waiting times at public hospitals, high consultation fees, and the cost of diagnostic tests push individuals to seek cheaper, faster alternatives. Furthermore, widespread poverty, proximity to open drug markets, and aggressive pharmaceutical advertising make purchasing over-the-counter remedies directly from patent and proprietary medicine vendors an attractive, accessible option.

Risks and Complications of Unsupervised Drug Use

Using medications without professional oversight exposes patients to distinct clinical dangers. Adverse drug reactions, severe allergic responses, and organ toxicity frequently occur when dosages are guessed rather than calculated.

  • Masking Symptoms: Potent analgesics or anti-inflammatory drugs can hide critical signs of acute surgical conditions like appendicitis, leading to delayed life-saving interventions.
  • Drug Interactions: Combining herbal remedies with orthodox medicines can cause unexpected chemical clashes that harm the liver and kidneys.
  • Incorrect Dosing: Underdosing fails to treat the illness, while overdosing can precipitate acute poisoning or fatal organ failure.

Antimicrobial Resistance and Self-Medication

The indiscriminate use of antibiotics and antimalarials is a major public health crisis. When individuals take antibiotics for viral infections, or stop taking them midway through a course because they feel better, surviving microorganisms adapt and multiply. This process creates drug-resistant strains that render standard treatments ineffective, transforming easily manageable infections into life-threatening conditions.

Nursing Assessment and History Taking

Nurses play a vital role in uncovering hidden self-medication practices during patient admissions. Because patients often forget or omit over-the-counter products, herbs, and supplements from their medication history, nurses must ask targeted, non-judgmental questions.

  • Inquire specifically about traditional remedies, syrups bought from chemists, and leftover drugs.
  • Check for discrepancies between reported symptoms and actual physiological signs of toxicity.
  • Document the exact frequency, duration, and source of any self-administered products.

Patient Counselling Strategies

Effective counselling transforms how patients view medications. Nurses must educate clients on the risks of sharing drugs, reading product labels, and completing full medication courses. Teaching should be simple, clear, and adapted to the patient's literacy level. Using visual aids and asking the patient to repeat instructions back ensures true comprehension before they leave the clinic or ward.

Promoting Responsible Healthcare Seeking

Combating dangerous self-medication requires shifting community mindsets toward early professional healthcare seeking. Nurses lead community health outreaches to educate the public on the dangers of patent medicine abuse and the importance of visiting accredited clinics. By empowering communities with health literacy, nurses foster a culture where professional diagnosis precedes treatment, safeguarding public health.
