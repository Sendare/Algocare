Drugs Used in the Treatment of Peptic Ulcers

Introduction to Peptic Ulcer Disease and Treatment Goals

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD) involves erosions in the mucosal lining of the stomach or duodenum, caused by an imbalance between aggressive gastric factors and protective mucosal mechanisms. The two primary etiologies encountered in clinical practice are **Helicobacter pylori** infection and the chronic use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Treatment strategies focus on symptom relief, accelerating ulcer healing, and preventing dangerous complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, and gastric outlet obstruction.

Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Mechanism and Clinical Use

Proton Pump Inhibitors represent the most potent class of acid-suppressive agents available. Drugs such as **omeprazole**, **pantoprazole**, and **lansoprazole** act by irreversibly binding to the hydrogen-potassium adenosine triphosphatase enzyme system on the secretory surface of gastric parietal cells. Because this mechanism blocks the final step of acid production, PPIs effectively reduce both basal and stimulated gastric acid secretion. Clinically, they are indicated for the management of active duodenal and gastric ulcers, erosive esophagitis, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and as a core component of **H. pylori** eradication regimens. Administration timing is critical for optimal efficacy; these medications should typically be taken orally thirty to sixty minutes before the first meal of the day to ensure peak drug levels coincide with maximal activation of proton pumps.

H2-Receptor Antagonists (H2RAs)

Histamine-2 receptor antagonists, including **famotidine** and **cimetidine**, competitively block the binding of histamine to H2 receptors located on gastric parietal cells. This blockade suppresses stimulated gastric acid secretion, particularly nocturnal acid output, making them useful for healing duodenal ulcers and managing mild GERD symptoms. While generally well-tolerated, clinical usage requires awareness of specific pharmacological profiles. Cimetidine, an older agent in this class, is known for inhibiting hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes, leading to potential drug interactions with medications like warfarin, phenytoin, and theophylline. Famotidine has a much cleaner metabolic profile and has largely superseded cimetidine in modern hospital formularies across Nigeria.

Antacids and Acid Neutralization Strategies

Antacids are basic compounds that chemically neutralize existing hydrochloric acid in the gastric lumen, rapidly elevating gastric pH and providing fast, short-term relief from dyspepsia and acute heartburn. Common formulations combine magnesium hydroxide, which tends to cause diarrhea, with aluminum hydroxide, which causes constipation, to balance bowel function. Timing is an important variable when administering antacids alongside other oral medications. Because they alter gastric pH and can bind to other drug molecules, antacids generally require a separation of one to two hours from the administration of other drugs to avoid impairing systemic absorption.

Mucosal Protective Agents and Cytoprotectants

- **Sucralfate**: Forms a viscous, sticky substance in acidic environments that binds selectively to ulcer craters, creating a physical barrier against acid and pepsin for up to six hours. - **Bismuth Subsalicylate**: Coats ulcers, possesses weak antacid properties, and exhibits direct antibacterial action against Helicobacter pylori while turning stools harmlessly black. - **Misoprostol**: A synthetic prostaglandin E1 analog that replaces endogenous prostaglandins depleted by chronic NSAID use, increasing bicarbonate and mucus production while decreasing acid secretion.

Antibiotic Regimens for Helicobacter pylori Eradication

Because Helicobacter pylori is a primary driver of chronic gastritis and recurrent peptic ulcers, acid suppression alone is insufficient for permanent cure. Eradication requires multi-drug antimicrobial therapy, typically combining a proton pump inhibitor with at least two different antibiotics to prevent treatment failure and microbial resistance. Standard first-line regimens often involve triple therapy consisting of a PPI, clarithromycin, and amoxicillin (or metronidazole if the patient has a penicillin allergy) taken for 10 to 14 days. Patient adherence is a major clinical hurdle; nurses must emphasize completing the full course even after symptoms resolve, as premature discontinuation promotes resistant bacterial strains.

Nursing Considerations, Patient Education, and Side Effect Management

Comprehensive nursing care for patients receiving anti-ulcer therapy extends beyond medication administration to encompass lifestyle modifications, side effect monitoring, and recognizing red flag symptoms. Nurses must educate patients to avoid known gastric irritants such as cigarette smoking, alcohol, caffeine, and regular NSAID use. Monitoring for adverse effects is essential: long-term PPI therapy is associated with risks of hypomagnesemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, and reduced calcium absorption predisposing to fractures. Furthermore, nurses must remain vigilant for signs of treatment failure or serious complications, instructing patients to report immediately any occurrences of hematemesis, melena, persistent vomiting, or severe, unremitting abdominal pain.
