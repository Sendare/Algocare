Drugs Used in the Treatment of Peptic Ulcers

Introduction to Peptic Ulcer Disease and Treatment Goals

Peptic ulcer disease involves open sores that develop on the inside lining of the stomach and the upper portion of the small intestine. The primary goal of medical management is to relieve patient pain, heal the ulcer crater, and prevent annoying or dangerous recurrences. Drug therapy focuses on correcting the physiological imbalance between aggressive digestive factors, such as gastric acid and pepsin, and the stomach's natural mucosal defense mechanisms. Successful treatment regimens also require addressing underlying root causes, most notably bacterial infections.

Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Mechanism and Clinical Use

Proton pump inhibitors represent the most potent class of acid-suppressing medications available for clinical practice. These drugs work by irreversibly binding to and blocking the hydrogen-potassium adenosine triphosphatase enzyme system located on the secretory surface of gastric parietal cells. By shutting down this final step of acid production, PPIs effectively reduce both daytime and nocturnal acid secretion.

  • Omeprazole: A widely used oral PPI that requires conversion in an acidic environment.
  • Pantoprazole: Frequently administered intravenously or orally in acute hospital settings.
  • Lansoprazole: Offers rapid symptom relief and mucosal healing properties.

Nursing staff must instruct patients to swallow delayed-release capsules whole without crushing or chewing to protect the active drug from premature stomach degradation.

H2-Receptor Antagonists (H2RAs)

Histamine-2 receptor antagonists block the action of histamine on parietal cells, which successfully decreases the volume and hydrogen ion concentration of gastric secretions. While less powerful than proton pump inhibitors, these agents still provide reliable relief for mild to moderate acid-related discomfort and healing of uncomplicated duodenal ulcers.

Antacids and Acid Neutralization Strategies

Antacids are basic compounds that chemically neutralize existing hydrochloric acid in the stomach lumen, raising the pH and providing rapid, short-term symptom relief. They do not heal ulcers or stop acid production at the cellular level.

Different formulations carry distinct side effect profiles that nurses must monitor:

  • Aluminum hydroxide: Frequently causes constipation and can bind phosphate in the gut.
  • Magnesium hydroxide: Often causes osmotic diarrhea, requiring careful use in kidney disease.
  • Calcium carbonate: Can cause acid rebound and constipation, along with hypercalcemia risks.

Mucosal Protective Agents and Cytoprotectants

Cytoprotective agents do not significantly lower gastric acid levels. Instead, they reinforce the stomach's natural defense barriers against corrosive digestive juices. Sucralfate creates a sticky, viscous paste that binds directly to ulcer craters, forming a protective physical barrier that shields the raw tissue from acid and pepsin. Bismuth subsalicylate provides local antimicrobial action against bacterial pathogens while stimulating defensive prostaglandin and mucus production. Nurses must schedule sucralfate administration carefully, keeping it separate from other oral medications to prevent impaired drug absorption.

Antibiotic Regimens for Helicobacter pylori Eradication

The discovery of Helicobacter pylori revolutionized peptic ulcer treatment by shifting management from lifelong acid suppression to curative antibiotic therapy. Eradication regimens generally combine a proton pump inhibitor with at least two different antibiotics to prevent treatment failure caused by drug resistance. Standard triple therapy includes a PPI alongside clarithromycin and amoxicillin, typically prescribed for fourteen days. Quadruple therapy adds a bismuth compound and is often chosen if local resistance rates to clarithromycin are high. Nurses play a vital role in educating patients on completing the entire antibiotic course despite early symptom relief.

Nursing Considerations and Patient Education

Managing ulcer medications requires careful assessment of lifestyle factors, dietary triggers, and medication adherence. Nurses must screen patients for regular use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, as these agents suppress protective prostaglandins and frequently cause ulceration. Dietary counseling involves identifying individual trigger foods, such as spicy dishes, caffeine, and alcohol, that exacerbate dyspepsia. Furthermore, patients should understand that smoking delays ulcer healing and increases recurrence rates, making smoking cessation counseling an essential component of comprehensive nursing care.
