Drugs Used in the Treatment of Peptic Ulcers

Introduction to Peptic Ulcer Disease and Treatment Goals

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD) encompasses painful sores or erosions that develop in the lining of the stomach (gastric ulcers) or the first part of the small intestine (duodenal ulcers). For Nigerian nursing students preparing for clinical practice and professional examinations, understanding PUD is crucial because it is a prevalent gastrointestinal condition encountered in local healthcare settings. The primary pathophysiology involves an imbalance between aggressive mucosal factors—most notably gastric acid, pepsin, and Helicobacter pylori infection—and defensive mucosal mechanisms like mucus production, bicarbonate secretion, and adequate mucosal blood flow. Consequently, the primary goals of pharmacological treatment are multifaceted: to relieve abdominal pain, promote rapid ulcer healing, prevent ulcer recurrence, and avert life-threatening complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, and gastric outlet obstruction. Achieving these clinical outcomes requires a strategic combination of acid-suppressing agents, mucosal protectants, and specific antimicrobial therapies tailored to eradicate underlying bacterial pathogens. Nursing management revolves around meticulous patient assessment, monitoring for therapeutic efficacy, watching out for adverse drug reactions, and delivering comprehensive health education that targets modifiable lifestyle risk factors common in the West African environment, such as the frequent use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) for pain management and dietary habits.

Gastric Acid Physiology and Defensive Mucosal Factors

To understand how anti-ulcer medications work, nursing students must first grasp the normal physiology of gastric acid secretion and mucosal defense. Gastric acid, primarily hydrochloric acid (HCl), is secreted by parietal cells located within the gastric mucosa. This secretion is stimulated by three primary physiological pathways: acetylcholine (via vagal neural stimulation), histamine (acting on H2-receptors), and gastrin (acting on cholecystokinin-2 receptors). These secretagogues stimulate the final common pathway—the H+/K+ ATPase enzyme pump—to extrude hydrogen ions into the gastric lumen. While acid and pepsin are essential for protein digestion and sterilizing ingested food, they possess the destructive potential to digest the stomach lining itself if unchecked. To counteract this, the gastrointestinal tract maintains a robust defensive mucosal barrier. This barrier consists of a continuous layer of viscous mucus secreted by surface epithelial cells, an underlying layer of alkaline bicarbonate that neutralizes acid at the cell surface, a rich microvascular network supplying oxygen and nutrients, and rapid cellular renewal (epithelial restitution). Prostaglandins (specifically PGE2 and PGI2) play a pivotal role in maintaining these defenses by stimulating mucus and bicarbonate production while inhibiting acid secretion. When local defense mechanisms are compromised by agents like Helicobacter pylori or medications such as NSAIDs, which inhibit protective prostaglandin synthesis, mucosal erosion and ulceration ensue.

Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Mechanism and Clinical Use

Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs) represent the gold standard and most potent class of medications used in the management of peptic ulcer disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), and Zollinger-Ellison syndrome. Common examples include omeprazole, esomeprazole, pantoprazole, and lansoprazole. The mechanism of action involves irreversible binding and inhibition of the H+/K+ ATPase enzyme system on the secretory surface of gastric parietal cells. Because they block the final step of acid production, PPIs effectively suppress both basal and meal-stimulated gastric acid secretion by over 90%. Nursing students must note that PPIs are prodrugs that require an acidic environment in the parietal cell canaliculi to be converted into their active form; therefore, they are typically administered orally about 30 to 60 minutes before the first meal of the day to coincide with maximal activation of proton pumps. Clinically, PPIs promote rapid healing of gastric and duodenal ulcers and are essential components of eradication regimens for Helicobacter pylori. While generally well-tolerated, long-term use of PPIs is associated with potential adverse effects, including an increased risk of enteric infections (like Clostridioides difficile), bone fractures due to impaired calcium absorption, hypomagnesemia, and vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nurses should monitor patients for signs of nutritional deficiencies and advocate for the lowest effective dose when chronic therapy is indicated.

H2-Receptor Antagonists (H2RAs)

H2-Receptor Antagonists (H2RAs), such as cimetidine, ranitidine, famotidine, and nizatidine, are another vital class of antisecretory medications. Their mechanism of action involves the competitive and reversible blockade of histamine type-2 (H2) receptors located on the basolateral membrane of gastric parietal cells. By blocking histamine from binding to these receptors, H2RAs suppress the secretion of gastric acid and decrease both the volume and hydrogen ion concentration of gastric juice. Although they are less potent than Proton Pump Inhibitors at suppressing overall 24-hour gastric acidity, H2RAs are very effective at reducing nocturnal acid secretion, making them useful for managing mild to moderate PUD, stress ulcer prophylaxis, and intermittent heartburn. In clinical nursing practice, it is important to remember specific pharmacological profile differences. For instance, cimetidine is known for significant drug interactions because it inhibits the hepatic cytochrome P450 enzyme system, potentially leading to toxic accumulation of co-administered drugs like warfarin, phenytoin, and theophylline; it also possesses anti-androgenic side effects such as gynecomastia and impotence with prolonged use. Newer H2RAs like famotidine have a much safer profile regarding hepatic enzyme inhibition. Nurses must monitor renal function parameters, as dosage adjustments are frequently required for patients with renal impairment due to the renal excretion of these medications.

Antacids and Acid Neutralization Strategies

Antacids are among the oldest and most accessible agents used for rapid, symptomatic relief of dyspepsia, acute gastritis, and peptic ulcer disease. These substances are weak alkaline chemical compounds that directly neutralize hydrochloric acid already present in the gastric lumen, thereby raising the pH of gastric fluid and reducing pepsin activity (which diminishes significantly when pH rises above 4). Common antacid formulations include aluminum hydroxide, magnesium hydroxide, calcium carbonate, and sodium bicarbonate, often combined to balance side effects. For example, magnesium-based antacids tend to cause osmotic diarrhea, whereas aluminum-based antacids cause constipation; combining them helps maintain normal bowel function. Calcium carbonate can cause acid rebound and constipation, while sodium bicarbonate carries a risk of systemic alkalosis and fluid overload due to its high sodium content—a critical consideration for patients with hypertension or heart failure. From a nursing standpoint, patient education on administration timing is essential. Antacids can significantly alter the absorption of numerous oral medications, including iron supplements, tetracyclines, and fluoroquinolones, by binding to them or changing gastric pH. Therefore, nurses must instruct patients to separate the administration of antacids and other oral drugs by at least one to two hours to prevent therapeutic failure.

Mucosal Protective Agents and Cytoprotectants

Mucosal protective agents and cytoprotectants are specialized drugs designed to defend the gastrointestinal mucosa against acid-pepsin attack without necessarily suppressing systemic acid secretion. Key agents in this category include sucralfate, bismuth subsalicylate, and prostaglandin analogues like misoprostol. Sucralfate is a complex sucrose sulfate-aluminum salt that, in the acidic environment of the stomach (pH < 4), undergoes cross-linking to form a sticky, viscous, gel-like substance. This substance selectively binds to the positively charged proteins of exposed ulcer craters, forming a protective physical barrier that shields the lesion from acid and pepsin for up to six hours. Sucralfate requires an acidic pH for activation and should not be given concurrently with antacids, H2RAs, or PPIs that raise gastric pH. Bismuth subsalicylate exhibits direct antimicrobial properties against Helicobacter pylori, stimulates prostaglandin and mucus synthesis, and binds to ulcer sites, giving stools a harmless black coloration that nurses must explain to patients. Misoprostol is a synthetic prostaglandin E1 analogue that replaces endogenous prostaglandins depleted by NSAID use; it inhibits gastric acid secretion and enhances mucosal defense. However, misoprostol is a potent uterine stimulant and is strictly contraindicated in pregnant women due to the risk of inducing spontaneous abortion or premature labor. Nursing students must always verify pregnancy status before administering misoprostol.

Antibiotic Regimens for Helicobacter pylori Eradication

Helicobacter pylori is a spiral-shaped, Gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucosa and is responsible for the vast majority of peptic ulcers and chronic gastritis cases. Because H. pylori weakens the protective mucosal barrier and sustains chronic inflammation, successful treatment requires bacterial eradication rather than just acid suppression. Eradication reduces ulcer recurrence rates to near zero. Current clinical guidelines recommend multi-drug regimens typically referred to as triple or quadruple therapy. Standard triple therapy combines a Proton Pump Inhibitor (PPI) with two broad-spectrum antibiotics, usually clarithromycin and either amoxicillin or metronidazole, administered for 10 to 14 days. In regions with high clarithromycin resistance rates—which is an increasing concern in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa—bismuth quadruple therapy is preferred. This regimen consists of a PPI, bismuth subsalicylate, tetracycline, and metronidazole given for 10 to 14 days. Nursing students must emphasize to patients the critical importance of completing the full course of antibiotic therapy, even if symptoms resolve within a few days. Premature cessation of medications leads to treatment failure and promotes the development of resistant bacterial strains. Nurses should also educate patients about expected side effects, such as metallic taste with metronidazole, harmless black stools with bismuth, and gastrointestinal upset, to promote adherence.

Nursing Considerations, Patient Education, and Side Effect Management

Comprehensive nursing care for patients receiving anti-ulcer medications extends far beyond medication administration; it encompasses meticulous assessment, targeted patient education, and proactive side effect management. Nurses must conduct thorough baseline health assessments, checking for signs of active bleeding (e.g., melena, hematemesis, pallor, dizziness), evaluating pain characteristics, and reviewing the patient's medication history for ulcerogenic drugs such as NSAIDs, corticosteroids, and anticoagulants. Patient education is a cornerstone of recovery. Nurses should instruct clients on lifestyle modifications, including smoking cessation (as nicotine decreases bicarbonate secretion and delays healing), moderating or avoiding alcohol consumption, and identifying and avoiding specific dietary triggers that exacerbate dyspepsia (such as spicy foods, caffeine, and heavy fatty meals). Education regarding drug administration is equally vital: patients must understand the timing of PPIs before breakfast, the separation of antacids from other medications, and the absolute necessity of completing full antibiotic courses for H. pylori eradication. Furthermore, nurses must monitor for adverse effects specific to each drug class, such as potential nutritional deficiencies with long-term PPI therapy, bowel pattern changes with antacids, black discoloration of stool with bismuth, and pregnancy warnings associated with misoprostol. Through vigilant monitoring and supportive education, nurses play a pivotal role in ensuring safe, effective patient outcomes and preventing chronic PUD complications.
