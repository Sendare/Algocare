Purgatives

Introduction to Purgatives and Definition of Terms

Purgatives are substances used to encourage bowel movements and relieve constipation. In clinical practice, you will hear several terms used interchangeably, which can sometimes confuse new nursing students.

  • Laxatives: Mild agents that promote soft, formed stools with minimal discomfort, often used for chronic, gentle relief.
  • Purgatives: Stronger agents that cause a more fluid and rapid evacuation of the bowel, typically used before surgical procedures or diagnostic tests.
  • Cathartics: Often synonymous with purgatives, these drugs produce prompt, fluid evacuation of the bowel, usually within hours.

Understanding these subtle differences helps nurses grasp the intensity of the drug ordered by the doctor and anticipate patient needs regarding bathroom access.

Physiology of Bowel Movement and Constipation

Normal bowel function relies on coordinated muscle contractions known as peristalsis and adequate water absorption within the large intestine. As digested food moves through the colon, the mucosa extracts water and electrolytes, turning liquid chyme into a solid stool.

Constipation occurs when waste moves too slowly through the colon, allowing too much water to be absorbed. The stool becomes hard, dry, and difficult to pass. Common contributors include low dietary fiber, inadequate fluid intake, physical inactivity, and certain medications like opioids. Recognizing these underlying factors allows nurses to recommend lifestyle modifications alongside any prescribed drug therapy.

Classification and Mechanism of Action of Purgatives

Pharmacologists classify purgatives based on how they alter the environment inside the gastrointestinal tract. Knowing these categories helps you predict how quickly a patient will respond to a specific medication order.

  1. Bulk-forming: Absorb water to increase stool mass, stretching the bowel wall to trigger peristalsis.
  2. Osmotic (Saline): Draw water into the lumen through high osmotic pressure, liquefying the stool.
  3. Stimulant (Irritant): Direct action on the intestinal mucosa or nerve plexus to speed up muscle contractions.
  4. Stool Softeners (Emollients): Lower surface tension so fat and water mix easily into the stool, making it softer.

Bulk-Forming and Osmotic Purgatives

Bulk-forming agents like psyllium and methylcellulose are the safest choice for long-term use because they mimic natural dietary fiber. They require adequate fluid intake; otherwise, the powder can swell in the esophagus and cause dangerous blockages.

Osmotic agents include compounds like magnesium hydroxide, polyethylene glycol, and lactulose. They are very effective for quick relief and bowel preparation before medical procedures. However, patients with compromised renal function must use magnesium-based osmotics with extreme caution due to the risk of electrolyte toxicity.

Stimulant and Stool Softener Purgatives

Stimulant purgatives such as bisacodyl and senna act directly on the intestinal smooth muscle and nerve endings. They produce strong bowel movements within six to twelve hours. Because they can cause abdominal cramping and dependency with chronic use, they are best reserved for short-term relief or bowel cleansing.

Stool softeners, commonly known as emollients (like docusate sodium), do not actually cause bowel movements directly. Instead, they act as detergents, allowing water and lipids to penetrate hardened fecal masses. They are ideal for patients who must avoid straining, such as those recovering from childbirth or recent abdominal surgery.

Clinical Indications and Therapeutic Uses

Purgatives play a vital role across various healthcare settings. The most common indication is simple, functional constipation caused by diet or inactivity.

Beyond routine relief, doctors order powerful purgatives to empty the bowel before diagnostic tests like a colonoscopy or barium enema, as well as before abdominal surgeries to reduce the risk of infection from fecal contamination. They are also used to flush out ingested poisons or toxic substances from the gastrointestinal tract in emergency situations.

Contraindications and Adverse Effects

While common, purgatives are powerful drugs that can cause harm if used improperly. The most dangerous contraindication is undiagnosed abdominal pain or suspected intestinal obstruction. Giving a purgative when a patient has a bowel blockage or appendicitis can cause the bowel to rupture, leading to peritonitis.

Common adverse effects include abdominal cramping, nausea, and electrolyte imbalances resulting from excessive fluid loss. Chronic, unregulated use of stimulant purgatives can lead to an atonic colon, where the bowel loses its natural ability to contract on its own.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education in Nigeria

As a nurse in a Nigerian hospital or clinic, your role goes beyond simply handing out medications. You must assess the patient's bowel habits, hydration status, and diet before administering any purgative.

Patient education is critical because many people overuse herbal concoctions or over-the-counter laxatives, leading to dependency. Teach your patients to increase dietary fiber through local foods like beans, vegetables, and whole grains, drink plenty of clean water, and exercise regularly. Always warn patients against the daily use of stimulant laxatives unless explicitly prescribed by a physician.
