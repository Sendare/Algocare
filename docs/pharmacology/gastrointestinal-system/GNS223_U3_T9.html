Rational Drug Use

Introduction to Rational Drug Use in Nursing

Rational drug use ensures that patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their own individual requirements, for an adequate period, and at the lowest cost to them and their community. When medications are prescribed correctly, therapeutic goals are achieved swiftly while minimizing adverse effects. For the nursing professional, understanding this concept forms the foundation of safe medication administration. Nurses act as the final safety barrier between the prescriber and the patient, intercepting errors before they cause harm. In busy healthcare environments across Nigeria, where resource limitations can sometimes pressure quick-fix prescribing habits, upholding these standards protects vulnerable populations from preventable complications and unnecessary financial burden.

Core Principles of the WHO Rational Use of Medicines

The World Health Organization outlines specific criteria to evaluate whether medicines are being used appropriately. These pillars guide clinical governance and pharmacy boards globally. - **Appropriate Indication**: A valid clinical diagnosis must justify every prescription. - **Appropriate Drug**: The chosen agent must have proven efficacy and safety for that specific condition. - **Appropriate Patient**: Contraindications, allergies, and physiological states must rule out risk factors. - **Appropriate Dose**: Dosage regimens must match age, weight, and organ function. - **Appropriate Information**: Clear, understandable instructions must be provided to the recipient. - **Appropriate Monitoring**: Clinical outcomes and potential side effects require active tracking.

Consequences of Irrational Drug Use and Polypharmacy

Irrational drug use manifests in several dangerous ways, including polypharmacy, overuse of antimicrobials, and inappropriate self-medication. Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—exponentially increases the likelihood of adverse drug reactions and dangerous drug-drug interactions. In many settings, patients routinely combine prescribed orthodox drugs with unregulated herbal concoctions, risking severe hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity. Furthermore, the indiscriminate use of antibiotics without clear bacterial confirmation drives antimicrobial resistance, rendering common infections difficult to treat. Healthcare systems also suffer immense financial strain as scarce resources are wasted on unnecessary or harmful drug therapies.

Role of the Nurse in Medication Assessment and Reconciliation

Medication reconciliation is a formal process of obtaining and verifying a complete and accurate list of a patient's current medications across transitions of care. Nurses play a frontline role during admission, transfer, and discharge by meticulously interviewing patients about every pill, supplement, and herbal remedy they ingest. Discrepancies found during reconciliation prevent omission errors, duplication, and harmful interactions. During clinical assessment, the nurse evaluates renal and hepatic clearance markers alongside vital signs to catch early signs of drug toxicity before they escalate into medical emergencies.

Promoting Medication Adherence and Patient Education

Prescribing the correct medication holds no value if the patient fails to take it properly. Non-adherence stems from complex factors including complex dosing schedules, financial constraints, cultural beliefs, and bothersome side effects. Nurses bridge this gap through targeted patient education. Explaining the purpose of a drug, how to manage minor side effects, and why finishing a full course matters transforms passive recipients into active partners in care. Using simplified language, pictorial aids, and including family caregivers in teaching sessions improves compliance, especially within communities with diverse literacy levels.

Pharmacovigilance and Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem. Every nurse must remain vigilant for unexpected reactions, ranging from mild rashes to severe anaphylaxis or organ damage. In Nigeria, bodies like the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) rely heavily on spontaneous reporting systems to track drug safety post-market. Reporting suspected adverse drug reactions using pharmacovigilance yellow cards contributes to national safety databases and protects the wider public from unsafe pharmaceutical products.

Strategies to Improve Rational Drug Therapy in Clinical Practice

Optimizing drug therapy requires an interprofessional approach involving nurses, pharmacists, and physicians. Implementing hospital-based antimicrobial stewardship programs, computerized prescriber order entry systems, and regular multidisciplinary medication reviews drastically reduces prescribing errors. Nurses contribute by advocating for patients during ward rounds, questioning unclear orders without fear, and ensuring rigorous adherence to the five rights of medication administration. Continuous professional education keeps clinical staff updated on current treatment guidelines and emerging drug safety alerts.
