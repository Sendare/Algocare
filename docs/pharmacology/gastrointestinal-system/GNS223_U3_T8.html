Drug Revolving Fund Recovery System

Introduction to the Drug Revolving Fund

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-sustaining financing mechanism designed to guarantee continuous availability and affordability of essential medicines in public health institutions. Historically introduced to combat the chronic scarcity of pharmaceuticals in government hospitals, the scheme operates on a simple premise: seed capital is used to procure an initial stock of medicines, and the cash generated from patient sales is plowed back strictly to purchase replacement stock. When managed effectively, this system insulates healthcare delivery from bureaucratic delays associated with government budgetary releases, ensuring that patients can access prescribed medications directly within the facility at any given time.

Core Principles and Objectives of the DRF Scheme

A successful DRF operates on strict operational pillars designed to prevent capital erosion.

  • Affordability: Prices must remain lower than private retail outlets while still covering replacement costs.
  • Replenishment: Every unit of medicine dispensed must translate to cash that returns to the procurement pool.
  • Accountability: Transparent bookkeeping ensures that capital is never diverted to unrelated hospital overheads.
  • Essential Medicine Focus: Stock is restricted strictly to vital, high-demand pharmaceuticals rather than lifestyle or cosmetic products.

The Drug Revolving Fund Cycle and Operations

The operational life of a DRF follows a closed loop. It begins with quantification and procurement based on consumption patterns. Once medications arrive at the hospital pharmacy, they are dispensed to patients. The cash collected is deposited into a dedicated DRF bank account. From this account, payments are made to vetted pharmaceutical manufacturers or accredited distributors for the next batch of supplies. A break at any point in this loop—such as unrecorded credit sales, pilferage, or delayed banking—halts the entire cycle, leading directly to drug stockouts.

Financial Management and Cost Recovery Strategies

Maintaining the real value of the fund requires deliberate pricing strategies. Inflation and currency devaluation can quickly erode purchasing power, meaning the original seed capital will buy fewer drugs over time. To counter this, facilities apply a modest markup percentage to wholesale prices. This markup absorbs operational losses, covers minimal distribution costs, and accommodates inflationary trends. However, markups must be carefully regulated; setting prices too high defeats the social objective of healthcare accessibility, while setting them too low guarantees eventual capital bankruptcy.

Inventory Control and Supply Chain Management

Effective inventory management protects pharmaceutical stock from waste and loss. Pharmacy staff must utilize reliable stock control cards, electronic inventory systems, and regular physical audits.

FIFO (First-In, First-Out)Ensures older stock is dispensed before recent arrivals to prevent expiration

Poor storage conditions, such as high ambient temperatures and humidity, compromise drug stability, rendering expensive inventory useless before it ever reaches a patient.

Challenges and Bottlenecks in DRF Implementation

Many public health institutions struggle to maintain healthy revolving funds due to systemic vulnerabilities. Pilferage, expired stock accumulation, and weak internal controls frequently drain capital. In many Nigerian public hospitals, the practice of issuing drugs on credit to privileged individuals or politicians without repayment severely cripples the cash flow. Furthermore, delayed processing of procurement tenders and bureaucratic red tape create prolonged stockouts, driving patients toward expensive private vendor alternatives.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and Sustainability

Long-term survival of a DRF depends on continuous performance tracking. Management teams must regularly review financial statements, inventory turnover rates, and loss reports. Key performance indicators include stockout frequency, inventory holding cost, and cash recovery rate. Periodic internal audits help detect discrepancies early, preventing minor record errors from escalating into systemic financial collapse. Sustainability is ultimately achieved when governance structures insulate the fund from political interference and maintain transparent bookkeeping.

The Role of Nurses and Healthcare Personnel in DRF Sustainability

While pharmacy personnel manage direct dispensing, nurses and other frontline clinicians play an indirect yet vital role in protecting the DRF. Nurses influence the supply chain by practicing accurate prescription habits, avoiding wastage, and promptly reporting damaged or near-expiry medications found in ward stock cupboards. Furthermore, clinicians help preserve fund capital by discouraging irrational drug prescribing, which prevents unnecessary depletion of fast-moving essential items. Interprofessional collaboration ensures that resources are conserved for patients who genuinely need them.
