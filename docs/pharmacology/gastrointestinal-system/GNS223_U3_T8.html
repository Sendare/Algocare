Drug Revolving Fund Recovery System

Introduction and Purpose of the Drug Revolving Fund

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a specialized financing mechanism introduced in public healthcare facilities to ensure a continuous and reliable supply of essential medicines. In many resource-constrained settings, government budgets alone cannot consistently purchase enough pharmaceuticals. The DRF solves this by using seed capital to buy an initial stock of medicines. When patients pay for these drugs, the money collected does not disappear into a general government account. Instead, it is plowed right back into the system to purchase fresh stock, creating a continuous circle of buying, selling, and restocking. This system protects patients from catastrophic out-of-pocket expenses at private pharmacies while keeping the hospital pharmacy stocked.

Core Principles and Operational Objectives

  • Self-Financing: The fund must generate enough revenue from sales to replace sold stock without relying on continuous government subventions.
  • Affordability: Prices must remain lower than commercial retail rates so that low-income patients can actually buy what they need.
  • Equity: Vulnerable populations must have access to essential treatments without facing financial ruin.
  • Accountability: Transparent bookkeeping ensures that every tablet and every coin is accounted for to prevent theft or diversion.

The Drug Revolving Fund Cycle

The operational cycle of a DRF moves through several critical phases. It begins with quantification and forecasting, where the facility calculates how many drugs it needs based on past consumption. Next is procurement, where quality medicines are purchased from trusted manufacturers at competitive bulk prices. Once delivered, the drugs go through inventory management and storage, protecting them from theft, heat, and spoilage. They are then dispensed to patients who pay the regulated fee. Finally, revenue collection and banking close the loop, depositing cash securely so the procurement phase can begin all over again. A break at any point in this loop leads straight to a pharmacy stockout.

Cost Recovery Strategies and Pricing Mechanisms

Setting the right price is the tightrope walk of any DRF. If prices are set too low, inflation and operating costs will eat away at the capital, and the fund will eventually collapse. If prices are set too high, the facility defeats its social mission, and poor patients will stay away. Most sustainable schemes use cost-recovery pricing, which covers the baseline cost of the drug plus a small, calculated markup. This markup absorbs shipping fees, minor wastage, and bank charges. Strict credit control is also vital; giving out medications on credit without a watertight recovery plan is the fastest way to sink a revolving fund.

Inventory Control and Supply Chain Management

Good inventory control prevents drugs from expiring on the shelf or running out before new shipments arrive. Pharmacy staff must religiously practice the FEFO (First-Expired, First-Out) principle, ensuring older batches are dispensed before newly delivered boxes. Storage conditions matter deeply; failing to protect vaccines and syrups from excessive heat or humidity destroys their chemical potency long before the labeled expiry date. Regular physical stock audits must match the ledger balances. Discrepancies between physical counts and record books usually point toward pilferage, poor record-keeping, or outright theft.

Common Challenges and Implementation Bottlenecks

Many DRF schemes start with high hopes but struggle or collapse due to predictable pitfalls. Bureaucratic delays in government procurement can stall purchases for months while inflation reduces the buying power of the fund's cash. Poor governance and corruption, such as staff taking medications without paying or inflating supply invoices, drain the coffers. Additionally, political interference sometimes forces facilities to offer free drugs to favored groups without reimbursing the pharmacy account, bleeding the fund dry until it can no longer buy anything.

The Role of Nurses and Healthcare Personnel

While pharmacists manage procurement, nurses and clinicians play a frontline role in safeguarding the DRF. Nurses often administer treatments, manage ward stock cupboards, and record medication usage. Accurate charting directly feeds into the hospital's consumption data, helping pharmacists forecast future needs correctly. Furthermore, nurses help educate patients on completing their full courses of medication, reducing wasteful drug hoarding at home. When clinical staff report damaged supplies, expired items, or inventory discrepancies early, they act as an important safety net for the entire financial ecosystem.
