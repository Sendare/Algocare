Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs

Introduction to Corticosteroids and Mechanism of Action

Corticosteroids are powerful medications that mimic the natural hormones produced by the adrenal cortex. They play a critical role in managing inflammation and immune system overactivity. When the body experiences trauma or infection, the immune system mounts an inflammatory response. Corticosteroids short-circuit this process by blocking an enzyme called phospholipase A2, which stops the production of inflammatory chemicals like prostaglandins and leukotrienes.

Unlike non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs that mainly target COX enzymes, corticosteroids work much higher up the inflammatory cascade. This broad action makes them extraordinarily effective at calming severe swelling, pain, and allergic reactions. However, this deep suppression of the immune system also leaves the body vulnerable to hidden infections.

Classification and Potency of Steroidal Drugs

Steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs vary widely in how strong they are and how long they stay active in the body. Clinicians group them by their mineralocorticoid and glucocorticoid effects. Mineralocorticoid activity controls salt and water balance, while glucocorticoid activity handles metabolism and inflammation.

  • Short-acting: Hydrocortisone, lasting around 8 to 12 hours, with high salt-retaining properties.
  • Intermediate-acting: Prednisone, prednisolone, and methylprednisolone, lasting 12 to 36 hours.
  • Long-acting: Dexamethasone and betamethasone, lasting 36 to 72 hours, with very little salt retention but immense anti-inflammatory power.

A common clinical mistake is assuming all steroids have the same strength. A tiny dose of dexamethasone packs a much heavier anti-inflammatory punch than a standard dose of hydrocortisone.

Administration Routes and Pharmacokinetics

Corticosteroids can enter the body through many different routes depending on how fast relief is needed and how widespread the condition is.

Absorption is generally rapid and complete for oral preparations. The liver metabolizes these drugs, and the kidneys excrete the breakdown products. When giving systemic doses, nurses must consider the timing of administration to mimic the body's natural circadian rhythm.

Therapeutic Indications

Because inflammation and immune reactions underlie many illnesses, corticosteroids are used across nearly every medical specialty. In rheumatology, they control painful flare-ups of rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus. In pulmonology, they open airways during severe asthma attacks or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations.

Other major uses include treating severe skin conditions, reducing brain swelling associated with tumors or trauma, preventing organ rejection after a transplant, and managing certain blood disorders. They do not cure the underlying disease process, but they successfully control the damaging inflammatory symptoms that make patients miserable.

Acute and Chronic Adverse Effects

Short-term use of steroids is usually well tolerated, but long-term therapy brings a heavy burden of side effects. Patients often experience increased appetite, weight gain, fluid retention, and mood changes ranging from mild anxiety to outright psychosis.

Extended use transforms the body's appearance and function significantly:

  • Metabolic changes: Elevated blood sugar levels, redistribution of fat resulting in a "moon face" and "buffalo hump."
  • Musculoskeletal system: Muscle wasting, weakness, and osteoporosis due to calcium depletion in the bones.
  • Integumentary system: Skin thinning, easy bruising, and delayed wound healing.
  • Gastrointestinal tract: Increased risk of peptic ulcers, especially when combined with pain medications like ibuprofen.

Contraindications, Precautions, and Drug Interactions

Before prescribing or administering steroids, nurses must check for conditions that could worsen under therapy. Active, untreated systemic fungal or bacterial infections are major contraindications because the drug will suppress the immune defense needed to fight them. Patients with diabetes, active peptic ulcer disease, heart failure, or severe osteoporosis require extreme caution.

Drug interactions can create dangerous situations. Combining corticosteroids with NSAIDs drastically increases the risk of stomach bleeding and ulcers. Furthermore, steroids lower potassium levels, which can become dangerous if the patient is also taking certain heart medications like digoxin. Live vaccines should be avoided because the suppressed immune system cannot handle the challenge safely.

Patient Assessment and Monitoring Guidelines

Continuous monitoring is essential for patients on ongoing steroid therapy. Nurses play a frontline role by tracking baseline vitals and watching for subtle signs of complications. Weight must be measured regularly to catch fluid retention early. Blood glucose levels require frequent checking, even in patients who do not have a prior history of diabetes, because steroids routinely cause blood sugar spikes.

Nurses should regularly inspect the skin for bruises, check stool color for signs of gastrointestinal bleeding, and assess mood changes or sleep disturbances. Monitoring bone density scans over time helps catch early signs of bone loss before fractures happen.

HPA Axis Suppression and Safe Tapering

When a person takes external corticosteroids for more than a couple of weeks, the body senses the excess hormone and shuts down its own natural production from the adrenal glands. This shutdown is known as Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) axis suppression.

If the medication is stopped abruptly, the adrenal glands cannot instantly resume hormone production. This leads to a life-threatening crisis called addisonian crisis, marked by severe low blood pressure, vomiting, and shock. To prevent this, patients must undergo a slow, gradual tapering schedule. Tapering gives the adrenal glands time to wake up and start making natural cortisol again.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care involves empowering patients with the knowledge they need to manage their therapy safely at home. Nurses must teach patients never to stop taking their pills suddenly. Medication should be taken with food or milk to minimize stomach upset.

Patients need instruction on watching for signs of infection, such as a mild sore throat or fever, because steroids can mask these symptoms. Emphasizing the importance of a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D helps protect bone health. Finally, nurses should encourage patients to wear a medical alert bracelet indicating they take daily corticosteroids, ensuring emergency responders have vital information during a crisis.
