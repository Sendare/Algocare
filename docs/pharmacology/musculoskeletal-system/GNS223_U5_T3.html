Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs

Introduction to Corticosteroids and Mechanism of Action

Corticosteroids are powerful synthetic analogs of hormones secreted by the adrenal cortex, specifically glucocorticoids. They diffuse across cell membranes and bind to intracellular glucocorticoid receptors, modulating gene transcription. This genomic action downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and adhesion molecules while upregulating anti-inflammatory mediators like annexin-1. Beyond genomic effects, these drugs act rapidly via non-genomic pathways through membrane-bound receptors, inhibiting the enzyme **phospholipase A2**. By blocking phospholipase A2, corticosteroids halt the entire arachidonic acid cascade, preventing the synthesis of both **prostaglandins** and **leukotrienes**. This dual inhibition of cellular and humoral inflammatory responses makes them among the most potent anti-inflammatory agents available in clinical practice.

Classification, Potency, and Duration of Action

Corticosteroids vary significantly in their relative anti-inflammatory potency, mineralocorticoid activity, and duration of biological action. These differences dictate their clinical application, ranging from acute severe allergic reactions to chronic autoimmune maintenance. - **Short-acting (Biological half-life < 12 hours)**: - Hydrocortisone: Potency 1, significant mineralocorticoid activity, frequently used for adrenal replacement. - Cortisone: Inactive prodrug requiring hepatic conversion to hydrocortisone. - **Intermediate-acting (Biological half-life 12-36 hours)**: - Prednisone: Prodrug converted in the liver to prednisolone; moderate anti-inflammatory potency. - Methylprednisolone: High potency with minimal mineralocorticoid effect; excellent for pulse therapy. - Triamcinolone: Potent agent often used in intra-articular injections or dermatological preparations. - **Long-acting (Biological half-life 36-54 hours)**: - Dexamethasone: Exceptionally high anti-inflammatory potency with virtually no mineralocorticoid activity; ideal for cerebral edema.

Administration Routes and Pharmacokinetics

Corticosteroids are administered through diverse routes depending on the clinical urgency and target tissue. Oral administration is standard for chronic inflammatory diseases, while intravenous delivery is reserved for acute, life-threatening exacerbations like status asthmaticus or anaphylaxis. Topical and inhaled formulations deliver high local concentrations to the skin or respiratory mucosa while minimizing systemic absorption. Intra-articular injections provide localized relief within a specific joint space. Pharmacokinetically, most synthetic glucocorticoids are well absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract and bound extensively in the plasma to corticosteroid-binding globulin and albumin. Hepatic metabolism via CYP3A4 converts them to inactive water-soluble metabolites, which are subsequently excreted by the kidneys.

Therapeutic Indications across Clinical Specialties

The broad anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive actions of corticosteroids make them foundational treatments across multiple medical specialties. In rheumatology, they induce rapid remission in acute rheumatoid arthritis flares, systemic lupus erythematosus, and vasculitis. Pulmonologists rely on them to suppress airway inflammation in severe asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations. In hematology and oncology, they manage lymphoid malignancies and autoimmune hemolytic anemia. They are also vital in transplantation medicine to prevent acute organ rejection by suppressing T-cell activation. Furthermore, high-dose intravenous therapy prevents cerebral edema associated with brain tumors or bacterial meningitis.

Acute and Chronic Adverse Effects

Prolonged or high-dose corticosteroid therapy alters nearly every major organ system. Short-term use commonly triggers hyperglycemia, mood changes, insomnia, and increased appetite. Long-term administration produces a constellation of clinical signs resembling Cushing's syndrome. Metabolic changes include central obesity, muscle wasting in the extremities, and secondary osteoporosis due to inhibited calcium absorption and increased bone resorption. Mineralocorticoid action leads to sodium retention, edema, and hypertension. Immune suppression increases susceptibility to opportunistic infections, often without classic inflammatory warning signs. Skin thinning, easy bruising, delayed wound healing, and cataracts are also frequent clinical manifestations.

Contraindications, Precautions, and Drug Interactions

Absolute contraindications to systemic corticosteroids are rare during life-threatening emergencies, but they are strictly contraindicated in active untreated systemic fungal infections and hypersensitivity to the drug. Caution is required in patients with latent tuberculosis, peptic ulcer disease, diabetes mellitus, and severe hypertension. Drug interactions can significantly alter therapeutic efficacy or toxicity. Co-administration with **potassium-wasting diuretics** (such as furosemide or thiazides) dramatically increases the risk of severe hypokalemia. Concurrent use of **nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)** greatly multiplies the risk of gastric ulceration and gastrointestinal bleeding. Furthermore, enzyme inducers like rifampin can accelerate corticosteroid metabolism, requiring dosage adjustments.

HPA Axis Suppression and Safe Tapering Protocols

Exogenous glucocorticoids exert negative feedback on the hypothalamus and anterior pituitary, inhibiting the release of corticotropin-releasing hormone and adrenocorticotropic hormone. Over time, this leads to atrophy of the adrenal cortex and endogenous suppression of the **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis**. Abrupt cessation of long-term therapy can precipitate **acute adrenal crisis**, characterized by profound hypotension, hypoglycemia, nausea, and circulatory collapse. To prevent this, patients undergoing prolonged therapy require gradual dose tapering. Tapering allows the atrophied adrenal glands time to regain endogenous cortisol production capacity. Alternate-day dosing schedules are also utilized to minimize HPA axis suppression during chronic maintenance treatment.

Nursing Assessment, Monitoring, and Patient Education

Nurses play a vital role in monitoring patients receiving steroidal anti-inflammatory therapy. Baseline and ongoing assessments must include daily weight checks, blood pressure measurement, and routine blood glucose monitoring. Nurses should regularly inspect the skin for thinning, petechiae, or delayed wound healing, and assess stool characteristics for occult blood. Comprehensive patient education is essential for safe therapy. Patients must be instructed never to stop taking corticosteroids abruptly. They should understand the importance of taking oral doses with food to minimize gastric upset and report signs of infection immediately, given that standard inflammatory fevers may be masked. Encouraging a diet rich in calcium and vitamin D helps mitigate bone density loss.
