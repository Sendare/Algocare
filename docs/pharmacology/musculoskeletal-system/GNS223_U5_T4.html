Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)

Mechanism of Action and Classification

Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs achieve their therapeutic effects primarily by inhibiting cyclooxygenase enzymes, which are catalysts required to convert arachidonic acid into prostaglandins. Prostaglandins are lipid compounds that mediate inflammation, sensitize nerve endings to pain, and regulate body temperature in the hypothalamus. Furthermore, they maintain the protective gastric mucosal barrier and regulate renal blood flow.

There are two primary isoforms of the cyclooxygenase enzyme:

  • COX-1: Constitutively expressed in various tissues. It protects the gastric mucosa, supports platelet aggregation, and maintains normal renal perfusion.
  • COX-2: Inducible at sites of tissue injury and inflammation, though it also has baseline physiological functions in the kidneys and vascular endothelium.

Traditional NSAIDs inhibit both isoforms to varying degrees, which explains why reducing inflammation often results in unwanted gastric and renal side effects. Newer agents were developed to selectively target COX-2, aiming to reduce gastrointestinal toxicity while preserving anti-inflammatory efficacy.

Common Examples and Pharmacological Categories

Clinical practice utilizes a wide array of NSAIDs, categorized mainly by their chemical structure and their relative selectivity for COX-1 versus COX-2. Understanding these categories helps clinicians anticipate potency and side effect profiles.

  • Salicylates: Includes acetylsalicylic acid (aspirin), which irreversibly inhibits platelet aggregation and is frequently used for cardiovascular protection at low doses.
  • Propionic Acids: Includes ibuprofen, naproxen, and ketoprofen. These agents are widely used for musculoskeletal pain, fever, and dysmenorrhea due to a favorable balance of efficacy and tolerability.
  • Acetic Acids: Includes diclofenac, indomethacin, and ketorolac. These are potent anti-inflammatory agents often reserved for acute, severe pain or chronic arthritic conditions.
  • Oxicams (Enolic Acids): Includes meloxicam and piroxicam. They possess long half-lives that allow for once-daily dosing, improving adherence in chronic musculoskeletal disorders.
  • Selective COX-2 Inhibitors (Coxibs): Includes celecoxib. Designed to spare the gastric mucosa by avoiding COX-1 inhibition, though cardiovascular risks require careful patient evaluation.

Therapeutic Indications

The clinical utility of NSAIDs stems from their triple pharmacological properties: analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic. They form a cornerstone in managing both acute and chronic conditions encountered in hospital and community care settings across Nigeria.

Analgesic applications include mild to moderate nociceptive pain such as tension headaches, dental pain, postoperative discomfort, and primary dysmenorrhea. Their anti-inflammatory properties make them indispensable for treating musculoskeletal disorders like osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, and acute gouty arthritis. As antipyretics, they lower elevated body temperature by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis in the preoptic area of the anterior hypothalamus, resetting the body's thermal set point during febrile illnesses such as malaria or viral infections.

Adverse Effects and Systemic Complications

While effective, systemic inhibition of prostaglandins triggers several adverse events affecting multiple organ systems. Nurses must remain vigilant for these complications during drug therapy.

  • Gastrointestinal Tract: Suppressing protective mucosal prostaglandins leads to dyspepsia, erosive gastritis, peptic ulcer disease, and potentially life-threatening gastrointestinal bleeding or perforation.
  • Renal System: Prostaglandins maintain renal vasodilation, especially in volume-depleted states. NSAIDs can precipitate acute kidney injury, fluid retention, peripheral edema, and worsening hypertension.
  • Cardiovascular System: Regular use, particularly with COX-2 inhibitors and high-dose diclofenac, is associated with an increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and thrombotic events due to an imbalance between thromboxane and prostacyclin.
  • Hematologic System: Non-selective agents impair platelet function by blocking thromboxane A2 synthesis, prolonging bleeding time. Aspirin does this irreversibly for the lifespan of the platelet.
  • Respiratory System: Some individuals experience bronchospasm, known as NSAID-exacerbated respiratory disease, triggered by a shift in arachidonic acid metabolism toward leukotrienes.

Contraindications and Drug Interactions

Certain patient profiles and concurrent drug regimens make NSAID administration hazardous. Careful screening prevents preventable hospital admissions.

Absolute and relative contraindications include active peptic ulcer disease, severe heart failure, advanced chronic kidney disease, third-trimester pregnancy (due to the risk of premature closure of the ductus arteriosus), and known hypersensitivity or aspirin-induced asthma.

Dangerous pharmacological interactions include:

  • Anticoagulants and Antiplatelets: Combining NSAIDs with warfarin, heparin, or clopidogrel exponentially increases the risk of major bleeding.
  • Antihypertensives and Diuretics: NSAIDs blunt the efficacy of ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and diuretics by causing sodium retention and reducing renal blood flow.
  • Corticosteroids: Co-administration significantly multiplies the risk of gastric ulceration and severe gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Lithium and Methotrexate: NSAIDs decrease the renal clearance of these drugs, leading to toxic drug accumulations in the bloodstream.

Nursing Assessment and Interventions

Safe medication administration requires rigorous pre-administration assessment and vigilant post-administration monitoring. The nurse acts as the final safety barrier before a patient ingests these agents.

Prior to administration, the nurse must review the patient's medical history for renal impairment, peptic ulcers, cardiovascular disease, and bleeding disorders. Baseline vital signs, particularly blood pressure, should be documented since these drugs can exacerbate hypertension. Assessing pain intensity using a standardized scale establishes a baseline to evaluate treatment efficacy.

Administering oral NSAIDs with food, milk, or a proton pump inhibitor minimizes local gastric irritation. Nurses must monitor for signs of occult bleeding, such as black tarry stools or coffee-ground emesis, and observe for peripheral edema or sudden weight gain indicating fluid retention. Patient education must emphasize adhering strictly to prescribed dosage limits, avoiding concurrent over-the-counter pain relievers, and reporting unusual bruising or abdominal pain immediately.
