Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs (NSAIDs)

Mechanism of Action and Classification

Non-Steroidal Anti-Inflammatory Drugs work primarily by inhibiting the enzyme cyclooxygenase, which blocks the synthesis of prostaglandins from arachidonic acid. Prostaglandins are lipid compounds that mediate inflammation, fever, and pain transmission in peripheral tissues.

  • COX-1: Constitutively expressed in tissues; responsible for gastric mucosal protection, renal blood flow, and platelet aggregation.
  • COX-2: Induced at sites of injury and inflammation by cytokines and inflammatory mediators.

Classification separates agents based on their selectivity for these two enzyme isoforms:

  • Non-selective NSAIDs: Inhibit both COX-1 and COX-2 (e.g., Ibuprofen, Diclofenac, Aspirin, Indomethacin).
  • Selective COX-2 Inhibitors: Target primarily the COX-2 enzyme to reduce inflammation while sparing gastric mucosal protection (e.g., Celecoxib).

Therapeutic Indications

Clinical application of NSAIDs relies on their triple pharmacological properties: analgesic, anti-inflammatory, and antipyretic actions. Nurses administer these agents across various healthcare settings for musculoskeletal disorders, acute trauma, postoperative pain, and mild-to-moderate pyrexia.

  • Musculoskeletal Conditions: Osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis management.
  • Pain Management: Dysmenorrhea, tension headaches, and mild dental pain.
  • Fever Reduction: Symptomatic management of elevated body temperature during systemic infections.

Adverse Effects and Systemic Complications

Inhibition of housekeeping prostaglandins leads to predictable systemic side effects. The gastrointestinal tract frequently bears the brunt of non-selective NSAID therapy, ranging from dyspepsia to occult bleeding and peptic ulcer perforation.

Contraindications and Drug Interactions

Certain clinical profiles preclude safe administration of NSAIDs. Active peptic ulcer disease, severe renal impairment, and known hypersensitivity reactions absolute contraindicate use. Pregnant women in their third trimester must avoid NSAIDs due to the risk of premature closure of the ductus arteriosus in the fetus.

Concurrent administration with anticoagulants like warfarin or heparin exponentially increases bleeding risks. Combining NSAIDs with ACE inhibitors or loop diuretics diminishes the antihypertensive and diuretic efficacy while heightening the risk of nephrotoxicity.

Nursing Assessment and Interventions

Comprehensive pre-administration assessment prevents avoidable complications. The nurse must review the patient's medical history for a prior history of asthma, peptic ulcers, kidney disease, or cardiovascular disorders. Baseline blood pressure, serum creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen values establish reference points for monitoring.

Administering oral NSAIDs with food, milk, or a proton pump inhibitor minimizes gastric irritation. Nurses must monitor for signs of occult blood loss, black tarry stools, and unexplained bruising. Patient education emphasizes adhering strictly to prescribed dosage limits and avoiding concurrent use of over-the-counter analgesics without professional guidance.
