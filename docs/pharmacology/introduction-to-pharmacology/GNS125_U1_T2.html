Pharmacology

Introduction and Core Terminologies in Pharmacology

Pharmacology is the formal scientific study of how drugs interact with living systems. It combines biology, chemistry, and physiology to understand therapeutic interventions. To master this subject, you must grasp a few foundational terms:

  • Drug: Any chemical substance that alters biological function when introduced to a living organism.
  • Pharmacology: The broad science of drug action, uses, and adverse effects.
  • Pharmacy: The clinical science of preparing, compounding, and dispensing medicines.
  • Toxicology: The specific study of poisons, toxins, and the harmful effects of chemicals on the body.

Historically, early healers relied entirely on crude plant extracts, animal products, and minerals. Modern pharmacology evolved as scientists learned to isolate active chemical compounds, synthesize medicines in laboratories, and test them rigorously for safety and efficacy.

The Dual Pillars: Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacology divides neatly into two main branches that explain the life cycle of a drug inside the body. Think of pharmacokinetics as what the body does to the drug, and pharmacodynamics as what the drug does to the body.

Pharmacokinetics tracks four distinct stages, often remembered by the acronym ADME:

  1. Absorption: How the drug enters the bloodstream from its administration site.
  2. Distribution: How the drug travels through the blood to various tissues and organs.
  3. Metabolism: How the body chemically alters the drug, mostly taking place in the liver.
  4. Excretion: How the body removes the drug waste, primarily through the kidneys and urine.

Pharmacodynamics explores cellular receptors, chemical signaling, and the mechanisms that produce therapeutic results or side effects.

Drug Nomenclature: Naming Medications

Every medication can be identified by three distinct names. Understanding these names prevents clinical errors when reading prescriptions or charting.

  • Chemical Name: Describes the exact chemical structure and molecular formula using complex scientific terminology. It is rarely used in daily clinical practice.
  • Generic Name: The official, nonproprietary name assigned by the United States Adopted Names Council. It is universal, cheaper, and lowercase (e.g., paracetamol, furosemide).
  • Brand Name: Also known as the trade name, this is chosen by the pharmaceutical company that manufactures and markets the drug. It always starts with a capital letter (e.g., Panadol, Lasix).

A common student misconception is that generic drugs are weaker than brand-name drugs. By law, generic medications must contain the exact same active ingredients and achieve similar blood concentrations as their brand-name counterparts.

Sources and Classification of Drugs

Medicines come from diverse origins across the natural and synthetic worlds. Historically, plants were the primary source, yielding life-saving compounds like digoxin from foxglove and morphine from poppies. Today, modern science also sources drugs from animals (such as insulin), minerals (such as magnesium sulfate), and synthetic laboratory creations.

To manage this vast pharmacopeia, drugs are classified by their therapeutic use or pharmacological action. For instance, medications used to lower high blood pressure are grouped as antihypertensives, while those that kill bacteria are classified as antibiotics. This system helps clinicians quickly understand a drug's primary purpose when reviewing a treatment plan.

Routes of Administration and Dosage Forms

How a medicine enters the body dictates how fast and how effectively it works. The two primary routes are enteral (involving the gastrointestinal tract, such as oral, sublingual, and rectal) and parenteral (injecting medications directly into tissues, veins, or muscles, bypassing the gut).

Topical forms, such as skin creams, eye drops, and inhalers, provide localized treatment directly to the affected organ or surface.

Adverse Reactions and Pharmacovigilance

Every drug carries the potential for unwanted effects alongside its therapeutic benefits. Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are harmful, unintended responses that occur at normal doses.

  • Side effects: Predictable, secondary effects that happen even at normal doses (e.g., drowsiness from antihistamines).
  • Allergic reactions: Immune-mediated responses ranging from mild rashes to life-threatening anaphylaxis.
  • Idiosyncratic reactions: Rare, unpredictable, and abnormal responses unique to an individual patient's genetics.

Pharmacovigilance refers to the ongoing science and clinical activities surrounding the detection, assessment, and prevention of adverse effects. Nurses play a frontline role in pharmacovigilance by monitoring patients closely after drug administration and reporting unusual reactions.

Safe Medication Administration: The Nurse's Role

Administering medications is one of the most frequent and critical responsibilities of a professional nurse. To prevent errors, nurses rely on the foundational framework known as the Rights of Medication Administration, which traditionally include the right patient, right drug, right dose, right time, right route, and right documentation.

Before giving any medication, the nurse must verify the prescription, check for known allergies, assess vital signs or lab values, and calculate dosages accurately. Never administer a drug prepared by someone else, and always stay with the patient until the oral medication is swallowed. Effective patient education completes the process, ensuring the client understands their treatment plan at home.
