Pharmacogenetics

Introduction to Pharmacogenetics and Pharmacogenomics

Pharmacogenetics and pharmacogenomics represent the convergence of pharmacology and genetics, shifting medical treatment from a trial-and-error approach to targeted, individualized therapy. While these terms are frequently used interchangeably, subtle distinctions exist. **Pharmacogenetics** typically examines how a single gene variant influences an individual's response to a specific drug. **Pharmacogenomics** is a broader discipline, studying the entire genome to understand how multiple genes influence drug interactions, efficacy, and toxicity. In clinical practice across Nigeria, understanding these concepts helps explain why two patients with identical diagnoses and weights can experience vastly different outcomes or toxicities when given the exact same standard dosage. Genetic polymorphisms in drug-metabolizing enzymes, receptors, and transporters dictate whether a drug is cleared too quickly, leading to therapeutic failure, or metabolized too slowly, resulting in dangerous accumulation and adverse events.

Genetic Variations and Drug Response

DNA sequence variations, most commonly **single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs)**, are the primary drivers of inter-individual variability in drug response. A single base pair substitution can alter the amino acid sequence of a protein, changing the conformation of an enzyme, receptor, or transporter. Based on these genetic differences, individuals are generally classified into distinct metabolic phenotypes: - **Poor metabolizers (PM)**: Individuals carrying variant alleles that severely reduce or abolish enzyme activity, increasing the risk of drug accumulation and toxicity. - **Intermediate metabolizers (IM)**: Individuals possessing one reduced-activity allele or two partially deficient alleles, showing metabolic rates between poor and extensive phenotypes. - **Extensive metabolizers (EM)**: Individuals with normal, fully functional wild-type alleles who metabolize drugs at the expected standard rate. - **Ultrarapid metabolizers (UM)**: Individuals with gene duplications resulting in excessive enzyme production, causing rapid drug clearance and potential therapeutic failure at standard doses.

Role of Cytochrome P450 Enzymes in Drug Metabolism

The **cytochrome P450 (CYP450)** enzyme superfamily, primarily located in the endoplasmic reticulum of hepatocytes, is responsible for the phase I oxidative metabolism of a vast majority of clinical drugs. Among the dozens of isoforms, **CYP2D6**, **CYP2C19**, and **CYP3A4/5** exhibit significant genetic polymorphism that profoundly affects pharmacotherapy. For instance, codeine is a prodrug that relies on the CYP2D6 enzyme for conversion into its active analgesic metabolite, morphine. An ultrarapid metabolizer receiving codeine can experience rapid, toxic conversion, resulting in severe respiratory depression. Conversely, a poor metabolizer will experience little to no pain relief because the conversion fails to occur. Recognizing these enzymatic pathways prevents both therapeutic failure and life-threatening adverse reactions in vulnerable patient populations.

Phase II Drug-Metabolizing Enzymes and Transporters

Beyond phase I oxidation, phase II conjugation reactions render lipophilic compounds more water-soluble for renal or biliary excretion. Enzymes such as **UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (UGTs)**, **sulfotransferases (SULTs)**, and **N-acetyltransferases (NATs)** add large polar groups to drug molecules. Genetic polymorphisms in these pathways can lead to severe toxicities. A classic example involves **thiopurine S-methyltransferase (TPMT)**, which metabolizes thiopurine drugs used in leukemia and immunosuppression; deficient variants cause fatal bone marrow suppression if doses are unadjusted. Furthermore, membrane transport proteins like **P-glycoprotein (ABCB1)** regulate the cellular influx and efflux of drugs across the blood-brain barrier, intestinal mucosa, and renal tubules. Variations in transporter genes alter drug bioavailability and tissue penetration, further complicating predictable clinical dosing.

Clinical Implications of Adverse Drug Reactions and Efficacy

Adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are a leading cause of hospital admissions and morbidity globally. Pharmacogenetic screening provides a powerful tool to mitigate these risks before a drug is ever administered. When clinicians match drug selection to a patient's genetic profile, the incidence of severe hypersensitivity reactions, organ toxicities, and therapeutic failure drops significantly. A prominent landmark example is the association between the human leukocyte antigen allele **HLA-B*57:01** and hypersensitivity to the antiretroviral medication abacavir. Routine screening for this allele virtually eliminates life-threatening severe cutaneous and systemic hypersensitivity reactions. Integrating genetic insights protects patients from preventable harm and optimizes therapeutic efficacy across diverse clinical settings.

Diagnostic Testing Methods in Pharmacogenetics

Translating genetic discoveries into clinical workflows relies on robust, accessible diagnostic technologies. Laboratories utilize several molecular techniques to detect specific genetic polymorphisms, including **polymerase chain reaction (PCR)**, **microarrays**, and emerging **next-generation sequencing (NGS)** platforms. PCR-based assays are popular for quick turnaround times when testing for common, well-characterized single nucleotide polymorphisms, such as specific CYP2C19 or CYP2D6 alleles. Microarrays can screen hundreds of thousands of genetic variants simultaneously, making them useful for comprehensive panels. Despite these technological advancements, challenges remain in resource-limited environments like Nigeria, where high testing costs, cold-chain maintenance, and limited specialized molecular laboratories can restrict routine clinical implementation.

Nursing Responsibilities and Personalized Patient Care

As personalized medicine expands, nurses occupy a pivotal frontline role in translating pharmacogenetic data into safe, holistic bedside care. Nurses participate in obtaining thorough family and ancestral medication histories, identifying patterns of adverse drug reactions or treatment failures within family lineages. Furthermore, nurses manage the collection of biological samples such as buccal swabs or blood draws for genetic testing, ensuring proper labeling and chain of custody. When complex dosing regimens based on genetic profiles are implemented, nurses monitor patients closely for subtle signs of toxicity or therapeutic failure, provide clear patient education regarding personalized treatment plans, and advocate for safe interprofessional medication management.
