Pharmacogenetics

Introduction to Pharmacogenetics and Pharmacogenomics

Pharmacogenetics examines how individual DNA differences alter drug behavior inside the body. While people often use pharmacogenetics and pharmacogenomics interchangeably, a subtle distinction exists. Pharmacogenetics typically looks at a single gene influencing drug response, whereas pharmacogenomics evaluates the broader picture involving the entire genome.

Understanding these genetic blueprints helps explain why two patients with the same malaria diagnosis can take an identical dose of an antimalarial drug: one recovers smoothly while the other develops severe toxicity. Recognizing these variations transforms prescribing habits from a trial-and-error approach into tailored, precision medicine.

Genetic Variations and Drug Response

Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms, commonly known as SNPs, are the most frequent source of genetic variation in human DNA. A single nucleotide change can alter an amino acid sequence, ultimately changing protein structure and function. When this occurs on a drug receptor, transporter, or metabolizing enzyme, the therapeutic outcome shifts dramatically.

Patients are categorized into distinct metabolizer phenotypes based on these genetic variations:

  • Poor metabolizers: Carry two variant alleles causing deficient enzyme activity, often leading to drug accumulation and toxicity.
  • Intermediate metabolizers: Possess one deficient allele, resulting in reduced clearance rates compared to normal standards.
  • Extensive metabolizers: Have normal gene function, processing drugs at the expected rate.
  • Ultrarapid metabolizers: Contain multiple gene copies, breaking down medications so quickly that standard doses fail to reach therapeutic levels.

Cytochrome P450 Enzymes in Drug Metabolism

The cytochrome P450 superfamily, abbreviated as CYP450, serves as the primary enzyme system responsible for metabolizing xenobiotics and drugs in the liver. Key isoenzymes include CYP2D6, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4, each handling a massive share of commonly prescribed pharmaceuticals.

  • CYP2D6: Highly polymorphic enzyme responsible for metabolizing many antidepressants, antipsychotics, and opioids like codeine.
  • CYP2C19: Processes proton pump inhibitors and antiplatelet agents such as clopidogrel.
  • CYP3A4: Metabolizes a vast range of statins, calcium channel blockers, and immunosuppressants, though it displays less genetic polymorphism than CYP2D6.

When a patient is a poor metabolizer for a specific CYP enzyme, prodrugs that require activation may fail to work, whereas active drugs may build up to dangerous levels.

Phase II Drug-Metabolizing Enzymes and Transporters

While Phase I enzymes modify drug structures through oxidation or reduction, Phase II enzymes perform conjugation reactions. These processes attach a polar, water-soluble molecule to the drug metabolite to ensure efficient excretion through urine or bile.

Clinical Implications of Adverse Drug Reactions and Efficacy

Adverse drug reactions claim thousands of lives annually, and genetic mismatches account for a significant percentage of these events. When a patient receives a standard dose of a drug their genetics cannot handle, the resulting toxicity can cause organ failure or hospitalization.

Conversely, lack of efficacy poses an equally dangerous clinical problem. If a patient fails to clear or activate a medication properly, their condition deteriorates while they take a useless pill. Pharmacogenetic testing minimizes these clinical failures by guiding drug selection before therapy begins, reducing adverse events and shortening hospital stays.

Diagnostic Testing Methods in Pharmacogenetics

Laboratory scientists use various molecular techniques to identify specific genetic variants. Polymerase Chain Reaction, commonly known as PCR, amplifies tiny segments of DNA so technicians can detect single nucleotide polymorphisms easily.

Microarrays and next-generation sequencing allow screening of multiple genes simultaneously, offering a comprehensive genetic profile from a single blood or buccal swab sample. Despite these advancements, clinical laboratories face hurdles such as high testing costs, turnaround delays, and the challenge of interpreting complex genetic reports for non-specialist clinicians.

Nursing Responsibilities and Personalized Patient Care

Nurses play a vital role in the pharmacogenetic workflow, starting from patient assessment to medication administration and monitoring. Nurses often collect buccal swabs or blood samples for genetic panels, ensuring correct labeling and handling.

Beyond sample collection, nurses must review medication administration records for potential drug-gene interactions. When a pharmacogenetic report indicates an altered metabolizer status, the nurse collaborates with the interprofessional team to advocate for dose adjustments or alternative drug therapies. Patient education remains central; nurses must explain the purpose of genetic testing in clear, accessible language, reassuring patients that these insights help protect them from preventable drug reactions.
