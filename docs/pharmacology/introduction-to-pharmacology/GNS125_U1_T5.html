Pharmacogenetics

Introduction to Pharmacogenetics and Pharmacogenomics

Pharmacogenetics is the study of how human genetic variations influence individual responses to medications. While pharmacogenetics traditionally looks at a single gene, pharmacogenomics examines the wider genome to understand complex drug interactions. Together, these fields aim to maximize drug effectiveness while minimizing adverse events. Medications that work well for one person may cause severe toxicity or fail entirely in another, largely due to inherited differences in our DNA. By understanding these biological differences, healthcare teams can move away from a one-size-fits-all prescribing model toward safer, individualized drug therapy.

Genetic Variations and Drug Response

Most genetic variations affecting drug response involve single nucleotide polymorphisms, commonly known as SNPs. A SNP represents a tiny alteration in a single building block of DNA. These small changes can alter protein structures, receptor sensitivity, or the speed at which enzymes process chemical substances. Based on how these variations impact drug metabolism, patients are generally grouped into distinct metabolic phenotypes:

  • Poor metabolizers: Break down drugs very slowly, which can lead to a dangerous buildup of the medication in the body.
  • Intermediate metabolizers: Process drugs slightly slower than average.
  • Extensive metabolizers: Process drugs at a normal, expected rate.
  • Ultrarapid metabolizers: Break down medications so quickly that the drug may leave the system before it can provide any therapeutic benefit.

Role of Cytochrome P450 Enzymes in Drug Metabolism

The cytochrome P450 enzyme system, located primarily in the liver, is responsible for breaking down a vast majority of clinical drugs. Key enzymes in this family include CYP2D6, CYP2C19, and CYP3A4. When a patient has a genetic variant affecting one of these enzymes, drug processing changes dramatically. For example, prodrugs—medications that require activation by liver enzymes—will fail to work in poor metabolizers because the enzyme cannot convert the drug into its active form. Conversely, in ultrarapid metabolizers, a prodrug might convert too fast, causing sudden peak toxicity.

Phase II Enzymes and Drug Transporters

While Phase I cytochrome P450 enzymes modify drugs through oxidation or reduction, Phase II drug-metabolizing enzymes add a chemical group to make the drug water-soluble for easy elimination. Enzymes like thiopurine S-methyltransferase and UDP-glucuronosyltransferase handle these conjugation reactions. Additionally, membrane drug transporters like the P-glycoprotein pump control how drugs move across cell barriers and enter target tissues or the central nervous system. Genetic variations in these transport proteins can alter how much medication reaches an organ, changing both efficacy and side effect risks.

Clinical Implications of Adverse Drug Reactions

Inherited genetics play a massive role in unexpected adverse drug reactions. A classic clinical example involves human leukocyte antigen alleles, such as HLA-B*5701, which strongly predicts severe hypersensitivity reactions to the antiviral drug abacavir. Testing patients for this specific gene before starting therapy prevents life-threatening skin and organ reactions. Recognizing these genetic risk factors shifts medicine from reactive treatment—waiting for a patient to get sick from a pill—to proactive prevention.

Diagnostic Testing Methods

Several laboratory methods are used to determine a patient's genetic profile. Polymerase chain reaction and microarray assays can rapidly detect specific SNPs or identify whole panels of drug-metabolizing gene variants from a simple cheek swab or blood draw. While these tests provide valuable guidance, nurses must remember that laboratory results are only one piece of the puzzle. Factors like age, liver function, kidney health, diet, and concurrent medications also heavily influence how a drug behaves in the living body.

Nursing Responsibilities in Personalized Care

Nurses play a vital frontline role in translating pharmacogenetic insights into safe bedside care. Responsibilities include taking a thorough family medication history, checking for gene-drug warnings before administration, and monitoring patients closely for unexpected side effects or therapeutic failure. Nurses also help educate patients on why genetic testing is ordered and explain that these results remain relevant for life, guiding future prescriptions long after the current hospital discharge.
