Pharmacokinetics

Introduction to Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics is simply what the body does to a drug from the moment it enters until it leaves completely. Nurses often summarize this using the four steps of ADME: Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, and Excretion. Understanding these steps helps explain why we give medications through different routes, why some drugs work instantly while others take hours, and why doses must be adjusted for patients with failing organs. When a patient takes a pill, the body does not just passively let the chemical sit there; it attacks it, breaks it down, moves it around, and flushes it out. Every single step involves moving across biological membranes, which rely heavily on fat and water solubility.

Absorption of Drugs Across Biological Membranes

Absorption is how a drug gets from its site of administration into the bloodstream. Before a drug can enter the blood, it must cross cellular membranes made of lipid bilayers.

  • Passive diffusion: Most drugs move this way, sliding naturally from an area of high concentration to low concentration without needing energy.
  • Active transport: Special carrier proteins pick up the drug and use cellular energy to move it across the membrane.
  • Pinocytosis: The cell membrane swallows up the drug droplet to bring it inside.

Factors like stomach pH, blood flow to the area, and lipid solubility greatly affect how fast and how much of the drug gets absorbed. For example, oral drugs must survive acidic stomach juices and liver enzymes before reaching systemic circulation.

Drug Distribution and Volume of Distribution

Once a drug enters the bloodstream, it travels to various tissues and organs throughout the body, a process called distribution. Blood flow determines how fast a drug arrives: organs with rich blood supplies like the heart, brain, and liver get the drug almost immediately, while fat and skin take much longer.

Proteins floating in the plasma, mainly albumin, can bind to these drugs. Only the free drug—the portion not attached to a protein—can leave the blood vessels to interact with target receptor sites and produce an effect.

Volume of distribution (Vd) is a theoretical concept nurses use to understand how widely a drug spreads. A drug that stays mostly in the blood has a small Vd, while a drug that hides deeply inside body fat and tissues has a very large Vd.

Hepatic Metabolism and Biotransformation

Metabolism, also called biotransformation, chemically changes a drug so the body can eventually get rid of it. The liver is the main organ responsible for this, largely thanks to a family of enzymes known as the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) system.

Metabolism usually turns an active drug into an inactive, water-soluble metabolite that the kidneys can easily filter out. However, some drugs start as inactive substances called prodrugs and require liver metabolism to become active. Certain medications can speed up (induce) or slow down (inhibit) these liver enzymes, creating serious risks for drug-to-drug interactions. For example, if a drug inhibits a CYP enzyme, another drug processed by that same enzyme can build up to toxic levels in the blood.

Renal and Extra-Renal Drug Excretion

Excretion is the removal of drugs and their metabolites from the body. The kidneys are the primary organs for this job, filtering blood through the glomerulus and eliminating waste via urine. If a patient has poor renal function, drugs can accumulate quickly, leading to dangerous toxicity.

While the kidneys handle water-soluble waste, extra-renal routes eliminate drugs through other channels:

  • Biliary excretion: The liver dumps drugs into the bile, which passes into the GI tract and leaves via feces.
  • Lungs: Volatile gases and anesthetics leave the body every time a patient exhales.
  • Sweat and breast milk: Minor pathways, though lipid-soluble drugs in breast milk can pass from a nursing mother to her infant.

Half-Life and Clearance

Pharmacokinetic parameters give nurses mathematical tools to predict how long a drug stays active in the body.

  • Half-life ($t_{1/2}$): The time it takes for the concentration of a drug in the body to drop by exactly 50%. Drugs with a short half-life must be given frequently, while drugs with a long half-life stay in the system for days after the last pill.
  • Clearance: The volume of blood cleared of a drug per unit of time, reflecting how efficiently the kidneys and liver remove the substance.

It generally takes about four to five half-lives for a drug to be almost completely eliminated from the body once it is stopped.

Bioavailability and Steady-State Concentration

Bioavailability measures the fraction of an administered drug that reaches systemic circulation unchanged. Intravenous (IV) medications have a bioavailability of 100% because they go directly into the blood, bypassing the digestive tract and liver. Oral medications often have much lower bioavailability due to poor absorption and first-pass metabolism.

When a patient takes regular doses of a medication over time, the drug accumulates until the amount being absorbed equals the amount being eliminated. This balanced plateau is called steady-state concentration. It usually takes about four to five half-lives of repeated dosing to reach steady-state, which is why antibiotics or blood pressure pills take several days of consistent dosing before achieving their full therapeutic effect.

Clinical Implications in Nursing Practice

Applying pharmacokinetics at the bedside keeps patients safe. Nurses monitor lab values like serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) to evaluate kidney function, and liver enzymes like ALT and AST to assess hepatic health. If organ function drops, drug dosages must be adjusted downward to prevent toxicity.

Nurses also teach patients why compliance matters: skipping doses or taking medications irregularly disrupts steady-state levels, causing drug concentrations to drop below the therapeutic window. Recognizing age-related changes is equally critical; elderly patients often have reduced kidney and liver function, requiring lower doses of many common drugs to avoid adverse effects.
