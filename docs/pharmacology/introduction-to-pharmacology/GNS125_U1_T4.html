Pharmacokinetics

Introduction to Pharmacokinetics

Pharmacokinetics is the mathematical study of how the human body alters a drug over time. It tracks four fundamental physiological phases, often summarized by the acronym ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. While pharmacodynamics describes what a drug does to the body, pharmacokinetics describes what the body does to the drug. Understanding these movement patterns helps explain why a specific dose must be given every eight hours rather than twelve, or why a patient with liver failure might experience prolonged drug effects. Every clinical administration relies on these principles to maintain therapeutic drug concentrations without tipping into toxicity.

Absorption of Drugs Across Biological Membranes

Drug absorption is the movement of a medication from its site of administration into the systemic circulation. Most drugs cross cellular barriers via passive diffusion, moving down a concentration gradient. Lipid-soluble, unionized molecules cross these phospholipid bilayers with ease, whereas water-soluble or ionized molecules often require specialized transport proteins.

  • Route Dependency: Intravenous administration bypasses absorption entirely, achieving 100% immediate bioavailability. Oral medications face physical barriers like gastric acidity, first-pass metabolism in the gut wall and liver, and varying GI motility.
  • Physicochemical Factors: Molecular size, lipid solubility, and the surrounding pH profoundly influence whether a drug remains in an absorbable form.

Drug Distribution and Volume of Distribution

Once in the bloodstream, a drug distributes into interstitial and intracellular fluids. Distribution is governed by cardiac output, regional blood flow, capillary permeability, and tissue binding affinity. Highly vascular organs like the brain, heart, and liver receive drugs rapidly, while adipose tissue, skin, and bone receive them much slower.

Volume of distribution (Vd) is a theoretical parameter that relates the total amount of drug in the body to the concentration of drug in the plasma. A very high Vd indicates that the drug leaves the vascular compartment extensively and accumulates in peripheral tissues, such as fat or muscle, meaning higher doses might be required to achieve an effective plasma level.

Hepatic Metabolism and Biotransformation

Biotransformation chemically alters a drug to facilitate its removal from the body, primarily taking place in the liver. This process typically converts lipid-soluble active drugs into water-soluble inactive metabolites that the kidneys can easily filter out. Hepatic clearance relies heavily on the cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme system.

Phase I reactions involve oxidation, reduction, or hydrolysis, which expose or create a polar functional group. Phase II reactions involve conjugation, attaching a bulky, polar moiety like glucuronic acid to the drug molecule. Certain medications act as enzyme inducers, accelerating the breakdown of other drugs, while others act as enzyme inhibitors, potentially causing dangerous drug toxicity due to drug accumulation.

Renal and Extra-Renal Drug Excretion

Excretion removes drugs and their metabolites from the body. The kidneys serve as the primary organ of elimination, utilizing three distinct processes: glomerular filtration, active tubular secretion, and passive tubular reabsorption. Small, unbound water-soluble molecules filter freely through the glomerulus.

Extra-renal routes also play vital roles. Volatile anesthetics and alcohol are largely eliminated through the lungs via expired air. Lipophilic compounds and heavy metals can be excreted through bile into the feces. Minor routes include sweat, tears, and breast milk, which holds clinical significance for nursing mothers regarding neonatal drug exposure.

Pharmacokinetic Parameters: Half-Life and Clearance

Drug half-life ($t_{1/2}$) is the time required for the plasma concentration of a drug to decrease by fifty percent. It dictates dosing intervals; drugs with short half-lives require frequent administration or sustained-release formulations to maintain stable therapeutic effects. Clearance describes the volume of plasma cleared of a drug per unit of time, reflecting the combined efficiency of hepatic metabolism and renal excretion.

In clinical practice, assuming normal organ function, it takes approximately four to five half-lives for a drug to reach near-complete elimination or steady-state concentrations after dosage adjustments.

Bioavailability and Steady-State Concentration

Bioavailability represents the fraction of an administered drug dose that reaches the systemic circulation in an unchanged form. Intravenous administration achieves 100% bioavailability, whereas oral drugs often have lower values due to incomplete gut absorption and first-pass hepatic metabolism.

Steady-state concentration occurs when the rate of drug input equals the rate of drug elimination. Reaching steady state does not depend on the dose size, but rather on the drug's half-life. Continuous dosing at regular intervals achieves this equilibrium, ensuring that therapeutic highs and toxic lows are successfully avoided during ongoing therapy.

Clinical Implications in Nursing Practice

Translating pharmacokinetics to the bedside prevents adverse events and optimizes patient outcomes. Nurses must assess renal function (via serum creatinine and estimated creatinine clearance) and liver function (via transaminases and bilirubin) before administering medications cleared by these pathways. Impaired organ function slows elimination, requiring calculated dose reductions.

Geriatric and pediatric populations require special attention. Neonates have immature renal and hepatic clearance mechanisms, predisposing them to toxicity. Older adults experience age-related declines in glomerular filtration rates, meaning standard adult doses can easily lead to drug accumulation and overdose if adjustments are missed.
