Drugs

Fundamental Concepts and Sources of Drugs

A drug is any chemical substance that alters biological function when administered to a living organism. In clinical practice, therapeutic substances are utilized for diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of disease. These agents originate from diverse sources, which dictate their purity, complexity, and manufacturing cost.

  • Plant sources: Botanical extracts such as morphine from the opium poppy or digoxin from digitalis leaves have formed the backbone of pharmacotherapy for centuries.
  • Animal sources: Hormones and biological fluids, historically including insulin extracted from porcine and bovine pancreases, provide vital replacement therapies.
  • Mineral sources: Inorganic elements like magnesium sulfate and iron salts serve specific therapeutic roles in electrolyte balance and hematology.
  • Synthetic sources: Laboratory-created compounds, produced through organic chemistry, dominate modern formularies because they offer consistent potency and fewer batch-to-batch impurities.

Drug Nomenclature: Understanding Names

Every pharmaceutical agent possesses up to three distinct designations, which frequently confuses patients and junior clinicians alike.

  • Chemical name: Describes the exact molecular structure and atomic composition of the drug, utilizing complex organic chemistry nomenclature. It is rarely used in clinical settings.
  • Generic name (Nonproprietary name): Assigned by the United States Adopted Names Council or the International Nonproprietary Names committee. This official name identifies the active pharmacological ingredient, remains constant regardless of the manufacturer, and is the standard terminology utilized on nursing licensure examinations.
  • Trade name (Brand name): Designated by the pharmaceutical company that markets the product. A single generic medication, such as paracetamol, may have dozens of distinct trade names depending on the manufacturing laboratory.

Classification Systems of Therapeutics

Medications are categorized into groups to simplify clinical study and safe administration. These systems generally reflect either the body system the drug affects or its primary therapeutic utility.

A single medication frequently belongs to multiple classes. For instance, furosemide functions therapeutically as an antihypertensive and diuretic, while pharmacologically acting as a loop diuretic by inhibiting sodium and chloride reabsorption in the ascending loop of Henle.

Pharmacokinetics: Journey of a Drug Through the Body

Pharmacokinetics examines what the body does to a drug, broken down into four distinct physiological phases: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME).

  • Absorption: The movement of the drug from its site of administration into the systemic circulation. Factors like route, blood flow, and lipid solubility dictate absorption rates.
  • Distribution: The transport of the drug throughout body fluids to target tissues. Plasma protein binding, particularly to albumin, heavily influences how much active drug reaches receptors.
  • Metabolism: The enzymatic biotransformation of the drug, primarily occurring in the liver via the cytochrome P450 enzyme system, turning active compounds into water-soluble metabolites.
  • Excretion: The elimination of drug metabolites from the body, chiefly executed by the kidneys through urine, though biliary and respiratory routes also contribute.

Pharmacodynamics: Mechanisms of Drug Action

Pharmacodynamics explores what a drug does to the body and the biochemical changes that ensue. Medications exert their effects by interacting with cellular receptors, enzymes, or physical membranes.

An agonist is a substance that binds to a receptor and initiates a physiological response, mimicking endogenous signaling molecules. Conversely, an antagonist binds to the same receptor but blocks external signaling molecules from activating it, preventing cellular responses. The relationship between the concentration of a drug at receptor sites and the magnitude of the resulting clinical response determines its potency and maximum efficacy. Understanding these cellular interactions helps clinicians predict therapeutic windows and avoid toxicity.

Drug Legislation, Regulation, and Safety in Nigeria

Pharmaceutical safety requires strict statutory frameworks to protect the public from substandard, falsified, and adulterated medications. In Nigeria, the primary regulatory body is the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC), established to regulate and control the importation, exportation, manufacture, advertisement, distribution, sale, and use of drugs.

Counterfeit drugs pose a severe public health hazard across sub-Saharan Africa. Nursing professionals must actively inspect medication packaging for NAFDAC registration numbers, expiration dates, and physical integrity before administration. Clinical vigilance prevents toxic reactions and ensures that patients receive authentic therapeutic agents.

The Nurse's Role in Safe Medication Administration

The nurse acts as the final safeguard in the medication delivery system, bridging the gap between medical prescription and patient consumption. Professional accountability demands strict adherence to safety protocols.

  • Right Patient: Verify identity using two unique identifiers, such as checking the hospital identification band and asking the patient to state their full name.
  • Right Medication: Compare the medication label with the physician's order sheet three times.
  • Right Dose: Perform independent double-checks for high-alert medications like insulin or heparin.
  • Right Route: Confirm the administration pathway matches the order precisely.
  • Right Time: Administer medications within the prescribed clinical window to maintain therapeutic blood levels.
  • Right Documentation: Record administration immediately after delivery, never beforehand.
