Drugs

Introduction and Fundamental Concepts of Drugs

A drug is any chemical substance that alters biological function when introduced into a living body. In nursing, we deal with therapeutic substances used to prevent, diagnose, treat, or relieve symptoms of disease. Understanding how these substances interact with human physiology forms the bedrock of safe clinical practice. Drugs do not create new cellular functions; instead, they alter existing cellular activity by either mimicking or blocking natural body chemicals. Every drug carries both a desired therapeutic effect and potential unwanted effects. As nurses, our primary duty is maximizing the therapeutic benefit while actively monitoring for adverse reactions to protect the patient.

Sources of Drugs: Natural and Synthetic

Medications come from diverse origins across the globe. Plant sources have provided healing remedies for centuries, offering vital compounds like digitalis from the foxglove plant or morphine from the opium poppy. Animal sources supply important replacement products, such as insulin derived from animal pancreases in early medical history. Mineral sources contribute inorganic elements like magnesium sulfate and potassium chloride. However, modern medicine relies heavily on synthetic drugs created in laboratories. These laboratory-produced compounds often provide greater purity, consistency, and mass-production capability than harvesting raw materials from nature.

Drug Nomenclature: Chemical, Generic, and Trade Names

Every medication can be referred to by three distinct names, which often causes confusion for student nurses.

  • Chemical name: Describes the exact atomic and molecular structure of the drug using complex chemical terminology.
  • Generic name: The official, nonproprietary name assigned by the United States Adopted Names Council or international bodies, universally used regardless of the manufacturer.
  • Trade name: Also known as the brand name, this is chosen by the pharmaceutical company that manufactures and markets the drug, usually featuring a trademark symbol.

For example, the pain reliever with the chemical name N-(4-hydroxyphenyl)acetamide has the generic name paracetamol and trade names like Panadol or Tylenol.

Classification Systems of Drugs

Drugs are categorized into classes based on their therapeutic use, physiological effect, or chemical structure. Categorization helps healthcare workers manage vast pharmaceutical knowledge by grouping drugs with similar properties. For instance, antihypertensives group all drugs that lower blood pressure, even though an ACE inhibitor works entirely differently than a beta-blocker. A single drug can belong to multiple classes depending on how it is being studied. Understanding these categories allows you to anticipate patient responses and recognize standard treatment protocols for specific disease states.

Overview of Pharmacokinetics and Pharmacodynamics

Pharmacology relies on two core pillars: pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics.

  • Pharmacokinetics: What the body does to the drug. It involves four key stages: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME).
  • Pharmacodynamics: What the drug does to the body. It examines cellular receptors, mechanism of action, and the relationship between drug concentration and clinical effect.

Poor kidney function will alter pharmacokinetics by slowing drug excretion, which can lead to toxic accumulation. Meanwhile, pharmacodynamics explains why a specific beta-blocker slows down the heart rate.

Drug Legislation and Safety in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the regulation and control of importation, manufacture, distribution, and use of drugs are overseen by specific regulatory bodies, primarily the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) and the Pharmacists Council of Nigeria (PCN). These agencies protect the public from fake, substandard, and expired medications, which remain a significant public health challenge. Healthcare facilities enforce strict policies regarding controlled substances, storage security, and prescription protocols. Nurses must remain vigilant, verify drug registration numbers, and report any suspected adverse drug reactions or counterfeit products through official channels.

The Nurse's Role in Safe Medication Administration

Safe medication administration is one of your most critical clinical responsibilities. Errors can happen at any point from prescription to administration. To prevent mistakes, nurses strictly apply the foundational safety rules known as the rights of medication administration—including the right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, right documentation, and right reason. Always perform the three safety checks against the medication administration record (MAR): when retrieving the drug, when preparing it, and right before administering it at the bedside. Patient assessment, checking for allergies, and patient education complete this vital safety circle.
