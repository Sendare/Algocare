Intravenous Route

Introduction and Definition of the Intravenous Route

The **intravenous (IV) route** involves delivering medications, fluids, and blood products directly into a patient's venous circulation. By bypassing the digestive tract and first-pass hepatic metabolism, this method achieves immediate bioavailability and precise control over blood concentrations. It serves as a cornerstone of acute hospital care across Nigerian tertiary and secondary health facilities, particularly for emergency resuscitation, rapid electrolyte correction, and the administration of drugs that are irritating to subcutaneous or muscle tissues. Because the substance enters the bloodstream instantly, clinicians must maintain rigorous precision regarding dosage calculations and aseptic technique, as adverse reactions unfold with alarming speed.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Intravenous Therapy

Choosing the intravenous route involves weighing distinct clinical benefits against potential risks. - **Immediate Onset**: Ideal for emergency resuscitation, pain crises, and critical care. - **Precise Titration**: Infusion rates can be adjusted continuously to match patient hemodynamic response. - **Gastrointestinal Bypass**: Useful for patients with severe vomiting, malabsorption, or nil per os (NPO) status. - **Irritant Administration**: Concentrated or vesicant drugs can be diluted in large blood volumes, reducing local tissue injury. - **Infection Risk**: Breaks the skin barrier, creating a direct port of entry for microorganisms. - **Fluid Overload**: Rapid infusion can precipitate acute pulmonary edema, especially in cardiac or renal impairment. - **Infiltration and Phlebitis**: Mechanical irritation or vessel rupture causes localized pain and tissue damage.

Anatomical Sites and Vein Selection for Venipuncture

Successful cannulation depends heavily on strategic vein selection. For peripheral access, the veins of the dorsal hand and forearm—such as the cephalic, basilic, and median cubital veins—are standard first choices. Clinicians should systematically inspect and palpate potential sites, moving from distal to proximal areas of the upper extremities so that future sites remain available higher up if a vein infiltrates. Avoid veins in the lower extremities whenever possible due to sluggish blood flow and a significantly elevated risk of thromboembolism. Furthermore, nurses must avoid sites over joints, areas of previous phlebitis, scarred skin, or limbs affected by lymphedema, paralysis, or an active dialysis fistula.

Equipment and Materials Needed for IV Cannulation

Gathering and organizing equipment before approaching the patient ensures a smooth, aseptic procedure. Essential items include: - **IV Cannula / Catheter**: Sized appropriately; 18G to 20G for rapid fluid resuscitation or blood, and 22G to 24G for standard medications or fragile veins. - **Tourniquet**: Used to distend veins by obstructing venous return while keeping arterial flow intact. - **Antiseptic Swab**: 70% alcohol, chlorhexidine, or povidone-iodine to decontaminate the insertion site. - **Transparent Dressing**: Secures the catheter and allows continuous visual inspection of the puncture site. - **Extension Set and Flush**: Prefilled saline flush syringe to confirm vein patency post-insertion. - **Personal Protective Equipment**: Clean gloves are mandatory to prevent bloodborne pathogen exposure.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Initiating IV Access

Initiating peripheral IV access requires methodical technique and strict infection control. Begin by verifying the medical order, explaining the procedure to the patient, and performing hand hygiene. Apply the tourniquet 10 to 15 centimeters above the chosen site and select a bouncy, straight vein. Cleanse the skin using a friction scrub with an antiseptic agent and allow it to air-dry completely to maximize antimicrobial efficacy. Anchor the vein by pulling the skin taut distally. Insert the needle with the bevel facing upward at a 15 to 30-degree angle. Upon observing a flashback of blood in the chamber, lower the catheter almost parallel to the skin, advance the catheter slightly off the needle, and withdraw the stylet while releasing the tourniquet. Connect the extension set, flush with normal saline to check for swelling or resistance, and secure the site with a sterile transparent dressing.

Methods of IV Administration: Bolus, Infusion, and Piggyback

Intravenous medications are administered through three primary delivery methods depending on clinical goals. An **IV bolus** (or push) delivers a concentrated medication directly into the vein over a short timeframe, requiring careful monitoring for rapid side effects. **Continuous infusion** involves large volumes of fluids mixed with medications, delivered steadily via gravity or an electronic infusion pump over several hours to maintain constant therapeutic blood levels. **Intermittent infusion** (commonly known as a **piggyback** setup) attaches a secondary medication bag to the primary line, allowing secondary antibiotics or compatible drugs to infuse intermittently without altering the primary fluid rate.

Calculation of IV Flow Rates and Infusion Doses

Nurses must accurately calculate infusion rates to ensure patient safety and prevent fluid overload or under-medication. The standard formula for gravity infusion using drop factor is: $$\text{Flow Rate (gtt/min)} = \frac{\text{Total Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Time (minutes)}}$| Common drop factors include standard sets (10, 15, or 20 gtt/mL) and microdrip sets (60 gtt/mL). For electronic infusion pumps, the rate is simply programmed in milliliters per hour (mL/hr). Practicing these calculations prevents severe medication errors, particularly when administering potent vasoactive drugs or managing pediatric fluid requirements.

Monitoring, Maintenance, and Complications of IV Therapy

Routine surveillance preserves line patency and protects patients from localized and systemic complications. Nurses must inspect the insertion site and surrounding tissue every shift for signs of **infiltration** (cool skin, swelling, and leakage), **extravasation** (pain, blistering, or necrosis from vesicant drugs), and **phlebitis** (erythema, warmth, and a palpable cord along the vein). Dressings must remain clean, dry, and intact, and IV tubing must be replaced according to institutional infection control policy (typically every 72 to 96 hours). If complications arise, stop the infusion immediately, disconnect the line, remove the catheter, and apply warm or cold compresses depending on the specific fluid or drug involved.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Safety Guidelines

Safe intravenous therapy relies on unwavering adherence to professional standards and institutional protocols. Nurses must verify the five rights of medication administration before every infusion and check compatibility tables when administering concurrent solutions. Universal precautions and hand hygiene remain non-negotiable standards to prevent catheter-related bloodstream infections. Additionally, nurses should educate patients and their caregivers to report any localized pain, burning, or sudden wetness around the dressing site promptly, fostering a collaborative approach to safety in clinical wards.
