Intravenous Route

Introduction and Definition of the Intravenous Route

The intravenous (IV) route involves delivering medications, fluids, and blood products directly into a patient's systemic circulation through a vein. Unlike oral or subcutaneous routes that rely on absorption across membranes, IV administration provides 100% immediate bioavailability. This makes it the preferred route during medical emergencies, critical care, and situations requiring precise, rapid blood concentration levels of powerful medications.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Intravenous Therapy

While IV therapy is a powerful clinical tool, balancing its rapid therapeutic benefits against inherent patient risks is a daily nursing duty.

  • Rapid onset: Medications reach target organs almost instantly, crucial for life-saving interventions.
  • Precise dosing: Continuous infusions allow exact control over blood levels without erratic gastrointestinal absorption.
  • Fluid replacement: Restores blood volume and electrolytes quickly in dehydrated or hemorrhaging patients.
  • Irreversibility: Once a drug is injected into a vein, you cannot recall it if the patient has an unexpected adverse reaction.
  • Infection risk: Puncturing the skin creates a direct pathway for bacteria to enter the bloodstream.
  • Local complications: Infiltration and phlebitis can cause significant tissue damage and patient pain.

Anatomical Sites and Vein Selection for Venipuncture

Selecting the right vein prevents complications and ensures successful cannulation. Nurses typically begin with the most distal veins of the upper extremities, saving proximal sites for future use if complications arise.

Metacarpal veinsLocated on the back of the hand; highly visible, but movement can dislodge the cannula.

Equipment and Materials Needed for IV Cannulation

Gathering your tools before entering the patient's room ensures a smooth, aseptic procedure. A standard IV start kit includes a peripheral catheter (angiocatheter), a tourniquet, antiseptic wipes like chlorhexidine, sterile transparent dressings, tape, extension tubing with a needleless connector, a saline flush syringe, and clean gloves. Having extra sizes nearby is wise, as vein conditions often reveal themselves only after applying the tourniquet.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Initiating IV Access

  1. Prepare the patient: Explain the procedure, check for allergies, and position the arm in a dependent position.
  2. Apply the tourniquet: Place it 3 to 4 inches above the chosen site to engorge the veins without cutting off arterial pulse.
  3. Perform antisepsis: Clean the skin vigorously with antiseptic and allow it to air dry completely.
  4. Insert the needle: Hold the skin taut, insert the angiocatheter at a 15 to 30-degree angle with the bevel up until you see a flash of blood.
  5. Advance the catheter: Lower the angle, advance the catheter slightly further into the vein, then slide the plastic cannula off the needle while withdrawing the needle.
  6. Secure and flush: Release the tourniquet, connect the extension tubing, flush with normal saline to check patency, and apply the sterile dressing.

Methods of IV Administration: Bolus, Infusion, and Piggyback

Intravenous medications enter the body through different delivery schedules depending on the drug's properties and the patient's needs. An IV bolus or push delivers a concentrated dose directly into the vein over a short timeframe, requiring careful monitoring for adverse reactions. A continuous infusion administers large volumes of fluids or steady medication doses over many hours using an electronic pump or gravity tubing. A piggyback setup (IVPB) delivers secondary intermittent medications—like antibiotics—through an existing primary line, hanging higher than the main bag so the secondary medication infuses first.

Calculation of IV Flow Rates and Infusion Doses

Nurses must calculate infusion rates accurately to prevent fluid overload or under-medication. For gravity tubing, the drop factor (gtt/mL) found on the packaging is critical. The standard formula is:

$$\text{Flow Rate (gtt/min)} = \frac{\text{Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Time (minutes)}}$$

When using electronic infusion pumps, the calculation is simpler because pumps operate in milliliters per hour (mL/hr). Always double-check calculations involving high-alert medications with a colleague.

Monitoring, Maintenance, and Complications of IV Therapy

Routine assessment of the IV site protects the patient from local and systemic harm. Nurses must inspect the insertion site every shift for signs of complications.

  • Infiltration: Fluid leaks into surrounding tissue; the site appears cool, swollen, and pale.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall; the site shows redness, warmth, and a palpable cord along the vein.
  • Air embolism: Air enters the circulatory system; presents with sudden shortness of breath, drop in blood pressure, and chest pain.
  • Fluid overload: Excess fluid in the circulatory system; causes crackles in the lungs, edema, and elevated blood pressure.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Safety Guidelines

Safe intravenous therapy relies on strict adherence to aseptic technique, institutional policies, and patient rights. Nurses must verify the five rights of medication administration before every infusion, inspect IV bags for clarity and expiration dates, and change dressing seals according to hospital protocol—usually every 7 days or whenever integrity is compromised. Patient education is equally vital; teach patients to report burning, wet dressings, or discomfort immediately rather than waiting for scheduled nursing rounds.
