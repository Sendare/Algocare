Rectal Route

Introduction and Clinical Context

The rectal route involves placing medications into the rectum for local or systemic effects. Nurses use this route when oral administration is unsafe or impossible. Common scenarios include patients experiencing severe nausea and vomiting, swallowing difficulties, or altered levels of consciousness. Medications given rectally can bypass part of the digestive breakdown, making it a reliable alternative in emergency or palliative care settings.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Rectum

The rectum is the final segment of the large intestine, measuring about 12 to 15 centimeters long. Its mucous membrane is thin and richly supplied with blood vessels, which allows drugs to be absorbed directly into the systemic circulation.

  • Inferior rectal veins: Blood from these lower vessels drains directly into the inferior vena cava, bypassing the liver.
  • Superior rectal veins: Blood from these upper vessels drains into the portal system, passing through the liver first.

Because of this dual drainage, a variable portion of a rectally administered drug undergoes first-pass metabolism in the liver.

Dosage Forms and Preparation

Medications designed for the rectum come in several specialized forms tailored for local relief or systemic absorption.

  • Suppositories: Solid or semisolid objects formulated with a base like cocoa butter or glycerinated gelatin that melts or dissolves at body temperature.
  • Enemas: Liquid preparations introduced into the rectum to cleanse the bowel or deliver topical medications.
  • Rectal gels and ointments: Semi-solid preparations used mostly for local conditions like hemorrhoids or anal fissures.

Pharmacokinetics and Drug Absorption

Absorption through the rectal mucosa depends heavily on the presence of stool and the depth of insertion. If the rectum contains hard stool, the medication cannot touch the mucosal surface, leading to poor and unpredictable drug levels in the blood. When placed correctly against the mucous membrane, the drug dissolves in the residual fluid and diffuses across the membrane into local blood vessels. Absorption can be erratic compared to intravenous or oral routes, which nurses must consider when monitoring drug effects.

Indications and Clinical Advantages

The rectal route offers distinct clinical advantages when standard routes fail. It is invaluable for administering anti-nausea medications to patients who cannot keep oral drugs down, or giving antipyretics to pediatric patients during high fevers. Additionally, it provides an alternative pathway when gastrointestinal disorders, severe dysphagia, or bowel obstructions prevent oral intake. For unconscious patients in home care or emergency settings, caregivers can sometimes administer specific rectal emergency medications safely with proper instruction.

Disadvantages and Contraindications

Despite its usefulness, the rectal route has notable drawbacks. Absorption is often irregular and incomplete, meaning drug blood levels can be unpredictable. Local irritation from the medication can cause discomfort, and patients may feel embarrassed or experience psychological distress.

  • Recent rectal surgery: Inserting objects can disrupt surgical healing.
  • Active rectal bleeding: Can worsen trauma or obscure clinical findings.
  • Severe diarrhea: The body will quickly expel the medication before it absorbs.
  • Neutropenia or thrombocytopenia: High risk of tissue injury and infection.

Nursing Procedure and Safety

Administering a rectal suppository requires clinical skill and strict adherence to patient privacy and infection control.

  1. Verify the medical order and check for any contraindications like bleeding or recent surgery.
  2. Assist the patient into a Sims position, lying on the left side with the upper knee flexed.
  3. Put on clean gloves and apply a water-soluble lubricant to the rounded tip of the suppository.
  4. Gently insert the suppository past the internal anal sphincter, about 2.5 to 5 centimeters deep in adults.
  5. Instruct the patient to remain lying down for 15 to 20 minutes to prevent expelling the medication.

Patient Education and Comfort

Because the rectal route can feel invasive and uncomfortable, patient communication is paramount. Nurses must explain each step of the procedure clearly before starting to reduce anxiety and gain cooperation. Instruct patients to try to hold the medication inside for the recommended time, typically 15 to 30 minutes, especially for retention enemas or laxative suppositories. Finally, ensure the patient's privacy is maintained throughout the process and provide tissue or clean-up assistance afterward.
