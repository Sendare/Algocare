Intraperitoneal Route

Introduction to the Intraperitoneal Route

The intraperitoneal (IP) route involves delivering medications, fluids, or nutritional solutions directly into the peritoneal cavity, which is the space surrounding the abdominal organs. While less common than oral or intravenous methods in routine ward nursing, this route plays a vital specialized role in specific medical fields such as oncology, emergency care, and nephrology. Understanding how and why drugs are given this way helps nurses prepare for procedures they will encounter in surgical units, renal wards, and cancer centers.

Anatomical Considerations of the Peritoneal Cavity

The peritoneum is a large, continuous serous membrane that lines the abdominal cavity and covers the internal organs. It features a massive surface area rich in blood vessels and lymphatic channels, making it a very effective membrane for fluid and drug exchange. When a solution enters this space, it bathes the visceral organs, allowing localized treatment of the peritoneal lining and underlying structures before the drug eventually enters the systemic circulation.

Pharmacokinetics and Drug Absorption

Drug absorption through the peritoneum occurs primarily through the capillaries of the parietal and visceral peritoneum, as well as the lymphatic system. Solutions administered intraperitoneally often exhibit a concentration gradient, meaning drugs move slowly from the cavity into the systemic circulation. For chemotherapy, this creates a regional advantage, exposing abdominal tumors to high local drug concentrations while reducing systemic toxicity.

Primary Clinical Indications

  • Peritoneal Dialysis: Used for patients with kidney failure to remove waste products and excess fluid from the blood.
  • Intraperitoneal Chemotherapy: Administered to treat ovarian cancer or other malignancies that have spread to the peritoneal cavity.
  • Diagnostic Lavage: Infusing and retrieving sterile fluid to check for internal bleeding after abdominal trauma.

Administration Technique and Equipment

Administering fluids or medications intraperitoneally requires strict aseptic technique to prevent peritonitis. Nurses must ensure that dialysate or medication bags are warmed to body temperature before infusion to prevent cramping and hypothermia. Gravity or specialized pumps control the flow rate during instillation and drainage. The nurse must accurately record fluid balance, noting the exact volume infused versus the volume drained.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

  • Peritonitis: Severe infection of the peritoneal membrane marked by cloudy drainage, fever, and abdominal pain.
  • Catheter Occlusion: Blockage caused by kinking, fibrin clots, or constipation, preventing proper fluid drainage.
  • Fluid Overload or Dehydration: Imbalances occurring when fluid removal or retention is not closely monitored.
  • Bleeding or Organ Injury: Accidental trauma during insertion of needles or catheters into the abdominal wall.

Advantages and Disadvantages

The intraperitoneal route offers distinct benefits, including high local drug concentrations for regional tumors and an alternative access point when vascular access is poor. However, drawbacks include a high risk of infection, discomfort during fluid infusion, and complex procedural requirements. Nurses weigh these factors when educating patients and managing ongoing care plans.
