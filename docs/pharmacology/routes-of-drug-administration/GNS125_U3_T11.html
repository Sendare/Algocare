Intraperitoneal Route

Introduction and Anatomical Considerations

The **intraperitoneal route** involves the direct administration of medications, fluids, or diagnostic agents into the peritoneal cavity, which is the potential space bounded by the visceral and parietal peritoneum. This serous membrane lines the abdominal cavity and covers internal organs, providing a vast surface area rich in blood and lymphatic capillaries. When working in clinical settings across Nigeria, nursing personnel encounter this route primarily in specialized units such as renal dialysis centers and oncology wards. Because the peritoneal membrane acts as a semi-permeable barrier, it allows for the bidirectional transport of solutes and water between the capillary network and the instillation fluid. Understanding this anatomy clarifies why drugs administered here bypass first-pass hepatic metabolism initially, entering portal circulation gradually.

Pharmacokinetics and Absorption Mechanics

Drug absorption through the peritoneum occurs primarily via two pathways: direct capillary uptake into the blood supply and lymphatic absorption through stomata in the diaphragmatic peritoneum. Small molecules pass rapidly across the peritoneal membrane into the portal venous system, traveling directly to the liver. Larger molecules, particularly proteins and colloids, are absorbed more slowly through the lymphatic system, eventually reaching the systemic circulation via the thoracic duct. Local concentration gradients heavily influence diffusion rates. Instilling large volumes of solution establishes a high concentration gradient that drives medications into tissues. This unique kinetic profile permits high local drug concentrations directly inside the abdominal cavity while maintaining relatively lower systemic levels, an effect highly desired in regional treatments like intraperitoneal chemotherapy.

Indications and Clinical Applications

- **Peritoneal Dialysis**: The most common and routine application, utilized for patients with acute kidney injury or end-stage renal disease to remove toxins and excess fluid. - **Intraperitoneal Chemotherapy**: Administered to manage localized peritoneal malignancies, such as ovarian or appendiceal cancers, maximizing local cytotoxicity. - **Diagnostic Lavage**: Used in emergency trauma settings to detect hidden intra-abdominal hemorrhage following blunt abdominal trauma. - **Fluid and Electrolyte Resuscitation**: Historically or occasionally used in pediatric or emergency veterinary practice when vascular access is completely unobtainable.

Administration Equipment and Technique

Executing intraperitoneal procedures requires strict aseptic technique to prevent peritonitis, a catastrophic infection of the lining. Essential equipment includes sterile drapes, antiseptic skin solutions, local anesthetics, specialized catheters or automated cyclers, and prescribed dialysate or therapeutic solutions. The procedure generally follows these steps: 1. **Preparation**: Ensure the patient has emptied their bladder to reduce the risk of accidental puncture. 2. **Positioning**: Place the patient in a comfortable supine position. 3. **Asepsis**: Clean the insertion site on the lower abdomen thoroughly using chlorhexidine or iodine. 4. **Insertion**: Introduce the catheter carefully into the peritoneal cavity, utilizing local infiltration for comfort. 5. **Infusion**: Allow the fluid to flow by gravity or infusion pump, monitoring closely for patient distress or leakage.

Complications and Nursing Interventions

Intraperitoneal therapy carries distinct clinical risks that demand vigilant nursing surveillance. **Peritonitis** stands out as the most serious complication, characterized by cloudy effluent, severe abdominal pain, and fever. Nurses must inspect drained fluid color and report abnormalities instantly. Mechanical complications also frequently occur. Catheter obstruction or displacement can trap fluid inside or halt drainage, often managed by instructing the patient to change positions or gently flushing the line with heparinized saline per protocol. Other risks include dialysate leakage around the insertion site, electrolyte imbalances due to rapid solute shifts, and respiratory compromise caused by diaphragmatic elevation from large fluid volumes.
