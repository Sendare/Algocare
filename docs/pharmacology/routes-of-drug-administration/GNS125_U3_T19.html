Inhalation Route

Introduction and Principles of the Inhalation Route

The inhalation route delivers medication directly into the respiratory tract, primarily targeting the lungs for local or systemic effects. By bypassing the hepatic first-pass metabolism, this administration method allows drugs to enter the bloodstream rapidly through the vast alveolar surface area. Clinical practitioners utilize inhalation therapy extensively for managing chronic respiratory conditions like asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, as well as delivering emergency bronchodilators. In Nigerian clinical settings, where environmental dust and seasonal harmattan winds frequently trigger acute bronchospasm, proper mastery of inhalational drug delivery remains an essential nursing competency.

Respiratory Tract Anatomy and Drug Deposition

The respiratory tract acts as a sophisticated filter system that dictates where aerosolized particles land. The upper airway traps large, heavy particles in the nasal passages and pharynx, preventing them from reaching the lower lungs. As the respiratory tree branches into smaller bronchioles and alveoli, airflow velocity decreases, allowing smaller particles to settle.

  • Upper respiratory tract: Filters large particles (>10 micrometers) through impaction.
  • Conducting airways: Captures medium particles (3 to 5 micrometers) via sedimentation.
  • Alveolar region: Reaches particles between 1 and 5 micrometers, which are ideal for deep lung penetration and systemic absorption.

Types of Inhalation Devices and Delivery Systems

Various devices transform liquid or solid medications into aerosols suitable for inhalation. Selecting the correct device depends on patient age, cognitive ability, manual dexterity, and acute clinical status.

  • Pressurized Metered-Dose Inhalers (pMDIs): Deliver a specific chemical propellant-driven puff of medication requiring close hand-breath coordination.
  • Dry Powder Inhalers (DPIs): Breath-actuated devices where the patient's own inspiratory effort aerosolizes a micronized powder capsule or strip.
  • Nebulizers: Convert liquid drug solutions into a fine mist using compressed gas or ultrasonic waves, requiring passive breathing through a mouthpiece or mask.
  • Valved Holding Chambers (Spacers): Attach to pMDIs to trap medication droplets, reducing oral deposition and easing coordination demands.

Inhalation therapy treats both acute bronchoconstriction and chronic inflammatory airway diseases. Selecting the appropriate drug class determines whether the patient experiences immediate rescue or long-term preventive control.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before administering any inhaled medication, structured nursing assessment ensures safety and therapeutic efficacy. The nurse evaluates baseline respiratory rate, depth, rhythm, breath sounds, and oxygen saturation levels. Assessment must also include checking the patient's cognitive capability, hand-strength, and physical coordination to ensure they can operate their assigned device correctly. Identifying previous compliance barriers, such as improper technique or inability to afford spacers, prevents therapeutic failure. Preparing the patient involves verifying the correct drug, dosage, and scheduled timing while creating a calm environment to reduce anxiety-induced tachypnea.

Administration Techniques and Patient Education

Proper technique dictates whether medication reaches the target lung tissue or goes to waste in the throat. For pressurized metered-dose inhalers, the patient must exhale completely, seal their lips around the mouthpiece, actuate the canister during a slow, deep inspiration, and hold their breath for ten seconds before exhaling. If taking a second puff, waiting about one minute between doses ensures proper dosing. Patient education must emphasize that rinsing the mouth with water and spitting it out after using an inhaled corticosteroid prevents oral candidiasis and hoarseness.

Potential Complications, Side Effects, and Safety Precautions

Although inhalation limits systemic exposure compared to oral routes, adverse effects still occur locally and systemically. High doses of beta-2 agonists frequently induce systemic side effects such as tachycardia, skeletal muscle tremors, and nervousness. Inhaled corticosteroids can cause local irritation, dysphonia, and thrush if oral hygiene is neglected. Long-term high-dose steroid use can rarely contribute to systemic concerns like adrenal suppression or bone density loss. Nurses must teach patients to track their rescue inhaler usage frequency; requiring a rescue inhaler more than twice a week outside of exercise prophylaxis indicates poorly controlled underlying disease.
