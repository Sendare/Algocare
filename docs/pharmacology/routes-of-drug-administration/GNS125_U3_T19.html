Inhalation Route

Introduction and Physiological Pathways of the Inhalation Route

The inhalation route introduces medications directly into the respiratory tract, allowing drugs to act locally in the airways or pass quickly through the alveolar membrane into the systemic circulation. Because the lungs possess a massive surface area and a rich network of blood vessels, inhaled drugs often work much faster than oral pills or subcutaneous injections. This rapid onset makes inhalation the preferred method for treating acute respiratory distress, such as an unexpected asthma attack or a sudden bout of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) wheezing.

However, reaching the target site inside the lungs is not always simple. The human respiratory system is naturally designed to filter out foreign particles, meaning dust, pollen, and large droplets of medicine get trapped in the nose, throat, or upper airways before they ever reach the alveoli. Nurses must understand how particle size dictates where a drug lands. Particles larger than 5 microns usually settle in the upper airway, while particles between 1 and 5 microns successfully penetrate the smaller bronchioles and alveoli where therapeutic action is most needed.

Types of Inhalation Devices

Different delivery systems require entirely different patient techniques. Selecting the right device ensures the correct dose reaches the lungs rather than sticking to the back of the throat.

  • Metered-Dose Inhalers (MDIs): These pressurized canisters deliver a specific dose of medication suspended in a propellant. They require careful coordination between pressing the canister and taking a slow, deep breath.
  • Dry Powder Inhalers (DPIs): These devices deliver micronized powder without propellants. Instead of pressing a canister, the patient must inhale forcefully and quickly to activate the release and dispersion of the powder.
  • Nebulizers: These machines convert liquid medication into a fine aerosol mist using compressed air or oxygen. They are ideal for young children, elderly patients, or anyone struggling to coordinate their breathing with an MDI.
  • Spacers and Valved Holding Chambers: These plastic tubes attach to an MDI, holding the aerosol cloud momentarily so the patient can inhale it calmly without frantic hand-breath coordination.

Administration Techniques and Patient Education

Proper technique determines whether an inhaled drug works or simply goes to waste. Many patients make critical errors, such as exhaling into the device or breathing too fast.

  1. Preparation: Check the dose counter and shake the MDI canister well if required by the manufacturer instructions.
  2. Exhalation: Instruct the patient to breathe out completely away from the mouthpiece to empty the lungs as much as possible.
  3. Actuation and Inhalation: Place the mouthpiece between the teeth and seal the lips tightly around it. For an MDI, press down once while starting a slow, deep breath lasting about 3 to 5 seconds.
  4. Breath-Holding: Ask the patient to hold their breath for 10 seconds, or as long as comfortably possible, allowing the medication particles to settle in the small airways.
  5. Post-Administration Care: If the inhaler contains a corticosteroid, instruct the patient to rinse their mouth thoroughly with water and spit it out to prevent oral fungal infections like thrush.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Nurses play a vital role in evaluating both before and after a patient uses an inhaler. Assessment starts with checking baseline respiratory status, including breath sounds, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation levels. Nurses should also evaluate the patient's manual dexterity and cognitive ability to choose the correct delivery device.

After administration, the nurse must reassess the patient to determine if the medication worked. Listen for reduced wheezing, check for eased breathing effort, and monitor pulse rates, particularly when bronchodilators are given. A common mistake is assuming the patient knows how to use their inhaler simply because they have owned one for years. Asking the patient to demonstrate their technique reveals hidden errors that could block successful treatment.

Potential Side Effects and Safety Precautions

While inhalation targets the lungs directly, some medication inevitably enters the systemic circulation or causes local irritation. Short-acting beta-agonists, frequently used as rescue inhalers, often cause systemic side effects like tremors, nervousness, and tachycardia due to stimulation of beta receptors in the heart and muscles.

Inhaled corticosteroids tend to produce local side effects such as hoarseness, throat irritation, and fungal overgrowth, which can be minimized with proper rinsing techniques. Nurses must also teach patients to keep their rescue inhalers easily accessible at all times while tracking how often they use them. Needing a rescue inhaler more than twice a week usually indicates poor baseline disease control that requires a physician review.
