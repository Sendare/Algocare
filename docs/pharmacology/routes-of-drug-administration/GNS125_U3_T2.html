Nurse-Centered Drug Administration

Introduction and Professional Responsibilities

Drug administration is one of the most frequent yet legally binding duties a nurse performs on the ward. When a nurse hands a tablet to a patient or pushes medication through an intravenous line, they act as the ultimate safety net between the pharmaceutical product and the patient's bloodstream. In Nigerian clinical practice, this role is governed strictly by the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) scope of practice. You are never merely a passive messenger carrying out a doctor's order; you carry independent legal and ethical liability for every single dose you give. If a prescription is unclear, dangerous, or mathematically impossible, blindly following it makes you legally culpable alongside the prescriber. Understanding this immense responsibility shapes how every ward nurse approaches the treatment trolley each morning.

The Expanded Rights of Medication Administration

Every nursing student must memorize and religiously apply the expanded rights of medication administration. While older textbooks traditionally listed five rights, modern clinical safety demands checking at least eight distinct parameters before administering any substance.

  • Right Patient: Verify identity using two unique identifiers, such as checking the hospital identification wristband and asking the patient to state their full name.
  • Right Drug: Compare the medication label against the drug chart three separate times.
  • Right Dose: Crosscheck calculations and dosage ranges, especially with potent drugs like digoxin or insulin.
  • Right Route: Confirm the specific pathway ordered, such as oral, sublingual, intravenous, or intramuscular.
  • Right Time: Administer drugs within the safe clinical window, keeping to prescribed hospital scheduling.
  • Right Documentation: Record the medication immediately after administration, never before.
  • Right Reason: Ensure you understand why the patient is receiving this specific drug based on their diagnosis.
  • Right Response: Monitor the patient afterward to see if the drug worked or caused adverse reactions.

Pre-Administration Assessment and Patient Preparation

Before touching the drug trolley, you must assess the patient's current clinical baseline. A blood pressure medication is useless or dangerous if the patient's systolic pressure has already dropped below 90 mmHg. Check vital signs, review recent laboratory results like serum creatinine or potassium, and screen for known allergies by checking both the chart and asking the conscious patient directly. Never skip asking about herbal supplements or traditional concoctions, as many patients in Nigerian wards take local remedies alongside orthodox prescriptions. Preparing the patient also involves simple communication: explain what drug you are giving, what it does, and what mild side effects they might experience. This builds trust and ensures informed cooperation.

Calculation, Preparation, and Dispensing Techniques

Mathematical errors in drug dosage can turn a healing intervention into a fatal event. Ward nurses must master basic clinical math using the standard formula: (Desired Dose / Stock Dose) × Stock Volume. When drawing up medications from multi-dose vials or ampoules, inspect the clarity, color, and expiration date carefully. A common rookie mistake is rushing preparation in a noisy, crowded ward. Take your time, calculate away from distractions, and never administer a drug prepared by someone else. If you draw it up, you give it, and you own the safety of that action.

Aseptic Technique and Error Prevention

Infection control and medication safety go hand in hand on the clinical floor. When preparing injectables, maintaining strict asepsis prevents introducing pathogens directly into deep tissues or the bloodstream. Clean your hands, disinfect the neck of ampoules or rubber ports of vials with alcohol swabs, and never touch sterile needle hubs or syringe plungers. Preventing errors also relies on systemic habits: do not converse while dispensing medications, and use barcode scanning or dual-nurse verification when dealing with high-alert medications like heparin or concentrated electrolytes. If an error does occur, immediately stabilize the patient, report it honestly to the clinical team, and complete an incident report without fear of punitive blame.

Post-Administration Monitoring and Patient Education

Your job does not end when the drug is swallowed or injected. Post-administration monitoring involves returning to the bedside to evaluate therapeutic efficacy and watch for unexpected side effects, such as anaphylaxis, dizziness, or respiratory depression. Educate the patient before they leave the hospital or take unsupervised doses at home. Explain how to store medications away from damp hospital lockers or humid Nigerian household environments, what common side effects to expect, and warning signs that require urgent medical return. Clear instructions empower patients and improve compliance.
