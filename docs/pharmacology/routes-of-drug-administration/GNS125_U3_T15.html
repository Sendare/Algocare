Mucous Membrane Route

Introduction to Mucous Membrane Drug Routes

The mucous membrane route involves administering medication through the moist linings of the body, such as the mouth, nose, eyes, ears, rectum, and vagina. These membranes are covered by a thin layer of epithelial tissue and contain a rich network of blood vessels. Because the tissue is delicate and well-supplied with blood, drugs can pass quickly into the bloodstream. This makes mucous membrane administration useful when a fast therapeutic effect is needed or when a patient cannot swallow oral medications safely.

Sublingual and Buccal Administration

  • Sublingual: Medication is placed under the tongue until it completely dissolves. The area under the tongue has thin mucosa and abundant blood vessels, leading to rapid systemic absorption.
  • Buccal: Medication is placed between the cheek and the gum. Absorption is usually slower compared to the sublingual route, making it useful for sustained local or systemic effects.

A major benefit of both sublingual and buccal routes is that they bypass the first-pass metabolism in the liver. Because blood drains directly from the mouth into the superior vena cava, the drug enters general circulation unchanged and potent.

Ophthalmic, Otic, and Nasal Administration

Instilling medications into the eyes, ears, and nose requires careful technique to prevent injury and ensure proper absorption.

  • Ophthalmic: Eye drops or ointments are placed into the lower conjunctival sac. The nurse must avoid touching the tip of the dropper to any part of the eye to prevent contamination.
  • Otic: Ear drops are administered into the ear canal. For adults, the pinna is pulled upward and back; for young children, it is pulled downward and straight back to straighten the canal.
  • Nasal: Nasal sprays or drops deliver localized relief for congestion or systemic absorption for specific hormones.

Rectal and Vaginal Administration

Rectal and vaginal routes are utilized when oral medications cannot be given due to nausea, vomiting, or decreased level of consciousness.

  • Rectal: Suppositories or enemas are inserted past the internal anal sphincter and placed against the rectal mucosa. This route can achieve both local effects (such as relieving constipation) and systemic effects (such as controlling fever or nausea).
  • Vaginal: Creams, tablets, or pessaries are inserted into the vaginal canal to treat local infections or deliver localized hormones.

Pharmacokinetics and Absorption Mechanics

Absorption across mucous membranes depends heavily on blood supply, surface area, and the lipid solubility of the drug. Because mucous membranes are thin and well-vascularized, lipid-soluble drugs pass rapidly through cell membranes into the capillary bed. A major pharmacokinetic advantage of routes like sublingual, buccal, and rectal (lower portion) is the partial evasion of the hepatic first-pass effect. Drugs absorbed in these areas enter the systemic venous system directly, increasing their bioavailability compared to oral drugs that pass entirely through the liver first.

Advantages and Limitations of Mucous Membrane Routes

While mucous membrane administration offers clear clinical benefits, it also presents distinct challenges that nurses must manage.

  • Advantages: Rapid onset of action, bypass of harsh gastrointestinal fluids, and suitability for unconscious or nauseated patients.
  • Limitations: Local irritation at the site of application, erratic absorption if the membrane is inflamed or covered in mucus, patient discomfort or embarrassment with rectal and vaginal routes, and unpleasant taste for many oral mucosal drugs.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Nurses play a critical role in safe medication administration across all mucous membrane sites. Before administering, the nurse must verify the right patient, drug, dose, route, and time. Educating the patient is equally vital; for instance, the nurse must instruct patients not to eat, drink, or smoke while a sublingual tablet is dissolving. Nurses should also inspect the application site for signs of irritation, breakdown, or infection, and document the procedure and patient response clearly in the medical record.
