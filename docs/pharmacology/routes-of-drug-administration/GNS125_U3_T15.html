Mucous Membrane Route

Introduction and Physiological Basis of Mucous Membrane Drug Delivery

The mucous membrane route involves administering medications onto moist epithelial surfaces throughout the body for either local or systemic therapeutic effects. Unlike the skin, mucous membranes lack a heavily keratinized stratum corneum layer, presenting a significantly thinner and more permeable barrier to pharmacological agents. Epithelial tissues lining the oral cavity, nasal passages, eyes, ears, rectum, and vagina are well-vascularized, which facilitates rapid diffusion of drug molecules directly into the bloodstream. This route bypasses the hostile acidic environment of the stomach and avoids immediate first-pass hepatic metabolism for many sites, making it highly valuable when rapid onset is clinically indicated or when oral ingestion is contraindicated due to nausea, unconsciousness, or bowel obstruction.

Sublingual and Buccal Drug Administration

Sublingual and buccal routes leverage the rich vascular network of the oral cavity.

  • Sublingual administration: Placing a drug underneath the tongue. The tissue is exceptionally thin, and the rich blood supply via the sublingual veins leads directly to the superior vena cava, entirely avoiding first-pass hepatic metabolism and yielding an extremely rapid onset of action.
  • Buccal administration: Placing the medication between the cheek and the gum. Absorption here is generally slower and more sustained compared to the sublingual route, as the buccal mucosa is somewhat thicker and less vascular.

Clinically, patients must be instructed not to chew, swallow, or drink anything until the tablet is completely dissolved, as swallowing alters the intended pharmacokinetic profile.

Topical Mucosal Routes: Ophthalmic, Otic, and Nasal

Delivering medications to the eyes, ears, and nose requires specific anatomical considerations to ensure local therapeutic success without causing systemic toxicity.

For ophthalmic drops, applying gentle pressure to the inner canthus for one to two minutes minimizes systemic absorption through the nasolacrimal duct. When administering nasal sprays, alternate nostrils during chronic therapy to prevent mucosal irritation and epistaxis.

Enteric and Pelvic Mucosal Routes: Rectal and Vaginal

Rectal and vaginal routes provide excellent alternatives when oral administration is impaired.

Rectal suppositories and enemas rely on the rectal mucosa's rich vascular supply. The inferior and middle rectal veins route absorbed drug molecules directly into the systemic circulation, partially evading first-pass liver metabolism, while the superior rectal vein drains into the portal system. The nurse must insert the suppository beyond the internal sphincter to prevent it from expelling.

Vaginal medications—available as creams, tablets, or pessaries—are primarily used for localized infections, hormonal therapy, or contraception. The vaginal epithelium responds to cyclical hormonal changes, which can slightly alter absorption rates across different life stages.

Pharmacokinetics, Advantages, and Clinical Limitations

Absorptive efficiency across mucous membranes depends heavily on surface area, vascularity, lipid solubility, and the molecular weight of the drug. Highly lipophilic compounds cross epithelial barriers rapidly via passive diffusion.

The primary advantages include rapid onset of action, avoidance of harsh gastrointestinal degradation, and suitability for patients with swallowing difficulties or severe emesis.

However, limitations are notable: mucosal surfaces have limited surface areas compared to the small intestine, erratic absorption can occur if excessive mucus or local pathology is present, and some drugs cause localized irritation, stinging, or inflammation at the application site. Compliance can also be challenging due to the perceived inconvenience or discomfort of routes like rectal or nasal administration.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Safe administration via mucous membranes requires rigorous nursing assessment, correct technique, and clear patient instruction. The nurse must inspect the target mucosal tissue integrity before every dose, checking for signs of ulceration, bleeding, excessive exudate, or severe inflammation that could alter drug absorption or cause injury. Universal precautions and clean gloves are essential during application.

Patient education empowers individuals to manage self-administration safely at home. Nurses should explain the correct positioning, emphasize the necessity of completing the full course of topical therapies like vaginal anti-fungals, and warn against altering dosage forms, such as crushing long-acting buccal tablets or swallowing sublingual medications prematurely.
