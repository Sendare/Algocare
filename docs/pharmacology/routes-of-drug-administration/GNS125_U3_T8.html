Intradermal Route

Introduction and Purpose of the Intradermal Route

The intradermal route involves injecting medication or diagnostic substances directly into the dermis, the layer of skin located just beneath the epidermis. Because blood supply in the dermal layer is extremely limited, absorption happens very slowly. This makes the route ideal for diagnostic testing where a systemic reaction is either unnecessary or dangerous, and where a localized tissue response needs to be observed over time.

Nurses use this specific route primarily for three clinical purposes:

  • Diagnostic testing: Screening for exposure to tuberculosis using the Mantoux tuberculin skin test, or checking for allergies to environmental or food substances.
  • Immunization: Administering specialized vaccines, such as the BCG vaccine for tuberculosis protection.
  • Local anesthesia: Desensitizing a small patch of skin before inserting a deeper line or performing a minor procedure.

A common beginner misconception is that a shallow injection automatically means intradermal. In reality, if the needle enters the subcutaneous fat just a millimeter deeper, the medication will absorb too quickly, invalidating a allergy test or causing a systemic reaction.

Anatomical Site Selection and Landmarking

Choosing the correct anatomical site is critical for accurate reading and patient safety. The ideal site must have little hair, minimal pigmentation, and sparse subcutaneous tissue so the skin response is easy to see and measure.

  1. Volar surface of the forearm: This is the most common and preferred site. It is located on the inner, hairless side of the forearm, roughly a hand's span above the wrist and a hand's span below the elbow.
  2. Upper back: Often used for large panels of allergy skin prick or intradermal testing when multiple sites are needed.
  3. Upper chest: An alternative site occasionally used for specific allergy panels.

When preparing the site, clean the skin with an antiseptic swab starting from the center and moving outward in a circular motion. Allow the skin to dry completely before insertion. Applying a wet alcohol swab right as you puncture can push stinging alcohol into the needle track and cause unnecessary pain.

Equipment Selection: Syringes, Needles, and Dosages

Because intradermal injections deliver very small amounts of fluid into a tight dermal space, specialized equipment is required.

  • Syringe: A tuberculin syringe (usually 1 mL capacity) calibrated in hundredths of a milliliter is standard. This precision is necessary because doses rarely exceed 0.1 to 0.5 mL.
  • Needle Gauge: Fine needles ranging from 25 to 27 gauge (or sometimes 30 gauge) are used to minimize tissue trauma and prevent fluid leakage.
  • Needle Length: Short needles between 1/4 to 1/2 inch long are selected to prevent accidental deep penetration into the subcutaneous tissue.

Exceeding a volume of 0.5 mL in a single intradermal site will cause the tight dermal layers to tear or the fluid to leak out around the puncture site, ruining the diagnostic test.

Step-by-Step Administration Technique

Mastering the physical technique ensures the medication forms the correct depot in the skin.

  1. Position the patient's arm comfortably on a firm, flat surface.
  2. Put on clean gloves. Hold the syringe in your dominant hand like a pencil.
  3. With your non-dominant hand, pull the skin taut across the site. Do not bunch or pinch the skin up, as pinching lifts the tissue and encourages a subcutaneous entry.
  4. Position the needle almost parallel to the skin surface, pointing the bevel upward at a 10 to 15-degree angle.
  5. Advance the needle tip about 3 millimeters into the skin until the entire bevel is hidden beneath the epidermal surface.
  6. Inject the medication slowly without aspirating. As the fluid enters, a pale, raised blister or bleb (wheal) should immediately form under the skin.
  7. Withdraw the needle smoothly at the exact same angle it entered, and do not massage the site afterwards.

Interpreting Results and Post-Procedure Care

Reading an intradermal test requires careful timing and tactile assessment. For a Mantoux tuberculin skin test, the patient must return to the clinic within 48 to 72 hours for evaluation.

When evaluating a reaction, the nurse must assess for induration—a hard, dense, raised swelling—rather than simple surface redness (erythema). Using a flexible millimeter ruler, measure the diameter of the hard raised area across the forearm, ignoring any flat pink redness around it. Document the exact millimetres of induration. If no induration is present, document it as zero millimeters.

Instruct the patient to keep the site uncovered, clean, and dry. Crucially, warn the patient never to scratch, rub, or apply bandages over the bleb, as irritation can alter the local immune response and produce a false reading.
