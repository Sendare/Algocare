Intradermal Route

Introduction and Purpose of the Intradermal Route

The intradermal route involves injecting medication or diagnostic agents into the dermis layer of the skin, located just beneath the epidermis. Because blood supply in the dermal layer is extremely sparse and drug absorption occurs very slowly, this route is rarely used for systemic therapeutic effects. Instead, healthcare providers rely on it primarily for diagnostic testing, such as tuberculin skin testing (Mantoux test) and allergy hypersensitivity screenings, or for local anesthesia and specific immunizations like the intradermal rabies vaccine.

When a substance is deposited correctly into the dermis, it creates a small, pale blister or wheal on the skin surface, often referred to as a bleb. The presence of this bleb confirms that the needle tip reached the correct tissue depth rather than slipping into the subcutaneous fat below. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, mastering this technique is crucial for public health outreach programs involving mass tuberculosis screening or BCG administration in neonates.

Anatomical Considerations and Depth of Injection

Human skin consists of three primary layers: the epidermis, the dermis, and the subcutaneous tissue. For an intradermal injection to succeed, the needle must enter exclusively into the dermis, situated beneath the outer epidermal skin cells and above the subcutaneous adipose layer. The dermis is remarkably thin, measuring approximately 0.1 to 0.5 millimeters in thickness depending on the anatomical site and the age of the patient.

Targeting a layer this thin demands exceptional precision. If the needle enters at too steep of an angle, the medication penetrates past the dermis into the subcutaneous tissue, rendering diagnostic tests invalid. Conversely, if the angle is too shallow, the medication leaks out onto the skin surface rather than forming the required intradermal pocket.

Equipment Selection: Syringes and Needles

  • Syringe size: A small-capacity syringe, typically 1 mL (tuberculin syringe), is required because intradermal doses are extremely small, usually ranging from 0.01 mL to 0.1 mL.
  • Needle gauge: Fine-bore needles ranging from 25 to 27 gauge (or even 30 gauge) minimize tissue trauma and discomfort.
  • Needle length: Short needles measuring between 1/4 inch and 1/2 inch prevent accidental deep penetration into subcutaneous layers.

Site Selection and Skin Preparation

Selecting the proper anatomical site ensures accurate reading of diagnostic reactions and minimizes patient discomfort. Common sites include:

  • Volar forearm: The traditional and preferred site due to its sparse hair, lack of heavy pigmentation, and thin skin, making visual inspection of reactions straightforward.
  • Upper back: Often utilized when forearm skin is compromised by eczema, scars, or widespread rashes, or when conducting panels of multiple allergen tests.
  • Upper chest: An alternative diagnostic site, though less commonly used in routine outpatient screening.

Before injection, clean the chosen site using an alcohol swab in a circular motion starting from the center outward, allowing the skin to dry completely before needle insertion to prevent stinging and inactivation of live biological agents.

Step-by-Step Administration Technique

Proper execution of an intradermal injection requires meticulous hand positioning and precise control of the needle angle. First, position the patient's arm comfortably on a firm surface. Using your non-dominant hand, gently pull the skin taut over the site to create a firm surface for needle entry. Hold the syringe in your dominant hand with the needle bevel facing upward.

Insert the needle at a very shallow angle, typically 5 to 15 degrees relative to the skin surface. Advance the needle only about 1/8 of an inch so that the bevel is completely visible beneath the epidermis. Slowly inject the medication without aspirating; you should feel resistance, and a distinct raised bleb resembling a mosquito bite or orange peel should immediately form. Withdraw the needle at the exact angle of insertion and gently dab the site with dry cotton without massaging, as massage disperses the medication and distorts diagnostic results.

Reading and Interpreting Diagnostic Results

Reading intradermal diagnostic tests requires precise timing and clinical objectivity. For instance, a Mantoux tuberculin skin test must be evaluated between 48 and 72 hours post-injection. Clinicians do not measure the surrounding skin redness or erythema, because redness indicates a minor inflammatory response rather than true cellular immunity. Instead, palpate the skin gently to find the margins of the induration, which is the hard, dense, raised area caused by cell-mediated immune infiltration.

Measure the diameter of the induration transverse to the long axis of the forearm using a flexible ruler in millimeters. Document the exact millimeter measurement along with the date, time, and injection site. In clinical practice, misinterpreting redness as a positive induration leads to false-positive diagnoses and unnecessary prophylactic drug therapy.

Potential Complications and Nursing Management

Although generally safe, intradermal injections carry a risk of localized adverse events if technique is flawed. Common complications include severe localized pain, accidental subcutaneous deposition, bleeding, and localized tissue necrosis or ulceration in hypersensitive individuals. If a patient displays signs of a severe systemic allergic reaction, such as generalized urticaria, wheezing, or respiratory distress following allergen testing, emergency interventions including epinephrine administration must be initiated immediately.

Document all injection details accurately, noting the exact drug, dose, lot number, expiration date, and anatomical location. Patient education is vital: instruct the individual to keep the site clean and dry, avoid scratching or applying tight clothing over the bleb, and return at the designated hour for result reading.
