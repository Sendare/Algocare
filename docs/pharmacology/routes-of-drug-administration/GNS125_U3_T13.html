Intraarterial Route

Introduction and Purpose of the Intraarterial Route

The intraarterial route involves delivering medications, fluids, or diagnostic agents directly into an artery rather than a vein. Because arteries carry oxygenated blood away from the heart straight to targeted organs or limbs, this method achieves a very high local concentration of a drug before it spreads through the rest of the body. You will most commonly see this route used in specialized settings like the intensive care unit or interventional radiology suite. It is rarely used for routine daily medications because the pressure inside arteries is much higher than in veins, making access and maintenance far more technically demanding and risky.

Clinical Indications and Common Medications

Intraarterial administration is reserved for specific, high-stakes medical interventions where systemic delivery falls short.

  • Diagnostic angiography: Injecting radiopaque contrast media to visualize blood vessels in the brain, heart, or kidneys.
  • Chemotherapy infusion: Delivering anti-cancer drugs directly to a localized tumor, such as in the liver, to maximize cell destruction while limiting full-body toxicity.
  • Vasodilator therapy: Infusing medications directly into the circulation of a compromised limb to treat severe vasospasm or ischemia.
  • Hemodynamic monitoring: Continuous, real-time tracking of blood pressure in critically ill patients.

Equipment and Procedural Setup

Setting up for intraarterial access requires specialized sterile equipment designed to withstand arterial pressures. Clinicians typically use the radial, femoral, or brachial arteries. The setup includes a short arterial catheter, a pressurized flush system to keep the line patent against high arterial pressure, and a transducer setup if continuous blood pressure monitoring is attached. Maintaining strict aseptic technique during insertion is non-negotiable, as bacteria introduced directly into an arterial line can rapidly cause severe systemic infection or local vascular damage.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring Responsibilities

When a patient has an intraarterial line, your ongoing nursing surveillance focuses heavily on tissue perfusion and line integrity. You must check the extremity distal to the insertion site regularly for color, temperature, capillary refill, and pulse strength. If you notice the hand or foot becoming pale, cold, or pulseless, report it immediately as it signals compromised blood flow. Additionally, always keep the insertion site visible and secure all connections to prevent catastrophic hemorrhage if a line disconnects.

Potential Complications and Safety Measures

Intraarterial therapy carries significant risks due to direct vessel trauma and high blood pressure. Recognizing these hazards early allows for prompt rescue actions.
