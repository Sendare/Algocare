Psychoactive Substances

Introduction to Psychoactive Substances and Terminology

Psychoactive substances are chemical compounds that cross the blood-brain barrier to alter conscious experience, mood, perception, cognition, and behavior. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding fundamental terminology is essential for effective psychiatric-mental health nursing practice. Psychoactive substances can originate naturally, be semi-synthetic, or be entirely synthetic. Key terms include psychoactive substance use, which refers to the consumption of these agents; misuse, which implies use outside of medical or social norms; and substance use disorder (SUD), a complex brain condition characterized by compulsive seeking and use despite harmful consequences. Stigma remains a major barrier to care in Nigeria; therefore, utilizing recovery-oriented, person-first language—such as referring to a 'person with a substance use disorder' rather than 'an addict'—is vital for therapeutic engagement and reducing moral judgment. Nurses must recognize that psychoactive substances encompass both legal substances (like alcohol and nicotine) and illicit substances (like cannabis, cocaine, and opioids), each posing distinct public health challenges within the Nigerian context. Comprehensive knowledge of these definitions aids clinical communication, interprofessional collaboration, and the delivery of compassionate, evidence-based nursing interventions across community and hospital settings.

Neurobiological Mechanisms of Action in the Central Nervous System

Psychoactive substances exert their primary psychological and physiological effects by altering normal neurobiological mechanisms within the central nervous system (CNS). Specifically, these chemicals interact with neurotransmitter systems—such as dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and glutamate—either by mimicking endogenous neurotransmitters, blocking their reuptake, or enhancing their release. The mesolimbic dopamine pathway, often referred to as the brain's reward system, plays a central role in the reinforcement of substance use. When a psychoactive substance is consumed, it typically triggers a surge of dopamine in the nucleus accumbens, creating a powerful sense of pleasure and teaching the brain to repeat the behavior. Over time, repeated exposure alters receptor sensitivity, leading to neuroadaptations where the brain struggles to experience pleasure from natural rewards. Understanding these neurobiological pathways helps nursing students demystify substance use disorders, framing them as chronic, relapsing brain diseases rather than mere moral failings or lack of willpower. This scientific perspective reinforces the rationale for medical interventions, counseling, and long-term supportive management in psychiatric nursing.

Classification of Psychoactive Substances

Psychoactive substances are broadly categorized based on their primary pharmacological effects on the central nervous system into depressants, stimulants, hallucinogens, and cannabis (which possesses unique mixed properties). Depressants, such as alcohol, benzodiazepines, and opioids, slow down brain activity, lower blood pressure, and depress respiration, presenting significant risks of fatal overdose. Stimulants, including amphetamines, cocaine, and caffeine, accelerate CNS activity, heightening alertness, energy, and heart rate, but can induce severe anxiety, paranoia, and cardiovascular collapse. Hallucinogens, such as LSD, psilocybin, and ketamine, distort sensory perception, trigger synesthesia, and provoke profound alterations in thought and mood without necessarily causing prominent physical dependence. Cannabis can act as a mild CNS depressant, stimulant, and hallucinogen, containing psychoactive cannabinoids like THC that bind to specific receptors in the brain. Recognizing these classifications allows nurses to anticipate specific clinical presentations, design tailored assessment protocols, and manage acute intoxication effectively based on the pharmacological profile of the specific substance involved.

Patterns of Use, Tolerance, Dependence, and Withdrawal

Patterns of psychoactive substance use span a continuum from experimental use to severe dependence. Tolerance develops when the body adapts to repeated substance exposure, requiring increasingly larger doses to achieve the initial pharmacological effect. Dependence can be physiological—where the body physically relies on the substance and undergoes a predictable, often uncomfortable or dangerous syndrome upon cessation—or psychological, characterized by intense emotional cravings and a drive to use despite negative outcomes. Withdrawal syndromes occur when chronic use is abruptly reduced or stopped, manifesting with specific signs and symptoms unique to each substance class (e.g., tremors, autonomic instability, and seizures in alcohol withdrawal, or severe myalgia, yawning, and diarrhea in opioid withdrawal). Nurses must carefully monitor clients for withdrawal phenomena, as some withdrawal states, particularly from alcohol or sedative-hypnotics, are medical emergencies requiring close supervision, vital sign monitoring, and pharmacological stabilization to prevent morbidity and mortality.

Clinical Assessment and Screening for Substance Use

Clinical assessment and screening for substance use are critical nursing responsibilities that facilitate early identification, diagnosis, and intervention. Nurses should utilize validated screening tools, such as the CAGE questionnaire, AUDIT (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test), or DAST (Drug Abuse Screening Test), to evaluate patterns of substance use objectively and non-judgmentally. A comprehensive psychiatric and physical nursing assessment should gather details regarding the frequency, route, quantity, and last time of substance use, alongside a thorough history of past attempts at reduction or cessation. It is also vital to screen for concurrent mental health disorders, as dual diagnosis (co-occurring substance use and psychiatric disorders) is highly prevalent and requires integrated treatment approaches. Nurses must maintain a compassionate, empathetic demeanor, reassuring patients of medical confidentiality to encourage honest disclosure. Early identification through systematic screening enables the multidisciplinary healthcare team to formulate targeted care plans, mitigate complications, and support the patient's journey toward health and recovery.

Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Management of Intoxication and Withdrawal

The management of substance intoxication and withdrawal requires a combination of pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions aimed at stabilizing the patient's physiological status and ensuring safety. During acute intoxication, priorities include maintaining a patent airway, monitoring vital signs, managing agitation, and preventing injury or aspiration. For withdrawal management (detoxification), protocols vary based on the substance: long-acting benzodiazepines are typically tapered for alcohol and sedative withdrawal, while medications such as methadone or buprenorphine may be used for opioid withdrawal under medical supervision. Non-pharmacological interventions are equally critical and include providing a low-stimulus, quiet, and safe environment, frequent reorientation, supportive verbal communication, hydration, and nutritional support. Nurses play a frontline role in monitoring withdrawal rating scales (such as CIWA-Ar for alcohol or COWS for opioids), administering prescribed medications safely, observing for adverse drug reactions, and offering emotional reassurance to alleviate fear and agitation during vulnerable moments of recovery.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Advocacy in Mental Health

Developing an individualized nursing care plan for a patient with a substance use disorder requires a holistic, patient-centered approach that prioritizes safety, therapeutic communication, and behavioral change. Nursing diagnoses frequently include Risk for Injury, Ineffective Coping, Disturbed Sensory Perception, and Powerlessness. Interventions should focus on establishing a trusting nurse-patient relationship, reinforcing positive coping mechanisms, educating the patient and their family about addiction as a chronic health condition, and encouraging participation in support groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) or Narcotics Anonymous (NA). Patient advocacy is a cornerstone of this role; nurses must actively advocate for non-stigmatizing, equitable healthcare access for individuals struggling with substance use within the Nigerian health system. Additionally, nurses collaborate with social workers, clinical psychologists, and community health officers to ensure continuity of care upon discharge, promoting relapse prevention strategies and long-term community reintegration.
