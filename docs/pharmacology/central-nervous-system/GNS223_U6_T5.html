Psychoactive Substances

Introduction and Core Terminology

Psychoactive substances are chemical agents that alter brain function, resulting in temporary changes in perception, mood, consciousness, and behavior. Nurses encounter these substances across all healthcare settings, from emergency departments to community mental health clinics. Understanding basic terms prevents stigma and guides safe patient care.

  • Psychoactive substance: Any chemical compound that crosses the blood-brain barrier and affects the central nervous system.
  • Intoxication: A reversible syndrome caused by a specific substance that impairs mental and physical functioning.
  • Substance use: Any consumption of a psychoactive substance, whether recreational, medicinal, or experimental.
  • Harmful use: A pattern of substance use that causes damage to physical or mental health.

Neurobiological Mechanisms in the Central Nervous System

At the cellular level, psychoactive substances exert their effects by interacting with neurotransmitters and neural pathways in the brain. Most drugs of abuse hijack the brain's natural reward system, flooding it with chemical messengers.

Key neurotransmitters involved include:

  • Dopamine: Drives pleasure, reinforcement, and motivation.
  • Serotonin: Regulates mood, sleep, and perception.
  • GABA: Acts as the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter to slow down brain activity.
  • Glutamate: Serves as the main excitatory neurotransmitter.

When a substance enters the brain, it may mimic natural neurotransmitters, block their reuptake, or force excessive release. Over time, the brain tries to protect itself from this chemical overload by reducing its own natural neurotransmitter production or downregulating receptor sites, which lays the biological foundation for tolerance and dependence.

Tolerance, Dependence, and Withdrawal

Repeated use of psychoactive substances triggers profound physiological adaptations.

  1. Tolerance: The body adapts to the presence of the drug, requiring larger amounts to achieve the same initial effect.
  2. Physical dependence: The biological system adjusts to functioning with the drug present; removing it disrupts normal equilibrium.
  3. Psychological dependence: An emotional or mental drive to continue using the substance to cope with daily life or avoid emotional distress.
  4. Withdrawal: A predictable cluster of physical and psychological symptoms that occurs when a dependent person abruptly stops or reduces substance intake.

Withdrawal symptoms are generally the opposite of the drug's acute effects. For example, withdrawing from a CNS depressant like alcohol causes central nervous system hyperexcitability, which can lead to tremors, seizures, and delirium tremens.

Clinical Assessment and Screening

Early identification of substance use disorders improves patient outcomes and prevents medical complications during hospitalization. Nurses should conduct routine, non-judgmental screenings during admission assessments.

Standard screening tools include:

  • AUDIT: Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test, used to detect hazardous drinking.
  • CAGE: A brief four-question tool focusing on Cut down, Annoyed, Guilty, and Eye-opener.
  • SBIRT: Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment framework used in primary care.

A thorough assessment must evaluate the specific substance used, route of administration, frequency, last dose taken, and any history of severe withdrawal symptoms like seizures.

Management of Intoxication and Withdrawal

Managing acute intoxication and withdrawal requires balancing immediate physical safety with supportive clinical care. The priority during intoxication is maintaining airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs), along with preventing injury.

For withdrawal management, standardized rating scales like the CIWA-Ar for alcohol or COWS for opioids guide medication dosing. Medical management often involves cross-tapering with safer replacement medications—such as long-acting benzodiazepines for alcohol withdrawal or methadone/buprenorphine for opioid withdrawal. Non-pharmacological care includes placing the patient in a quiet, low-stimulus room, frequent vital sign checks, reorientation, and ensuring adequate hydration and nutrition.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Advocacy

Compassionate, evidence-based nursing care helps break the cycle of stigma that often prevents individuals from seeking help for substance use disorders. The nurse acts as both a direct caregiver and an advocate.

Key nursing interventions include:

  • Using non-stigmatizing, person-first language (e.g., saying 'person with a substance use disorder' rather than 'addict').
  • Establishing a therapeutic, trusting nurse-patient relationship without moral judgment.
  • Collaborating with social workers, counselors, and peer support specialists.
  • Providing harm reduction education, such as clean needle access and naloxone distribution for opioid overdose prevention.
