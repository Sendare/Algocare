Psychoactive Substances

Introduction to Psychoactive Substances and Terminology

Psychoactive substances are chemical agents that alter brain function, resulting in temporary changes in perception, mood, consciousness, cognition, or behavior. These agents interact with the central nervous system to produce subjective and observable effects. Understanding core terminology is essential for effective psychiatric nursing practice and accurate documentation. - **Substance Use**: The ingestion of psychoactive substances without necessarily experiencing negative social or medical consequences. - **Intoxication**: A reversible syndrome caused by a specific substance that affects mental, emotional, and physical functioning. - **Tolerance**: A physiological state where increasing doses of a substance are required to achieve the same initial effect. - **Dependence**: An adaptive state characterized by physical or psychological withdrawal symptoms when substance use abruptly ceases. - **Addiction**: A chronic, relapsing brain disease characterized by compulsive substance seeking and use despite harmful consequences.

Neurobiological Mechanisms of Action

At the cellular level, psychoactive substances exert their effects by interacting with neurotransmitter systems, receptors, and transporters within the central nervous system. Most drugs of abuse hijack the brain's natural reward circuitry, primarily the **mesolimbic dopamine pathway**, which originates in the ventral tegmental area and projects to the nucleus accumbens. Different classes of substances utilize distinct mechanisms. Stimulants such as amphetamines block dopamine reuptake and increase presynaptic release, flooding the synaptic cleft. Depressants like alcohol and benzodiazepines enhance the inhibitory effects of gamma-aminobutyric acid at GABA-A receptors. Opioids bind to mu-opioid receptors, inhibiting pain transmission and elevating dopamine levels indirectly. Understanding these pathways helps nurses anticipate withdrawal syndromes, which typically present with symptoms that are the physiological opposite of acute drug action.

Classification of Psychoactive Substances

Psychoactive substances are broadly categorized based on their predominant pharmacological effects on the central nervous system. Recognizing these categories guides both acute assessment and long-term care strategies in Nigerian psychiatric and general clinical settings. - **Depressants**: Slow down brain activity, reduce anxiety, and lower physiological arousal. Examples include alcohol, barbiturates, and benzodiazepines. - **Stimulants**: Accelerate central nervous system activity, increase alertness, and elevate heart rate. Examples include cocaine, methamphetamine, and prescription amphetamines, alongside widely used substances like caffeine and nicotine. - **Opioids**: Powerful analgesic agents that induce euphoria and sedation. Examples include heroin, morphine, codeine, and tramadol. - **Hallucinogens**: Distort sensory perception, alter thought patterns, and cause visual or auditory illusions. Examples include lysergic acid diethylamide, psilocybin, and phencyclidine. - **Cannabinoids**: Produce mixed depressant, mild stimulant, and hallucinogenic properties. Derived from the Cannabis sativa plant, cannabis remains one of the most commonly encountered substances in local clinical practice.

Patterns of Use, Tolerance, Dependence, and Withdrawal

Substance use disorders develop along a continuum, moving from experimental use to problematic patterns, tolerance, physical and psychological dependence, and eventually severe withdrawal states. Physical dependence occurs when neuroadaptation alters cellular set-points, requiring the continuous presence of the drug to maintain normal homeostatic balance. When use stops abruptly, homeostatic rebound triggers an acute **withdrawal syndrome**. For alcohol and sedative-hypnotics, withdrawal can be medically catastrophic, progressing to autonomic instability, delirium tremens, and seizures. Conversely, opioid withdrawal, while intensely distressing with severe muscle cramps, lacrimation, diarrhea, and vomiting, is rarely life-threatening on its own. Psychological dependence involves intense emotional cravings driven by environmental cues, representing a major hurdle during long-term rehabilitation.

Clinical Assessment and Screening

Systematic screening is vital for early identification and intervention in substance-related disorders. Standardized assessment tools help quantify severity, evaluate risk, and guide clinical decision-making without relying solely on subjective impressions. Tools such as the **AUDIT** (Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test) screen for hazardous drinking, while the **CAGE** questionnaire offers a rapid four-question clinical check. Nursing assessments must evaluate the timeline of last use, quantity consumed, route of administration, and history of past withdrawal episodes. Assessing concurrent mental health conditions is equally crucial, as substance use disorders frequently co-occur with depression, anxiety disorders, or psychotic illnesses. Creating a non-judgmental, confidential interviewing environment encourages honest disclosure, particularly in clinical settings where social stigma remains a barrier to treatment.

Management of Intoxication and Withdrawal

Managing acute intoxication and withdrawal requires prioritizing patient safety, continuous physiological monitoring, and timely pharmacological intervention. In acute intoxication, supportive care forms the foundation of treatment: maintaining a patent airway, monitoring vital signs, and preventing self-harm or injury in a low-stimulus environment. For alcohol withdrawal, symptom-triggered regimens utilizing long-acting benzodiazepines help prevent seizures and delirium tremens. Opioid withdrawal is managed with replacement pharmacotherapy such as methadone or buprenorphine, alongside supportive medications like clonidine to control autonomic symptoms. Non-pharmacological approaches, including clear orientation, hydration, reassurance, and minimizing environmental triggers, are indispensable across all phases of acute care.

Nursing Care Planning and Patient Advocacy

Developing an individualized nursing care plan requires a holistic approach that extends beyond medical stabilization to address psychosocial rehabilitation and stigma reduction. Nurses play a vital role in advocating for patients, challenging judgmental attitudes within the healthcare team, and promoting evidence-based addiction care. Nursing interventions focus on establishing a therapeutic alliance, reinforcing adaptive coping strategies, and connecting patients with community support networks. Relapse prevention education helps patients identify personal triggers, develop high-risk situation plans, and reframe setbacks as learning opportunities rather than total failures. Empowering patients through education and compassionate advocacy fosters long-term recovery and improves overall health outcomes.
