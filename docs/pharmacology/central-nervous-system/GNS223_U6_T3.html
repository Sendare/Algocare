Anticonvulsant Drugs

Introduction to Seizures and Epilepsy

A seizure is a sudden, uncontrolled burst of electrical activity in the brain that disrupts how cells communicate. This electrical storm can alter a person's consciousness, movements, sensations, or behavior. When a person experiences recurrent, unprovoked seizures, the condition is diagnosed as epilepsy. It is vital to separate a single seizure triggered by a high fever or low blood sugar from chronic epilepsy. The brain contains complex networks of neurons that normally fire in an orderly, balanced way. In an epileptic focus, a group of neurons becomes hyper-excitable, firing prematurely and recruiting neighboring cells into a synchronized wave of excessive electrical discharge.

Mechanism of Action of Anticonvulsant Drugs

Anticonvulsant medications, also known as antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), work by calming the hyper-excitable neurons responsible for seizures. They target specific cellular pathways in the brain to restore normal electrical balance.

  • Sodium channel blockers: Medications like phenytoin and carbamazepine bind to voltage-gated sodium channels, keeping them inactive longer and preventing rapid, repetitive firing.
  • Calcium channel blockers: Drugs like ethosuximide reduce calcium influx in thalamic neurons, making them very effective for absence seizures.
  • Enhancing GABA activity: GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system. Drugs like benzodiazepines and barbiturates increase GABA effects, reducing brain cell excitability.
  • Glutamate antagonists: These drugs block glutamate, the main excitatory neurotransmitter, preventing it from hyperexciting neighboring neurons.

Anticonvulsants are grouped into chemical and functional classes based on their structure and how they affect brain chemistry. Understanding these classes helps nurses anticipate therapeutic responses and specific side effects.

Pharmacokinetics and Administration Guidelines

Most anticonvulsants are administered orally for long-term maintenance therapy, but certain urgent situations require intravenous or rectal routes. Because many of these drugs have a narrow therapeutic index, maintaining steady blood levels is critical. If levels drop too low, breakthrough seizures occur; if they rise too high, toxicity sets in.

Intravenous phenytoin must be infused slowly and diluted strictly in normal saline because it causes severe venous irritation and tissue necrosis if it extravasates. Furthermore, intravenous phenytoin mixed with dextrose will precipitate immediately. Nurses must always check compatibility and monitor infusion sites closely.

Adverse Effects and Toxicity Management

Anticonvulsant therapy carries a wide range of potential side effects due to central nervous system depression and systemic impacts. Common early side effects include drowsiness, dizziness, ataxia, and blurred vision.

Long-term use brings unique challenges. Phenytoin frequently causes gingival hyperplasia (overgrowth of the gums), requiring meticulous oral hygiene and regular dental checkups. Valproic acid can cause hepatotoxicity and pancreatitis, requiring baseline and periodic liver function tests. Carbamazepine carries a risk of bone marrow suppression, leading to leukopenia and anemia. Nurses must monitor complete blood counts and watch closely for signs of infection or unusual bruising.

Drug Interactions and Contraindications

Many older anticonvulsants are potent inducers of hepatic cytochrome P450 enzymes. When liver enzymes are stimulated, they metabolize other drugs much faster, reducing their effectiveness. A classic example is the interaction between enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants and hormonal contraceptives, which can render birth control pills ineffective and lead to unplanned pregnancies.

Conversely, drugs like valproic acid are enzyme inhibitors, which can raise blood levels of other medications to toxic ranges. Nurses must review all concurrent medications, including over-the-counter remedies and herbal preparations, before therapy begins.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care involves baseline assessments before starting treatment and continuous monitoring thereafter. The nurse should document the exact frequency, duration, and triggers of any seizure activity. Neurological status, level of alertness, and gait stability must be evaluated regularly, especially after a dosage adjustment.

Safety precautions are essential in clinical settings. Padded side rails, functioning suction apparatus, and oxygen equipment should be readily available for patients at risk of seizures. Nurses must never force objects or padded tongue blades into a seizing patient's mouth, as this causes dental damage and airway obstruction.

Patient Education and Safe Adherence in Nigeria

In many Nigerian communities, epilepsy is shrouded in strong cultural misconceptions and social stigma, sometimes attributed to supernatural causes rather than a neurological condition. This stigma can lead patients to hide their illness or seek traditional remedies instead of modern anticonvulsant therapy.

Nurses play a critical role in community and clinic education. Patients and their families must understand that abrupt discontinuation of anticonvulsants can trigger status epilepticus, a life-threatening medical emergency. Education should emphasize strict adherence to prescribed schedules, regular clinic attendance for blood level checks, and practical lifestyle adjustments such as avoiding open fires, deep water, and dangerous machinery.
