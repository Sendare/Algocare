Antipsychotic Drugs

Classification and Mechanism of Antipsychotic Drugs

Antipsychotic medications, historically known as neuroleptics, form the cornerstone of pharmacological management for severe psychiatric disorders such as schizophrenia, bipolar mania, and delusional states. These medications are broadly divided into two major classes based on their receptor affinity and side effect profiles: first-generation (typical) and second-generation (atypical) agents.

  • First-Generation Antipsychotics (FGAs): Primarily act as strong antagonists at the dopamine D2 receptors in the mesolimbic pathway, effectively reducing positive symptoms like hallucinations and delusions. However, their lack of receptor selectivity often leads to significant extrapyramidal symptoms.
  • Second-Generation Antipsychotics (SGAs): Exhibit a broader pharmacological profile by blocking both dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT2A receptors simultaneously. This dual action generally lowers the risk of movement disorders while introducing metabolic challenges.

First-Generation (Typical) Antipsychotics

First-generation antipsychotics are older, potent medications that remain clinically relevant due to their rapid action and lower financial cost, making them widely utilized in resource-limited settings across Nigeria. Haloperidol and chlorpromazine are classic examples of this group. Haloperidol is frequently administered intramuscularly in acute psychiatric emergencies to rapidly calm severely agitated or aggressive patients.

The therapeutic success of FGAs in subduing positive symptoms comes with a trade-off. Because these drugs block dopamine uniformly across all brain pathways, they frequently disrupt normal motor control. Clinicians must balance the urgent need for behavioral stabilization against the high probability of acute dystonia, akathisia, and parkinsonian rigidity.

Second-Generation (Atypical) Antipsychotics

Second-generation antipsychotics were developed to overcome the debilitating movement disorders associated with older drugs. Agents such as olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, and clozapine are now often preferred as first-line therapies. By modulating both serotonin and dopamine transmission, they provide robust control over both positive and negative symptoms of schizophrenia with a significantly reduced incidence of extrapyramidal side effects.

Despite sparing the motor system from severe rigidity, atypicals present a different clinical challenge: metabolic syndrome. Patients on long-term therapy frequently experience rapid weight gain, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and elevated blood glucose levels. Clozapine stands apart as uniquely effective for treatment-resistant schizophrenia, though its use requires rigorous hematological monitoring due to the risk of agranulocytosis.

Extrapyramidal Symptoms and Movement Disorders

Extrapyramidal symptoms arise from the blockade of dopamine receptors in the nigrostriatal pathway. Recognizing these clinical manifestations early prevents unnecessary patient suffering and permanent impairment.

  • Acute Dystonia: Involuntary, painful muscle spasms typically affecting the eyes, neck, tongue, and back, often occurring within hours or days of starting treatment.
  • Akathisia: A profound, distressing sense of inner restlessness characterized by an inability to sit or stand still, frequently mistaken for worsening agitation.
  • Pseudoparkinsonism: A clinical picture mirroring Parkinson's disease, featuring resting tremor, mask-like facies, shuffling gait, and generalized rigidity.
  • Tardive Dyskinesia: A late-onset, potentially irreversible syndrome involving rhythmic, involuntary movements of the tongue, lips, jaw, and extremities, resulting from long-term dopamine blockade.

Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a rare, unpredictable, but life-threatening medical emergency triggered by exposure to dopamine antagonists. It can develop at any point during therapy, though it most frequently appears shortly after initiation or following a sudden dosage increase.

The syndrome is characterized by a distinct tetrad of clinical findings: extreme hyperthermia (often exceeding 40°C), severe generalized muscle rigidity described as 'lead-pipe', altered mental status ranging from confusion to coma, and autonomic instability demonstrated by labile blood pressure, tachycardia, and profuse diaphoresis. Laboratory investigations typically reveal profound elevations in serum creatine kinase due to severe rhabdomyolysis and leukocytosis. Immediate cessation of the offending antipsychotic drug and aggressive supportive care in an intensive care setting are mandatory to prevent multi-organ failure and death.

Metabolic and Other Systemic Adverse Effects

While second-generation antipsychotics reduce movement disorders, they introduce substantial metabolic liabilities that require vigilant surveillance. Weight gain is a prominent issue, driven by increased appetite and altered metabolic pathways, which subsequently elevates the risk for type 2 diabetes mellitus. Dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated triglycerides and LDL cholesterol, further compounds cardiovascular vulnerability.

Additional systemic effects include anticholinergic manifestations such as dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, and urinary retention. Alpha-adrenergic blockade can induce orthostatic hypotension, increasing the danger of falls, particularly among older adults. Prolactin elevation, common with risperidone and typical agents, can lead to galactorrhea, gynecomastia, and menstrual irregularities, causing considerable psychological distress for patients.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care for patients prescribed antipsychotic medications extends far beyond medication administration. Nurses must establish baseline clinical parameters before therapy begins, including weight, body mass index, fasting blood glucose, lipid profile, and blood pressure. Ongoing monitoring is essential to catch adverse metabolic and neurological shifts early.

Assessment tools like the Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale (AIMS) should be utilized regularly to detect early signs of tardive dyskinesia before movements become permanent. In clinical settings across Nigeria, nurses also play a pivotal role in bridging the gap between hospital care and community reintegration by educating patients and families about adherence, managing anticholinergic side effects with adequate hydration, and watching for early warning signs of relapse or toxicity.
