Antipsychotic Drugs

Introduction and Classification of Antipsychotic Drugs

Antipsychotic medications, sometimes called major tranquilizers or neuroleptics, form the cornerstone of pharmacological management for severe psychiatric disorders. These agents are broadly divided into two major classes based on their chemical structure, receptor affinity profile, and side effect tendencies: first-generation antipsychotics (FGAs), also known as typicals, and second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs), known as atypicals.

FGAs were developed first and are primarily known for blocking dopamine receptors. SGAs arrived later, possessing a more complex receptor binding profile that involves both dopamine and serotonin blockade. Recognizing the difference between these classes helps nurses anticipate specific therapeutic outcomes and adverse reactions in clinical practice.

Mechanism of Action in the Central Nervous System

The therapeutic power of antipsychotics lies primarily within their interaction with neurotransmitters in the brain, especially dopamine. The human brain contains several dopamine pathways, and psychiatric symptoms often link to imbalances in these tracts.

  • Mesolimbic pathway: Overactivity here drives positive symptoms of psychosis like hallucinations and delusions. Antipsychotics work by blocking dopamine receptors in this area to calm these symptoms.
  • Nigrostriatal pathway: Blockade here can cause movement issues because this tract controls normal motor function.
  • Tuberoinfundibular pathway: Dopamine here normally keeps prolactin levels low. Blocking receptors can raise prolactin, leading to breast milk production or menstrual changes.
  • Mesocortical pathway: Low dopamine here can worsen negative symptoms like emotional flatness, which atypical drugs manage better than older medications.

First-Generation (Typical) Antipsychotics

First-generation antipsychotics, such as haloperidol and chlorpromazine, were introduced in the mid-20th century. Their primary pharmacological action is strong antagonism of dopamine D2 receptors in the brain. While they are very effective at reducing positive symptoms of schizophrenia, their lack of receptor selectivity frequently causes troublesome movement disorders.

Because they block dopamine throughout the entire central nervous system rather than just the limbic areas, extrapyramidal side effects are common. Nurses must remain vigilant for muscle stiffness, tremors, and restlessness when caring for patients receiving typical agents.

Second-Generation (Atypical) Antipsychotics

Second-generation antipsychotics, including drugs like clozapine, olanzapine, and risperidone, represent a major advance in psychopharmacology. They combine antagonism of dopamine D2 receptors with strong blockade of serotonin 5-HT2A receptors. This dual action reduces the risk of movement disorders compared to older drugs.

However, atypicals introduce a different set of clinical challenges. Patients often experience significant metabolic syndrome, characterized by weight gain, elevated blood glucose, and dyslipidemia. Regular monitoring of weight, waist circumference, and lipid panels is therefore necessary during therapy.

Beyond primary psychotic disorders, antipsychotics are frequently utilized in various clinical scenarios where reality testing is impaired or mood is dangerously elevated. Nurses must evaluate the specific behavioral target when administering these medications.

Adverse Effects and Extrapyramidal Symptoms (EPS)

Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) are drug-induced movement disorders resulting from dopamine blockade in the basal ganglia. Recognizing these signs early prevents long-term distress and non-adherence:

  • Acute dystonia: Sudden, painful muscle spasms, often in the neck, eyes, or back, requiring rapid treatment with anticholinergics like benztropine.
  • Akathisia: An intense inner restlessness where the patient cannot stay still, constantly pacing or shifting weight.
  • Pseudoparkinsonism: Staggering gait, pill-rolling tremors, drooling, and mask-like facial expression resembling Parkinson's disease.
  • Tardive dyskinesia: Involuntary, rhythmic movements of the tongue, lips, and jaw, which can become permanent if the medication is not stopped.

Management of Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) is a rare, life-threatening emergency associated with the use of antipsychotic drugs. It can develop days or weeks after starting a medication or changing the dose.

Key signs include extreme hyperthermia, severe muscle rigidity often described as 'lead pipe,' autonomic instability with fluctuating blood pressure and tachycardia, and altered mental status. Immediate nursing actions include stopping the offending antipsychotic at once, notifying the medical team, and providing supportive cooling and hydration. Medical treatment may involve muscle relaxants like dantrolene.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care involves continuous assessment and patient education to ensure safe medication administration. Nurses must monitor vital signs, assess for early movement side effects using standardized rating scales like the AIMS test, and track metabolic labs.

Patient education plays a vital role in adherence. Nurses should teach patients not to stop taking their medication abruptly, as this can trigger a relapse or withdrawal symptoms. Emphasizing the importance of reporting unusual muscle stiffness, blurred vision, or rapid weight gain empowers patients to participate actively in their own recovery journey.
