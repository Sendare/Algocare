Drug Over-Prescription

Introduction to Drug Over-Prescription and the National Drug Policy

Drug over-prescription involves the unnecessary, excessive, or inappropriate administration of pharmaceutical agents to patients. Within the Nigerian healthcare landscape, this practice undermines clinical outcomes and strains limited resources. The **National Drug Policy (NDP)** serves as the foundational framework designed to guarantee the availability, affordability, safety, and rational use of essential medicines across all tiers of care. Rational drug use dictates that patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their individual requirements, for an adequate period, and at the lowest cost to them and their community. When prescribers deviate from these principles, over-prescription occurs. This deviation is frequently observed in busy outpatient departments where diagnostic uncertainty prompts clinicians to rely on prescription pads rather than thorough investigation. Understanding the scope of over-prescription requires recognizing how easily pharmaceutical solutions are substituted for lifestyle modifications, watchful waiting, or non-pharmacological interventions.

Underlying Behavioral and Systemic Drivers of Over-Prescription

Multiple intersecting factors propel the habit of excessive prescribing in clinical practice. Clinicians often face immense psychological pressure from patients who equate a consultation with receiving physical commodities. In many Nigerian communities, clients measure the quality of care by the physical volume or color of pills dispensed, reinforcing the prescriber's urge to satisfy rather than educate. - **Diagnostic Uncertainty**: Fear of missing a diagnosis or overlooking a developing infection leads defensive practitioners to prescribe broad-spectrum antibiotics or combination analgesics 'just in case.' - **Commercial Influence**: Aggressive pharmaceutical marketing, detailing visits, and sponsored medical education subtly bias prescribing habits toward newer, costlier brand-name medications. - **Time Constraints**: High patient-to-doctor or patient-to-nurse ratios leave inadequate time for detailed clinical history-taking, making a quick prescription script an efficient escape valve. - **Habit and Inertia**: Repeating historical prescription patterns without reviewing contemporary clinical guidelines perpetuates outdated and redundant drug regimens.

Common Forms and Classifications of Inappropriate Prescribing

Inappropriate prescribing manifests in several distinct clinical patterns that extend beyond simple over-prescription. Recognizing these categories helps nursing personnel identify hidden medication risks during routine ward rounds or community health outreaches. | Classification | Clinical Description | Typical Example | | --- | --- | --- | | **Polypharmacy** | The concurrent use of multiple medications exceeding clinical necessity | Taking five different analgesics simultaneously for mild back pain | | **Overuse** | Prescribing a drug when no pharmacological indication exists | Issuing antibiotics for a self-limiting viral upper respiratory tract infection | | **Misprescription** | Selecting an incorrect drug, dose, frequency, or duration for a condition | Giving standard adult doses of gentamicin without renal function monitoring | | **Misuse** | Continuing a medication long after the therapeutic window has closed | Maintaining long-term proton pump inhibitor therapy without re-evaluation |

Consequences of Over-Prescription on Patient Safety and Public Health

The indiscriminate distribution and consumption of excess pharmaceuticals trigger severe cascading effects on both individual bodies and entire populations. At the individual level, taking surplus medications spikes the likelihood of adverse drug reactions, allergic responses, and accidental toxicity. Elderly patients are particularly vulnerable due to age-related declines in hepatic metabolism and renal clearance, turning minor polypharmacy into life-threatening renal failure or acute confusion states. On a broader public health scale, the routine over-prescription of antimicrobials accelerates antimicrobial resistance (AMR). When pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and parasites are continuously exposed to sub-therapeutic or unwarranted chemical agents, they mutate and survive, rendering standard first-line therapies obsolete. Nigeria faces mounting challenges with multi-drug resistant strains of organisms responsible for malaria, tuberculosis, and common bacterial infections, directly fueled by historical over-prescription practices in both formal and informal healthcare sectors.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of Over-Prescribed Regimens

Nurses occupy a frontline position to intercept inappropriate prescribing before administration occurs. Conducting a comprehensive medication reconciliation upon admission, transfer, or clinic visit is essential. This process involves comparing the patient's actual home regimen against current medical records, checking for duplications, overlapping drug classes, and expired prescriptions brought from patent medicine vendors. Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion when reviewing complex charts. Look for 'prescribing cascades,' where an adverse drug reaction from one medication is misdiagnosed as a new medical condition, prompting the addition of yet another drug to the regimen. For example, a patient developing a dry cough from an ACE inhibitor might be inappropriately prescribed a cough syrup rather than having the offending antihypertensive reviewed. Evaluating patient complaints through a pharmacological lens ensures that newly emerging symptoms are analyzed as potential drug toxicities before escalating therapeutic interventions.

Nursing Interventions for Medication Review and Rational Prescribing

Active nursing participation in multidisciplinary ward rounds provides an ideal platform to advocate for rational prescribing. When nurses spot redundant, excessive, or potentially interacting drug orders, they should engage the medical team constructively, presenting clear clinical observations rather than passive compliance. - **Patient Education**: Teach patients about the specific purpose, expected duration, and potential side effects of each prescribed medication, empowering them to question unnecessary refills. - **Deprescribing Advocacy**: Collaborate with clinical pharmacists and physicians to safely taper or stop medications where the harms outweigh potential benefits, particularly in palliative or geriatric care. - **Symptom Monitoring**: Track clinical parameters meticulously to evaluate whether a drug is producing tangible therapeutic gains or simply adding chemical burden. - **Promoting Non-Pharmacological Care**: Encourage alternative comfort measures such as physical positioning, heat therapy, or psychological support where drugs are not strictly required.
