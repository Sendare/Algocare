Drug Over-Prescription

Introduction to Drug Over-Prescription and the National Drug Policy

Drug over-prescription remains a critical healthcare challenge globally and within Nigeria, posing significant risks to patient safety, treatment efficacy, and public health outcomes. In the context of the Nigerian healthcare system, the National Drug Policy (NDP) serves as the primary guiding framework established to ensure the availability, accessibility, affordability, and rational use of safe, efficacious, and quality drugs. Rational drug use dictates that patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their individual requirements for an adequate period, and at the lowest cost to them and their community. However, deviations from this principle through over-prescription undermine the foundational goals of the NDP. For nursing students, understanding drug over-prescription goes beyond simple medication administration; it requires a deep appreciation of how prescribing patterns impact patient well-being and healthcare sustainability. Over-prescription often leads to wastage of scarce healthcare resources, increased out-of-pocket expenses for patients, and a heightened risk of adverse drug reactions. By aligning clinical practices with the tenets of the National Drug Policy, healthcare professionals can mitigate these risks. Nurses, as the primary advocates for patient safety at the bedside, play a pivotal role in identifying inappropriate drug regimens and promoting rational pharmacotherapy. A comprehensive grasp of these concepts is essential for safeguarding public health in Nigeria and fostering a culture of evidence-based medication management.

Underlying Pathophysiological and Behavioral Drivers of Over-Prescription

The phenomenon of drug over-prescription is driven by a complex interplay of pathophysiological misunderstandings and behavioral factors among both clinicians and patients. From a clinical perspective, prescribers sometimes misinterpret complex disease states or overlapping symptoms, leading to the erroneous belief that polypharmacy is necessary to cover all potential diagnoses. For instance, managing chronic conditions like hypertension or diabetes improperly can trigger a cascade where new symptoms—often adverse drug effects—are treated with additional medications rather than by adjusting or discontinuing the offending agent. Behavioral drivers are equally potent. Clinicians may experience anxiety regarding potential malpractice suits or patient dissatisfaction, prompting them to write a prescription 'just in case' or to appease demanding patients who equate medical care with receiving physical items like drugs. Furthermore, patient-level behavioral factors heavily influence prescribing habits. Many patients hold deeply ingrained beliefs that a consultation is incomplete without a prescription, often requesting specific brand-name drugs seen in advertisements. In Nigeria, cultural perceptions linking recovery strictly to potent or numerous medications can pressure practitioners into over-prescribing. Addressing these underlying drivers requires targeted educational interventions that shift both clinical mindsets toward evidence-based deprescribing and public perceptions toward holistic, non-pharmacological health maintenance.

Common Forms and Classifications of Inappropriate Prescribing

Inappropriate prescribing manifests in various forms, requiring nurses and other healthcare professionals to recognize distinct classifications of medication misuse. One primary form is polypharmacy, which involves the concurrent use of multiple medications by a patient, often defined as taking five or more drugs daily. While polypharmacy can be clinically justified in complex multi-morbid patients, it frequently becomes inappropriate when drugs are redundant, lack clear indications, or interact negatively. Another classification is the use of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs), where the risk of an adverse event outweighs the anticipated clinical benefit, particularly in vulnerable populations such as the elderly or pregnant women. Over-prescription also encompasses excessive duration and incorrect dosing, where patients receive medications for longer than necessary or at levels that increase toxicity risks without enhancing therapeutic outcomes. Additionally, the indiscriminate prescribing of broad-spectrum antibiotics for viral infections represents a severe form of inappropriate prescribing that accelerates antimicrobial resistance, a major public health crisis in Nigeria. Therapeutic duplication—prescribing two or more drugs from the same pharmacological class for the same indication—is another frequent error. Recognizing these specific classifications enables nursing professionals to pinpoint exact vulnerabilities in a patient's regimen and communicate effectively with the multidisciplinary healthcare team to rectify them.

Systemic Causes and Contributing Factors in Healthcare Settings

Systemic causes and contributing factors within healthcare settings heavily foster environments where drug over-prescription thrives. Structural deficiencies, such as severe time constraints during patient consultations, force clinicians to rely on quick-fix prescription pads rather than conducting thorough diagnostic workups or patient education. When a doctor has only a few minutes per patient, prescribing a drug is often faster than explaining lifestyle modifications or investigating underlying psychosocial issues. Furthermore, healthcare financing and supply chain dynamics play a critical role. In systems where healthcare facilities or providers generate revenue from drug sales, there is a perverse financial incentive to dispense more medications. Promotional practices by pharmaceutical marketing representatives, including gifts, sponsored trips, and free drug samples, can subtly or overtly bias prescribing habits toward newer, more expensive, or excessive drug regimens, regardless of patient need. In resource-constrained settings like Nigeria, a lack of electronic health records and poor communication among multidisciplinary teams lead to fragmented care, where different specialists independently prescribe medications without checking existing profiles. Addressing these systemic issues requires organizational reforms, transparent supply chain regulations, and robust electronic systems that support safe, coordinated care delivery.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of Over-Prescribed Regimens

Clinical assessment and the systematic identification of over-prescribed regimens require a rigorous, structured approach by healthcare professionals, particularly nurses and clinical pharmacists. The cornerstone of this process is the comprehensive medication reconciliation and review, where every drug a patient is taking is cross-referenced against their current clinical status, lab results, and diagnostic indications. Nurses must skillfully gather a complete medication history—including over-the-counter medications, herbal remedies, and traditional supplements commonly used in Nigeria—to uncover hidden risks of duplication or herb-drug interactions. During the assessment, nurses look for specific clinical red flags, such as the prescribing of a medication to treat the side effect of another medication (known as a prescribing cascade), or regimens that lack a clear, documented indication in the medical record. Utilizing standardized screening tools, such as explicit criteria for identifying potentially inappropriate medications in older adults, aids in objective evaluation. By actively engaging with patients and observing changes in their functional status, cognitive clarity, or vital signs, nurses can identify subtle signs of drug toxicity or adverse effects that signal an over-prescribed regimen. Early identification empowers the healthcare team to streamline therapy, reduce pill burdens, and improve overall patient safety outcomes.

Consequences of Drug Over-Prescription on Patient Safety and Public Health

The consequences of drug over-prescription extend far beyond simple economic waste, profoundly impacting patient safety and broader public health indices. At the individual level, taking unnecessary or excessive medications exposes patients to heightened risks of adverse drug reactions (ADRs), drug-drug interactions, and cumulative toxicity. These complications frequently result in emergency room visits, prolonged hospital stays, and, in severe cases, life-threatening events or mortality, particularly among vulnerable groups like geriatric and pediatric patients. Polypharmacy and over-prescription also severely impair patient adherence; the greater the pill burden and dosing complexity, the more likely a patient is to miss doses or abandon their treatment regimen entirely. On a public health scale, the widespread over-prescription of antimicrobials drives the alarming rise of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), rendering standard infections increasingly difficult or impossible to treat across Nigerian communities. Economically, out-of-pocket spending on unneeded medications depletes household finances, pushing families deeper into poverty, while simultaneously straining the operational budgets of healthcare facilities. Recognizing these multi-layered consequences underscores the urgent need for robust stewardship programs, vigilant nursing oversight, and strict adherence to rational prescribing practices to protect both individuals and the wider population.

Nursing Interventions for Medication Review and Rational Prescribing

Nurses occupy a vital frontline position in combating drug over-prescription through systematic medication reviews and the active promotion of rational prescribing. The nursing intervention process begins with routine medication reconciliation upon admission, transfer, and discharge, ensuring that discrepancies between prescribed and actual drugs are immediately identified. Nurses utilize their close, continuous contact with patients to monitor for therapeutic responses, early signs of toxicity, and unexpected adverse reactions that may signal an inappropriate regimen. Armed with pharmacological knowledge, nurses can initiate collaborative discussions with physicians and pharmacists, advocating for deprescribing—the planned, supervised withdrawal of inappropriate medications where the harms outweigh the benefits. Furthermore, nurses provide essential patient education, empowering individuals and their families to understand the specific indications for each prescribed drug, recognize potential side effects, and question redundant therapies. In Nigerian clinical settings, where interprofessional communication can sometimes be hierarchical, assertive and evidence-based nursing advocacy is crucial for challenging questionable prescriptions safely. By integrating these review and advocacy practices into daily care routines, nurses actively champion patient safety, optimize therapeutic outcomes, and uphold the highest standards of professional practice.

Regulatory Strategies and Policy Enforcement in Nigeria

Addressing drug over-prescription effectively requires robust regulatory strategies and stringent policy enforcement at national and institutional levels in Nigeria. Regulatory bodies such as the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) and the Pharmacists Council of Nigeria (PCN) hold the mandate to monitor drug distribution, enforce ethical pharmaceutical marketing, and curb illicit over-the-counter dispensing. Policy enforcement involves cracking down on patent medicine vendors and unregulated outlets that sell prescription-only drugs without valid authorizations, which is a significant driver of community-level over-prescription and drug misuse. At the institutional level, hospitals and healthcare facilities must implement mandatory antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) and pharmacy and therapeutics committees to audit prescribing habits, establish local formularies, and enforce adherence to clinical guidelines. Furthermore, continuous professional development and educational campaigns for healthcare providers are essential regulatory-backed initiatives that reinforce rational prescribing behaviors and update practitioners on emerging safety alerts. For nursing students, understanding these regulatory frameworks provides a comprehensive view of how systemic oversight complements bedside care, ensuring that multi-faceted efforts successfully protect the Nigerian population from the dangers of inappropriate drug use.
