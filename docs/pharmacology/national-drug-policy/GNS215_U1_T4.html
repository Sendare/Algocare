Unit Dispensing System

Introduction and Objectives of the Unit Dispensing System

The unit dispensing system is a method of medication distribution where drugs are supplied in single-dose packages, ready for immediate administration to a specific patient. Unlike traditional bulk supply methods where medications are kept in large ward stocks, this system prepares individualized doses—usually for a 24-hour period—in the pharmacy department. The primary objective is to enhance patient safety by reducing medication administration errors, improving inventory control, and ensuring precise financial accounting for pharmaceutical supplies in Nigerian healthcare facilities.

Core Components and Operational Workflow

A successful unit dose drug distribution system relies on three vital elements:

  • Pharmacy-based medication profile: A centralized patient profile maintained by the pharmacist to screen for drug interactions and allergies.
  • Unit dose packaging: Medications repackaged into single-unit containers clearly labeled with the generic name, strength, batch number, and expiration date.
  • 24-hour cart exchange: A system where mobile medication carts containing a day's supply of drugs are swapped between the pharmacy and the ward at a fixed daily hour.

During daily rounds, the nurse administers the medication directly from the patient-specific drawer on the cart, reducing the need for calculation or manipulation at the bedside.

Collaborative Roles of Nurses and Pharmacists

Interprofessional collaboration forms the backbone of safe unit-dose administration. The pharmacist interprets the physician's order, maintains the patient profile, and ensures the accuracy of dispensed individual doses. Meanwhile, the nurse performs the final safety check using the five rights of medication administration at the bedside. Clear communication channels between both professions ensure that missed doses, dosage modifications, or adverse drug reactions are addressed promptly without compromising patient care.

Beyond safety, financial transparency is a major benefit. Patients are only billed for medications actually consumed, eliminating the financial burden of paying for bulk drugs left unused when a prescription changes or a patient is discharged.

Implementation Challenges in Nigerian Healthcare

Implementing a unit dose system across Nigerian hospitals faces distinct hurdles. Chronic staff shortages among both nurses and pharmacists strain the labor-intensive demands of cart exchanges and individual packaging. Financial constraints limit the acquisition of automated packaging machinery and specialized medication carts. Furthermore, erratic power supply disrupts refrigerated storage for sensitive drugs, while infrastructural deficits and bureaucratic delays can slow down the seamless transport of medications between the central pharmacy and distant wards.

Future Outlook and Policy Recommendations

Overcoming current limitations requires deliberate policy interventions and phased integration. Hospital managements must prioritize continuous staff training and invest in basic technological tools, such as electronic health records and barcoding systems, to streamline workflow. Government support through health sector funding can facilitate the procurement of essential packaging equipment. As Nigerian hospitals gradually digitize, combining unit dispensing with electronic prescribing will further close safety gaps and elevate the standard of clinical pharmacy practice nationwide.
