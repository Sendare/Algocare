Role of the Nurse in Essential Drug Programme

Introduction to the Essential Drug Programme in Nigeria

The Essential Drug Programme (EDP) was introduced by the World Health Organization and adopted by countries like Nigeria to ensure that life-saving medicines are available at all times in adequate amounts, in the appropriate dosage forms, and at an affordable price. In many public health facilities across Nigeria, nurses form the backbone of healthcare delivery and interact most frequently with patients. Because of this frontline position, nurses carry a major responsibility in implementing the EDP. They do not merely administer drugs; they oversee how these drugs move through the health facility, monitor patient responses, and help prevent stockouts. Understanding the EDP allows nursing students and practitioners to appreciate why proper drug management is just as critical to patient recovery as clinical bedside nursing care.

Selection and Quantification of Essential Medicines

Selecting and quantifying drugs requires careful planning based on the actual health needs of the community rather than guesswork. Under the Essential Medicines List (EML), medicines are chosen based on disease prevalence, safety evidence, and cost-effectiveness. Nurses often contribute data to quantification teams by tracking consumption patterns and disease trends in their wards.

  • Morbidity-based quantification: Calculating drug needs based on the number of patients treated for specific diagnosed illnesses.
  • Consumption-based quantification: Estimating future drug requirements using historical records of how many units were used over a past period.
  • Buffer stock calculation: Adding an extra percentage of inventory to prevent running out during unexpected supply chain delays.

Procurement, Storage, and Inventory Management

Good inventory management prevents waste, spoilage, and stockouts at the clinical level. Nurses working in ward settings often manage local medicine cupboards and treatment rooms. Improper storage can ruin expensive medications, rendering them completely inactive or even toxic.

Dispensing and Rational Use of Drugs

Rational drug use means patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their individual requirements, for an adequate period, and at the lowest cost to them and their community. Irrational drug use wastes scarce resources and breeds antimicrobial resistance.

When nurses dispense or administer medications, they must strictly follow the five rights: right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, and right time. A common clinical error is polypharmacy—giving too many drugs to a single patient—which increases the risk of dangerous drug interactions and severe side effects. Nurses must advocate for clear, justified prescriptions and question ambiguous orders before administration.

Patient Education and Adherence Support

Even the best-supplied drug programme fails if the patient does not take their medicine correctly at home. Nurses bridge the gap between clinical prescriptions and patient behavior. Education should be delivered in simple, clear local languages, avoiding complex medical jargon that confuses patients. Adherence support involves helping clients understand why finishing a drug course matters—especially with chronic conditions like tuberculosis or hypertension where symptoms might disappear before the infection or disease is fully controlled. Nurses also teach patients how to recognize common mild side effects so they do not panic and abandon their treatment prematurely.

Pharmacovigilance and Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem. Not all adverse drug reactions (ADRs) are discovered during clinical trials before a drug is approved for public use. Nurses monitor patients closely after medication administration and are often the first to notice unexpected rashes, breathing difficulties, or unusual changes in lab results. Reporting these events through official pharmacovigilance channels helps national regulators protect the wider population from unsafe or defective medicine batches.

Multidisciplinary Teamwork and Quality Assurance

A successful Essential Drug Programme relies on strong cooperation between nurses, pharmacists, physicians, hospital administrators, and logisticians. Each professional brings unique skills to the drug management cycle. Pharmacists ensure quality procurement and compounding, doctors prescribe based on clinical diagnosis, and nurses manage safe administration and patient feedback. Quality assurance programs audit these processes regularly to identify gaps, stop the circulation of fake or substandard drugs, and ensure accountability at all levels of healthcare delivery.
