Role of the Nurse in Essential Drug Programme

Introduction to the Essential Drug Programme in Nigeria

The Essential Drug Programme (EDP) forms a cornerstone of healthcare delivery in Nigeria, designed to ensure that safe, effective, and affordable medicines are continuously available at all levels of healthcare. Stemming from the World Health Organization's essential medicines concept, the programme addresses critical historical challenges in resource-limited settings, such as stockouts of life-saving commodities, the proliferation of substandard drugs, and out-of-pocket impoverishment from catastrophic health expenditures. Within primary health care centers and tertiary teaching hospitals alike, the nurse functions as the operational bridge between drug availability and patient health outcomes. Because nurses manage daily clinical workflows, they possess direct insight into consumption patterns and medicine utilization. Recognizing how the EDP structures public health supply chains allows nursing professionals to anticipate supply bottlenecks, minimize medication wastage, and advocate for sustainable pharmaceutical procurement policies within their health institutions.

Selection and Quantification of Essential Medicines

Selecting and quantifying medicines requires systematic calculation based on morbidity profiles rather than guesswork. The Nigerian National Essential Medicines List (NEML) guides this selection, prioritizing drugs that address the greatest disease burdens, such as malaria, maternal hemorrhage, and childhood infections. - **Morbidity-based forecasting**: Calculating required stock quantities by analyzing historical disease prevalence and patient attendance records. - **Consumption-based estimation**: Reviewing past inventory dispersion rates to project future stock needs. - **Standard treatment guidelines**: Adhering strictly to approved clinical protocols to prevent the ordering of non-essential or obsolete drugs. - **Budgetary alignment**: Balancing clinical needs against allocated institutional finances to avoid over-procurement and subsequent expiry.

Procurement, Storage, and Inventory Management

Maintaining pharmaceutical integrity from the point of delivery to the bedside is a core nursing responsibility in ward-level asset management. This practice ensures that items with the nearest expiration dates are dispensed first, actively minimizing institutional financial losses caused by drug spoilage and expiry on hospital shelves.

Dispensing and Rational Use of Drugs

Rational drug use ensures that patients receive medications appropriate to their clinical needs, in doses that meet their individual requirements, for an adequate period, and at the lowest cost to them and their community. Irrational prescribing and dispensing practices—such as unnecessary polypharmacy, overuse of injections when oral routes suffice, and inappropriate antimicrobial selection—drive antimicrobial resistance and treatment failures across Nigeria. Nurses act as the final safety barrier before medication reaches the patient. By performing independent checks against patient allergy profiles, organ function statuses, and therapeutic duplications, nurses intercept dispensing errors originating from earlier stages of the medication cycle. Promoting rational use also involves educating junior colleagues and patients on the dangers of self-medication and unprescribed antibiotic sharing.

Patient Education and Adherence Support

Prescribing an effective medicine is clinically meaningless if the patient fails to take it correctly. Treatment non-adherence remains a persistent hurdle in chronic disease management, contributing to disease relapse, increased hospital admissions, and wasted healthcare resources. Nurses transform complex pharmacological instructions into actionable, culturally appropriate guidance. When counseling patients, nursing staff verify comprehension by asking patients to repeat dosing schedules in their own words, overcoming language barriers or literacy gaps common in community settings. Adherence support extends beyond simple instruction; it involves exploring financial or logistical barriers to acquiring refills and connecting patients with social support structures or community health extension workers to maintain treatment continuity.

Pharmacovigilance and Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problems. In Nigeria, regulatory bodies like NAFDAC rely heavily on healthcare worker surveillance to track post-market drug safety. Every nurse must recognize and report Adverse Drug Reactions (ADRs). Because nurses monitor post-administration recovery closely, they are often the first to notice unexpected skin rashes, respiratory changes, or organ dysfunction following drug administration. Prompt completion of the national yellow form or electronic ADR reporting tools ensures that dangerous medication batches or unexpected toxicities are flagged nationally, protecting the wider public from systemic pharmaceutical harm.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration and Quality Assurance

A functioning Essential Drug Programme depends on seamless cooperation among pharmacists, physicians, nurses, logisticians, and health administrators. Each professional brings distinct expertise: pharmacists manage supply integrity and clinical pharmacokinetics, physicians diagnose and prescribe, while nurses coordinate holistic patient care and manage ward-level drug inventories. Quality assurance requires regular clinical audits, inventory reconciliations, and adherence to hospital formulary committees. Nurses participate actively in these multidisciplinary teams by providing empirical data on drug efficacy, highlighting medication error trends, and advocating for patient safety protocols. Collaborative audits bridge the gap between policy design and clinical reality, ensuring that essential medicines remain available, safe, and effective for every patient.
