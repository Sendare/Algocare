Patient Drug History

Introduction to Patient Drug History in Nursing

A patient drug history is a structured record of all pharmaceutical substances a person currently uses or has recently used. Gathering this data is a core clinical competency for nurses across all specialties in Nigerian healthcare settings. Without an accurate medication profile, clinicians risk prescribing contraindicated treatments, missing adverse drug reactions, or failing to identify therapeutic duplications. The process extends far beyond simply asking patients what they take. It requires an understanding of prescription drugs, non-prescription remedies, traditional concoctions, and social substances.

Components of a Comprehensive Drug History

A thorough medication assessment must capture specific details to be clinically actionable. Incomplete records frequently lead to preventable adverse events during admission or outpatient care. - **Prescription Medications**: Name, exact dose, route of administration, and frequency of all physician-ordered drugs. - **Over-the-Counter Drugs**: Analgesics, antacids, and other pharmacy items bought without a prescription. - **Herbal and Traditional Remedies**: Local concoctions, roots, and supplements popular in West African homes. - **Allergies and Adverse Reactions**: The specific substance and the exact nature of the previous negative reaction, distinguishing true allergy from common side effects.

Techniques and Strategies for Effective Medication Interviewing

Extracting an accurate drug history requires intentional communication strategies. Patients often forget names of drugs, confuse dosages, or omit items they consider insignificant. Asking open-ended questions prevents patients from simply giving a yes or no answer. Instead of asking "Do you take blood pressure pills?", a nurse should ask "What medications do you take when your blood pressure rises?". The "brown bag" method is exceptionally effective in community and outpatient clinics: instruct patients to bring every single bottle, sachet, and tube they currently use to the consultation. When elderly patients are interviewed, involving a reliable caregiver or family member helps bridge memory gaps, especially regarding complex dosing schedules.

Uncovering Herbal, Dietary, and Over-the-Counter Use

In many Nigerian communities, self-medication with over-the-counter analgesics and traditional herbal preparations is culturally normative and widespread. Patients frequently fail to report these substances because they view them as natural, harmless, or separate from 'real medicine' prescribed by doctors. However, herbal products can cause profound herb-drug interactions. For example, certain local roots and supplements can potentiate anticoagulants, causing severe bleeding risks, or alter hepatic enzyme activity, changing the metabolism of orthodox drugs. Nurses must inquire specifically about local teas, bitter preparations, and patent medicine vendor purchases without judgment.

Assessing Medication Adherence and Barriers to Compliance

Adherence refers to how closely a patient follows their agreed-upon treatment plan. Non-adherence is a silent clinical crisis contributing to treatment failure, disease progression, and avoidable hospital admissions. Barriers are diverse and contextual. In resource-limited settings, the out-of-pocket cost of pharmaceuticals is a massive deterrent, often forcing patients to skip doses or split tablets. Polypharmacy, complex dosing regimens, unpleasant side effects, and forgetfulness also disrupt compliance. When assessing adherence, nurses must avoid an accusatory tone. Asking questions like "Many people find it hard to remember every pill; how often do you miss a dose?" creates a safe space for honest disclosure.

Documenting and Communicating Drug History in Clinical Practice

Gathering a drug history is clinically useless if the information remains locked in the interviewer's head or a stray piece of paper. Accurate documentation in the nursing care plan and medical record ensures safe handovers between shifts and interprofessional continuity of care. The record must be legible, dated, and structured according to facility protocols. During clinical handovers or referrals, the nurse must explicitly highlight high-risk agents, recent changes, and active sensitivities. Any discrepancies discovered between home regimens and hospital orders must be immediately clarified with the attending medical officer before administration occurs.
