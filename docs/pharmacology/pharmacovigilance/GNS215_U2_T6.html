Adverse Drug Reaction Monitoring Form

Introduction to Pharmacovigilance and Adverse Drug Reactions

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problem. An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is an appreciably harmful or unpleasant reaction resulting from an intervention related to the use of a medicinal product. Unlike side effects, which are often predictable and known pharmacological actions, ADRs are frequently unexpected and can occur at normal therapeutic doses. Monitoring these reactions helps regulatory bodies ensure that medicines available to the public maintain an acceptable risk-benefit ratio throughout their lifecycle.

Purpose and Importance of the ADR Monitoring Form in Nigeria

In Nigeria, post-market surveillance of medicines is critical due to variations in local drug supply chains, potential circulation of substandard products, and diverse patient demographics. The ADR monitoring form serves as the primary tool for healthcare workers to report suspected reactions to the national regulatory agency. Collecting these reports allows authorities to identify rare safety signals that clinical trials could not detect because trial sample sizes are too small. Without active reporting from clinical settings, dangerous drug trends would remain hidden, putting more patients at risk.

Overview of the NAFDAC Yellow Form

The National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) uses a standardized reporting tool known universally as the Yellow Form. This paper-based instrument is distributed across hospitals, clinics, and pharmacies nationwide for spontaneous reporting. Although digital reporting platforms are emerging, the physical Yellow Form remains a staple in clinical units. Every healthcare professional should know where these forms are kept in their department, as quick access encourages prompt documentation when an unexpected reaction occurs.

Key Components of the ADR Reporting Form

  • Patient Information: Initials, hospital number, age, gender, and weight to help identify demographic trends.
  • Suspected Medication(s): Brand name, generic name, batch number, dose used, route, start date, and stop date.
  • Adverse Reaction Details: Description of the reaction, date of onset, and how long it lasted.
  • Concomitant Medications: Other drugs the patient took within the past three months to rule out drug interactions.
  • Outcome and Severity: Whether the patient recovered, suffered disability, or died, alongside the clinical classification of seriousness.
  • Reporter Details: Name, profession, and contact information of the healthcare worker completing the form for follow-up inquiries.

Step-by-Step Guide to Completing the ADR Form

  1. Identify the Reaction: Recognize that an unexpected symptom may be linked to a drug therapy rather than the underlying disease.
  2. Gather Clinical Records: Pull the patient folder to confirm exact dates, dosages, and laboratory results.
  3. Complete Patient Section: Fill in demographic details accurately while protecting patient confidentiality using initials instead of full names.
  4. Document the Drug History: Record every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, and herbal product used recently.
  5. Describe the Event: Write a clear, chronological narrative of how the reaction started, progressed, and resolved.
  6. Sign and Submit: Provide your professional contact details so NAFDAC pharmacovigilance officers can reach you if they need clarification.

Assessing Causality and Severity of Adverse Drug Reactions

Determining whether a drug caused a specific reaction requires careful clinical reasoning. Causality assessment involves checking if the reaction stopped when the drug was withdrawn (dechallenge) and if it reappeared when the drug was reintroduced (rechallenge—though rechallenge is rarely done intentionally if the reaction was severe). Severity assessment categorizes the reaction as mild, moderate, severe, or life-threatening. A severe reaction typically results in hospitalization, disability, or death, demanding immediate regulatory attention.

Submitting and Following Up on ADR Reports

Once the Yellow Form is completed, it must be transmitted to NAFDAC through official channels, such as the zonal office, designated hospital pharmacovigilance coordinators, or online portals. Submitting the form is not the end of the process; nurses should participate in any follow-up investigations requested by regulatory pharmacovigilance officers. Feedback loops from national agencies help clinical units learn about emerging safety alerts, reinforcing the value of local reporting in global drug safety.

The Role of Nurses in Pharmacovigilance and Patient Safety

Nurses are at the frontline of patient care and are often the first to notice subtle changes after medication administration. Their close, continuous contact with patients places them in the best position to catch early signs of adverse reactions. Beyond filling out forms, nurses educate patients on what symptoms to watch out for at home and encourage them to report unusual feelings. Cultivating a proactive reporting culture among nursing staff directly reduces preventable drug-related morbidity and mortality.
