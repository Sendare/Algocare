Adverse Drug Reactions

Introduction and Definition of Adverse Drug Reactions

An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is defined as any noxious, unintended, and undesired effect that occurs at doses normally used in humans for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or treatment. This distinguishes ADRs from accidental or intentional poisoning, where supratherapeutic doses are ingested. ADRs represent a significant clinical challenge in healthcare settings across Nigeria, frequently contributing to extended hospital stays, increased treatment costs, and avoidable patient morbidity.

Understanding the boundary between an adverse drug reaction and a simple side effect is essential for clinical practice. While a side effect is a predictable, secondary pharmacological effect occurring at normal doses—such as drowsiness caused by certain antihistamines—an ADR is broader and encompasses harmful, unintended tissue responses. Recognizing these reactions early prevents escalation into life-threatening organ damage.

Classification of Adverse Drug Reactions: The Rawlins-Thompson Model

The Rawlins-Thompson classification categorizes adverse drug reactions into two primary types based on their dose-dependency and predictability.

  • Type A (Augmented): These reactions are an exaggeration of the drug's normal pharmacological action. They are common, dose-dependent, and generally predictable. Examples include hypoglycemia caused by insulin or excessive bleeding from warfarin.
  • Type B (Bizarre): These reactions are idiosyncratic, unrelated to the drug's known pharmacology, unpredictable, and typically dose-independent. They often involve immunological or genetic mechanisms, such as anaphylaxis to penicillin or severe skin eruptions.

Advanced classification models also include Type C (chronic effects), Type D (delayed effects), Type E (end-of-treatment effects), and Type F (failure of therapy). However, Types A and B remain the core focus for everyday clinical nursing assessments.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms

The development of an adverse drug reaction stems from diverse biological pathways within the human body. Understanding these mechanisms helps explain why certain patients react adversely while others tolerate the same regimen.

  • Pharmacodynamic variability: Altered target receptor sensitivity can cause exaggerated responses even when drug concentration in the blood is within normal therapeutic limits.
  • Pharmacokinetic alterations: Changes in drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion lead to drug accumulation and toxicity.
  • Immunological hypersensitivity: The drug or its metabolite acts as an antigen or hapten, triggering an immune cascade ranging from mild rashes to life-threatening anaphylaxis.
  • Idiosyncrasy: Genetically determined abnormal reactivity to a chemical substance, often linked to specific enzymatic deficiencies like glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency.

Patient Risk Factors and Susceptibility

Certain individuals carry a much higher vulnerability to experiencing adverse drug reactions. Nurses must identify these high-risk profiles during admission assessments.

Risk FactorClinical Implication
Extremes of ageNeonates and older adults have immature or declining hepatic and renal clearance pathways.
PolypharmacyConcurrent use of multiple medications exponentially increases the likelihood of drug-drug interactions.
Organ impairmentPre-existing renal or liver disease delays drug elimination, causing systemic accumulation.
GeneticsVariations in cytochrome P450 enzymes affect how fast or slow drugs are metabolized.

In busy clinical wards across Nigeria, self-medication and the concurrent use of herbal preparations alongside orthodox prescriptions further complicate these risk profiles, raising the incidence of unexpected toxicities.

Clinical Assessment and Identification in Nursing Practice

Spotting an adverse drug reaction requires continuous vigilance and astute clinical observation. Nurses are often the first healthcare professionals to notice subtle physiological shifts in hospitalized patients.

Routine assessment involves monitoring vital signs, checking laboratory values such as serum creatinine and liver enzymes, and inspecting the skin for rashes or jaundice. Nurses must actively ask patients how they feel after starting a new medication rather than waiting for complaints to arise. A thorough medication history should capture prescription drugs, over-the-counter remedies, and traditional herbal supplements. Mistaking an ADR for a new disease pathology can lead to the dangerous practice of prescribing additional drugs to treat symptoms caused by the initial medication, known as a prescribing cascade.

Management, Treatment, and Prevention

When an adverse drug reaction is identified, swift action is required to minimize patient harm and prevent permanent injury.

  1. Discontinuation: Stop the offending drug immediately unless the reaction is mild and the medication is critical for survival, in which case a dose reduction may be considered.
  2. Supportive Care: Manage acute symptoms, such as administering epinephrine and antihistamines for anaphylaxis or providing intravenous fluids for severe hypotension.
  3. Antidote Administration: Administer specific reversing agents where applicable, such as naloxone for opioid overdose.
  4. Documentation: Clearly record the reaction in the patient's chart, specifying the exact drug and clinical manifestation to prevent future re-exposure.

Prevention relies on rigorous pre-administration checks, patient education regarding warning signs, and careful dose adjustments based on individual organ function.

Pharmacovigilance and Reporting Systems in Nigeria

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problems. In Nigeria, the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) serves as the principal regulatory body overseeing drug safety.

Healthcare professionals use the Yellow Card Scheme to report suspected adverse drug reactions and product quality issues. This spontaneous reporting system allows regulatory authorities to track safety signals, issue public warnings, and withdraw unsafe products from circulation. Nurses play a frontline role in pharmacovigilance by completing and submitting Yellow Cards whenever an unusual or severe drug reaction is observed in clinical practice, thereby contributing to national public health safety.
