Adverse Drug Reactions

Introduction and Definition of Adverse Drug Reactions

An Adverse Drug Reaction (ADR) is defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a noxious and unintended response to a medicine which occurs at doses normally used in humans for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy of disease, or for the modification of physiological function. Unlike side effects, which are often predictable and may be secondary or beneficial depending on the clinical context, ADRs are inherently harmful and undesirable. Understanding ADRs is a fundamental competency for nursing students and professional nurses, as they are frequently on the frontline of medication administration and patient monitoring. Nurses must distinguish between true ADRs, medication errors, therapeutic failures, and intentional overdoses. Recognizing an ADR early can prevent patient morbidity, reduce prolonged hospital stays, and save lives. In the Nigerian healthcare setting, identifying and reporting these unintended responses contributes significantly to national pharmacovigilance data, ensuring that medications circulating in the market are continuously monitored for safety and efficacy. By maintaining vigilance during routine vital sign checks, laboratory parameter reviews, and patient interviews, nurses act as the ultimate safety net for patients receiving pharmacotherapy.

Classification of Adverse Drug Reactions (Rawlins-Thompson Classification)

The Rawlins-Thompson classification is a widely accepted system that categorizes Adverse Drug Reactions into two main types: Type A (Augmented) and Type B (Bizarre). Type A reactions are dose-dependent, predictable based on the known pharmacologic properties of the drug, and generally have a high morbidity but low mortality rate. Examples include hypoglycemia caused by insulin or excessive bleeding from warfarin therapy. Managing Type A reactions typically involves reducing the drug dosage or withholding the medication temporarily. Conversely, Type B reactions are dose-independent, completely unpredictable, bizarre, and unrelated to the drug's normal pharmacological action. They usually occur in susceptible individuals, are mediated by immunologic or genetic mechanisms, and carry a high mortality rate, such as severe anaphylactic shock to penicillin or Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Management of Type B reactions requires immediate discontinuation of the offending drug and strict lifelong avoidance. In addition to Types A and B, expanded classifications include Type C (Chronic effects related to cumulative dose), Type D (Delayed effects appearing long after cessation), Type E (End-of-treatment withdrawal effects), and Type F (Failure of therapy). For nursing students, mastering this classification framework is essential for anticipating adverse events and planning appropriate interventions.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms of ADRs

The pathophysiological mechanisms driving Adverse Drug Reactions vary widely and explain why certain patients experience toxic or allergic responses. Pharmacodynamic mechanisms occur when a drug produces exaggerated effects at its receptor site, either in the target tissue or an off-target organ, such as hypotension caused by antihypertensive agents. Pharmacokinetic mechanisms involve alterations in the absorption, distribution, metabolism, or excretion (ADME) of a drug, frequently leading to drug accumulation and toxicity. For example, hepatic impairment or renal failure can drastically slow drug clearance, turning standard therapeutic doses into toxic ones. Immunological (hypersensitivity) mechanisms involve the immune system reacting to a drug or its metabolite acting as a hapten, precipitating Gell and Coombs types I through IV allergic reactions ranging from mild rashes to life-threatening anaphylaxis. Idiosyncratic mechanisms are genetically determined, abnormal reactivities to a chemical, such as hemolytic anemia in patients with Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency exposed to certain antimalarial drugs like primaquine. Understanding these underlying processes helps nursing students connect pharmacology to pathophysiology, enabling them to anticipate organ-specific toxicities during patient rounds and clinical placements.

Patient Risk Factors and Susceptibility

Patient risk factors and individual susceptibility play a massive role in determining who will experience an Adverse Drug Reaction. Age is a critical determinant: neonates have immature hepatic and renal clearance pathways, while older adults experience physiological declines in glomerular filtration rate, hepatic blood flow, and plasma protein binding, predisposing them to polypharmacy-related ADRs. Gender can also influence drug metabolism due to differences in body composition and enzyme activity. Genetic polymorphisms in cytochrome P450 enzymes can categorize patients into poor, intermediate, extensive, or ultrarapid metabolizers, radically altering how drugs are processed. Comorbidities such as kidney disease, liver dysfunction, and cardiovascular disorders significantly impair drug elimination or increase target-organ vulnerability. Furthermore, a history of prior allergies or multiple drug hypersensitivities increases the statistical likelihood of future immune-mediated reactions. Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—exponentially raises the risk of drug-drug interactions, compliance errors, and cumulative toxicity. Nurses must perform thorough holistic assessments, taking comprehensive medication histories that include herbal and traditional remedies, which are widely utilized in Nigeria, to identify patients at highest risk.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of ADRs in Nursing Practice

Clinical assessment and identification of ADRs require sharp observational skills and systematic evaluation by the nurse. Because ADRs can mimic common disease symptoms—such as fatigue, nausea, rash, confusion, or diarrhea—they are frequently misdiagnosed as worsening of the primary pathology, leading to the prescribing cascade where a new drug is added to treat the ADR. Nurses must establish baseline clinical parameters for every patient before drug therapy begins, paying close attention to vital signs, lab results (such as serum creatinine, liver enzymes, and complete blood counts), and general behavioral status. When assessing a suspected ADR, the nurse should utilize clinical causality assessment tools, evaluate the temporal relationship between drug administration and symptom onset, note whether symptoms resolve upon drug discontinuation (dechallenge), and observe if symptoms return upon re-administration (rechallenge, when safe). Nurses should also actively encourage patients to report unusual physical or psychological changes. In busy Nigerian hospital wards, where nurse-to-patient ratios can be high, remaining vigilant to subtle shifts in patient stability is vital for catching adverse drug events before they escalate into life-threatening emergencies.

Management, Treatment, and Prevention of ADRs

Management, treatment, and prevention of ADRs form a core responsibility of the interprofessional healthcare team, with nurses playing a central coordination role. Once an ADR is identified, immediate management typically involves stopping or withholding the offending medication, notifying the prescribing physician, and providing supportive care tailored to the severity of the reaction. For mild reactions like localized rashes, antihistamines or topical agents may suffice, whereas severe reactions like anaphylaxis require emergency resuscitation protocols, including airway maintenance, intravenous fluids, and epinephrine administration. Prevention strategies are equally vital and include conducting thorough pre-administration allergy screenings, verifying patient identity, cross-checking medication orders, calculating weight-based dosages accurately (especially in pediatric and geriatric care), and educating patients about warning signs to watch for at home. Nurses must also reconcile medications across care transitions—such as admission, transfer, and discharge—to eliminate discrepancies and prevent duplicate therapies. By implementing these rigorous safety protocols, nursing professionals actively minimize patient harm and foster a culture of safety within healthcare institutions across Nigeria.

Pharmacovigilance and Reporting Systems in Nigeria (NAFDAC)

Pharmacovigilance is the science and activities relating to the detection, assessment, understanding, and prevention of adverse effects or any other drug-related problems. In Nigeria, the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) is the regulatory body responsible for safeguarding public health through rigorous post-marketing surveillance of medicines, vaccines, and medical devices. A cornerstone of NAFDAC's pharmacovigilance framework is the spontaneous reporting system utilizing the 'Yellow Form' (Pharmacovigilance Adverse Drug Reaction Reporting Form). Healthcare professionals, including nurses, doctors, and pharmacists, are ethically and professionally obligated to report suspected ADRs using these yellow forms or via electronic reporting platforms. Spontaneous reporting helps capture rare, delayed, or unknown adverse reactions that could not be identified during pre-market clinical trials due to limited sample sizes. Nursing students must recognize that adverse event reporting is not about assigning blame or fault, but about generating vital epidemiological data that can lead to label updates, safety warnings, or even the withdrawal of unsafe pharmaceutical products, thereby protecting the entire Nigerian population.
