Adverse Drug Reactions

Introduction and Definition of Adverse Drug Reactions

An adverse drug reaction (ADR) is any noxious, unintended, and unwanted response that occurs at normal doses used for prophylaxis, diagnosis, or therapy. Unlike a side effect—which is often an anticipated, secondary pharmacological action—an ADR is typically harmful and unexpected. Every drug carries some risk of provoking an ADR, ranging from mild skin rashes to life-threatening organ failure. Nurses must maintain a high level of vigilance during medication administration because distinguishing between a normal therapeutic response and the early warning signs of an adverse reaction can prevent severe patient harm.

Classification of Adverse Drug Reactions (Rawlins-Thompson Classification)

The Rawlins-Thompson classification divides adverse drug reactions into two main types based on their predictability and dose-dependence.

  • Type A (Augmented): These reactions are predictable, dose-dependent, and stem from an exaggerated pharmacological effect of the drug. Examples include excessive bleeding from warfarin or hypoglycemia from insulin. They are very common and usually manageable by reducing the dose.
  • Type B (Bizarre): These reactions are unpredictable, dose-independent, and idiosyncratic. They do not relate to the drug's known pharmacology and often involve immune-mediated hypersensitivity, such as anaphylaxis to penicillin. They are less common but frequently severe.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms of ADRs

Understanding how ADRs develop at the cellular and tissue level helps explain why patients react differently to medications. Mechanisms range from simple pharmacokinetic alterations—where impaired liver or kidney function leads to drug accumulation—to complex immunological hypersensitivity. Type I reactions involve IgE-mediated mast cell degranulation leading to bronchospasm and urticaria, whereas Type IV reactions involve delayed cell-mediated immune responses like severe contact dermatitis. Other reactions stem from off-target binding, where a drug accidentally attaches to unintended cellular receptors in different tissues.

Patient Risk Factors and Susceptibility

Not all patients face the same level of risk when taking medications. Several host factors increase vulnerability to adverse drug reactions:

  • Age: Neonates and older adults have altered drug metabolism and excretion capabilities.
  • Genetics: Variations in liver enzyme genes can make a patient a poor or ultra-rapid metabolizer.
  • Comorbidities: Kidney and liver diseases impair drug clearance, while multiple illnesses lead to polypharmacy.
  • Polypharmacy: Taking five or more medications drastically raises the chance of harmful drug-drug interactions.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of ADRs in Nursing Practice

Nurses occupy the front line of defense in spotting adverse drug reactions. Assessment begins with a thorough medication history, including prescription drugs, over-the-counter remedies, and local herbal preparations. During daily care, nurses must observe for subtle shifts in vital signs, new skin rashes, changes in mental status, or sudden laboratory abnormalities. A common clinical mistake is assuming a new symptom is a new disease process rather than an adverse reaction to a recently started medication. Regular head-to-toe assessments and open communication with patients remain essential tools for early identification.

Management, Treatment, and Prevention of ADRs

When an adverse drug reaction is identified, immediate action is required to ensure patient safety. The first step usually involves stopping the offending medication and notifying the prescriber. Supportive care must be initiated promptly, ranging from antihistamines for mild rashes to emergency resuscitation with epinephrine and airway management for severe anaphylaxis. Prevention relies on careful reconciliation of medication charts, checking for known allergies before administration, and educating patients to report unusual body changes right away.

Pharmacovigilance and Reporting Systems in Nigeria (NAFDAC)

Pharmacovigilance is the science and practice of detecting, assessing, understanding, and preventing adverse effects or any other drug-related problem. In Nigeria, the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) oversees this crucial safety monitoring. Healthcare professionals use the NAFDAC Yellow Form to report suspected adverse drug reactions. Spontaneous reporting helps regulatory bodies track rare safety issues that clinical trials missed, ensuring safer medication use for the entire population.
