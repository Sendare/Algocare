Costing Under Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction to the Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) Model

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-financing revolving fund scheme widely adopted in healthcare institutions, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria, to ensure continuous availability and accessibility of essential medicines. Established initially with seed capital from government grants, donor agencies, or institutional funds, the core mechanism of the DRF relies on using revenue generated from the sale of drugs to continuously procure new stock. When patients pay for medications dispensed through the hospital pharmacy, the cash collected is looped back into the revolving account to purchase subsequent inventories, thereby eliminating the delays and bureaucratic bottlenecks associated with traditional government procurement cycles. For nursing students and healthcare administrators, understanding the DRF model is vital because medicine stockouts directly compromise patient safety, disrupt treatment plans, and undermine public trust in health facilities. Effective management of the DRF prevents out-of-stock syndromes, guarantees rational drug use, and maintains a stable supply chain without requiring constant, unpredictable capital injection from the central government. However, the system's success heavily depends on precise pricing, stringent financial discipline, and accurate costing methodologies to ensure that the fund does not shrink due to inflation, pilferage, or uncollected credits.

Basic Principles of Drug Costing in Pharmacy Practice

Basic principles of drug costing in pharmacy practice form the bedrock of financial sustainability for any Drug Revolving Fund scheme. Drug costing goes far beyond simply looking at the invoice price printed by the manufacturer or the wholesale vendor. In a functional DRF, costing involves a comprehensive accounting approach that determines the true, total financial outlay required to make a specific pharmaceutical product available to the end-user at the hospital dispensary counter. Nursing and healthcare management students must appreciate that ignoring hidden or incidental expenses during costing can lead to underpricing, which subsequently erodes the capital base and causes the revolving fund to collapse. Costing principles dictate that every package of medicine must carry its fair share of operational expenses, wastage allowances, and inflation buffers. Furthermore, transparency and consistency in applying costing formulas prevent corruption, reduce financial leakages, and foster accountability among pharmacy personnel and hospital administrators. By mastering basic drug costing, healthcare professionals can balance the dual mandate of the DRF: maintaining affordability for vulnerable patient populations while generating enough revenue to replenish inventory without fail.

Components of Drug Costing: Direct and Indirect Costs

To accurately price medications in a Drug Revolving Fund, one must clearly differentiate between direct and indirect costs. Direct costs are expenses that can be unequivocally and easily traced to a specific unit of a purchased drug. The primary direct cost is the Free-On-Board (FOB) or purchase price paid to the manufacturer or supplier for the pharmaceutical product itself, alongside specific shipping or freight charges tied exclusively to that batch of medicines. Conversely, indirect costs—often referred to as overhead or operational costs—cannot be directly linked to a single drug item but are essential for the overall operation of the pharmacy supply chain. Examples of indirect costs include warehousing utilities, storage facility rent, pharmacy personnel salaries, administrative stationery, computer software for inventory tracking, security, and losses due to expired or damaged stock. When calculating the final dispensing price, a standardized formula must apportion these indirect costs across all drugs based on volume, value, or turnover rate. Failing to account for indirect costs means the hospital absorbs these losses, which steadily drains the capital pool and threatens the long-term viability of the revolving fund. Nursing students should recognize that efficient management of indirect costs directly protects the financial health of clinical supply chains.

Calculating Procurement and Logistics Expenses

Procurement and logistics expenses represent a significant portion of the total landed cost of pharmaceutical products in any health system. When drugs are purchased, the financial commitment does not stop at the manufacturer's gate; administrators must factor in expenses related to transport, freight forwarding, port clearance, customs duties, quality control laboratory testing, insurance during transit, and local warehousing distribution. Logistics also involves warehousing management, inventory control systems, and secure transport to peripheral clinics and hospital wards. For instance, cold-chain products like vaccines and insulin incur heavy logistical costs due to the necessity of continuous refrigeration, specialized packaging, and uninterrupted power supply backups (such as generators and solar panels). If these logistical expenses are omitted from the drug costing formula, the revenue generated from sales will fall short of what is required to re-order the same volume of goods, especially when dealing with fragile or temperature-sensitive supplies. Therefore, a realistic percentage or flat fee representing procurement and logistics overhead must be added to every drug's base cost. Understanding this helps nursing administrators appreciate why certain essential medicines may experience slight price adjustments following changes in transport or fuel costs.

Establishing the Mark-Up Percentage and Sustainability Margins

Establishing the correct mark-up percentage and sustainability margins is the most critical policy decision in managing a Drug Revolving Fund. The mark-up is the percentage added to the total landed cost of a drug to arrive at the final patient-selling price. This margin must be carefully calculated to cover all indirect operational costs, absorb inventory shrinkage (such as losses from expired, damaged, or pilfered drugs), account for inflation, and yield a modest surplus to cushion the fund against market shocks. Unlike commercial retail pharmacies that aim for high profit margins to enrich owners, a DRF mark-up is strictly non-profit or not-for-loss; any surplus generated is retained entirely within the fund to expand the drug list or cushion future price hikes. Setting the mark-up too low leads to rapid capital depletion and stockouts, while setting it too high defeats the primary social objective of the DRF by making essential medicines unaffordable for low-income patients. Healthcare managers and nursing leaders must collaborate with pharmacists to conduct regular financial reviews, ensuring that mark-up percentages are dynamic, evidence-based, and tailored to different categories of drugs (e.g., lower margins for life-saving chronic disease medications and slightly higher margins for fast-moving analgesics or consumables).

Pricing Strategies for Equity and Affordability

Balancing financial sustainability with equity and affordability is one of the greatest challenges in managing a Drug Revolving Fund in developing health systems. Healthcare is a fundamental human right, yet DRFs must generate enough internal revenue to survive. To resolve this tension, pricing strategies must incorporate equity-driven models such as cross-subsidization and tiered pricing. Cross-subsidization involves applying a slightly higher mark-up on fast-moving, non-critical, or elective items (such as vitamins, cosmetics, and common analgesics purchased by patients who can afford them) while maintaining very low, highly subsidized mark-ups on life-saving essential medicines (such as anti-hypertensives, insulin, and pediatric antibiotics). Additionally, vulnerable groups—such as pregnant women, neonates, the elderly, and indigents—may benefit from special exemption programs or insurance integration (like the Basic Healthcare Provision Fund or State Health Insurance Schemes in Nigeria) where the DRF is reimbursed by third-party payers. Nursing professionals play a pivotal role here by identifying vulnerable patients who qualify for social safety nets and ensuring that pricing policies do not inadvertently force impoverished families to abandon necessary medical treatment due to out-of-pocket costs.

Managing Inflation, Exchange Rates, and Market Fluctuations

Operating a Drug Revolving Fund in volatile economic environments requires robust strategies to manage inflation, severe currency devaluation, and fluctuating market prices. In many developing economies, the depreciation of local currency against major international currencies (such as the US Dollar or Euro) heavily impacts the cost of imported active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs) and finished drug products. When exchange rates spike, the initial seed capital of the DRF rapidly loses purchasing power, making it impossible to restock the same quantity of medicines with previously collected cash. To mitigate this, DRF managers must implement dynamic pricing models, maintain buffer stocks of fast-moving items, and establish framework contracts with reliable local and international manufacturers that lock in prices for specific periods. Furthermore, establishing a dedicated inflation and exchange rate fluctuation reserve fund—carved out as a small percentage of past revenues—can provide an emergency cushion when macroeconomic shocks occur. Nursing leaders and healthcare administrators must understand these fiscal realities so they can advocate for prompt tariff adjustments and prevent sudden, crippling stockouts caused by external economic downturns.

Financial Monitoring, Auditing, and Preventing Stock Depletion

Financial monitoring, rigorous auditing, and proactive inventory management are the ultimate safeguards against the collapse and stock depletion of a Drug Revolving Fund. Because a DRF handles substantial amounts of cash and high-value medical commodities, it is highly susceptible to financial leakages, pilferage, poor record-keeping, and unrecovered credit sales. Continuous financial monitoring involves daily reconciliation of sales cash with dispensed prescriptions, tracking stock turnover rates, and identifying slow-moving or obsolete inventory before expiration dates pass. Regular internal and external audits ensure transparency, verify financial statements, and deter fraudulent activities among storekeepers, cashiers, and dispensing personnel. Additionally, robust inventory control systems—such as the implementation of computerized Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) software or standardized stock cards—help maintain optimal stock levels, preventing both overstocking (which ties up cash and risks expiration) and understocking (which leads to stockouts). Nursing personnel, who often oversee ward stocks and emergency trolleys, play an essential role in this monitoring chain by practicing accurate documentation, reporting near-expiry items promptly, and ensuring that all dispensed items are properly accounted for.
