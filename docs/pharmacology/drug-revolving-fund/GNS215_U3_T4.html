Costing Under Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction to the Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) Model

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-sustaining financing mechanism widely used in public healthcare institutions to ensure a steady, reliable supply of essential medicines. When a hospital or clinic runs a DRF, the money generated from selling drugs to patients is not returned to a general government purse. Instead, that money goes right back into buying new stock. This creates a closed loop where inventory constantly renews itself without needing fresh cash injections from the government every single time.

At its core, the system relies entirely on cash discipline. If drugs are given out on credit too often, or if the revenue is diverted to pay for unrelated hospital bills, the account runs dry. Soon, the shelves empty out, and patients are forced to buy their medicines from expensive private vendors outside. Understanding how the DRF works helps nurses appreciate why accountability at the dispensary counter protects the whole hospital ecosystem.

Basic Principles of Drug Costing in Pharmacy Practice

Drug costing is the careful process of determining the exact financial value of a pharmaceutical product from the manufacturer to the patient's hand. In a DRF scheme, guessing prices or copying local market rates blindly will cause the fund to collapse. Costing must be systematic, accurate, and completely transparent.

Two major principles guide this process:

  • Full Cost Recovery: The price charged must cover not just the raw purchase price of the medicine, but every single operational expense incurred to bring that medicine to the patient.
  • Affordability Balance: While the fund must recover its costs, prices should not become so high that vulnerable patients stop seeking care at the facility.

Components of Drug Costing: Direct and Indirect Costs

To price a drug correctly, you must account for two main categories of expenses. Ignoring either category leads to hidden losses that slowly drain the revolving fund.

  • Direct Costs: These are expenses tied directly to acquiring the specific pharmaceutical product. They include the invoice price paid to the manufacturer or wholesale supplier, import duties, and specific freight or shipping charges for that batch of medicines.
  • Indirect Costs: These are overhead expenses required to keep the pharmacy running, even if they do not attach to a single pill. They include storage facility rent, electricity for cold chain refrigerators, salaries for pharmacy technicians, dispensing tools, and packaging materials like pill envelopes.

Calculating Procurement and Logistics Expenses

Getting drugs from a manufacturing plant to a rural health clinic involves many invisible steps, each carrying a price tag. Procurement and logistics expenses often make up a significant portion of the true cost of a medicine.

When calculating these logistics costs, pharmacy managers must factor in:

  • Transport and freight charges across rough terrain
  • Insurance coverage against theft or damage in transit
  • Customs clearance fees and port handling charges for imported goods
  • Warehousing costs, including pest control and security
  • Cold chain maintenance, such as diesel for generators powering vaccine refrigerators

Establishing the Mark-Up Percentage and Sustainability Margins

Once all direct and indirect costs are added together, a mark-up percentage is applied to determine the final selling price to the patient. This mark-up is not a profit margin in the commercial business sense; rather, it is a sustainability margin designed to protect the fund against future financial shocks.

If the mark-up is set too low, inflation will quickly eat away at the capital, and the fund will soon be unable to buy replacement stock. If the mark-up is set too high, patients will complain of exploitation. A standard sustainability margin usually accounts for three specific cushions:

  • Percentage loss from expired, damaged, or broken drugs
  • Allowance for expected inflation between purchase cycles
  • A small growth buffer to expand the drug list over time

Managing Inflation, Exchange Rates, and Market Fluctuations

Running a DRF in a developing economy means constantly fighting macroeconomic instability. When local currency loses value against foreign currencies, imported pharmaceuticals instantly become more expensive. If a drug was bought last month for one thousand naira, replacing it today might require fifteen hundred naira due to currency devaluation.

To survive these market shocks, pharmacy management committees must adopt proactive pricing strategies. Waiting until stock runs out before recalculating prices guarantees failure. Instead, pricing models should use replacement cost pricing—meaning goods are priced based on what it will cost to buy them *tomorrow*, not what they cost yesterday. Additionally, maintaining a portion of the fund in stable currency accounts or fast-moving inventory helps shield the system from sudden economic downturns.

Financial Monitoring, Auditing, and Preventing Stock Depletion

A healthy DRF requires rigorous financial tracking. Without regular audits and strict inventory control, funds can slowly leak away through pilferage, uncollected debts, and unaccounted expired goods.

Nurses and healthcare workers often serve as the first line of observation. When nurses report unusual dispensing patterns, missing prescription logs, or frequent stock-outs of basic items, management can intervene before the revolving fund completely collapses.
