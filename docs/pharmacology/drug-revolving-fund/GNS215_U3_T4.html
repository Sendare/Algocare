Costing Under Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction to the Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) Model

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-sustaining financing mechanism designed to guarantee a continuous supply of essential medicines in public health institutions. Launched widely under primary healthcare reforms in developing nations, the core concept relies on a closed financial loop. Patients pay a subsidized fee for medicines, and the revenue generated is used exclusively to procure fresh stock, preventing the perennial stockouts typical of centrally budgeted pharmaceutical stores.

In Nigerian tertiary and secondary health facilities, the DRF operates as a shield against erratic government budgetary releases. When properly managed, it insulates essential services from bureaucratic procurement delays. However, its survival depends heavily on strict discipline: capital must not be diverted to pay non-pharmaceutical overheads, and dispensing without recovery must be tightly regulated through targeted exemption policies rather than ad-hoc giveaways.

Basic Principles of Drug Costing in Pharmacy Practice

Accurate drug costing forms the bedrock of any functional revolving fund. Without knowing the true expense incurred to land a product on the pharmacy shelf, pricing becomes guesswork, leading either to rapid capital erosion or extortionate pricing for vulnerable patients. Costing begins at the manufacturer's invoice price and accumulates every legitimate financial burden required to make the medicine available for administration.

In public sector pharmacy practice, costing must balance commercial realism with social responsibility. A common pitfall in resource-limited settings is pricing medications solely on the initial unit purchase price while ignoring ancillary expenditures like port clearance, warehousing, and transportation. When these hidden drains are unaccounted for, the revolving fund slowly bleeds capital with every replenishment cycle until outright insolvency occurs.

Components of Drug Costing: Direct and Indirect Costs

To price pharmaceuticals accurately within a revolving scheme, expenses must be neatly categorized into direct and indirect elements.

  • Direct Costs: Expenses that can be unequivocally traced to a specific pharmaceutical product. This includes the Free-On-Board (FOB) price, manufacturer unit cost, specific batch testing fees, and direct freight charges.
  • Indirect Costs: Overhead expenditures that support the entire pharmacy supply chain but cannot be easily assigned to a single bottle of syrup or strip of tablets. Examples include central warehouse utility bills, security personnel wages, dispensing equipment depreciation, and inventory software licenses.

Calculating Procurement and Logistics Expenses

The landed cost of a pharmaceutical product extends far beyond the factory gates. Procurement and logistics expenses—often termed cost-in-use or landing costs—include international shipping, marine insurance, clearing charges at sea or airports, regulatory inspection fees by agencies like NAFDAC, and internal haulage to regional health facilities.

When calculating these expenses for pricing, management must factor in wastage and pilferage allowances. Products break, expire in transit, or disappear from warehouses. Adding a standard percentage buffer (typically 2 to 5 percent depending on local security and road network realities) for transit losses ensures the revolving fund remains mathematically whole even when logistical mishaps occur.

Establishing the Mark-Up Percentage and Sustainability Margins

Once the total landed cost of a medicine is established, a sustainability mark-up is added to determine the final dispensing price. This mark-up is not a commercial profit intended to enrich the institution; rather, it is a calculated survival buffer. It must cover inflation, minor currency devaluation, operational overheads, and the replacement cost differential between old stock and incoming batches.

Setting this percentage requires delicate stakeholder negotiation. If the mark-up is set too low, inflation rapidly outpaces the fund, and the pharmacy cannot purchase replacement stock. If set too exorbitantly high, patients abandon public facilities for unregulated patent medicine vendors, defeating the social protection mandate of the hospital. Most successful public health frameworks maintain dynamic mark-ups ranging between 15 and 30 percent, adjusted periodically against macroeconomic indicators.

Pricing Strategies for Equity and Affordability

A recurring ethical dilemma in public sector drug revolving funds is balancing financial self-sufficiency with healthcare equity. Vulnerable populations—such as pregnant women, pediatric patients, and the elderly—often lack the purchasing power to afford market-reflective medicine prices. Standard flat mark-ups can inadvertently price the poorest patients out of essential care.

To resolve this, sophisticated DRF models employ tiered pricing or cross-subsidization strategies. High-volume, fast-moving items or chronic maintenance drugs demanded by wealthier demographics may carry a slightly higher sustainable margin, while life-saving emergency commodities are subsidized. Furthermore, integration with formal health insurance schemes ensures that indigents have their drug costs absorbed by pooled risk funds rather than fracturing the DRF capital base.

Managing Inflation, Exchange Rates, and Market Fluctuations

Macroeconomic instability poses the most severe threat to drug revolving funds in developing economies. Because a significant proportion of active pharmaceutical ingredients and finished products are imported, sharp currency devaluations instantly inflate replacement costs. A fund with fixed pricing struggles immediately when the cost to re-order a consignment doubles overnight.

Defensive financial management requires continuous price reviews rather than rigid annual adjustments. Pharmacy managers must monitor foreign exchange trends and establish buffer stocks of fast-moving imported essentials before predicted currency depreciations. Additionally, prioritizing local manufacturers for stable, locally-produced generic formulations helps insulate the revolving fund from foreign exchange volatility.

Financial Monitoring, Auditing, and Preventing Stock Depletion

Sustainability requires rigorous financial oversight. Without continuous tracking of inventory turnover, cash flow, and accounts receivable, a DRF can experience silent depletion—where shelves appear stocked, but cash is tied up in slow-moving items or unpaid credit extensions, leaving the warehouse unable to place new orders.

Regular physical stock-counts reconciled with financial ledgers must be institutionalized. Automated inventory management systems help flag near-expiry items and dead stock before they become total financial losses. Furthermore, clear credit policies must prevent staff or emergency departments from taking unrecorded stock on credit without a guaranteed reimbursement mechanism from hospital administration.
