Pricing Under Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction to Pricing in the Drug Revolving Fund (DRF)

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a revolving drug supply system used in public health facilities to ensure a continuous, reliable availability of essential medicines. At its heart, the pricing model is designed to be self-sustaining. When patients pay for medications, the money collected is used strictly to procure a fresh batch of drugs, replacing what was consumed.

Unlike private pharmacies whose main goal is profit generation, a DRF operates on a cost-recovery basis. The price set for any drug must cover the initial purchase cost while also factoring in minimal operational overheads. If drugs are priced too high, vulnerable patients cannot afford them, defeating the public health mandate. If they are priced too low, the fund will fail to replace the stock, leading to a debilitating stock-out. Understanding this delicate financial balance is critical for healthcare managers and nurses involved in facility administration.

Core Objectives of the DRF Pricing Strategy

A well-structured DRF pricing policy is guided by specific public health goals rather than market speculation. These objectives work together to protect both the patient and the institution:

  • Affordability: Keeping essential medicines within the financial reach of low-income populations who rely on public hospitals.
  • Availability: Ensuring uninterrupted stock levels so patients do not have to resort to expensive, unregulated external vendors.
  • Sustainability: Protecting the capital base from inflation and currency devaluation to guarantee the long-term survival of the fund.
  • Equity: Providing uniform, transparent pricing across designated facilities so that vulnerable groups are not exploited.

When these objectives are successfully met, the health facility builds trust within the community and reduces out-of-pocket catastrophe spending for families.

Components of Drug Cost in Public Health Facilities

Setting a fair retail price requires a clear breakdown of all expenses incurred before a tablet reaches the patient's hand. Simply looking at the manufacturer's invoice price is insufficient.

Failing to account for hidden logistics expenses like transport and storage wastage will gradually erode the principal capital, leading to hidden losses even when drugs appear to be selling well.

Methodology for Calculating Mark-Ups and Retail Prices

Once the total landed cost of a medicine is established, a controlled mark-up percentage is applied to determine the final retail price paid by the patient. This mark-up is rarely arbitrary; it is usually guided by national or institutional policy guidelines.

The calculation generally follows a straightforward formula: the landed unit cost is multiplied by the approved percentage mark-up (designed to cover inflation, logistics, and wastage), and the resulting figure is added back to the base cost. For example, if an antimalarial drug lands at 100 Naira per pack and the institutional mark-up is set at 15 percent, the retail price becomes 115 Naira.

It is common practice to apply sliding scale mark-ups where fast-moving essential items have very low margins to encourage accessibility, while specialized or slow-moving items may carry slightly higher margins to absorb the risk of holding dead stock.

Balancing Affordability and Financial Sustainability

The central tension in managing a Drug Revolving Fund lies in the constant tug-of-war between social welfare and business realism. If prices are suppressed due to political pressure, the fund becomes insolvent. If prices match commercial market rates, poor patients are forced away.

To navigate this, facility managers use cross-subsidization strategies alongside tiered pricing structures. Basic pediatric antibiotics and oxytocin may be sold at near-zero markup, subsidized by modest margins on elective wellness supplies or chronic disease maintenance drugs where patients have more predictable purchasing patterns. Transparency with community representatives regarding why prices occasionally need upward adjustments helps maintain trust and prevents unrest during economic downturns.

Managing Price Inflation and Market Fluctuations

Economic instability, currency depreciation, and global supply chain disruptions pose a constant threat to revolving funds. When wholesale prices double overnight, a static mark-up model will quickly fail because the revenue collected from selling old stock will not buy half of the original quantity.

To survive inflation, progressive DRF committees adopt dynamic pricing mechanisms and replacement-cost accounting. Instead of pricing drugs based on historical purchase costs, pricing is pegged to the current replacement cost. Furthermore, maintaining a buffer stock and diversifying procurement sources helps shield the facility from sudden vendor price gouging. Nurses and pharmacy managers must understand that timely price adjustments, though uncomfortable for patients, are often the only safeguard against total system collapse during hyperinflationary periods.

Monitoring, Transparency, and Accountability in DRF Pricing

Because cash and sensitive medical commodities are involved, the DRF is vulnerable to mismanagement, pilferage, and undocumented price inflation. Establishing rigorous oversight mechanisms is non-negotiable for public trust.

Regular internal audits, public price boards displayed clearly at dispensary units, and automated electronic inventory tracking help prevent unauthorized price mark-ups by corrupt staff. Accountability also means involving hospital management committees and community stakeholders in reviewing financial reports. When patients see that prices are fair, transparently calculated, and directly tied to keeping shelves stocked with quality medicines, compliance and institutional trust improve significantly.
