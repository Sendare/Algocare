Sources of Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction to the Drug Revolving Fund (DRF)

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-sustaining financing mechanism designed to guarantee a continuous supply of essential medicines in healthcare institutions. Without a reliable funding buffer, public hospitals frequently experience severe stockouts, forcing patients to purchase medications from expensive proprietary chemist shops. The core mechanism relies on a cyclical process: initial capital purchases medicines, sales generate revenue, and that revenue is strictly reinvested to procure subsequent stock. In many Nigerian tertiary and secondary health facilities, the DRF operates as an autonomous fund separate from the general overhead account, shielding drug procurement funds from bureaucratic diversion.

Initial Seed Capital and Government Grants

Every functional DRF requires an initial injection of financial resources to purchase the first batch of bulk pharmaceuticals. This starting money is known as seed capital. Governments at federal, state, or local levels, alongside interventionist public health programs, typically provide these initial grants. Once this first inventory is sold to patients, the cash collected forms the baseline liquidity needed to place the next purchase order, effectively launching the revolving cycle without requiring continuous treasury funding.

Patient Out-of-Pocket Payments and User Fees

Direct cash payments made by patients at hospital pharmacies constitute the largest operational source of revenue for most facility-based DRFs. When patients purchase prescribed drugs, the money collected at the point of dispensing immediately flows back into the fund pool. To keep medicines accessible while maintaining the fund, drugs are typically priced at affordable wholesale-plus markup rates rather than exploitative market rates. However, heavy reliance on out-of-pocket user fees can create barriers for vulnerable populations if exemption mechanisms are not deliberately integrated.

Health Insurance Schemes and Managed Care

Modern healthcare financing increasingly incorporates health insurance, changing how DRFs collect revenue. Schemes such as the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) in Nigeria reimburse hospitals for medications dispensed to enrolled beneficiaries. Instead of collecting physical cash directly from every patient at the dispensary counter, the DRF receives structured claim payments from insurance third-party administrators. This mechanism stabilizes cash flow and reduces the financial shock of out-of-pocket spending, provided that claims are processed and paid promptly.

Donations, Partner Agencies, and Donor Funding

External assistance from developmental partners, non-governmental organizations, and international health agencies occasionally supplements DRF resources. This support often arrives in the form of disease-specific drug consignments (such as antiretrovirals or antimalarials) or monetary grants. While donor support expands access, relying on it long-term can be precarious because grants eventually phase out. Sustainable DRF management requires that external donations are integrated carefully without disrupting the core revolving procurement model.

Retained Earnings and Reinvested Surpluses

A healthy DRF generates a modest markup on drug prices above the direct procurement cost. This small margin covers operational overhead like shipping, storage waste, and minor losses, but it also produces a surplus. Retaining these earnings within the fund account allows the pool of capital to grow organically over time, absorbing inflation and accommodating the rising cost of pharmaceutical imports. Reinvested surpluses transform a stagnant fund into a growing economic asset for the hospital.

Sustaining the Revolving Cycle

Sustaining the DRF cycle requires rigorous inventory management, transparent accounting, and strict adherence to procurement protocols. If drugs expire on the shelf due to poor stock rotation, or if credit sales to staff and VIPs are left unpaid, the fund bleeds capital. Nurses and pharmacists play a vital frontline role by accurately tracking consumption, preventing wastage, and reporting inventory discrepancies early. Ultimately, the survival of the revolving cycle depends on balancing affordability for patients with disciplined financial governance.
