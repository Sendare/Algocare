Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction and Definition of the Drug Revolving Fund

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-sustaining financing mechanism designed to guarantee a steady, reliable supply of essential medicines within healthcare institutions. Initiated largely to combat the chronic scarcity of pharmaceuticals in public facilities, the scheme operates on a simple premise: money generated from the sale of drugs is used strictly to purchase more drugs, thereby creating an unbroken circular flow of capital and inventory.

In many Nigerian public hospitals, patients historically faced out-of-stock syndromes forcing them to purchase medications from expensive private chemist shops. The DRF bridges this gap by ensuring that affordable, high-quality pharmaceuticals are available directly within the hospital pharmacy. The fund starts with an initial seed capital—often provided by government grants, international donor agencies, or institutional allocations. Once operational, the capital transforms continuously between cash and drug stock, eliminating the bureaucratic bottlenecks of waiting for annual government budget releases to procure medical supplies.

Core Objectives and Principles of the Scheme

The operational framework of a successful DRF relies heavily on strict core principles designed to protect the capital base from depletion.

  • Affordability: Pricing structures must remain lower than private retail pharmacies while still covering replacement costs.
  • Availability: Essential medicines must remain in stock consistently, preventing emergency out-of-hospital purchases for vulnerable patients.
  • Accessibility: Drugs should be physically reachable within the clinical facility at the point of care.
  • Self-Sustainability: The fund must generate enough revenue to replenish stock without depending on endless external subsidies or government subventions.
  • Accountability: Transparent bookkeeping ensures that funds are never diverted to settle unrelated hospital debts or overhead expenses.

Structure and Operation of the Fund

A well-structured DRF operates through a multidisciplinary governance framework rather than a single individual's effort. Typically, a DRF Management Committee is constituted, comprising pharmacists, accountants, medical doctors, nursing representation, and internal auditors. This committee oversees pricing policies, reviews stock reports, and guards against financial leakages.

Operational workflows involve distinct stages: prescription issuance by clinicians, dispensing by pharmacists, cash collection or electronic payment processing, and inventory auditing. Separation of duties is vital. The personnel prescribing or dispensing drugs should ideally not be the sole custodians of physical cash. Regular reconciliation between pharmacy stock ledgers and bank accounts prevents discrepancies, pilferage, and unaccounted losses that frequently threaten the survival of revolving funds in public health institutions.

The Procurement and Supply Chain Process

The integrity of a drug revolving fund depends heavily on how medicines are procured and stored. Purchasing must rely strictly on an Essential Medicines List (EML) to avoid tying up capital in slow-moving or obsolete pharmaceutical brands. Bulk purchasing directly from reputable manufacturers or accredited wholesale distributors helps secure favorable unit prices.

Poor inventory management leads to expiration of drugs on shelves, which directly drains the revolving capital. Adequate stock control techniques, such as the First-Expire, First-Out (FEFO) rule, are non-negotiable daily practices in hospital pharmacies.

Financial Management, Pricing, and Sustainability

Sustaining a DRF requires deliberate pricing strategies. Drugs are typically sold at a markup percentage added to the direct cost of procurement. This markup covers operational losses, transport logistics, bank charges, and inflation, ensuring that the revenue generated can buy an identical or greater quantity of replacement stock.

A major threat to financial sustainability is credit sales and waivers. When political pressures or compassionate grounds force pharmacies to dispense drugs on credit to patients or staff without a reliable recovery plan, the fund bleeds cash. Once the cash pool shrinks below the threshold required to meet minimum order quantities, the revolving fund collapses into permanent stockouts.

Role of Nurses and Healthcare Professionals in DRF

Although pharmacists manage the physical inventory and financial accountants track the ledgers, nurses play an indispensable frontline role in protecting the DRF ecosystem. Nurses are often the first to notice shifting disease patterns or sudden surges in medication demand, providing vital feedback for stock quantification.

Furthermore, nurses promote rational drug use by educating patients on adherence, completing full courses of treatment, and preventing wastage through discarded medications. In clinical settings, accurate charting and timely prescription processing prevent delays that stall the turnover cycle. By advocating for prompt pharmacy billing and reporting damaged or near-expiry stock discovered in ward stock trays, nursing professionals actively safeguard institutional healthcare resources.

Challenges and Limitations in Implementation

Despite its noble design, the DRF model faces persistent operational hurdles, particularly in resource-constrained public health systems. Inflation and currency devaluation rapidly erode purchasing power; money recovered from sales often buys significantly fewer pharmaceutical units than it did during the previous purchase cycle.

Other notable bottlenecks include bureaucratic interference, pilferage, poor record-keeping, and the temptation by hospital managements to divert DRF funds to emergency overhead costs like fueling generators or paying utility bills. When capital is diverted, the revolving cycle breaks down, plunging the facility right back into the very drug scarcity crisis the fund was created to solve.

Strategies for Monitoring, Evaluation, and Improvement

To ensure long-term viability, a Drug Revolving Fund must undergo continuous assessment and performance tracking. Key performance indicators include stockout duration, inventory turnover rate, loss due to expiration, and capital recovery rate.

Routine supportive supervision, digital inventory management software, and unannounced physical stock audits help maintain accountability. Management must also engage in periodic price reviews to adjust markup percentages realistically against prevailing economic realities without pricing essential drugs out of the reach of impoverished patients. Continuous training of all personnel involved in the supply chain remains a cornerstone for institutionalizing best practices.
