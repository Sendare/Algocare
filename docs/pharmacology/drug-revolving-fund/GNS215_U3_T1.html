Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction and Definition of the Drug Revolving Fund

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a specialized financial system set up within healthcare facilities to ensure a steady, reliable supply of essential medicines. Instead of relying entirely on government budgets or donations, the DRF operates on a continuous cycle. Seed money is used to purchase an initial stock of drugs. When patients pay for these medicines, the money collected does not disappear into a general account. Instead, it is plowed right back into purchasing fresh stock. This creates a self-sustaining loop where drugs generate money, and that money buys more drugs.

In many public hospitals across West Africa, drug shortages historically crippled healthcare delivery. Patients were given prescriptions to buy medications at private chemist shops, often facing exorbitant prices and fake drugs. The DRF model was introduced to bridge this gap, ensuring that affordable, high-quality pharmaceuticals remain available directly inside the hospital pharmacy at all times.

Core Objectives and Principles of the Scheme

A successful DRF is guided by clear operational principles that protect both the patient and the institution. The overarching goal is equity and availability, meaning no patient should suffer complications simply because basic medications are out of reach.

  • Affordability: Drugs must be sold at prices lower than open-market retail rates while still covering replacement costs.
  • Availability: Essential medicines must be in stock consistently, preventing out-of-stock scenarios.
  • Accountability: Transparent record-keeping is mandatory so that cash and stock balances match at all times.
  • Quality Assurance: Only certified, safe pharmaceuticals from reputable manufacturers are procured, protecting patients from substandard products.

Structure and Operation of the Drug Revolving Fund

The machinery of a DRF requires distinct roles and coordination across hospital departments. At the apex is a management committee comprising pharmacists, accountants, clinicians, and nursing representatives who oversee policy and pricing.

The operational workflow moves from the central medical store down to the dispensing pharmacy units. When a patient receives a prescription, they pay into a designated DRF bank account or revenue point. The pharmacy dispenses the drug only upon proof of payment. Regular auditing of these transactions prevents inventory shrinkage and cash leakages. If the capital base is allowed to shrink through unpaid credits or freebies without replacement, the whole system collapses.

The Procurement and Supply Chain Process

Getting drugs from the manufacturer to the hospital shelf is a meticulous process. Procurement must follow strict public health guidelines, often favoring generic drugs purchased under their International Non-proprietary Name (INN) to keep costs low.

Bulk purchasing allows the hospital to negotiate volume discounts with accredited pharmaceutical manufacturers or distributors. Once delivered, items must undergo rigorous inspection for batch numbers, manufacturing dates, and expiration dates. Proper storage conditions—such as cold chain management for vaccines and insulin—are vital to prevent drug degradation before they ever reach the patient.

Financial Management and Sustainability

Financial discipline is the lifeblood of any Drug Revolving Fund. The pricing structure is usually determined by adding a small markup percentage to the cost-insurance-freight (CIF) price. This markup accounts for operational losses, inflation, and minor wastage, ensuring the capital base retains its purchasing power over time.

A common misconception is that the DRF should generate massive profits to fund general hospital overheads. Diverting DRF cash to pay electricity bills or repair ambulance vehicles rapidly depletes the pharmacy's capital, resulting in empty shelves. Ring-fencing DRF accounts ensures that pharmacy money stays in the pharmacy.

Role of Nurses and Healthcare Professionals in DRF

While pharmacists manage the procurement and dispensing of stock, nurses play a critical frontline role in supporting the DRF ecosystem. Nurses are often the first to notice when a specific medication is consistently unavailable, making their feedback vital for inventory planning.

Furthermore, nurses assist with inventory control in ward stock settings, ensuring that emergency drug trays are properly accounted for and billed. By educating patients on compliance and explaining why the hospital pharmacy offers reliable, affordable medications, nurses build confidence in the facility's DRF services.

Challenges and Limitations in Implementation

Despite its noble intent, many Drug Revolving Fund schemes struggle or collapse over time. Common obstacles include inflation, which erodes the purchasing power of the initial capital, making it harder to buy the same quantity of drugs later.

Other major threats include bureaucratic delays in procurement, pilferage, poor inventory record-keeping, and the accumulation of uncollected debts from patients or staff. When political interference forces hospitals to give out drugs on credit without a recovery plan, the revolving cycle breaks down permanently.

Strategies for Monitoring, Evaluation, and Improvement

To keep a Drug Revolving Fund healthy, continuous monitoring is non-negotiable. Management must conduct routine physical stock counts and reconcile them with financial ledger entries to catch discrepancies early.

Key performance indicators—such as stock turnover rate, lead time for deliveries, and the percentage of essential drugs available—help administrators gauge the health of the system. Periodic reviews of pricing structures also allow the hospital to adjust markups modestly in response to inflation, ensuring the fund remains viable without placing an unbearable burden on patients.
