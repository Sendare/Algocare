Role of the Nurse in Drug Revolving Fund

Understanding the Drug Revolving Fund (DRF)

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-sustaining financial and logistical system used in healthcare facilities to ensure a steady, uninterrupted supply of essential medicines. In a standard DRF model, money generated from the sale of drugs is used to buy new stock, making the fund 'revolve' continuously. When managed properly, the fund protects patients from catastrophic out-of-pocket expenses for external pharmacy runs and ensures that clinics never run dry of life-saving commodities. Nurses often sit at the frontline of this system, especially in primary and secondary health centres where dedicated pharmacy staff may be absent.

Objectives and Importance in Healthcare Delivery

  • Affordability: Providing drugs at prices lower than open commercial markets.
  • Availability: Eliminating stockouts of essential commodities like oxytocin, antimalarials, and antibiotics.
  • Quality Assurance: Sourcing medications from approved, verified manufacturers to curb counterfeit products.
  • Sustainability: Keeping the clinic functional without relying solely on unpredictable government subventions or donor funding.

Core Principles of DRF Management

A successful DRF depends on strict adherence to business and clinical discipline. The fund must be ring-fenced, meaning DRF money cannot be diverted to pay for generator repairs, diesel, or administrative travels. Cross-subsidization must be carefully controlled so that high-turnover drugs subsidize slow-moving life-saving items without collapsing the capital base. Transparency in pricing markup—usually a small, fixed percentage added to the procurement cost to cover logistics and minor losses—keeps drugs affordable while preserving the capital value against inflation.

Inventory Control and Preventing Wastage

Nurses managing ward stocks or mini-dispensaries must master basic inventory rules. The FIFO (First-In, First-Out) rule is non-negotiable: older batches must be placed in front and issued before newer shipments. Regular stock auditing prevents unaccounted shrinkage due to theft, misplacement, or spoilage. A common clinical error is hoarding drugs in ward cupboards 'just in case,' which leads to expiration before use while another ward faces a severe shortage.

Ensuring Rational Drug Dispensing and Patient Safety

Availability must never compromise safety. Nurses involved in dispensing must cross-check the prescription against the patient's diagnosis, allergies, and age. Clear labelling of instructions—specifying dosage, frequency, and route—prevents home medication errors. Furthermore, nurses educate patients on completing full courses of antibiotics, discouraging the dangerous habit of saving leftover pills for future minor illnesses.

Financial Accountability and Revenue Collection

Money collected from drug sales represents physical inventory turned into cash, and every kobo must be accounted for. Nurses handling cash sales must issue official receipts immediately and reconcile physical cash with stock depletion records at the end of every shift. Poor record-keeping or informal credit sales ('collect now, pay tomorrow') are the quickest ways to collapse a revolving fund through uncollected debts.

Overcoming Operational Challenges

Sustaining a DRF in resource-constrained settings requires anticipating common bottlenecks. Inflation quickly erodes purchasing power, meaning a budget that bought fifty malaria treatments last month might only buy forty today. To combat this, management must periodically review pricing in consultation with stakeholders. Staff training, reliable record ledgers, and supportive supervision help mitigate losses caused by human error, pilferage, or poor documentation.
