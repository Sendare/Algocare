Financial Control of Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction to the Drug Revolving Fund

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a self-financing revolving fund scheme established in public healthcare institutions to ensure a continuous and reliable supply of essential medicines. Initiated largely to combat frequent stockouts and out-of-stock syndromes in government hospitals across Nigeria, the model operates on a simple premise: drugs are sold to patients at affordable prices, and the revenue generated is recycled exclusively to procure fresh stock. Capital acts as a rotating loop rather than a static budget line. When financial control mechanisms fail, the fund suffers from rapid capital depletion, leading back to empty pharmacy shelves and compromised patient care.

Objectives and Principles of Financial Control

Financial control within the DRF framework relies on core accounting principles designed to protect capital integrity. The primary objective is ring-fencing—ensuring that funds generated from drug sales are strictly separated from the hospital's general overhead accounts and used solely for pharmaceutical replenishment.

  • Accountability: Assigning clear responsibility for every naira collected and disbursed.
  • Transparency: Maintaining open records that trace inventory movement alongside cash flow.
  • Segregation of duties: Ensuring the staff member who sells drugs is never the same person who reconciles the bank accounts.

Cash Flow Management and Revenue Collection

Effective cash flow management ensures that liquid assets are immediately available to meet supplier invoices without waiting for lengthy administrative approvals. Revenue collection points across hospital pharmacies must enforce strict accountability. Cash leakage often occurs at the point of sale if receipts are not issued concurrently with drug dispensing. Modernizing this process through Point of Sale (POS) terminals or electronic banking transfers minimizes physical cash handling, reduces shrinkage, and accelerates bank reconciliation. Daily reconciliation reports must match the physical count of prescriptions dispensed against the bank deposits recorded.

Pricing Strategies and Cost Recovery

Pricing within a DRF must balance affordability for patients with sustainability for the fund. Selling drugs below the true replacement cost guarantees eventual collapse, while excessive markups defeat the public health mandate. Cost recovery pricing must factor in procurement costs, freight, insurance, clearing charges, and a small percentage for operational shrinkage or inflation buffer.

Inventory Control and Stock Valuation Techniques

Pharmaceutical inventory represents locked-up capital. Poor inventory management leads to expired stock, tied-down cash, and artificial shortages. Hospitals utilize valuation techniques like FIFO (First-In, First-Out) to ensure older batches are dispensed before newly arrived supplies, directly minimizing losses from expiration. Routine physical stock counts must be reconciled with stock ledger cards or electronic inventory management systems. Discrepancies between physical inventory and ledger balances signal either pilferage, poor record-keeping, or unrecorded wastage.

Procurement Procedures and Financial Transparency

Procurement is the most capital-intensive phase of the DRF cycle and a common vulnerability for financial malpractice. Transparent procurement requires competitive bidding, adherence to essential medicine lists, and dealing only with pre-qualified, accredited pharmaceutical manufacturers or distributors. Kickbacks, inflated invoices, and single-source purchasing without justification drain the revolving fund rapidly. Financial control mandates that local purchase orders (LPOs), waybills, and inspection reports match vendor invoices before any payment is authorized.

Auditing and Record Keeping

Regular internal and external auditing safeguards the integrity of the DRF. Financial records—including cash books, receipt booklets, ledger cards, and bank statements—must be maintained meticulously and made available for inspection. Auditors look for red flags such as missing receipts, unapproved disbursements, prolonged outstanding credit sales, and unexplained inventory shrinkages. Robust record keeping not only satisfies regulatory oversight but also provides reliable data for forecasting future drug needs.

Common Financial Challenges and Mitigation Strategies

Drug Revolving Funds frequently encounter operational bottlenecks that threaten their viability. Prominent among these are credit sales to influential hospital departments, inflation eroding purchasing power, and pilferage. Mitigation requires enforcing a strict cash-and-carry policy with zero tolerance for informal credit. To combat inflation, revolving funds must implement periodic, moderate price reviews that reflect actual replacement costs without pricing drugs beyond the reach of the local population. Continuous staff training and automated inventory systems further seal leakages.
