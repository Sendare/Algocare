Financial Control of Drug Revolving Fund

Introduction to the Drug Revolving Fund (DRF)

The Drug Revolving Fund (DRF) is a sustainable financing system introduced in healthcare facilities to ensure a steady, uninterrupted supply of essential medicines. In this model, patients pay for the drugs they receive, and the money generated from these sales is used directly to buy fresh stock. This creates a closed financial cycle that prevents the pharmacy from running completely dry due to bureaucracy or delayed government funding.

However, a common misconception is that the DRF is a profit-making venture for the hospital. In reality, it is a cost-recovery mechanism designed to maintain capital and inventory levels, not to generate surplus revenue for unrelated hospital projects. When money meant for drug replenishment is diverted to other hospital overheads, the revolving fund collapses, leading to persistent stockouts.

Cash Flow Management and Revenue Collection

Effective cash flow management is the lifeblood of any DRF. Money must move smoothly from the patient point of sale to the bank account and finally to the drug suppliers. Hospitals must institute tight security around revenue collection points to prevent cash leakage, theft, or unofficial credit sales.

  • Segregation of duties: The person who dispenses drugs should ideally not be the same person collecting cash and balancing the daily ledger.
  • Daily reconciliation: Cash collected at the pharmacy counter must match the total value of stock dispensed at the end of every single shift.
  • Formal banking routines: Cash collections must be deposited into the dedicated DRF bank account promptly, rather than left sitting in a hospital safe for days.

Pricing Strategies and Cost Recovery

Setting the right price for medications in a DRF requires a delicate balance. Prices must be low enough to remain affordable for the average patient, yet high enough to cover the true landed cost of the drugs, transportation, storage loss, and inflation.

If prices are set too low out of sympathy for patients, the fund will suffer from depletion of capital because the revenue collected will no longer buy the same quantity of drugs next time.

Inventory Control and Stock Valuation

Inventory represents tied-up capital. If too much stock sits on shelves, it ties up cash that could be used elsewhere, and risks expiring before use. Conversely, too little stock leads to dangerous stockouts.

  • FIFO (First-In, First-Out): Always dispense the oldest stock first to prevent expiration losses.
  • ABC Analysis: Categorize inventory by value. Group A items are high-cost drugs that need strict monitoring; Group C items are cheap, high-volume supplies.
  • Periodic Stock Valuation: Calculate the total monetary value of all drugs in the pharmacy regularly to track assets accurately.

Financial Transparency and Procurement

Procurement is where a large chunk of DRF money leaves the facility. Without transparent processes, funds are easily lost to inflated prices, substandard drugs, or kickbacks. Transparent procurement protects the integrity of the health system and ensures value for money.

Facilities must use competitive bidding processes where multiple reputable vendors submit price quotations. A tender committee should evaluate these bids based on quality, price, and reliability of delivery. Furthermore, purchase orders, delivery notes, and invoices must always be cross-checked against physical goods received before any payment is approved.

Auditing and Record Keeping

Good financial controls mean little if they cannot be proven through accurate records. Every transaction—from a patient purchase to a bulk supplier payment—must leave a clear paper or digital trail.

Internal and external audits serve as safeguards to detect discrepancies, discourage fraud, and verify that the DRF is solvent. Auditors examine stock ledgers, receipt books, bank statements, and prescription logs. When records are missing or poorly kept, it becomes impossible to tell whether money is simply missing or has been mismanaged.

Mitigating Common DRF Financial Challenges

Even with good structures in place, DRFs face persistent threats. Understanding these challenges helps healthcare managers apply practical solutions before the fund collapses.

  • Credit sales and unpaid bills: Allowing staff or patients to collect drugs on credit drains the fund. Mitigation: Enforce a strict cash-and-carry policy.
  • Theft and pilferage: Loss of drugs directly shrinks capital. Mitigation: Install CCTV, restrict store access, and conduct surprise stock checks.
  • Bureaucratic delays: Slow approval processes stall re-stocking. Mitigation: Delegate financial approval limits to trusted local fund managers.
