Overview of Toxicology

Introduction to Toxicology and Historical Perspective

Toxicology is the scientific study of adverse effects of chemical, physical, or biological agents on living organisms. Long before modern laboratories existed, early civilizations recognized the lethal properties of plants and venomous animals, utilizing them for hunting, execution, and warfare. The **Ebers Papyrus** from ancient Egypt provides some of the earliest written records documenting specific poisons and remedies. Paracelsus, a Renaissance physician often regarded as the father of toxicology, radically shifted humanity's understanding of poisons. He established that substances previously thought to be purely toxic are simply chemicals, and the body's reaction depends entirely on quantity. This foundational concept moved medicine away from mystical explanations of illness and towards scientific observation.

Core Principles: The Dose-Response Relationship

The central dogma of toxicology states that **the dose makes the poison**. Without exception, virtually any substance—including water, oxygen, and essential vitamins—can act as a poison if the quantity absorbed by the body is excessive. The **dose-response relationship** correlates exposure magnitude with the severity of the biological effect. Key parameters derived from experimental data include: - **LD50 (Lethal Dose 50)**: The calculated dose of a substance that is expected to cause death in 50 percent of a defined experimental animal population. - **ED50 (Effective Dose 50)**: The dose that produces a therapeutic or desired biological response in 50 percent of the population. - **Threshold dose**: The highest dose level where no statistically or biologically significant adverse effect is observed.

Classification of Toxic Agents and Chemical Substances

Toxic agents are categorized based on their chemical structure, biological source, mechanism of action, or intended use. Recognizing these categories helps clinicians anticipate clinical syndromes when treating exposures. - **Pesticides**: Includes organophosphates, carbamates, and rodenticides; frequently encountered in agricultural settings across Nigeria. - **Heavy metals**: Elements such as lead, mercury, arsenic, and cadmium that accumulate in soft tissues and bone. - **Pharmaceuticals**: Prescription and over-the-counter medications taken in accidental overdoses or intentional self-harm, such as paracetamol and psychotropics. - **Toxins**: Biological poisons synthesized by living organisms, including bacterial exotoxins, snake venoms, and plant alkaloids.

Routes of Exposure and Toxicokinetics

For a poison to exert systemic effects, it must enter the body and reach its target receptor. The primary routes of exposure include **ingestion**, **inhalation**, **dermal absorption**, and **injection**. Toxicokinetics describes how the body processes a toxicant through four processes, commonly abbreviated as **ADME**: 1. **Absorption**: The movement of the toxic agent from the site of entry into the systemic circulation. 2. **Distribution**: The transport of the agent throughout extracellular and intracellular fluid compartments based on lipid solubility and protein binding. 3. **Metabolism (Biotransformation)**: The chemical alteration of the xenobiotic, primarily in the liver via cytochrome P450 enzymes, converting lipophilic compounds into water-soluble metabolites for excretion. 4. **Excretion**: The elimination of the substance and its metabolites from the body, chiefly through the kidneys via urine or the gastrointestinal tract via bile and feces.

Mechanisms of Toxicity at the Cellular Level

Toxicity ultimately manifests at the cellular and molecular levels. Toxins interfere with normal physiological processes through several distinct pathways: - **Enzyme inhibition**: Organophosphates bind to acetylcholinesterase, preventing the breakdown of acetylcholine and causing continuous neural stimulation. - **Hypoxia induction**: Carbon monoxide binds avidly to hemoglobin with a higher affinity than oxygen, shifting the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve to the left and starving tissues of oxygen. - **Cellular membrane disruption**: Caustic acids and strong bases cause direct chemical destruction of cell membranes through coagulation or liquefactive necrosis. - **Receptor blockade or overstimulation**: Opioids bind to central nervous system receptors, causing profound respiratory depression.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis of Poisoning

Evaluating a poisoned patient requires a systematic approach because history is frequently unreliable, unobtainable, or withheld due to fear or altered mental status. The initial assessment focuses on stabilizing the **airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCs)**. Clinicians utilize toxidromes—constellations of signs and symptoms—to rapidly identify broad classes of poisons: - **Cholinergic toxidrome**: Salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI upset, emesis (SLUDGE) caused by organophosphates. - **Anticholinergic toxidrome**: Blind as a bat, mad as a hatter, red as a beet, hot as a hare, dry as a bone (mydriasis, delirium, flushed skin, hyperthermia, dry skin). - **Opioid toxidrome**: Central nervous system depression, miosis (pinpoint pupils), and profound respiratory depression. - **Sympathomimetic toxidrome**: Hypertension, tachycardia, mydriasis, diaphoresis, and agitation caused by amphetamines or cocaine.

Principles of Management and Supportive Care

The cornerstone of clinical toxicology is meticulous supportive care rather than administering antidotes, as specific antidotes exist for only a small fraction of poisons. Management follows a stepped sequence: 1. **Resuscitation**: Securing the airway, supporting ventilation, and restoring hemodynamic stability. 2. **Decontamination**: Preventing further absorption of the poison. This may involve dermal washing, eye irrigation, or gastric decontamination using **activated charcoal** if presented within an appropriate window. 3. **Enhanced elimination**: Techniques such as multiple-dose activated charcoal, urinary alkalinization, or hemodialysis for severe, refractory poisonings. 4. **Antidote administration**: Utilizing specific pharmacological antagonists when indicated, such as naloxone for opioids or atropine for organophosphates.

Nursing Responsibilities and Safety in Toxicological Emergencies

Nursing care in toxicological emergencies requires acute vigilance, rapid assessment, and strict adherence to safety protocols. Nurses must protect themselves, healthcare staff, and other patients from secondary contamination, particularly when handling chemicals, pesticides, or patients exposed to hazardous materials. Key responsibilities include continuous monitoring of vital signs, neurological status, and cardiac rhythm. Nurses must save and label all vomitus, container labels, or suspected substances brought in by relatives for laboratory identification. Accurate documentation of fluid balance, Glasgow Coma Scale scores, and timing of interventions is critical for legal and clinical continuity.
