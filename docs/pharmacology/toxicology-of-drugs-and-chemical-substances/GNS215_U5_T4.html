Assessment of Immediate Severity of Drug Reactions

Introduction to Immediate Drug Reactions

Immediate drug reactions occur rapidly after administration, usually within minutes to a few hours. These events range from mild skin rashes to life-threatening systemic collapse. Nurses in Nigerian healthcare facilities must maintain high vigilance when administering medications such as antibiotics, analgesics, and local anesthetics, as these agents frequently trigger hypersensitivity episodes. Prompt recognition prevents irreversible complications.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms

Immediate drug reactions often stem from immune-mediated mechanisms, predominantly Type I hypersensitivity reactions. When a sensitized patient encounters a drug, the offending agent cross-links IgE antibodies on mast cells and basophils. This triggers degranulation and the rapid release of potent inflammatory mediators including histamine, tryptase, and leukotrienes. These substances cause vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, smooth muscle contraction, and glandular hypersecretion.

Classification of Reaction Severity

Grading the severity of a drug reaction guides the urgency of clinical interventions.

  • Mild: Localized skin manifestations such as urticaria, pruritus, or transient flushing without systemic involvement.
  • Moderate: Generalized cutaneous signs accompanied by mild respiratory distress, abdominal cramping, or slight dizziness.
  • Severe: Marked hemodynamic instability, significant upper airway obstruction, severe bronchospasm, or multi-organ system involvement.

Clinical Presentation of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is a multi-system emergency requiring immediate identification. Key signs involve skin changes, respiratory compromise, and cardiovascular collapse.

  • Skin: Generalized urticaria, angioedema of the lips, tongue, or eyelids.
  • Respiratory: Laryngeal edema, wheezing, dyspnea, and cyanosis.
  • Cardiovascular: Tachycardia, profound hypotension, dizziness, and loss of consciousness.
  • Gastrointestinal: Nausea, vomiting, and colicky abdominal pain.

Initial Triage and Rapid Assessment

When an adverse reaction is suspected, the nurse must instantly perform a primary survey focusing on the airway, breathing, and circulation (ABCDE framework). Stop the offending drug infusion immediately while maintaining intravenous access with normal saline. Call for emergency medical assistance early. Quick assessment prevents prolonged systemic exposure and reduces mortality.

Monitoring Vital Signs and Systemic Parameters

Continuous surveillance of vital signs is mandatory during and after an acute reaction. Blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation must be tracked every few minutes until stabilization occurs. A sudden drop in oxygen saturation or rising pulse rate signals deteriorating respiratory or cardiovascular status. Document every parameter trend to evaluate treatment efficacy.

Immediate Nursing Interventions

Adrenalin (epinephrine) is the definitive first-line pharmacological treatment for anaphylaxis, administered intramuscularly into the anterolateral thigh. Nurses must ensure high-flow oxygen delivery and position the patient flat with elevated legs, unless vomiting or respiratory distress dictates otherwise. Secondary medications like H1 antihistamines, corticosteroids, and bronchodilators serve as supportive adjuncts rather than primary rescue agents.

Documentation and Post-Reaction Surveillance

Thorough documentation in the patient case note protects future safety and ensures continuity of care. The nurse must record the exact drug name, time of onset, specific symptoms observed, interventions executed, and patient response. Furthermore, formal pharmacovigilance reporting to relevant health authorities helps track adverse drug trends across Nigerian hospitals.
