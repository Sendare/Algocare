Assessment of Immediate Severity of Drug Reactions

Introduction to Immediate Drug Reactions

Immediate drug reactions occur rapidly following administration, typically manifesting within minutes to a few hours. These events range from predictable pharmacological side effects to unpredictable immune-mediated hypersensitivity reactions. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, where injectable medications are frequently administered in outpatient injection shops and emergency rooms, recognizing the sudden onset of an adverse reaction is a critical lifesaving skill. Prompt identification prevents progression to multi-organ failure and death.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms of Acute Reactions

Acute immune-mediated drug reactions are primarily classified as Type I hypersensitivity reactions. When a susceptible individual is exposed to a triggering drug, it stimulates the production of specific **immunoglobulin E (IgE)** antibodies. These antibodies bind to the surface of mast cells and basophils. Upon re-exposure, the drug cross-links the cell-bound IgE, triggering degranulation and the massive release of preformed mediators such as histamine, tryptase, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins into the bloodstream.

Classification of Reaction Severity

Categorizing drug reactions helps determine the urgency of medical intervention. - **Mild**: Localized skin manifestations such as urticaria, pruritus, or mild erythema without systemic involvement. - **Moderate**: Generalized cutaneous signs accompanied by mild respiratory symptoms like a tight throat or wheezing, or mild gastrointestinal cramping. - **Severe**: Multisystem involvement presenting with profound hypotension, severe bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, cyanosis, or cardiovascular collapse.

Clinical Presentation of Anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis is the most severe form of an immediate drug reaction, characterized by rapid, multisystem compromise. Hallmark features involve the respiratory and cardiovascular systems. Patients frequently report a sense of impending doom, generalized warmth, or metallic taste. Physical assessment reveals laryngeal edema causing stridor, diffuse bronchospasm with expiratory wheezing, tachycardia, and a precipitous drop in blood pressure. Without immediate intervention, hypoxia and shock lead rapidly to cardiac arrest.

Initial Triage and Rapid Assessment

When an acute drug reaction occurs, rapid assessment uses the **ABCDE** framework (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure). Examiners must instantly check for airway patency, respiratory effort, pulse strength, and mental status. In emergency settings, stopping the offending medication infusion or removing the patch takes absolute priority before gathering detailed histories. Delaying immediate physical triage to search for a patient chart can prove fatal.

Monitoring Vital Signs and Systemic Parameters

Continuous monitoring of vital signs is essential during and after an acute reaction. Blood pressure must be tracked frequently to detect early vasodilatory shock, while pulse oximetry measures real-time oxygen saturation to catch developing hypoxia. Respiratory rate, depth, and cardiac rhythm require close observation. Persistent tachycardia or a rising respiratory rate often serves as an early warning sign that the patient is deteriorating despite initial treatment.

Immediate Nursing Interventions and Emergency Management

Emergency management of severe immediate drug reactions requires swift, decisive actions. **Epinephrine** is the definitive first-line medication, administered intramuscularly into the mid-outer thigh to reverse bronchospasm and vasoconstrict blood vessels. Oxygen therapy should be initiated via a non-rebreather mask, and intravenous access established rapidly for fluid resuscitation. Positioning the patient flat with elevated legs supports venous return, unless they are vomiting or in respiratory distress.

Documentation, Post-Reaction Surveillance, and Pharmacovigilance

Once the acute crisis resolves, thorough documentation and reporting are vital. The nurse must record the exact timeline of symptoms, specific interventions performed, and patient response in the clinical record. The offending drug must be clearly highlighted as an allergy in the chart and communicated to the multidisciplinary team. Additionally, submitting a formal report to national pharmacovigilance bodies—such as NAFDAC in Nigeria—ensures broader drug safety monitoring and protects future patients.
