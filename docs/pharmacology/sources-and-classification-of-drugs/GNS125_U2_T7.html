Administration of Drugs in Various Forms

Introduction to Drug Administration and Dosage Forms

Pharmacology relies heavily on how medications are formulated for the human body. A **dosage form** is the physical presentation of a drug product, combining the active pharmaceutical ingredient with non-medicinal excipients. These configurations are not arbitrary; they dictate how fast a drug dissolves, where it is absorbed, and how it resists degradation in the harsh environment of the gastrointestinal tract or other delivery sites. Different routes demand specific formulations. An intravenous injection requires an aqueous solution free of particulate matter, whereas a skin patch utilizes a polymer matrix for steady, multi-day systemic absorption. Understanding these forms prevents medication errors, such as crushing a sustained-release tablet or administering an enteric-coated pill via a nasogastric tube.

Oral Solid Medications: Tablets and Capsules

- **Tablets**: Compressed powders that may be scored, chewable, sublingual, or enteric-coated. Never crush enteric-coated or sustained-release tablets, as this destroys their protective mechanisms and causes rapid, toxic drug release. - **Capsules**: Gelatin shells containing powder, granules, or liquids. Hard gelatin capsules can sometimes be opened if a patient has swallowing difficulties, provided the contents are not modified-release granules. - **Sublingual and Buccal Forms**: Placed under the tongue or against the cheek, respectively. These bypass first-pass hepatic metabolism by absorbing directly into the rich vascular network of the oral mucosa.

Liquid Oral Preparations and Calculations

Liquid formulations offer flexible dosing and rapid absorption compared to solids, making them valuable in pediatric and geriatric care. 1. **Solutions**: Homogeneous mixtures where the drug is completely dissolved in a liquid vehicle. They require no shaking. 2. **Suspensions**: Undissolved solid drug particles dispersed throughout a liquid medium. They settle over time, demanding thorough agitation before every dose to ensure accurate potency. 3. **Elixirs and Syrups**: Sweetened hydroalcoholic or concentrated sugar solutions that mask bitter tastes. Diabetics require careful monitoring when taking high-sugar syrups. Accurate measurement is vital. Household spoons vary wildly in volume; nurses must always use calibrated oral syringes, medicine cups, or dropper devices for liquid medications.

Topical and Transdermal Preparations

Topical medications act primarily on the skin or mucous membranes, though some cross the epidermal barrier for systemic distribution. - **Lotions**: Aqueous or hydroalcoholic suspensions applied to soothe, cool, or dry localized skin lesions. - **Creams**: Emulsions of oil and water that absorb easily into the skin without leaving a heavy greasy residue. - **Ointments**: Oil-heavy preparations that provide a protective, occlusive barrier to trap moisture, ideal for dry, scaly skin conditions. - **Transdermal Patches**: Specialized adhesive systems delivering controlled, systemic doses over extended periods. Rotating patch sites prevents skin irritation, and removing the old patch prevents accidental overdose.

Inhalations and Specialized Delivery Devices

Respiratory conditions respond rapidly to medications delivered straight to the bronchial tree via metered-dose inhalers (MDIs), dry powder inhalers (DPIs), or nebulizers. This targeted approach maximizes local therapeutic effects while minimizing systemic adverse reactions. Using an MDI correctly requires precise coordination: shaking the canister, exhaling completely, activating the device while taking a slow, deep breath, and holding that breath for several seconds. Spacers or valved holding chambers improve drug deposition in the lungs, particularly for patients who struggle to coordinate hand actuation with inhalation. Thorough rinsing of the mouth with water is mandatory after using corticosteroid inhalers to prevent oral candidiasis.

Nursing Responsibilities and Administration Safety

Safe medication administration rests on clinical diligence and adherence to established protocols. The fundamental safety standard is the execution of the checks across the medication delivery process, traditionally anchored by the rights of administration: right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, right documentation, and right reason. Before administering any medication, the nurse must verify allergies, check renal and hepatic function parameters if applicable, and assess current vital signs. Any uncertainty regarding an order requires immediate clarification with the prescribing clinician rather than proceeding blindly. Safe practice protects both the patient from harm and the clinician from professional liability.
