Administration of Drugs in Various Forms

Introduction to Drug Administration and Dosage Forms

Drugs rarely enter the body as pure chemical substances. Instead, active pharmaceutical ingredients are combined with non-medicinal agents—known as excipients—to create a specific dosage form. These forms protect the active drug from degradation, mask unpleasant tastes, and control how quickly or slowly the medicine is released into the body. As a nurse, understanding why a drug comes in a particular physical form is the first step toward safe administration. The chosen route of administration, whether enteral, parenteral, or topical, dictates the physical design of the medication.

Oral Solid Medications: Tablets and Capsules

Tablets and capsules are the most common oral solid dosage forms. Tablets are manufactured by compressing powdered active ingredients, sometimes featuring a score line allowing them to be broken in half. Enteric-coated tablets have a special outer layer that resists stomach acid, dissolving only in the more alkaline environment of the small intestine. Capsules enclose the medication inside a gelatin shell, which can be hard or soft.

  1. Standard tablets: Dissolve rapidly in the stomach for quick systemic absorption.
  2. Enteric-coated tablets: Protect the stomach lining from irritation and prevent stomach acid from destroying the drug.
  3. Sustained-release capsules: Designed to dissolve slowly over many hours, maintaining steady blood levels.
  4. Sublingual and buccal tablets: Dissolve under the tongue or against the cheek for direct absorption into the bloodstream, bypassing the liver.

Liquid Oral and Parenteral Preparations

Liquids offer faster absorption than solids because the drug is already dissolved. Oral liquids include syrups (high sugar content), elixirs (alcohol and water solvents), and suspensions (undissolved particles that require vigorous shaking before pouring). Parenteral preparations—including solutions and suspensions given via intravenous, intramuscular, or subcutaneous routes—must be completely sterile, free from pyrogens, and isotonic to body fluids to prevent severe tissue damage or infection.

Topical Preparations: Lotions, Creams, and Ointments

Topical medications deliver active ingredients directly to the skin or mucous membranes for localized effects, though some are designed for systemic absorption.

LotionsPowder in water suspensionCools and dries acutely inflamed, weeping skin
CreamsWater-in-oil or oil-in-water emulsionEasily absorbed, washes off with water, good for broken skin
OintmentsGreasy oil base (petrolatum)Occlusive, traps moisture, best for dry, thick, scaly lesions

When applying topicals, nurses must wear gloves to avoid absorbing the medication themselves.

Specialized Drug Delivery Systems and Inhalations

Specialized delivery systems are engineered to target specific sites or provide prolonged therapeutic action. Metered-dose inhalers (MDIs) and dry powder inhalers deliver medication directly into the respiratory tree, providing rapid local bronchodilation while minimizing systemic side effects. Transdermal patches release drugs continuously through the skin into the dermal microvasculature, providing steady plasma concentrations over days. Nurses must remember to rotate patch application sites and remove old patches to prevent local skin irritation and accidental overdosing.

Nursing Responsibilities and Safety in Drug Administration

Safe medication administration rests on the nurse's clinical vigilance. Adhering to the standard rights of medication administration—right patient, right drug, right dose, right route, right time, and right documentation—prevents most medication errors. Nurses must also assess the patient before administration, verify allergies, and monitor therapeutic responses and adverse effects afterward. Never administer a medication you did not prepare yourself, and always clarify any ambiguous order with the prescribing clinician.
