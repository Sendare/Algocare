Drug Administration Abbreviations

Introduction to Drug Administration Abbreviations

Pharmacology and prescription writing rely heavily on shorthand notation derived primarily from Latin terms. These abbreviations save space on prescription charts and medication administration records (MARs). However, reliance on handwritten shorthand historically contributed to severe clinical errors. Modern nursing practice balances efficiency with patient safety, ensuring every symbol is fully understood before administration occurs.

Common Frequency and Timing Abbreviations

Timing shorthand dictates exactly when and how often a medication reaches the patient. Misinterpreting these instructions leads to missed doses or dangerous drug accumulation.

  • OD: Once daily (though prone to confusion, often written as daily)
  • BD or BID: Twice daily
  • TDS or TID: Three times daily
  • QDS or QID: Four times daily
  • PRN: As needed (pro re nata)
  • STAT: Immediately and only once
  • OM: Every morning
  • ON: Every night

Dosage Form and Measurement Abbreviations

Accurate calculation and measurement rely on standard metric and apothecary units.

  1. mg: Milligram, a unit of mass
  2. g or gm: Gram
  3. mcg: Microgram (often written out to avoid confusion with mg)
  4. mL or ml: Milliliter, a measure of liquid volume
  5. L: Liter
  6. gtt: Drop (from the Latin gutta)
  7. tab: Tablet
  8. cap: Capsule

Error Prevention and The Do Not Use List

Ambiguous handwriting and poorly interpreted abbreviations cause preventable adverse drug events. Regulatory bodies maintain a strict 'Do Not Use' list of dangerous abbreviations. For instance, writing 'u' for units is frequently misread as a zero or a four, leading to tenfold overdoses. Similarly, 'qd' or 'qod' can be mistaken for each other. Nurses must advocate for clear, written-out instructions whenever a prescription appears ambiguous.

Safe Transcription and Verification Practices in Nursing

Safe medication management extends beyond memorizing shorthand; it requires systematic verification. When receiving telephone orders or transcribing charts, nurses apply the read-back technique. Every ambiguous symbol must be spelled out. In busy Nigerian hospital wards where staffing pressures run high, pausing to verify unfamiliar notations prevents catastrophic administration errors and protects both the patient and the practitioner.
