Drug Abuse

Introduction and Definition of Drug Abuse

Drug abuse refers to the harmful or hazardous use of psychoactive substances, including alcohol and illicit drugs. While medicinal drugs are designed to cure or manage health conditions, abuse occurs when these substances are consumed outside of medical supervision, in excessive doses, or for purposes other than their intended therapeutic indication. In clinical and community health practice, the focus extends beyond the illegal status of a drug to the physical, psychological, and social consequences of its consumption. Repeated self-administration often leads to tolerance, where higher quantities are required to achieve the initial effect, and dependence, which creates a powerful compulsion to continue despite clear adverse outcomes.

Spectrum of Substance Involvement: Use, Misuse, Abuse, and Addiction

  • Drug Use: The ingestion of legal or illegal substances according to medical guidelines, cultural norms, or social practices without causing functional impairment.
  • Drug Misuse: The unintentional or intentional improper use of prescribed or over-the-counter medications, such as doubling a dose for faster pain relief or sharing prescription analgesics with a relative.
  • Drug Abuse: The continuous, deliberate use of a substance despite recurrent social, legal, occupational, or interpersonal problems.
  • Addiction (Dependence): A chronic, relapsing brain disease characterized by compulsive drug seeking and use, accompanied by profound neurochemical adaptations and severe withdrawal symptoms upon cessation.

Commonly Abused Substances in Nigeria

Substance abuse patterns in Nigeria reflect a mix of traditional intoxicants and modern pharmaceuticals. Public health surveys highlight several major categories:

  • Cannabis (Igbo/Indian Hemp): The most widely cultivated and consumed illicit drug in the country, often associated with early adolescent experimentation.
  • Opioids: Tramadol and codeine-containing cough syrups have seen alarming rates of non-medical use among youths, commercial vehicle drivers, and laborers seeking endurance.
  • Alcohol: Locally brewed gins (ogogoro) and commercial brands represent socially accepted substances that frequently lead to chronic abuse and dependence.
  • Stimulants: Amphetamine-type stimulants and prescription cognitive enhancers are frequently misused by students and workers to sustain prolonged wakefulness.
  • Inhalants: Solvents such as glue, correction fluid, and petrol are commonly sniffed by vulnerable street children due to their low cost and immediate accessibility.

Neurobiology and Pathophysiology of Addiction

Addiction alters the structural and functional integrity of the central nervous system. At the core of this pathology is the mesolimbic dopaminergic reward pathway, which connects the ventral tegmental area to the nucleus accumbens and the prefrontal cortex. Natural rewards like food and social interaction release moderate amounts of dopamine. Abused substances—whether alcohol, nicotine, or opioids—flood this circuit with massive surges of dopamine, reinforcing the behavior far beyond natural stimuli.

Over time, the brain attempts to restore homeostasis by downregulating dopamine receptors and reducing endogenous neurotransmitter production. This neuroadaptation accounts for tolerance and anhedonia, where the individual can no longer experience pleasure from normal daily activities without the drug. Structural damage to the prefrontal cortex impairs executive functioning, decision-making, and impulse control, explaining why relapse remains high even after prolonged abstinence.

Risk Factors and Vulnerability Drivers

Vulnerability to drug abuse is multifactorial, arising from an interplay of genetic predispositions, environmental stressors, and psychological characteristics. Family history of substance use disorders substantially elevates genetic risk, altering baseline neurochemistry. Environmental factors in urban and rural Nigerian settings include poverty, unemployment, peer pressure, community breakdown, and the easy availability of unmonitored pharmaceuticals in open drug markets.

Psychological vulnerabilities such as unresolved trauma, anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem frequently drive individuals toward self-medication. Adolescents and young adults represent a particularly high-risk demographic due to ongoing development in the prefrontal cortex, which governs risk assessment and impulse inhibition.

Clinical Manifestations and Comprehensive Assessment

Identifying substance abuse requires careful clinical observation and a non-judgmental approach. Physical signs vary by substance class but often include constricted or dilated pupils, tremors, unexplained weight changes, needle tracks, and chronic respiratory issues. Behavioral indicators involve sudden shifts in mood, declining academic or occupational performance, social withdrawal, and secretive financial behaviors.

Nursing assessment must incorporate validated screening tools, such as the CAGE questionnaire or the AUDIT for alcohol use. Clinicians should evaluate the frequency, route, and quantity of drug use, alongside screening for co-occurring mental health disorders (dual diagnosis). Establishing a trusting nurse-patient rapport is essential to overcome denial and obtain an accurate history.

Nursing Management and Therapeutic Interventions

Nursing care for individuals with substance use disorders spans acute stabilization, detoxification support, and long-term rehabilitation. During acute intoxication or withdrawal, the priority is maintaining a safe, low-stimulation environment, monitoring vital signs closely, and administering prescribed medications—such as benzodiazepines for alcohol withdrawal protocols—to prevent seizures and severe complications.

Beyond physiological stabilization, nurses utilize therapeutic communication and motivational interviewing to help patients explore ambivalence and build intrinsic motivation for change. Nurses collaborate with multidisciplinary teams, including psychiatrists, social workers, and counselors, to coordinate behavioral therapies, group support referrals, and holistic aftercare planning.

Complications and Public Health Impacts

Drug abuse generates profound health, social, and economic burdens at both individual and community levels. Medically, chronic injection drug use increases the transmission of blood-borne infections such as HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C. Other physiological complications include cardiomyopathy, hepatic cirrhosis, cognitive deficits, and acute overdose leading to respiratory depression and death.

From a public health standpoint, substance abuse fuels interpersonal violence, domestic abuse, motor vehicle accidents, and workplace absenteeism. In Nigeria, the economic impact is magnified by the loss of productive youth populations, overburdened healthcare facilities, and the expansion of criminal syndicates associated with illicit drug trafficking.

Prevention, Rehabilitation, and Policy Frameworks in Nigeria

Mitigating the crisis of drug abuse requires comprehensive strategies spanning primary prevention, evidence-based rehabilitation, and robust national policies. Primary prevention focuses on school health education, community awareness campaigns, and youth empowerment programs designed to discourage experimentation. Regulatory enforcement is spearheaded by bodies such as the National Drug Law Enforcement Agency (NDLEA), which combats drug trafficking and restricts the illicit circulation of controlled pharmaceuticals.

Rehabilitation models integrate medical detoxification, cognitive-behavioral therapy, peer support groups, and vocational training to facilitate reintegration into society. Establishing accessible mental health and addiction treatment centers across Nigeria remains vital to reducing recidivism and supporting long-term recovery.
