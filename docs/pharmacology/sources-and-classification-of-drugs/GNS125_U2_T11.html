Drug Abuse

Introduction and Definition of Drug Abuse

Drug abuse remains a critical public health concern globally and particularly within Nigeria, affecting individuals, families, and communities across all socioeconomic strata. For nursing students, understanding drug abuse begins with a clear, standardized definition. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), drug abuse—more accurately referred to as substance abuse—is defined as the harmful or hazardous use of psychoactive substances, including alcohol and illicit drugs. This non-medical use of chemical substances leads to significant physical, psychological, social, and occupational impairments. In the Nigerian context, the phenomenon involves both traditional substances and an escalating wave of synthetic and prescription drugs. Nurses play a frontline role in identifying, treating, and supporting individuals struggling with substance use disorders. Recognizing drug abuse not merely as a moral failing, but as a complex chronic brain disease, is essential for delivering compassionate, evidence-based nursing care. As future healthcare professionals in Nigeria, nursing students must appreciate the multidimensional nature of drug abuse, encompassing biological, psychological, and sociocultural determinants. This foundational knowledge empowers nurses to combat stigma, advocate for patients, and participate effectively in multidisciplinary care teams aimed at reducing the burden of substance abuse in society.

Difference Between Use, Misuse, Abuse, and Addiction

To accurately assess and care for patients, nursing students must clearly differentiate between drug use, misuse, abuse, and addiction. Drug use refers to the ingestion of a substance according to medical guidelines, cultural norms, or social customs without causing negative consequences, such as taking a prescribed analgesic for postoperative pain. Drug misuse occurs when a substance is used improperly, such as taking a higher dose of a prescribed medication than directed, skipping doses, or using someone else's prescription for a legitimate ailment. Drug abuse crosses the line into intentional, patterned, and harmful usage where the individual continues to consume the substance despite experiencing clear social, legal, occupational, or physical harms. It represents a maladaptive pattern of behavior. Addiction, clinically diagnosed as a severe substance use disorder, is the most extreme form. It is characterized by compulsive drug seeking and use despite devastating consequences, driven by neurochemical changes in the brain. Unlike simple misuse or abuse, addiction involves neuroadaptation, tolerance, and physical or psychological dependence, resulting in intense withdrawal symptoms when the substance is abruptly ceased. Understanding these distinct terminologies allows Nigerian nurses to document accurately, communicate effectively with multidisciplinary teams, and tailor appropriate interventions for individuals at varying stages of substance involvement.

Commonly Abused Substances in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the landscape of substance abuse is dynamic, encompassing both indigenous psychoactive substances and imported synthetic drugs. Cannabis, locally known as Igbo, remains the most widely cultivated and abused illicit substance in the country. It is frequently consumed by youths and commercial vehicle drivers, leading to cognitive impairment and respiratory complications. Alcohol is another universally abused legal substance, with heavy consumption of locally brewed gin (ogogoro) and commercial beers contributing significantly to domestic violence, road traffic accidents, and liver diseases. Prescription opioids, particularly tramadol and codeine-containing cough syrups, have reached epidemic proportions among Nigerian youths, students, and even professionals seeking energy or stress relief. The Nigerian government and health agencies have placed strict regulations on codeine, yet illicit trafficking persists. Other commonly abused substances include psychostimulants like methamphetamine (locally called 'Mkpuriri Mmiri'), cocaine, heroin, and inhalants such as 'solution' (vulcanizer gum). For nursing students, familiarity with these specific substances is crucial because clinical presentations vary widely. An understanding of local drug trends enables nurses in emergency departments and psychiatric units to rapidly recognize intoxication syndromes, manage acute overdoses, and implement targeted educational programs within Nigerian communities.

Pathophysiology and Neurobiology of Addiction

To understand why substance abuse progresses to chronic addiction, nursing students must examine the neurobiology of the brain. At the core of addiction is the brain's reward pathway, specifically the mesolimbic dopaminergic system, which originates in the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and projects to the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex. Natural rewards like food, social interaction, and sex release moderate amounts of dopamine, reinforcing survival behaviors. However, addictive substances—whether opioids, alcohol, cannabis, or stimulants—flood this circuit with massive surges of dopamine, sometimes two to ten times the natural amount. This unnaturally strong signal trains the brain to repeat the rewarding behavior, overriding normal self-control mechanisms. With repeated substance exposure, the brain undergoes neuroadaptation. It attempts to compensate for the chemical flood by reducing dopamine production or decreasing the number of functional dopamine receptors. This leads to tolerance, where the individual requires larger amounts of the drug just to feel normal or achieve the previous level of euphoria. Furthermore, structural and functional changes in the prefrontal cortex impair decision-making, impulse control, and judgment. When the drug is removed, the depleted dopamine system results in dysphoria, anhedonia, and severe withdrawal symptoms. Understanding these neurobiological mechanisms helps nurses explain to patients and families that addiction is a legitimate medical disorder rooted in physical brain changes, not simply a lack of willpower.

Risk Factors and Causes of Drug Abuse

Drug abuse rarely happens in isolation; it is usually driven by a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, psychological, and social risk factors. For Nigerian nursing students, identifying these risk factors is vital for community health outreach and preventive nursing interventions. Genetic predisposition plays a substantial role, as individuals with a family history of substance use disorders inherit a biological vulnerability to addiction. Environmental and socioeconomic factors are equally powerful, particularly in developing nations. High rates of youth unemployment, poverty, inadequate recreational facilities, and economic hardship in Nigeria create chronic stress, despair, and vulnerability, leading many individuals to turn to drugs as a form of self-medication or escape. Peer pressure is a monumental catalyst, especially among adolescents and university students eager to fit into peer groups or subcultures. Psychological factors, including untreated mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, trauma, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), frequently co-occur with substance abuse in what is known as a dual diagnosis. Family dynamics, such as parental neglect, domestic violence, broken homes, or parental substance abuse, also heavily increase a child's risk of future drug abuse. By recognizing these multifaceted risk factors, nurses can identify vulnerable populations early and design targeted educational and counseling initiatives to mitigate potential drug initiation.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Clinical manifestations of drug abuse vary widely depending on the type of substance used, the dose, the route of administration, and the chronicity of use. Nursing students must master comprehensive assessment skills to identify intoxication, withdrawal, and chronic complications. Intoxication signs differ across drug classes: CNS depressants (like alcohol and opioids) cause drowsiness, slurred speech, respiratory depression, and pinpoint pupils; CNS stimulants (like cocaine and methamphetamine) present with agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, dilated pupils, and grandiosity; while hallucinogens cause perceptual distortions, paranoia, and tremors. Conversely, withdrawal manifestations occur when use is abruptly stopped; for example, alcohol or sedative withdrawal can precipitate dangerous delirium tremens, characterized by tremors, hallucinations, autonomic instability, and seizures. Opioid withdrawal, though rarely fatal, causes severe nausea, vomiting, muscle cramps, lacrimation, and intense dysphoria. Nursing assessment must include a thorough history, physical examination, mental status examination, and the use of validated screening tools such as the CAGE questionnaire for alcohol or the DAST (Drug Abuse Screening Test). Nurses must approach patients with empathy, maintaining a non-judgmental attitude because individuals struggling with substance abuse often fear stigmatization and may conceal their drug history. Accurate assessment forms the bedrock for safe, individualized nursing care plans.

Nursing Management and Intervention Strategies

Nursing management and intervention strategies for patients with substance use disorders require a holistic, multidisciplinary approach centered on safety, stabilization, and rehabilitation. In the acute phase, the nurse's primary responsibility is physiological stabilization, airway maintenance, and monitoring vital signs, especially during overdose or severe withdrawal (e.g., managing a patient through delirium tremens with benzodiazepines and supportive care). Nurses administer prescribed medications such as naloxone for opioid overdose or methadone/buprenorphine for opioid substitution therapy to ease withdrawal safely. Beyond acute care, psychiatric-mental health nursing principles are applied through therapeutic communication, motivational interviewing, and cognitive-behavioral techniques to help patients explore ambivalence and build coping skills. Nurses establish a therapeutic alliance based on trust, empathy, and unconditional positive regard, actively working to minimize the stigma that often hinders patient recovery. Furthermore, nurses educate patients and their families about the chronic nature of addiction, relapse triggers, and coping mechanisms. Discharge planning is critical, connecting patients with local Nigerian rehabilitation centers, support groups like Narcotics Anonymous (NA) or Alcoholics Anonymous (AA), and outpatient psychiatric follow-ups. Through these comprehensive strategies, nurses facilitate long-term recovery and reintegration into society.

Complications and Public Health Impacts

Drug abuse carries profound physical, psychological, and socioeconomic complications that reverberate across public health systems, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria. Physically, chronic substance abuse damages major organ systems: intravenous drug use drastically elevates the transmission rates of blood-borne infections such as HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C due to unsterile needle sharing. Alcohol abuse leads to cirrhosis, pancreatitis, and cardiomyopathy, while smoking cannabis or inhaling substances damages respiratory tissues, increasing the incidence of chronic bronchitis and lung pathologies. Psychologically, long-term drug abuse precipitates severe mental health disorders, including major depression, paranoid psychosis, cognitive decline, and increased suicide risk. From a public health and societal perspective, the impacts are devastating. Drug abuse contributes significantly to domestic violence, child abuse, community insecurity, violent crime, and road traffic accidents caused by driving under the influence. Economically, it drains national productivity through absenteeism, job loss, and the immense financial burden placed on families and healthcare systems for chronic treatment. For nursing students, understanding these widespread complications underscores the urgent necessity for aggressive public health advocacy, harm reduction programs, and robust preventive healthcare policies in Nigeria.

Prevention, Rehabilitation, and Policy in Nigeria

Combating drug abuse effectively requires a comprehensive triad of primary prevention, structured rehabilitation, and robust national policy enforcement. In Nigeria, regulatory bodies such as the National Drug Law Enforcement Agency (NDLEA) and the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC) spearhead efforts to curb illicit drug trafficking, regulate pharmaceutical distribution, and penalize drug offenders. However, enforcement alone is insufficient; health promotion and prevention are paramount. Nurses are instrumental in community health education, leading anti-drug campaigns in secondary schools, universities, and youth centers to educate adolescents on the dangers of substance abuse. Rehabilitation in Nigeria involves medical detoxification, psychological counseling, behavioral therapies, and social support services. Unfortunately, the country faces a shortage of specialized psychiatric and rehabilitation facilities, making community-based psychiatric nursing and primary health care integration vital. Harm reduction strategies, such as needle-exchange programs and public health education on safe practices, are also gaining traction to minimize disease transmission among active users. For nursing students, advocacy for policy reform, better mental health funding, and destigmatization of addiction are crucial professional responsibilities. By engaging in policy implementation and compassionate rehabilitation care, nurses help steer Nigerian society toward healthier, drug-resilient communities.
