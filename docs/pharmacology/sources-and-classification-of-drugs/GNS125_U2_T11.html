Drug Abuse

Understanding Drug Abuse: Definitions and Classifications

Drug abuse refers to the harmful or hazardous use of psychoactive substances, including alcohol and illicit drugs. When chemical agents are taken in amounts or by methods that damage physical health, mental wellbeing, or social functioning, they fall under the umbrella of abuse. This pattern often persists despite negative consequences, separating it from casual experimentation. Nurses must recognize that psychoactive substances alter brain chemistry, mood, and perception. Understanding these foundational definitions helps clinicians communicate clearly with patients and families without using judgmental language that might discourage honest health disclosures.

Use, Misuse, Abuse, and Addiction

It helps to separate substance involvement into distinct clinical categories:

  • Use: The ingestion of a substance according to medical guidelines or cultural norms without causing functional impairment.
  • Misuse: Using a medication or substance for a purpose other than intended, or in incorrect doses, often seen with prescription painkillers.
  • Abuse: A chronic or periodic pattern of harmful use leading to social, occupational, or physical damage.
  • Addiction: A chronic, relapsing brain disease characterized by compulsive drug seeking and use, accompanied by physical dependence and tolerance.

Commonly Abused Substances in Nigeria

Substance abuse patterns vary by region, but Nigerian clinical settings frequently encounter several specific agents:

  1. Cannabis (Igbo/Weed): The most widely cultivated and consumed illicit drug, often linked to acute psychotic episodes in vulnerable young adults.
  2. Tramadol and Codeine: Prescription opioids heavily misused for euphoria and stamina, leading to severe respiratory depression risks.
  3. Alcohol: A socially accepted intoxicant whose chronic heavy use causes profound liver disease and domestic trauma.
  4. Solvents and Inhalants: Substances like glue and petrol sniffed by street youth for rapid, cheap intoxication.

Neurobiology of Addiction and Brain Pathways

Addiction is fundamentally a disorder of brain circuitry. The brain's reward system, centered in the mesolimbic dopamine pathway, reinforces survival behaviors by releasing dopamine. Addictive substances hijack this system, flooding synapses with dopamine at levels far exceeding natural rewards like food or social interaction. Over time, the brain responds to this chemical overload by reducing dopamine receptors or natural production—a process known as tolerance. Consequently, the individual requires the drug just to feel normal, while everyday pleasures lose their appeal.

Risk Factors and Contributing Causes

No single factor guarantees a person will develop a substance use disorder. Vulnerability arises from a complex web of biological, psychological, and environmental influences. Genetic predisposition can account for a significant portion of addiction risk, especially regarding alcoholism. Psychological triggers include untreated trauma, anxiety, and depression. Environmental pressures—such as peer pressure, neighborhood poverty, unemployment, and family dysfunction—play a massive role, particularly among Nigerian youth facing limited economic opportunities.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

Assessing a patient with suspected substance abuse requires keen observation and gentle inquiry. Physical signs can include dilated or constricted pupils, needle tracks, unsteadiness, tremors, and unexplained weight loss. Behavioral cues involve sudden mood swings, withdrawal from family, neglect of personal hygiene, and frequent financial difficulties. Nurses should utilize validated screening tools like the CAGE questionnaire or AUDIT to evaluate alcohol and drug involvement objectively without scaring the patient away.

Nursing Management and Intervention Strategies

Nursing care for substance-involved clients spans acute stabilization and long-term recovery support. During intoxication or overdose, the primary nursing priority is maintaining a patent airway, breathing, and circulation. Once stabilized, the nurse monitors vital signs during acute withdrawal—which can be life-threatening for alcohol and sedatives. Interventions also involve motivational interviewing, building therapeutic trust, and connecting clients to counseling services and support groups like Narcotics Anonymous.

Complications and Public Health Impacts

Drug abuse triggers widespread consequences that extend far beyond the individual user. Medically, chronic injection drug use increases the transmission of blood-borne infections such as HIV and Hepatitis B and C. Organ damage, malnutrition, and cognitive impairment are also frequent. On a societal level, drug abuse fuels domestic violence, crime, loss of workplace productivity, and severe strain on public healthcare systems. Public health initiatives must treat addiction as a disease rather than a moral failing to reduce these community burdens.

Prevention, Rehabilitation, and Policy in Nigeria

Addressing substance abuse effectively requires structured prevention and strong policy frameworks. In Nigeria, agencies like the National Drug Law Enforcement Agency (NDLEA) spearhead law enforcement and public sensitization campaigns. Prevention programs in schools educate adolescents on the dangers of drug experimentation. Rehabilitation centers offer medical detoxification, behavioral therapy, and vocational training to reintegrate recovering individuals into society. Nurses play a crucial role in advocating for humane treatment facilities and public education policies.
