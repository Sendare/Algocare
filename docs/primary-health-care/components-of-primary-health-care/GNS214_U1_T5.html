Pulmonary Tuberculosis

Introduction and Definition of Pulmonary Tuberculosis

Pulmonary tuberculosis, commonly called TB, is a chronic, infectious disease that primarily attacks the lungs, though it can spread to other parts of the body. It is caused by a slow-growing, rod-shaped bacterium known scientifically as Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Historically referred to as consumption due to the way it physically wastes away the infected person, TB remains a major public health concern, particularly in resource-limited settings. Understanding TB requires recognizing the difference between latent TB infection, where the bacteria live in the body without making the person sick or contagious, and active TB disease, where the immune system can no longer keep the bacteria contained, leading to illness and the ability to spread the germ to others.

Etiology and Transmission Dynamics

  • Causative Agent: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* is an aerobe, meaning it thrives in oxygen-rich environments like the upper lobes of the human lungs.
  • Airborne Spread: The disease spreads almost exclusively through tiny droplet nuclei released into the air when a person with active pulmonary TB coughs, sneezes, speaks, or sings.
  • Infectious Dose: Inhalation of only a few bacilli can be enough to establish an infection in a susceptible host.
  • Environmental Factors: Overcrowding, poor ventilation, and close prolonged contact with an untreated infectious patient significantly increase transmission rates in community and hospital settings.

Pathophysiology and Immune Response

When a person inhales *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, the bacteria reach the alveoli where alveolar macrophages try to engulf them. Instead of being destroyed, the clever bacteria often survive and multiply inside these immune cells. The body responds by mounting a cell-mediated immune response, recruiting more white blood cells to wall off the invaders. This creates a localized, hard nodule called a tubercle or granuloma. Inside the tubercle, the center may die off in a cheese-like state known as caseous necrosis. If the immune system remains strong, these granulomas heal and calcify. However, if immunity drops, the granuloma breaks down, releasing bacteria into the airways and causing tissue destruction.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

The clinical presentation of pulmonary TB can develop insidiously over weeks or months, often leading patients to ignore early signs. Classic systemic indicators include unexplained weight loss, night sweats that soak bed linens, low-grade afternoon fevers, and profound fatigue. Respiratory symptoms usually begin with a dry, hacking cough that eventually progresses to produce purulent or blood-tinged sputum, known medically as hemoptysis. Chest pain may occur due to pleural irritation. In young children, symptoms are often less specific, presenting as failure to thrive, persistent low-grade fever, and wheezing from airway compression by enlarged lymph nodes.

Diagnosis and Screening Methods

Detecting pulmonary TB promptly is essential for stopping transmission in the community. Primary health care facilities rely on a combination of clinical history, physical examination, and specific diagnostic tests.

  1. Sputum Smear Microscopy: Staining sputum samples using the Ziehl-Neelsen method reveals Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) under the microscope.
  2. Molecular Rapid Tests: GeneXpert MTB/RIF provides quick results and checks for rifampicin resistance simultaneously.
  3. Chest Radiography: X-rays help identify typical upper lobe infiltrates, cavities, and fibrotic scars.
  4. Tuberculin Skin Test (TST): Also known as the Mantoux test, it measures delayed hypersensitivity to purified protein derivative injected intradermally.

Directly Observed Therapy Short-Course (DOTS)

Treating tuberculosis successfully requires a strict, prolonged drug regimen, typically lasting six months or more using a combination of first-line agents like isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. The biggest threat to TB treatment is poor adherence, which leads to treatment failure and the emergence of dangerous multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB). To combat this, the World Health Organization champions Directly Observed Therapy (DOTS). Under this strategy, a trained health worker or community volunteer watches the patient swallow every single dose of medication. This direct supervision ensures compliance, monitors potential drug side effects, and guarantees successful cure rates.

Prevention, Control, and Public Health Strategies

Controlling tuberculosis relies heavily on a combination of preventive vaccination, early case finding, and environmental safety measures. The Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine is widely given to infants in high-burden countries to protect against severe forms of childhood TB like tuberculous meningitis. In healthcare facilities, infection control measures are critical: nurses must place patients with suspected or confirmed infectious TB in airborne infection isolation rooms with negative pressure ventilation and ensure staff wear fitted N95 respirators. Public health tracking, contact tracing around active cases, and community education campaigns further help break the chain of transmission.

Complications and Nursing Care Considerations

Advanced or poorly managed pulmonary tuberculosis can cause severe lung damage and life-threatening systemic complications. Permanent lung tissue scarring, bronchiectasis, massive hemoptysis from eroded blood vessels, and pneumothorax are notable physical concerns. From a nursing perspective, care goes beyond medication administration. Nurses must monitor for adverse drug reactions, such as hepatotoxicity from isoniazid and rifampicin or optic neuritis from ethambutol. Nutritional counseling is equally vital because wasting is severe; high-protein, calorie-dense diets help restore body mass. Furthermore, nurses provide empathetic counseling to reduce the social stigma often attached to a TB diagnosis.
