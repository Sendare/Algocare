Pulmonary Tuberculosis

Introduction and Definition of Pulmonary Tuberculosis

Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) is a chronic, infectious airborne disease that primarily affects the lungs, caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding PTB is crucial due to its high prevalence in the sub-region and its significant public health impact. The disease spreads when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or speaks, expelling microscopic droplets containing the bacilli into the air, which are then inhaled by susceptible individuals. Historically and clinically referred to in older texts as consumption due to the severe weight loss associated with advanced stages, PTB remains a major challenge in resource-limited healthcare settings. As future nurses, recognizing the systemic and respiratory nature of this disease is the first step toward effective management and community advocacy. It is essential to differentiate between latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), where the individual is infected but asymptomatic and non-contagious, and active pulmonary tuberculosis disease, where clinical manifestations appear and transmission is possible. Nursing care involves not only treating the physical symptoms but also understanding the socioeconomic determinants, such as poverty, overcrowding, and malnutrition, that fuel the tuberculosis epidemic in Nigerian communities.

Etiology and Transmission Dynamics

The etiology of pulmonary tuberculosis points directly to Mycobacterium tuberculosis, an obligate aerobe, slow-growing, acid-fast bacillus with a unique lipid-rich cell wall that makes it resistant to many standard disinfectants and immune responses. Transmission dynamics are heavily influenced by environmental and host factors. The primary route of transmission is airborne, occurring when droplet nuclei ranging from 1 to 5 micrometers in size remain suspended in the air for hours and are inhaled into the terminal alveoli of the lungs. The risk of transmission depends largely on the concentration of infectious droplet nuclei in the air, the duration of exposure, and the virulence of the strain. In densely populated urban environments, poorly ventilated spaces, and overcrowded healthcare wards in Nigeria, transmission rates soar. Individuals with smear-positive pulmonary tuberculosis are the most infectious sources in the community. Factors such as HIV coinfection, malnutrition, diabetes, and immunosuppressive states significantly increase a person's susceptibility to converting from a latent infection to active disease. Nurses play a vital role in breaking these transmission chains through prompt identification, isolation precautions, and health education on cough etiquette and proper ventilation.

Pathophysiology of Mycobacterium tuberculosis Infection

The pathophysiology of Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection begins when inhaled bacilli reach the alveoli, where they are phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages. Instead of destroying the bacteria, some mycobacteria survive and multiply intracellularly, triggering a robust cell-mediated immune response. Within two to eight weeks, T-lymphocytes and macrophages wall off the infection, forming a granuloma or tubercle, which limits the spread of the bacteria. At the center of this granuloma, caseous necrosis often develops, creating a cheesy, necrotic environment where bacilli can remain dormant yet viable for years (latent tuberculosis infection). If the host's immune system becomes compromised due to factors like HIV, aging, or malnutrition, the granuloma may break down, leading to liquefactive necrosis, cavity formation, and the release of massive numbers of bacilli into the airways. This process converts latent infection into active disease, allowing the bacteria to erode into blood vessels or bronchi, spreading throughout the lungs and potentially to other organs. Understanding this immunopathological mechanism helps nursing students appreciate why immunocompromised clients face such a rapid and severe progression of pulmonary tuberculosis.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms in Adults and Children

Clinical manifestations of pulmonary tuberculosis vary between adults and children, presenting distinct diagnostic challenges for healthcare personnel. In adults, classic symptoms include a persistent cough lasting more than two weeks, productive sputum (often blood-stained or hemoptysis), unexplained weight loss, night sweats, low-grade afternoon fevers, fatigue, and chest pain. Hemoptysis is a particularly alarming sign that indicates advanced tissue destruction and cavitation. In contrast, children with pulmonary tuberculosis often present with non-specific symptoms, making diagnosis trickier. Children frequently exhibit failure to thrive, persistent low-grade fever, chronic non-productive cough, wheezing due to bronchial compression by enlarged lymph nodes, and lethargy. Because children rarely produce sputum samples spontaneously, nurses must be familiar with alternative diagnostic approaches such as gastric aspirations or induced sputum techniques. Recognizing these age-specific clinical presentations enables nurses in primary health care settings to initiate timely evaluations, preventing severe morbidity and mortality associated with delayed diagnosis in vulnerable pediatric populations.

Diagnosis and Screening Methods in Primary Health Care

Diagnosis and screening of pulmonary tuberculosis in primary health care settings rely on a combination of clinical evaluation, bacteriological testing, and imaging. The cornerstone of bacteriological confirmation is sputum smear microscopy for Acid-Fast Bacilli (AFB) using the Ziehl-Neelsen staining technique, which is widely utilized across Nigerian primary health facilities due to its cost-effectiveness. However, rapid molecular diagnostic tools such as the GeneXpert MTB/RIF assay have become the gold standard where available, as they simultaneously detect M. tuberculosis and resistance to rifampicin within hours. Chest X-rays are another critical screening tool, often revealing infiltrates, consolidation, or cavitation in the upper lung lobes. Additionally, the Mantoux tuberculin skin test (TST) and Interferon-Gamma Release Assays (IGRAs) are utilized primarily to detect latent tuberculosis infection, though they do not differentiate between latent and active disease. Nurses play an indispensable role in patient education, ensuring proper collection of quality sputum specimens (early morning deep cough samples), handling biosafety protocols, and interpreting screening outcomes to facilitate rapid medical intervention and treatment initiation.

Directly Observed Therapy Short-Course (DOTS) Management

Directly Observed Therapy Short-Course (DOTS) is the internationally recommended strategy for tuberculosis control, endorsed by the World Health Organization and implemented nationally in Nigeria. The core principle of DOTS is that a trained health worker or a designated, supervised treatment supporter watches the patient swallow every dose of anti-tuberculosis medication. This direct observation ensures absolute adherence, prevents erratic drug ingestion, minimizes treatment failure, and stops the emergence of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB). The standard treatment regimen typically spans six months, divided into an intensive phase (usually two months of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol) and a continuation phase (usually four months of isoniazid and rifampicin). Nursing responsibilities within the DOTS framework extend beyond watching pill ingestion; they include monitoring clients for adverse drug reactions (such as hepatotoxicity from pyrazinamide/isoniazid or optic neuritis from ethambutol), providing nutritional counseling, conducting contact tracing, and offering psychological support to combat the stigma associated with the disease.

Prevention, Control, and Public Health Strategies

Prevention, control, and public health strategies are vital in curbing the burden of pulmonary tuberculosis, particularly in high-prevalence settings like Nigeria. Primary prevention focuses on the administration of the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine to newborns as part of routine childhood immunization schedules, which provides protection against severe forms of disseminated pediatric TB such as tuberculous meningitis. Secondary prevention involves active case finding, prompt diagnosis, and effective treatment of active cases to eliminate sources of infection in the community. Infection prevention and control (IPC) measures in healthcare facilities include ensuring adequate natural ventilation, placing infectious patients in isolation or cohort wards, using personal protective equipment (such as N95 respirators for healthcare workers during high-risk procedures), and educating patients on respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette. Public health campaigns are equally important for addressing community stigma, encouraging voluntary testing, and promoting nutritional support and socioeconomic empowerment. Nurses act as community health educators, spearheading awareness programs and enforcing safety protocols to safeguard both patients and medical personnel.

Complications and Nursing Care Considerations

Complications of pulmonary tuberculosis can be severe and life-threatening if the disease is left untreated or inadequately managed. Chronic lung damage frequently leads to pulmonary fibrosis, bronchiectasis, chronic respiratory failure, and massive hemoptysis caused by the erosion of bronchial arteries (Rasmussen's aneurysm). Other systemic complications include cor pulmonale secondary to chronic pulmonary hypertension, pleural effusion, empyema, and extrapulmonary dissemination (such as spinal tuberculosis or Pott's disease). Comprehensive nursing care considerations require continuous assessment of respiratory status, monitoring oxygen saturation, managing pain, and administering prescribed anti-tuberculosis drugs while watching closely for adverse drug effects like peripheral neuropathy or hepatotoxicity. Nutritional support is paramount, as wasting syndrome (cachexia) is common; nurses must collaborate with dietitians to provide high-protein, high-calorie diets. Furthermore, emotional and psychological care is indispensable, as patients often face social isolation and depression. Nurses must advocate for holistic care, facilitate support groups, and ensure robust discharge planning with continuity of care through community health extension workers.
