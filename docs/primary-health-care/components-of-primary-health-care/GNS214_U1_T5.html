Pulmonary Tuberculosis

Introduction and Definition of Pulmonary Tuberculosis

Pulmonary tuberculosis is a chronic, infectious disease primarily affecting the lung parenchyma, though it can disseminate to other organ systems. The condition is characterized by granulomatous inflammation, caseation necrosis, and cavity formation within pulmonary tissues. Globally and within West Africa, it remains a major public health challenge closely linked to socioeconomic status, overcrowding, and human immunodeficiency virus co-infection. Understanding the baseline pathology allows nursing students to anticipate the prolonged pharmacological requirements and complex psychosocial needs of affected individuals.

Etiology and Transmission Dynamics

The causative agent is **Mycobacterium tuberculosis**, an obligate aerobe, slow-growing, acid-fast bacillus with a lipid-rich cell wall that protects it from destruction by host immune cells. Transmission occurs almost exclusively via the airborne route. When an individual with active pulmonary disease coughs, sneezes, speaks, or sings, microscopic droplet nuclei ranging from 1 to 5 micrometers are expelled into the atmosphere. These tiny particles remain suspended in the air for hours and can be inhaled by susceptible individuals, reaching the terminal alveoli.

Pathophysiology and Immune Response

- **Inhalation**: Droplet nuclei bypass upper airway defenses and reach the alveoli. - **Phagocytosis**: Alveolar macrophages engulf the bacteria, but the organism inhibits phagosome-lysosome fusion, allowing intracellular survival. - **Ghon Focus**: Macrophages and lymphocytes aggregate, forming a primary inflammatory lesion known as the Ghon focus. - **Latent vs. Active**: Cell-mediated immunity walls off the infection, leading to latent infection, or fails, resulting in progressive primary disease and tissue liquefaction.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Pulmonary tuberculosis presents with a insidious onset of constitutional and respiratory symptoms. Classic systemic signs include drenching night sweats, low-grade afternoon fevers, unexplained weight loss, and progressive fatigue. Respiratory symptoms begin with a dry, persistent cough that eventually progresses to a productive cough yielding purulent or blood-streaked sputum. Hemoptysis occurs when expanding cavitary lesions erode adjacent bronchial blood vessels, a clinical sign that often prompts patients to seek medical evaluation.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening

Accurate diagnosis relies on a combination of clinical evaluation, sputum microbiology, and chest radiography. 1. **Sputum Smear Microscopy**: Direct visualization of acid-fast bacilli using Ziehl-Neelsen staining remains a cornerstone in resource-limited settings. 2. **Molecular Testing**: Rapid assays such as GeneXpert MTB/RIF detect DNA and simultaneously identify rifampicin resistance. 3. **Chest Radiography**: Typically reveals upper lobe infiltrates, cavitary lesions, and lymphadenopathy. 4. **Tuberculin Skin Test**: Measures delayed-type hypersensitivity to purified protein derivative, indicating immune sensitization.

Directly Observed Therapy Short-Course (DOTS)

The cornerstone of tuberculosis control is the **DOTS** strategy, endorsed globally to ensure treatment adherence and prevent drug resistance. Management is divided into an **intensive phase** and a **continuation phase**. During the intensive phase, patients receive a combination of first-line agents—typically **isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol**—under the direct observation of a healthcare worker or trained volunteer who watches the patient swallow every dose. The continuation phase consolidates the cure by eliminating remaining dormant bacilli.

Complications and Nursing Care Considerations

Complications of pulmonary tuberculosis extend beyond local tissue destruction. Chronic cavitary disease can lead to massive hemoptysis, bronchiectasis, pneumothorax, and chronic pulmonary Aspergillosis. Nursing care focuses on infection control through **airborne precautions**, patient education regarding medication adherence, and nutritional support to combat wasting syndrome. Nurses must monitor for adverse drug reactions, such as isoniazid-induced peripheral neuropathy—prevented by co-administering pyridoxine—and rifampicin-induced orange discoloration of body fluids.
