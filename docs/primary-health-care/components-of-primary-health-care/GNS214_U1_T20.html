Nutrition Counselling

Introduction to Nutrition Counselling in Primary Health Care

Nutrition counselling is a collaborative process between a healthcare provider and a client to guide food choices, promote wellness, and manage health conditions. In primary health care settings across Nigeria, it forms the cornerstone of preventive medicine. Nurses frequently provide this guidance during routine antenatal visits, immunisation clinics, and community outreach programs. By addressing dietary habits early, nurses help prevent malnutrition, stunting, and diet-related chronic non-communicable diseases like hypertension and type 2 diabetes. Effective nutrition counselling moves beyond simply handing out a printed diet sheet. It involves understanding the patient's daily reality, financial constraints, and cultural food preferences to co-create sustainable eating habits.

Nutritional Assessment and Dietary History Taking

Before offering any dietary advice, a nurse must gather accurate baseline data through a comprehensive nutritional assessment. This process combines anthropometric measurements, clinical examinations, and detailed dietary histories.

  • Anthropometry: Measuring weight, height, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), and body mass index (BMI) to detect wasting or overweight.
  • Clinical Examination: Inspecting the hair, skin, eyes, and oral cavity for physical signs of vitamin or mineral deficiencies, such as Bitot's spots or angular cheilitis.
  • Dietary History: Using tools like the 24-hour dietary recall or a food frequency questionnaire to understand what the patient actually eats.

A common mistake during dietary history taking is asking leading questions, such as "You eat vegetables every day, right?" Nurses must ask open-ended questions to get an honest picture of typical daily food intake.

Identifying Dietary Deficiencies and Vulnerable Groups

Certain segments of the population have increased nutritional needs and face higher risks of deficiency due to rapid growth, physiological stress, or socioeconomic challenges. Pregnant and lactating women require extra iron, folate, and calcium to support fetal development and milk production. Infants and young children are especially vulnerable to protein-energy malnutrition and micronutrient deficiencies during the transition to complementary feeding. Adolescents experience rapid growth spurts that demand high energy and iron intake. Older adults may suffer from reduced appetite, dental problems, and poor nutrient absorption. Recognizing these vulnerable groups allows nurses to prioritize targeted screening and preventive education during routine clinical encounters.

Communication Techniques and Behaviour Change Strategies

Dietary advice fails when it is delivered as an authoritarian lecture. Nurses must master active listening and motivational interviewing to encourage lasting lifestyle changes. Instead of telling patients what they are doing wrong, ask open-ended questions that prompt them to voice their own reasons for change. Reflective listening builds trust, showing the client that their concerns are genuinely heard. Behaviour change often happens in stages, from pre-contemplation to action. Tailoring counselling messages to match the client's current readiness stage prevents frustration for both the nurse and the patient.

Developing Practical and Culturally Acceptable Meal Plans

A good meal plan is useless if the family cannot afford the ingredients or dislikes the taste. Dietary counselling must be practical and grounded in local food systems. Nurses should promote diverse diets using locally available staples, seasonal fruits, and affordable protein sources like beans, groundnuts, eggs, and local fish. Cultural beliefs and taboos around food—such as restricting eggs from pregnant women—must be gently addressed with culturally sensitive education rather than outright dismissal. Involving family members, especially those responsible for cooking and food purchasing, ensures that the proposed meal modifications are sustainable at home.

Addressing Barriers to Proper Nutrition and Follow-Up Care

Poverty, food insecurity, lack of storage facilities, and demanding work schedules frequently obstruct nutritional goals. A nurse must identify these external barriers before assuming a patient is non-compliant. If a household lacks refrigeration or steady income, recommending perishable, expensive foods will lead to frustration. Referral to community social support programs, agricultural extension workers, or local empowerment schemes can provide long-term solutions. Furthermore, nutrition counselling does not end at the first visit. Scheduled follow-up appointments allow the nurse to monitor progress, celebrate small dietary victories, and adjust meal plans when challenges arise.
