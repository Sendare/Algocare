HIV/AIDS

Introduction to HIV/AIDS and Global Burden

The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is a lentivirus that gradually destroys the human immune system, specifically targeting CD4+ T-lymphocytes. When the immune system becomes severely compromised, the infection progresses to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS), the most advanced stage of the disease. At this point, the body can no longer fight off everyday germs, leaving the person vulnerable to serious opportunistic infections and cancers.

Sub-Saharan Africa bears the heaviest burden of the global HIV epidemic, accounting for the majority of new infections and AIDS-related deaths worldwide. In many local communities across Nigeria and neighboring countries, poverty, gender inequality, and limited access to health information drive transmission rates upward. Public health efforts rely heavily on community outreach, widespread testing campaigns, and free access to life-saving medications to curb the spread.

Pathophysiology and Modes of Transmission

Once HIV enters the bloodstream, it binds to surface receptors on CD4 cells, fuses with the cell membrane, and inserts its genetic material. The virus uses an enzyme called reverse transcriptase to convert its RNA into DNA, hijacking the host cell to replicate itself. As millions of new viral particles burst out, they destroy the host CD4 cell and move on to infect others. This steady depletion of immune cells over years leaves the body defenseless.

The primary modes of transmission include:

  • Sexual contact: Unprotected vaginal, anal, or oral sex with an infected partner.
  • Blood contact: Sharing contaminated needles, unsterile surgical tools, or receiving unscreened blood transfusions.
  • Mother-to-child: Transmission during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding.

Clinical Stages and Disease Progression

The clinical progression of HIV follows a predictable timeline if left untreated, moving from acute infection to late-stage disease.

  1. Acute HIV Infection: Occurs two to four weeks after exposure. Patients experience flu-like symptoms such as fever, fatigue, swollen lymph nodes, and body rashes as the viral load peaks.
  2. Clinical Latency (Chronic HIV): The virus multiplies at very low levels. The patient may feel completely healthy and show no symptoms for several years, though the virus continues to slowly destroy CD4 cells.
  3. Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS): CD4 counts drop below 200 cells/mm3, or the patient develops defining opportunistic illnesses like Kaposi's sarcoma, severe candidiasis, or wasting syndrome.

Prevention and Primary Health Care Strategies

Primary prevention aims to stop new infections before they occur through education, risk reduction, and biomedical interventions. Nurses play a major role in community clinics by promoting consistent and correct condom use, providing clean needle exchange information, and offering safe male circumcision services which significantly lower heterosexual transmission risk.

Biomedical prevention tools have transformed modern healthcare. Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP) involves HIV-negative individuals taking daily antiretroviral pills to prevent infection if exposed. Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) is an emergency course of medication started within 72 hours after a high-risk exposure, such as a needle-stick injury in a hospital or sexual assault, to stop the virus from taking hold.

Nursing Assessment, Counseling, and Testing

HIV testing is the gateway to treatment and care. Nurses must provide confidential, voluntary counseling before and after testing, often referred to as VCT (Voluntary Counseling and Testing). Pre-test counseling helps clients assess their personal risk, understand the testing process, and mentally prepare for any result. Post-test counseling delivers the result with empathy, offering immediate emotional support and linkage to care for those testing positive.

Assessment includes taking a thorough sexual and medical history, checking for signs of chronic weight loss, persistent fevers, or oral thrush, and building a trusting nurse-patient relationship. Confidentiality is paramount; breaching patient trust can cause individuals to avoid healthcare facilities entirely, increasing community transmission.

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) and Patient Management

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) involves taking a combination of daily medications that stop HIV from replicating. While ART does not cure the virus, it reduces the viral load in the blood to undetectable levels, allowing the immune system to recover and strengthen. An undetectable viral load also means the virus cannot be sexually transmitted to others, a concept known as Undetectable = Untransmittable (U=U).

Nursing management focuses heavily on promoting strict adherence. Missing doses allows the virus to multiply and mutate, leading to drug resistance where standard medications stop working. Nurses educate patients on managing mild side effects like nausea or fatigue, establish reminder routines, and monitor routine CD4 counts and viral load blood tests at scheduled clinic visits.

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT)

Without intervention, an HIV-positive mother has a significant chance of passing the virus to her child during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding. PMTCT programs successfully reduce this transmission risk to less than two percent through a comprehensive continuum of care.

Pregnant women presenting at antenatal clinics are routinely offered HIV screening. Those who test positive are started on lifelong ART immediately, regardless of their CD4 count. During labor, safe delivery practices are observed, and newborn babies receive prophylactic antiretroviral syrups for several weeks after birth. Infant feeding counseling helps mothers balance nutritional needs with safety, depending on local health guidelines regarding exclusive breastfeeding while on maternal ART.

Managing Opportunistic Infections and Complications

As immunity declines, patients become susceptible to specific opportunistic infections that rarely affect healthy individuals. Tuberculosis (TB) is the leading co-infection and cause of death among people living with HIV, requiring prompt dual management with both anti-TB drugs and ART. Other common challenges include cryptococcal meningitis, severe fungal mouth thrush, recurrent pneumonia, and chronic diarrhea leading to severe malnutrition and wasting.

Nurses monitor patients closely for early signs of secondary infections during routine assessments. Preventive therapies, such as cotrimoxazole prophylaxis, are routinely prescribed to guard against pneumocystis pneumonia and toxoplasmosis in clients with low CD4 counts.

Stigma Reduction, Psychosocial Support, and Home-Based Care

Social stigma and discrimination remain massive barriers in the fight against HIV/AIDS, often discouraging people from getting tested or taking their medications openly. Nurses act as strong patient advocates, educating communities to dispel myths and treating clients with dignity and respect. Psychosocial support groups help patients cope with depression, anxiety, and the emotional weight of living with a chronic condition.

Home-based care programs bridge the gap between hospital settings and family life, especially for bedridden or advanced-stage patients. Nurses train family caregivers on basic hygiene, nutritional meal preparation, proper wound care for pressure sores, and recognizing warning signs that require immediate return to the health facility.
