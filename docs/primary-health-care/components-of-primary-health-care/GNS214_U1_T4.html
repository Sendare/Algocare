HIV/AIDS

Introduction to HIV/AIDS and Global Burden

The Human Immunodeficiency Virus is a retrovirus that specifically targets the immune system, eroding the body's defense against opportunistic infections and malignancies. When the infection advances without therapeutic intervention, it culminates in Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome, characterized by a severely depleted CD4+ T-cell count and the presence of defining opportunistic illnesses. Sub-Saharan Africa bears the heaviest burden of this global epidemic, accounting for a disproportionate percentage of total infections and AIDS-related mortality. In Nigerian clinical practice, managing this epidemic requires addressing systemic challenges including late presentation, commodity stock-outs, and deeply entrenched community prejudice that deters individuals from seeking early diagnostic and treatment services.

Pathophysiology and Modes of Transmission

Once inside the host, HIV utilizes its surface glycoproteins to bind to receptor sites on target immune cells, fusing its membrane and releasing viral RNA into the cytoplasm. Through the action of reverse transcriptase, the viral RNA is transcribed into double-stranded DNA, which integrates into the host cell's genome via viral integrase. This hijacked cellular machinery then manufactures new viral particles, destroying the host cell in the process.

  • Sexual Transmission: Transfer of infectious bodily fluids across mucosal membranes during unprotected vaginal, anal, or oral intercourse.
  • Parenteral Transmission: Direct introduction of the virus into the bloodstream through contaminated injection equipment, blood transfusions, or needle-stick injuries.
  • Vertical Transmission: Passing the virus from an infected mother to her child during intra-uterine development, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding.

Clinical Stages and Disease Progression

The natural history of HIV infection unfolds across distinct clinical phases, providing a framework for monitoring health status and timing interventions.

  1. Acute Infection: Occurs two to four weeks post-exposure, marked by a rapid viral surge and flu-like symptoms including fever, lymphadenopathy, and maculopapular rash.
  2. Clinical Latency: Also known as chronic HIV, this phase features active viral replication at lower levels while the patient remains largely asymptomatic for years.
  3. Advanced Disease (AIDS): Immune exhaustion sets in, dropping CD4 counts below critical thresholds and inviting severe systemic opportunistic pathologies.

Prevention and Primary Health Care Strategies

Primary prevention remains the cornerstone of curbing viral spread, incorporating a combination of biomedical, behavioral, and structural interventions. Community outreach programs promote correct and consistent condom utilization, safe injection practices, and voluntary medical male circumcision, which significantly reduces female-to-male transmission risk. Biomedical advancements have introduced Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis and Post-Exposure Prophylaxis, utilizing antiretroviral drugs to block acquisition either before or immediately after high-risk exposure. In primary health care settings across Nigeria, nurses play a pivotal role in delivering targeted health education, distributing barrier methods, and normalizing discussions surrounding sexual health without judgment.

Nursing Assessment, Counseling, and Testing

Clinical encounters for HIV begin with a structured assessment incorporating a thorough history of risk factors, review of systems, and baseline physical evaluation. Pre-test counseling prepares the client for potential results, addresses anxiety, and establishes informed consent, while post-test counseling delivers the diagnosis with empathy and immediate linkage to care. Diagnostic testing algorithms typically utilize rapid diagnostic kits in a parallel or serial testing scheme, requiring confirmation protocols for reactive results. Confidentiality is paramount; breaching patient trust not only violates professional ethics but drives vulnerable populations away from essential health facilities.

Antiretroviral Therapy and Patient Management

Antiretroviral therapy suppresses viral replication to undetectable levels, preserving immune function and preventing transmission. Modern regimens combine multiple drugs from different classes—such as reverse transcriptase inhibitors, integrase strand transfer inhibitors, and protease inhibitors—to attack the virus at various stages of its lifecycle and prevent drug resistance.

Adherence to daily dosing schedules is non-negotiable; missing doses allows viral mutation and treatment failure. Nurses monitor adherence through pill counts, appointment tracking, and open dialogue, addressing side effects like nausea or fatigue before they cause default.

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission

Without intervention, transmission rates from an infected mother to her infant range between fifteen and forty-five percent during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding. Comprehensive PMTCT programs intervene at every stage: initiating immediate maternal ART during antenatal care, providing safe obstetric practices during labor, and administering infant prophylaxis syrups after birth. Infant feeding counseling must balance the nutritional needs of the baby with the risk of viral transmission through breast milk, adhering to national guidelines regarding exclusive breastfeeding while on maternal ART or safe replacement feeding when acceptable, feasible, affordable, sustainable, and safe.

Managing Opportunistic Infections and Complications

As immune compromise deepens, patients become susceptible to signature opportunistic illnesses that define advanced disease. Common culprits include pulmonary tuberculosis, cryptococcal meningitis, toxoplasmosis, and Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia. Prophylactic antibiotics, such as cotrimoxazole, are routinely prescribed to guard against bacterial and protozoal infections when CD4 counts drop below specific thresholds. Nurses must maintain high vigilance for subtle clinical signs—such as persistent cough, severe headache, visual changes, or neurological deficits—and report them promptly for diagnostic investigation and targeted antimicrobial management.

Stigma Reduction, Psychosocial Support, and Home-Based Care

Living with HIV involves navigating psychological distress, depression, and the heavy burden of social stigma, which often proves more disabling than the virus itself. Community sensitization and peer support groups empower clients by fostering a sense of shared experience and acceptance. Home-based care models bridge the gap between hospital facilities and family units, training caregivers in basic hygiene, nutritional support, palliative symptom relief, and adherence monitoring. Nurses act as advocates, confronting judgmental attitudes within healthcare institutions and fostering an environment of dignity, holistic support, and human rights for all affected individuals.
