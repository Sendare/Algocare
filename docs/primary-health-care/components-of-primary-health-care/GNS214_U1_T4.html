HIV/AIDS

Introduction to HIV/AIDS and Global Burden

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) remains one of the world's most significant public health challenges, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. HIV is a lentivirus that attacks the human immune system, specifically targeting CD4+ T lymphocytes, which are crucial for orchestrating the body's immune response. When the immune system becomes severely compromised, the infection progresses to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS), characterized by the onset of life-threatening opportunistic infections and cancers. Globally, millions of individuals are living with HIV, with Sub-Saharan Africa bearing the heaviest burden of the epidemic. As nursing students in Nigeria, understanding this global and regional epidemiology is vital because Nigeria has one of the highest HIV prevalence rates in the world. Nurses serve on the frontline of healthcare delivery, playing indispensable roles in community sensitization, stigma reduction, early detection, and the management of individuals living with HIV. Recognizing the socioeconomic and cultural determinants that influence the spread and management of HIV in Nigeria will enable you to provide holistic, culturally sensitive, and evidence-based care to affected populations across urban and rural healthcare settings.

Pathophysiology and Modes of Transmission

The pathophysiology of HIV centers on its ability to invade host immune cells, replicate, and progressively destroy the immune architecture. Once HIV enters the body, its envelope glycoproteins bind to CD4 receptors and co-receptors (such as CCR5 or CXCR4) on the surface of target cells, primarily CD4+ T cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Through fusion and viral entry, the viral RNA is released into the host cell cytoplasm. Using the enzyme reverse transcriptase, the viral single-stranded RNA is transcribed into double-stranded DNA. This viral DNA is then integrated into the host cell's genome via integrase, hijacking cellular machinery to produce new viral particles using protease. Over time, continuous viral replication leads to a progressive decline in CD4+ T cell counts, destroying immune competence. Transmission of HIV occurs primarily through specific bodily fluids, including blood, semen, pre-seminal fluid, rectal fluids, vaginal fluids, and breast milk. The primary modes of transmission globally and in Nigeria include unprotected sexual intercourse with an infected partner, vertical transmission from mother to child during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding, and parenteral exposure through contaminated needles, syringes, or unscreened blood products. Understanding these pathways is essential for nurses to design effective targeted education and prevention programs.

Clinical Stages and Disease Progression

The clinical course of untreated HIV infection is typically categorized into distinct stages that reflect the gradual deterioration of the immune system. The first stage is acute HIV infection, occurring within 2 to 4 weeks after initial exposure. During this phase, patients often experience a flu-like illness characterized by fever, fatigue, swollen lymph nodes, pharyngitis, and maculopapular rash, coinciding with a high viral load and a temporary drop in CD4+ T cell counts. This is followed by the clinical latency stage, also known as chronic HIV infection or asymptomatic stage. During this period, which can last for several years, the virus replicates at lower levels, and patients may remain entirely asymptomatic while experiencing a gradual, steady decline in CD4+ T cells. Without antiretroviral therapy, the disease eventually progresses to Stage 3, clinically recognized as AIDS. In this advanced stage, the CD4+ count drops below 200 cells/mm3, or the patient develops definitive AIDS-defining conditions such as Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma, esophageal candidiasis, or extrapulmonary tuberculosis. Monitoring these clinical stages through regular staging and immunological tests helps nurses anticipate complications and evaluate treatment efficacy.

Prevention and Primary Health Care Strategies

Prevention of HIV is a core component of primary health care and public health nursing practice. Implementing effective prevention strategies requires a multifaceted approach tailored to specific community needs and risk groups. Key behavioral interventions include promoting abstinence, encouraging mutual fidelity, and consistent, correct use of male and female condoms during all sexual encounters. Biomedical prevention strategies have revolutionized the field, featuring Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP), where antiretroviral medications are taken by HIV-negative individuals to prevent acquisition, and Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP), which involves emergency antiretroviral medications taken within 72 hours of potential exposure, such as occupational needle-stick injuries or sexual assault. Additionally, achieving 'Undetectable = Untransmissible' (U=U) through effective treatment is a powerful prevention tool, as individuals with a suppressed viral load cannot sexually transmit the virus. Primary health care clinics also provide harm reduction programs, such as needle and syringe exchange programs for people who inject drugs, and routine screening for sexually transmitted infections (STIs), which significantly increase the risk of HIV transmission when left untreated. As nurses, patient education and community outreach are vital tools to dispel myths and promote these evidence-based prevention modalities.

Nursing Assessment, Counseling, and Testing

Nursing assessment, counseling, and testing form the gateway to HIV diagnosis, care, and support services. Nurses must conduct comprehensive health assessments that include a thorough, non-judgmental sexual history, identification of high-risk behaviors, and screening for symptoms of opportunistic infections. HIV counseling and testing (HCT) follows the principles of the '5 Cs': Consent, Confidentiality, Counseling, Correct test results, and Connection to care. Pre-test counseling prepares the client for the testing process, assesses risk, and addresses anxiety, while post-test counseling delivers the results in a supportive environment. For individuals who test negative, post-test counseling focuses on prevention strategies like PrEP and safer sex practices. For those who test positive, counseling requires immense empathy, psychological support, reassurance, and immediate linkage to comprehensive HIV care and treatment services. Rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) are commonly used in primary healthcare settings across Nigeria for prompt screening, with confirmatory testing protocols following national guidelines. Nurses must maintain absolute confidentiality to build trust and encourage marginalized or stigmatized populations to seek testing without fear of discrimination or legal repercussions.

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) and Patient Management

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) has transformed HIV from a fatal diagnosis into a manageable chronic condition. The primary goal of ART is to suppress viral replication to undetectable levels, thereby preserving immune function, preventing disease progression, reducing HIV transmission, and ensuring a near-normal life expectancy. Modern ART regimens typically consist of a combination of three antiretroviral drugs drawn from at least two different drug classes, a strategy known as Highly Active Antiretroviral Therapy (HAART). Classes of medications include Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs), Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs), Protease Inhibitors (PIs), Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs), and Entry or Fusion Inhibitors. Current guidelines advocate for 'test and start,' meaning ART is initiated as soon as possible after diagnosis, regardless of CD4 count. Patient adherence is the single most critical determinant of ART success; missed doses can lead to treatment failure, drug resistance, and rapid viral rebound. As nurses, your role involves educating clients on proper drug administration, managing and anticipating side effects (such as nausea, rash, or fatigue), assessing adherence regularly using pill counts and self-reports, and providing continuous emotional support to foster long-term retention in care.

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT)

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) programs are vital public health interventions designed to interrupt the transmission of HIV from an HIV-positive mother to her infant during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding. Without interventions, the risk of vertical transmission ranges from 15% to 45%; however, comprehensive PMTCT programs can reduce this risk to below 5%. The continuum of PMTCT care begins with routine HIV testing for all pregnant women during their first antenatal care (ANC) visit. If a mother tests positive, she is immediately initiated on lifelong ART, regardless of her CD4 count, to achieve viral suppression. Intrapartum interventions include safe obstetric practices, such as avoiding unnecessary invasive procedures, and administering prophylactic ARVs to the newborn shortly after birth (typically within 6 to 12 hours). Infant feeding counseling is also a critical component: mothers are guided on either exclusive replacement feeding (if safe, feasible, affordable, sustainable, and hygienic) or exclusive breastfeeding while adhering strictly to maternal ART and providing infant prophylaxis. Early Infant Diagnosis (EID) using DNA PCR testing at 6 weeks of age is essential to confirm the infant's HIV status and ensure timely initiation of pediatric ART if positive.

Managing Opportunistic Infections and Complications

As HIV advances and the immune system weakens, individuals become highly susceptible to opportunistic infections (OIs) and other systemic complications. Common opportunistic infections include tuberculosis (TB), which is a leading cause of mortality among people living with HIV, cryptococcal meningitis, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), severe candidiasis, toxoplasmosis, and cytomegalovirus (CMV) infections. Managing these conditions requires a dual approach: treating the acute infection aggressively with targeted antimicrobial or antifungal therapy, and initiating or optimizing ART to restore immune function. Preventive interventions, such as Isoniazid Preventive Therapy (IPT) for latent TB and Cotrimoxazole preventive therapy (CPT), are standard nursing and medical protocols implemented to protect patients from opportunistic pathogens like Pneumocystis and toxoplasmosis. Nurses must maintain high clinical vigilance, routinely screening patients for chronic cough, persistent fever, neurological changes, and visual disturbances during every clinic visit. Prompt recognition, rigorous multidisciplinary collaboration, and adherence support are essential to reducing morbidity and mortality associated with advanced HIV disease.

Stigma Reduction, Psychosocial Support, and Home-Based Care

Stigma, discrimination, and inadequate psychosocial support remain formidable barriers to effective HIV prevention, testing, and treatment adherence. Internalized and societal stigma often leads patients to conceal their status, avoid clinical appointments, and experience severe anxiety, depression, and social isolation. Nurses play a pivotal role in creating a welcoming, non-judgmental healthcare environment where clients feel safe, respected, and heard. Psychosocial support structures, including peer support groups, counseling services, and active involvement of family members, are crucial in helping clients cope with a chronic diagnosis and maintain lifelong adherence to ART. Furthermore, home-based care (HBC) models are invaluable in providing continuous nursing support, palliative care, and monitoring for patients who are bedridden, chronically ill, or living in remote communities far from health facilities. Home-based care involves training family caregivers, managing chronic symptoms, ensuring nutritional support, and facilitating smooth referrals back to primary health care centers. By championing stigma reduction and holistic care, nursing professionals empower clients to lead healthy, productive, and dignified lives.
