Cancer

Introduction to Cancer and the Primary Health Care Context

Cancer is a major public health concern globally and increasingly in Nigeria. It is characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells, which can form masses of tissue called tumors, invade surrounding structures, and metastasize to distant parts of the body. In the Nigerian healthcare delivery system, Primary Health Care (PHC) serves as the first point of contact for individuals and families. Consequently, PHC nurses play a pivotal role in cancer control, acting as frontline educators, advocates, and health promotion agents. Many cancer patients in Nigeria present late to tertiary facilities due to a lack of awareness, financial constraints, and reliance on traditional or spiritual remedies. Integrating basic oncology education and screening into PHC bridges this gap, ensuring that communities receive early guidance and appropriate referrals. By understanding the fundamentals of cancer within the primary health care framework, student nurses can better appreciate how grassroots interventions reduce late-stage presentations and improve overall survival outcomes across urban and rural populations in Nigeria.

Pathophysiology: How Cancer Develops

The development of cancer, or carcinogenesis, is a multi-step process rooted in genetic alterations. Normal cells possess tightly regulated mechanisms for growth, division, and programmed cell death, known as apoptosis. Carcinogenesis typically begins with initiation, where a mutation occurs in the cellular DNA due to physical, chemical, or biological carcinogens. This is followed by promotion, a process where altered cells are stimulated to divide more rapidly than normal. Finally, progression involves further genetic mutations that confer malignant characteristics, such as invasion of basement membranes and angiogenesis, which is the ability to form new blood vessels to feed the growing tumor. Tumors can be benign, meaning they remain localized and do not spread, or malignant, meaning they invade surrounding tissues and metastasize through the blood or lymphatic systems. Understanding these cellular mechanisms helps nursing students comprehend why targeted therapies and chemotherapeutic agents act on specific phases of the cell cycle to halt abnormal cellular proliferation.

Common Types of Cancer in Nigeria

In Nigeria, the burden of cancer is heavily skewed toward specific malignancies that present unique challenges for the healthcare system. Breast cancer and cervical cancer are the leading malignancies among Nigerian women, causing significant morbidity and mortality. Breast cancer often presents as a painless lump, while cervical cancer is strongly linked to persistent high-risk Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infections. Among men, prostate cancer is the most prevalent malignancy, frequently diagnosed at advanced stages with bone metastasis due to a lack of routine screening protocols. Additionally, liver cancer remains common, largely driven by high rates of chronic Hepatitis B and C infections, as well as exposure to dietary aflatoxins. Burkitt lymphoma and Kaposi sarcoma are also frequently encountered in pediatric and immunocompromised populations respectively. For nursing students, recognizing these predominant cancer types is essential for tailoring effective community education, supporting screening initiatives, and providing culturally competent care to Nigerian patients.

Risk Factors and Causes of Cancer

Cancer development is influenced by a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle risk factors. Tobacco use, excessive alcohol consumption, unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, and obesity are major modifiable lifestyle risks associated with various cancers. Environmental and occupational exposures, such as asbestos, radiation, and certain chemical dyes, also elevate risk. Infectious agents play a prominent role in the cancer burden of developing nations; these include HPV for cervical cancer, Hepatitis B and C for liver cancer, and Epstein-Barr virus for certain lymphomas. Genetic predisposition accounts for a smaller percentage of cancers, though inherited mutations in genes such as BRCA1 and BRCA2 significantly increase the risk of breast and ovarian cancers. Understanding these risk factors enables nurses to conduct targeted health education campaigns, empowering individuals to modify lifestyle behaviors, seek vaccinations like the HPV vaccine, and undergo genetic counseling when indicated.

Early Detection and Screening in Primary Care

Early detection and screening are cornerstones of effective cancer control, particularly at the primary health care level. Screening aims to identify pre-cancerous lesions or early-stage malignancies in asymptomatic individuals, thereby drastically improving treatment success rates. In the Nigerian context, primary care nurses utilize cost-effective and accessible screening methods. For breast cancer, methods include clinical breast examinations (CBE) and teaching monthly breast self-examinations (BSE). For cervical cancer, Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) and Pap smears are vital tools for detecting cellular changes before they progress to invasive carcinoma. Prostate cancer screening involves digital rectal examinations (DRE) and Prostate-Specific Antigen (PSA) blood tests, especially for men aged forty and above or those with a family history. Nurses must actively combat cultural myths and stigma surrounding screening procedures, creating a supportive environment where patients feel comfortable undergoing routine examinations.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Signs

Clinical assessment of a patient with suspected cancer requires a thorough history and physical examination. Nurses must be vigilant in recognizing the classic warning signs of malignancy, often summarized by the mnemonic CAUTION: Change in bowel or bladder habits, A sore that does not heal, Unusual bleeding or discharge, Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere, Indigestion or difficulty in swallowing, Obvious change in a wart or mole, and Nagging cough or hoarseness. Other systemic signs include unexplained weight loss, persistent fatigue, night sweats, and low-grade fevers of unknown origin. Diagnostic confirmation typically requires histopathological examination through a biopsy, where tissue samples are evaluated by a pathologist. Blood tests, tumor markers, and imaging modalities such as ultrasound, X-rays, and CT scans further stage the disease. Nurses play an essential role in preparing patients psychologically and physically for these diagnostic procedures.

Nursing Management and Supportive Care

Nursing management of cancer patients is comprehensive, addressing both the physical disease and its distressing symptoms. Oncological treatments include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, each carrying distinct side effects. Nurses manage treatment-related complications such as myelosuppression (leading to risk of infection, anemia, and bleeding), nausea, vomiting, alopecia, and mucositis. Supportive care is vital to maintain the patient's quality of life throughout the illness trajectory. This involves rigorous pain management following the WHO analgesic ladder, nutritional support, hydration, and meticulous skin and mucous membrane care. Psychological and emotional support are equally crucial, as cancer diagnosis often triggers anxiety, depression, and existential distress for both the patient and their family. Nurses coordinate interdisciplinary care, advocate for the patient's rights, and educate them on self-care management strategies while undergoing debilitating therapies.

Community Education, Prevention, and Palliative Care

Community education, prevention, and palliative care form the final pillar of a robust cancer control program. Public health education campaigns must be culturally sensitive, using local languages and community leaders to dispel myths, reduce stigma, and promote early health-seeking behaviors across Nigeria. Prevention strategies focus on eliminating exposure to tobacco, promoting healthy nutrition, and expanding vaccination coverage against oncogenic viruses like HPV and Hepatitis B. For patients with advanced, life-limiting disease where cure is no longer possible, palliative care becomes the primary focus of nursing intervention. Palliative care aims to relieve pain and other physical symptoms while addressing psychological, social, and spiritual needs to enhance the quality of life for patients and their families. Nurses provide compassionate end-of-life care, support grieving families, and advocate for home-based palliative care models that respect the cultural values and dignity of Nigerian patients.
