Cancer

Introduction to Cancer and the Primary Health Care Context

Cancer is not a single disease, but a broad group of over two hundred different diseases characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells. If the spread is not controlled, it can result in death. In the primary health care setting, nurses are often the first point of contact for patients presenting with unusual, persistent symptoms. Understanding cancer from a grassroots perspective allows community health nurses to bridge the gap between complex hospital oncology and local health posts.

In Nigeria, the burden of cancer is rising rapidly, straining primary health care facilities that are traditionally equipped primarily for infectious diseases like malaria and tuberculosis. Nurses must recognize that cancer care is no longer just a tertiary hospital concern. Community-level screening, early identification of warning signs, and basic pain management are vital nursing responsibilities at the primary care level.

Pathophysiology: How Cancer Develops

The development of cancer, known as carcinogenesis, is a multi-step process involving genetic mutations. Normally, a cell grows, divides, and dies in an orderly fashion regulated by DNA. When mutations occur in proto-oncogenes (which promote cell growth) or tumor suppressor genes (which normally halt cell division), this balance is destroyed.

  • Initiation: A permanent DNA mutation occurs, often triggered by a carcinogen like tobacco smoke, chemicals, or viral infections.
  • Promotion: Various factors stimulate the mutated cell to divide repeatedly, forming a pre-cancerous lesion or benign tumor.
  • Progression: Additional mutations allow the cells to invade surrounding blood vessels and lymphatic channels, acquiring the ability to metastasize to distant organs.

Common Types of Cancer in Nigeria

Certain cancers carry a much higher burden in the Nigerian population due to a mix of genetic, environmental, and infectious factors. Breast and cervical cancers are the most frequent malignancies among Nigerian women, while prostate and liver cancers heavily impact men.

  • Breast Cancer: Often presents as a painless lump. Late presentation is common due to cultural stigma and reliance on alternative therapies.
  • Cervical Cancer: Strongly linked to persistent high-risk Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Preventable through vaccination and routine screening.
  • Prostate Cancer: Prevalent among older men, presenting with urinary difficulties. Screening via digital rectal examination and PSA blood tests is vital.
  • Liver Cancer: Frequently secondary to chronic hepatitis B and C infections, as well as aflatoxin exposure from improperly stored grains.

Risk Factors and Causes of Cancer

Cancer arises from an interaction between a person's genetic makeup and external agents. While some risk factors cannot be altered, many are preventable through lifestyle modifications and public health interventions.

  • Tobacco Use: Responsible for lung and various other respiratory and digestive tract cancers.
  • Infectious Agents: Hepatitis B and C cause liver cancer; HPV causes cervical cancer; Helicobacter pylori increases stomach cancer risk.
  • Dietary and Lifestyle Factors: Diets high in processed meats, physical inactivity, obesity, and heavy alcohol consumption.
  • Environmental Exposures: Prolonged exposure to sunlight (ultraviolet radiation), industrial chemicals, asbestos, and indoor air pollution from biomass fuel.

Early Detection and Screening in Primary Care

Early detection saves lives by catching malignancies before they metastasize. Primary care nurses play an indispensable role in promoting and performing screening tests appropriate for their communities.

  • Clinical Breast Examination (CBE): Physical examination of the breasts performed by a trained healthcare worker.
  • Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA): A simple, low-cost screening method for cervical cancer where vinegar is applied to the cervix to highlight pre-cancerous lesions.
  • Self-Examinations: Educating patients on monthly breast self-awareness and prompt reporting of any skin or testicular changes.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Signs

Nurses must be vigilant for clinical warning signs that suggest underlying malignancy. The classic mnemonic CAUTION summarizes key diagnostic red flags:

  • C: Change in bowel or bladder habits.
  • A: A sore that does not heal.
  • U: Unusual bleeding or discharge.
  • T: Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere.
  • I: Indigestion or difficulty in swallowing.
  • O: Obvious change in a wart or mole.
  • N: Nagging cough or hoarseness.

Diagnostic confirmation usually requires tissue biopsy, imaging studies (like ultrasound or X-ray), and laboratory blood work. Nurses prepare patients physically and psychologically for these investigations.

Nursing Management and Supportive Care

Nursing care for cancer patients goes beyond administering medications; it encompasses holistic management of physical symptoms and psychological distress. Common challenges include cancer-related fatigue, severe pain, nausea, and emotional vulnerability.

Pain management is a cornerstone of nursing care. Nurses should follow the WHO analgesic ladder, administering pain relievers regularly rather than only when pain becomes severe. Nutritional support is equally critical, as tumor metabolic changes and treatments often lead to severe weight loss and cachexia. Nurses encourage small, frequent, nutrient-dense meals and monitor hydration status closely.

Community Education, Prevention, and Palliative Care

When a cure is no longer possible, the focus of nursing care shifts gracefully to palliative care. Palliative care aims to improve the quality of life for patients and their families facing life-threatening illness through the prevention and relief of suffering.

Community education remains our strongest weapon against late-stage cancer presentations. Nurses must dispel myths—such as the belief that cancer is purely a spiritual attack or highly contagious—and encourage open dialogue in churches, mosques, and market squares. By integrating compassionate end-of-life care with robust primary prevention, nurses uphold dignity and comfort for every patient.
