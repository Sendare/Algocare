Cancer

Introduction to Cancer and the Primary Health Care Context

Cancer represents a broad group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells. If this cellular proliferation remains unchecked, it can result in death. In the Nigerian healthcare system, primary health care (PHC) centers serve as the first point of contact for communities, making them critical settings for early recognition and referral. Although advanced oncology services are concentrated in tertiary teaching hospitals, grassroots health workers bear the responsibility of identifying suspicious lesions, managing chronic pain, and dispelling cultural myths that attribute malignancies entirely to spiritual attacks or witchcraft. Integrating basic oncology awareness into primary care reduces the burden on secondary and tertiary facilities by ensuring timely interventions.

Pathophysiology: How Cancer Develops

Tumor genesis begins at the genetic level through damage or mutation to deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA). Normal cells follow a strict cycle of growth, division, and programmed cell death known as apoptosis. When proto-oncogenes mutate into active oncogenes, or when tumor suppressor genes are inactivated, cells lose their regulatory brakes.

  • Initiation: A permanent DNA mutation occurs, often induced by carcinogens, altering the genetic blueprint.
  • Promotion: Various factors stimulate the initiated cell to divide repeatedly, forming a pre-cancerous lesion.
  • Progression: Accumulated genetic changes grant the cells invasive capabilities and angiogenesis, allowing them to recruit their own blood supply and metastasize to distant organs.

Common Types of Cancer in Nigeria

The epidemiological landscape of oncology in Nigeria features a high burden of specific malignancies that heavily impact morbidity and mortality. Breast and cervical cancers dominate among women, while prostate cancer remains a leading diagnosis for men. Additionally, liver cancer linked to chronic hepatitis infections and non-Hodgkin lymphomas, including Burkitt lymphoma in children, present frequently in local clinical settings. Late presentation is a unifying challenge across these types, often driven by financial constraints, reliance on traditional remedies, and fear of social stigmatization.

Risk Factors and Causes of Cancer

Malignancies rarely stem from a single source; rather, they arise from complex interactions between genetic predispositions and environmental exposures. Tobacco use and heavy alcohol consumption remain major modifiable behavioral risks. Infectious agents also play a prominent role in sub-Saharan Africa: Human Papillomavirus (HPV) drives cervical cancer, Hepatitis B and C viruses lead to hepatocellular carcinoma, and Epstein-Barr virus is linked to specific lymphomas. Occupational exposures, such as chronic contact with industrial chemicals, asbestos, and ionizing radiation, further compound individual vulnerability.

Early Detection and Screening in Primary Care

Because curative outcomes depend heavily on the stage at which treatment begins, screening programs are essential tools in community health. Nurses working in primary care settings must actively promote and perform clinical breast examinations, teach self-breast examination techniques, and facilitate visual inspection of the cervix using acetic acid (VIA). Public health outreach should dismantle misconceptions and encourage individuals to seek evaluation for persistent, unexplained physical changes before overt pain manifests.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Signs

Recognizing warning signs enables nurses to triage patients effectively. The classic acronym CAUTION highlights key indicators: Change in bowel or bladder habits, A sore that does not heal, Unusual bleeding or discharge, Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere, Indigestion or difficulty swallowing, Obvious change in a wart or mole, and Nagging cough or hoarseness. Diagnostic confirmation, however, requires formal medical evaluation, including imaging studies, tumor marker blood tests, and histopathological analysis via tissue biopsy.

Nursing Management and Supportive Care

Caring for patients undergoing anti-cancer therapies requires rigorous, multi-system nursing interventions. Cytotoxic treatments such as chemotherapy and radiation damage rapidly dividing normal cells alongside malignant ones, resulting in side effects like myelosuppression, alopecia, mucositis, and severe nausea.

  • Infection Prevention: Monitor neutrophil counts closely; educate patients on avoiding crowds and raw foods.
  • Nutritional Support: Manage cachexia and taste alterations with small, frequent, high-calorie meals.
  • Symptom Relief: Administer prescribed antiemetics preemptively before chemotherapy cycles begin.

Community Education, Prevention, and Palliative Care

Comprehensive cancer care extends beyond acute treatment into community advocacy and end-of-life comfort. Nurses play a pivotal role in leading health campaigns that promote tobacco cessation, safe dietary practices, and vaccination against oncogenic viruses like HPV and Hepatitis B. For patients with advanced, incurable disease, palliative care becomes the cornerstone of nursing practice. This approach prioritizes holistic relief from physical pain, psychological distress, and spiritual suffering, ensuring dignity and quality of life for both the patient and their grieving family.
