Lassa Fever

Introduction and Epidemiology of Lassa Fever in Nigeria

Lassa fever is an acute viral haemorrhagic illness that remains a major public health concern in Nigeria. The disease is endemic in parts of West Africa, with Nigeria recording outbreaks almost every year, particularly during the dry season between November and May. This seasonal spike corresponds with increased agricultural burning and farming activities that drive the primary animal vector closer to human settlements. Understanding the epidemiological pattern helps student nurses anticipate surge periods in clinical wards and prepare public health responses before case numbers overwhelm facility resources.

Aetiology and Vector: The Role of the Mastomys Rat

The causative agent of this illness is the Lassa virus, a member of the Arenaviridae family. The natural reservoir and primary vector is the multimammate rat, scientifically known as *Mastomys natalensis*.

  • High Breeding Capacity: These rodents reproduce rapidly, producing large litters that sustain high viral circulation in nature.
  • Asymptomatic Carriage: Infected rats shed the virus in their urine, faeces, and saliva without showing any signs of illness themselves.
  • Domestic Habitation: They frequently live in and around human homes, especially where food is stored improperly in open containers.

Modes of Transmission and High-Risk Exposure

Humans primarily contract Lassa fever through contact with food or household items contaminated with the urine or faeces of infected rats. Direct contact with infected rodents, such as trapping, handling, or preparing them as a food source, is another major route.

Person-to-person transmission occurs through direct contact with blood, urine, faeces, or other bodily secretions of an infected individual. This poses a severe hazard to healthcare workers who fail to practice strict barrier nursing. Casual contact and airborne spread through normal social interaction do not transmit the virus.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

Once the virus enters the body, it targets the endothelial cells, causing generalized vascular leakage, poor blood clotting, and multi-organ dysfunction. The incubation period ranges from 6 to 21 days.

  • Early Symptoms: Gradual onset of fever, general weakness, headache, sore throat, and muscle aches.
  • Progressive Signs: Nausea, vomiting, chest pain, and abdominal pain.
  • Severe Complications: Facial swelling, fluid in the lung cavity, bleeding from mucosal sites, and profound sensorineural deafness in survivors.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Early recognition saves lives, but Lassa fever mimics common tropical illnesses like malaria and typhoid. Nurses must conduct a thorough health history, asking about travel to endemic zones, contact with rodents, or exposure to known cases.

Laboratory confirmation relies on Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) assay, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) serology, and virus isolation. Routine baseline tests often reveal elevated liver enzymes, proteinuria, and thrombocytopenia, reinforcing the suspicion of viral haemorrhagic fever.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Treatment

Prompt administration of antiviral therapy is the cornerstone of survival. Ribavirin is the specific antiviral drug of choice for Lassa fever. It works best when initiated within the first six days of symptom onset.

Supportive care is equally critical. Medical teams manage fluid and electrolyte balance, administer blood products for severe bleeding, and treat secondary bacterial infections with broad-spectrum antibiotics. Careful monitoring prevents fluid overload, which can worsen pulmonary edema in fragile patients.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Management

Providing holistic nursing care for a patient with Lassa fever demands meticulous organization and physical stamina.

  • Vital Signs Monitoring: Track temperature, blood pressure, and pulse hourly to detect early signs of hypovolemic or septic shock.
  • Fluid Balance Tracking: Maintain strict intake and output charts. Measure urine output hourly to assess renal function and prevent acute kidney injury.
  • Comfort and Symptom Relief: Administer prescribed analgesics for severe body aches and antipyretics to control high fever.
  • Nutritional Support: Encourage high-calorie, high-protein soft diets or administer enteral feeds as tolerated to combat profound catabolism.

Infection Prevention and Control in Primary Health Care

Primary health care clinics are often the first point of contact for patients. Standard precautions alone are insufficient when handling suspected viral haemorrhagic fevers.

Healthcare workers must wear full personal protective equipment, including gowns, gloves, face shields, and masks when entering isolation zones. Strict hand hygiene before and after patient contact is mandatory. Safe disposal of infectious waste, proper decontamination of instruments with chlorine solutions, and restricting unnecessary visitors protect staff and prevent nosocomial outbreaks.

Community Health Education and Disease Surveillance

Community engagement remains the most effective tool for long-term control. Nurses must lead public health campaigns to educate rural and urban populations on proper food storage. Food items like garri, maize, and beans should be stored in tightly sealed, rodent-proof containers rather than left in open bowls.

Communities must also learn to stop hunting and consuming rodents, practice good hand hygiene, and report suspected fever cases early to designated health facilities. Active surveillance by community health volunteers ensures quick isolation of new cases and curbs community spread.
