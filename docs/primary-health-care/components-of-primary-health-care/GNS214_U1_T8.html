Lassa Fever

Introduction and Epidemiology of Lassa Fever in Nigeria

Lassa fever is an acute viral haemorrhagic illness that remains a major public health concern across several West African nations, particularly Nigeria. First isolated in the Nigerian town of Lassa in Borno State during 1969, the disease is endemic in the region, with seasonal surges typically peaking during the dry season between November and May. These surges correlate directly with agricultural practices such as bush burning, which drives rodent populations closer to human dwellings in search of food and shelter. Healthcare workers face substantial occupational risks, especially when viral load is high and standard precautions are overlooked during initial patient presentation.

Aetiology and Vector: The Role of the Mastomys Rat

The primary reservoir for the Lassa virus is the multimammate rat, belonging to the genus **Mastomys**, most notably **Mastomys natalensis**. These rodents are prolific breeders widely distributed throughout rural and peri-urban settlements in sub-Saharan Africa. Infected rodents shed the virus in their urine, droppings, and saliva throughout their lifespan without necessarily falling ill themselves. Human exposure usually occurs when grain stores and household food supplies are contaminated by these secretions, turning domestic kitchens into primary sites of zoonotic spillover.

Modes of Transmission and High-Risk Exposure

While zoonotic transmission via contaminated food or direct contact with rodent excreta initiates most outbreaks, human-to-human transmission sustains epidemic clusters. Secondary spread occurs through direct contact with blood, urine, faeces, semen, or other bodily secretions of an infected person. Nosocomial transmission remains a severe hazard in under-resourced clinical settings where personal protective equipment is scarce. Inhalation of infectious aerosol particles generated during medical procedures, such as intubation or suctioning, also puts attending staff in extreme danger.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

Following an incubation period ranging from one to three weeks, the Lassa virus targets endothelial cells, leading to vascular leakage, hypotension, and multi-organ dysfunction in severe cases. The clinical picture varies widely, with approximately eighty percent of infections remaining asymptomatic or producing mild, non-specific symptoms. - **Early symptoms**: Gradual onset of fever, general malaise, weakness, and severe headache. - **Gastrointestinal signs**: Persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, and diarrhoea. - **Respiratory signs**: Cough, chest pain, and pharyngitis with characteristic exudative patches. - **Advanced complications**: Facial oedema, mucosal bleeding, shock, and sensorineural deafness.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Comprehensive nursing assessment begins with a meticulous travel and contact history, specifically probing for recent visits to endemic zones, contact with known cases, or evidence of rodent infestation in the home. Routine laboratory tests often reveal thrombocytopenia, elevated liver transaminases, and proteinuria. Definitive diagnosis relies on Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) assays or Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) serology performed in specialized biosafety laboratories. Nurses must handle all diagnostic specimens with extreme caution, adhering strictly to triple-packaging and infectious substance transport protocols.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Treatment

Early administration of antiviral therapy significantly reduces mortality rates in severe Lassa fever cases. **Ribavirin** is the specific nucleoside analogue utilized, administered intravenously or orally depending on disease severity. Supportive medical care is equally critical, focusing on aggressive correction of electrolyte imbalances, maintenance of hemodynamic stability with intravenous fluids, and management of secondary bacterial infections with appropriate broad-spectrum antibiotics. Blood transfusions may be required for patients presenting with severe haemorrhagic manifestations.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Management

The nursing care plan for a confirmed Lassa fever patient centers around hemodynamic monitoring, strict fluid balance charting, and meticulous supportive comfort measures. Nurses must monitor vital signs hourly, watching for sudden drops in blood pressure that signal impending shock. Oral hygiene and skin integrity require diligent attention, especially for patients suffering from mucosal ulcerations and severe lethargy. Psychological support is vital, as isolation wards can induce profound anxiety, fear, and depression in both the patient and their family members.

Infection Prevention and Control in Primary Health Care

Primary health care facilities serve as the first point of contact and must maintain high vigilance to prevent nosocomial outbreaks. Standard precautions must be supplemented with transmission-based droplet and contact precautions for any patient presenting with unexplained febrile illness during peak seasons. Personal protective equipment including water-resistant gowns, face shields, double gloves, and respiratory masks must be worn correctly during procedures generating splashes or aerosols. Safe burial protocols must also be enforced for individuals who succumb to the infection to prevent funeral-related transmission.

Community Health Education and Disease Surveillance

Community engagement forms the bedrock of sustainable Lassa fever prevention. Public health nurses lead outreach programs educating rural communities on proper food storage in airtight containers, prohibiting the hunting and consumption of rodents, and keeping domestic environments clean. Surveillance systems rely on rapid reporting of suspected cases from primary clinics to higher-level public health authorities. Empowering local leaders and traditional birth attendants with early recognition skills ensures timely referrals before complications overwhelm local health facilities.
