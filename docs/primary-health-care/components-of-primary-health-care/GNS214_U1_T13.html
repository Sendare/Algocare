Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder marked by high blood glucose levels resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Insulin is a hormone produced by the beta cells of the pancreas that allows glucose from the bloodstream to enter body cells for energy. When this system fails, glucose accumulates in the blood, leading to hyperglycemia, while cells are starved of their primary energy source.

  • Type 1 Diabetes: An autoimmune condition where the body's immune system destroys pancreatic beta cells, resulting in absolute insulin deficiency. Patients require lifelong exogenous insulin replacement.
  • Type 2 Diabetes: Characterized by insulin resistance, where body tissues fail to respond effectively to insulin, combined with a progressive decline in insulin secretion. It accounts for the majority of diabetes cases globally and locally.
  • Gestational Diabetes: Glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy, typically resolving after delivery but increasing the mother's future risk of developing overt Type 2 diabetes.

Clinical Manifestations and Risk Factors

Recognizing the classic signs of diabetes allows for early clinical identification, especially in busy primary health care settings across Nigeria. The hallmark symptoms stem from high blood sugar spilling into the urine and drawing water along with it.

  1. Polyuria: Frequent urination caused by osmotic diuresis as excess glucose pulls water into the renal tubules.
  2. Polydipsia: Excessive thirst triggered by ongoing fluid loss through frequent urination.
  3. Polyphagia: Increased hunger and appetite because body cells lack glucose for energy despite high circulating blood sugar.
  4. Unexplained Weight Loss: Rapid loss of body mass, particularly common in Type 1 diabetes, as the body breaks down fats and proteins for energy.

Common risk factors include increasing age, physical inactivity, family history, obesity (particularly central adiposity), and a history of gestational diabetes. Cultural dietary shifts and urbanization in Nigeria have also contributed to rising prevalence rates.

Screening, Diagnosis, and Blood Glucose Monitoring

Accurate diagnosis relies on biochemical testing of blood glucose levels. Healthcare providers utilize specific diagnostic criteria to differentiate normal glucose metabolism from prediabetes and overt diabetes.

Fasting Plasma GlucoseBelow 5.6 mmol/L7.0 mmol/L or higher
Oral Glucose Tolerance TestBelow 7.8 mmol/L11.1 mmol/L or higher

Routine self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) using a glucometer empowers patients to track daily fluctuations, evaluate responses to meals and physical activity, and detect episodes of hypoglycemia early. Nurses must teach patients proper finger-prick technique, device calibration, and how to safely dispose of lancets to prevent injury and infection.

Pharmacological Management: Oral Agents and Insulin

When lifestyle measures alone fail to maintain target blood glucose levels, pharmacological agents are introduced. The choice of medication depends on the type of diabetes, renal function, patient preference, and local availability.

  • Metformin: The first-line oral hypoglycemic agent for Type 2 diabetes. It works primarily by reducing glucose production in the liver and improving insulin sensitivity in muscle tissues.
  • Sulfonylureas: Agents such as glibenclamide that stimulate the pancreas to secrete more insulin. A major side effect nurses must watch for is hypoglycemia.
  • Insulin Therapy: Essential for all Type 1 diabetes patients and often required for advanced Type 2 cases. Insulin is classified by its onset, peak action, and duration (rapid-acting, short-acting, intermediate-acting, and long-acting). It is administered via subcutaneous injection, and rotation of injection sites is vital to prevent lipodystrophy.

Medical Nutrition Therapy and Lifestyle Modification

Medical nutrition therapy forms the bedrock of diabetes care. Dietary counseling should respect local culinary traditions, incorporating culturally appropriate staples while moderating simple carbohydrate intake and increasing dietary fiber.

Patients are encouraged to consume whole grains, legumes, vegetables, and lean proteins while minimizing sugary beverages and heavily processed foods. Portion control is vital, especially with carbohydrate-heavy Nigerian dishes like pounded yam, garri, and rice.

Regular physical activity improves insulin sensitivity, assists with weight management, and lowers cardiovascular risk. Nurses should advise patients to engage in at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise per week, such as brisk walking, dancing, or cycling, while remaining mindful of pre-exercise blood glucose checks to avoid exercise-induced hypoglycemia.

Acute and Chronic Complications

Uncontrolled diabetes leads to severe acute emergencies and debilitating long-term complications affecting multiple organ systems.

  • Hypoglycemia: Low blood sugar (below 3.9 mmol/L) caused by skipped meals, excess exercise, or too much medication. Symptoms include sweating, trembling, confusion, and palpitations. Treated promptly with fast-acting oral glucose or sweet drinks if conscious.
  • Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA): An acute emergency seen mostly in Type 1 diabetes, characterized by severe hyperglycemia, dehydration, ketosis, and metabolic acidosis. Requires urgent intravenous fluids, insulin, and electrolyte replacement.
  • Chronic Complications: Long-term vascular damage leads to microvascular problems (diabetic retinopathy causing blindness, nephropathy leading to kidney failure, and neuropathy causing foot ulcers) and macrovascular problems (cardiovascular disease, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease).

Daily foot inspections are critical for preventing diabetic foot ulcers and subsequent amputations due to nerve damage and poor circulation.

Patient Education, Self-Care, and Community Support

Empowering patients with knowledge is the cornerstone of effective chronic disease management. Because diabetes is managed largely by the patient at home, comprehensive education must cover medication administration, blood glucose monitoring, foot care, and emergency action plans for illness or low blood sugar.

Community-based support groups and peer education programs play a vital role in Nigerian healthcare settings. These networks reduce social isolation, encourage adherence to lifestyle changes, and provide practical coping strategies shared by fellow patients facing similar daily challenges. Nurses act as educators, advocates, and coordinators, bridging the gap between clinical facilities and community resources to foster long-term health and wellbeing.
