Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction and Overview of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. The body's inability to properly process glucose disrupts carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism, leading to widespread systemic effects over time. In primary healthcare settings across Nigeria, managing this condition requires understanding both the physiological burden and the socioeconomic challenges patients face when accessing continuous care.

Pathophysiology and Classification

Understanding the distinct types of diabetes directs appropriate clinical intervention. The condition is broadly grouped into primary categories based on underlying etiology. - **Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus**: Characterized by autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells, leading to absolute insulin deficiency. Patients require lifelong exogenous insulin replacement. - **Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus**: Driven by a combination of peripheral insulin resistance and progressive secretory defect by pancreatic beta cells. This form accounts for the vast majority of global and local diagnoses. - **Gestational Diabetes Mellitus**: Any degree of glucose intolerance recognized for the second or third trimester of pregnancy, resolving postpartum in most cases but increasing long-term maternal risk for overt type 2 diabetes.

Risk Factors and Clinical Manifestations

Clinical presentation often brings patients to healthcare facilities long after the disease process has begun. Recognizing both classic signs and contextual risk factors speeds up diagnosis. - **Classic Triad**: Polyuria (frequent urination), polydipsia (excessive thirst), and polyphagia (excessive hunger). - **Constitutional Signs**: Unexplained weight loss, chronic fatigue, and blurred vision resulting from osmotic fluid shifts in the eye lens. - **Risk Factors**: Advancing age, family history, central obesity, sedentary lifestyle, and urban dietary shifts common in modern Nigerian cities.

Screening, Diagnosis, and Blood Glucose Monitoring

Accurate identification relies on standardized biochemical testing. Fasting plasma glucose, oral glucose tolerance tests, and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) serve as primary diagnostic benchmarks. In resource-limited primary care settings, capillary blood glucose via glucometers provides rapid point-of-care data, though venous confirmation remains standard for formal diagnosis. HbA1c offers a retrospective view of average glycemic control over the preceding two to three months, reducing the confounding effects of acute stress or recent meals.

Pharmacological Management

When lifestyle adjustments fail to achieve glycemic targets, pharmacological therapy becomes necessary. Treatment strategies depend heavily on the specific type of diabetes and underlying organ function. | Medication Class | Primary Mechanism | Clinical Consideration | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Biguanides (Metformin)** | Decreases hepatic glucose production and improves insulin sensitivity | First-line oral agent for Type 2; monitor renal function | | **Sulfonylureas** | Stimulates insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells | Risk of hypoglycemia if meals are delayed | | **Insulin Therapy** | Replaces endogenous insulin; facilitates cellular glucose uptake | Requires subcutaneous injection and rigorous self-monitoring |

Medical Nutrition Therapy and Lifestyle Modification

Food choices and physical activity form the bedrock of metabolic control. Nutrition therapy emphasizes balanced intake, prioritizing complex carbohydrates with low glycemic indices, lean proteins, and healthy fats while reducing refined sugars and sweetened beverages. Incorporating culturally appropriate local staples—such as substituting excessive processed starches with fiber-rich vegetables, beans, and unrefined grains—improves adherence. Regular aerobic and resistance exercise enhances insulin receptor sensitivity, facilitating glucose uptake into skeletal muscles without requiring massive insulin output.

Prevention and Management of Complications

Prolonged hyperglycemia damages microvascular and macrovascular structures across multiple organ systems. Microvascular complications include retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy, while macrovascular issues manifest as coronary artery disease, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease. Acute emergencies like diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state demand immediate fluid resuscitation, electrolyte correction, and insulin infusion. Routine clinical assessments—such as annual foot exams, visual checks, and urine microalbumin screening—are vital for catching early tissue damage before it becomes irreversible.

Patient Education, Self-Care, and Community Support

Empowering individuals to manage their own condition transforms outcomes in chronic disease care. Education must cover proper foot inspection, recognition of hypo- and hyperglycemia symptoms, sick-day rules, and correct administration techniques for medications. Because healthcare resources can be strained, leveraging community health networks, peer support groups, and family involvement helps sustain motivation and adherence. Nurses play a pivotal role in translating clinical directives into practical, culturally sensitive daily routines.
