Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Sickle Cell Anaemia

Sickle cell anaemia is an inherited blood disorder marked by an abnormality in the oxygen-carrying protein haemoglobin found inside red blood cells. Normal adult haemoglobin, known as haemoglobin A, keeps red blood cells flexible and disc-shaped so they move easily through tiny blood vessels. In sickle cell anaemia, a single genetic mutation causes the body to produce haemoglobin S. When haemoglobin S gives up its oxygen to body tissues, it sticks together and forms long, rigid rods inside the cell.

These stiff rods distort the soft red blood cell, pulling it into a crescent or sickle shape. Sickled cells are sticky and fragile, breaking apart prematurely and causing chronic haemolytic anaemia. Furthermore, their rigid shape causes them to clump together and block narrow blood vessels, stopping blood flow to downstream tissues. This blockage leads to low oxygen levels, severe pain, and progressive tissue damage across multiple organ systems.

Genetic Inheritance and Carrier Screening

Sickle cell anaemia follows an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern. This means a child must inherit two abnormal copies of the haemoglobin gene—one from each parent—to develop the full disease.

  • Genotype AA: Normal haemoglobin with no disease traits.
  • Genotype AS: Sickle cell trait (carrier). The person has one normal gene and one mutated gene. They are generally healthy but can pass the trait to their children.
  • Genotype SS: Sickle cell anaemia. The person has inherited two mutated genes and shows clinical symptoms of the disease.
  • Genotype SC or S-Beta Thalassemia: Other variant forms where sickle haemoglobin pairs with another abnormal blood protein.

Carrier screening and pre-marital genotype counselling are vital public health tools in regions with high disease prevalence. Identifying carrier status helps couples make informed reproductive choices to reduce the incidence of affected children.

Clinical Manifestations and Common Symptoms

The clinical presentation of sickle cell anaemia varies widely from person to person, but most symptoms stem from chronic anaemia and periodic blood vessel blockages.

  • Chronic Fatigue and Paleness: Due to the continuous destruction of fragile red blood cells, haemoglobin levels remain low, reducing the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood.
  • Jaundice: Yellowing of the eyes and skin occurs because the rapid breakdown of red blood cells releases large amounts of bilirubin into the bloodstream.
  • Dactylitis: Also known as hand-foot syndrome, this is often the earliest sign in infants, marked by painful swelling of the fingers and toes due to blocked blood flow in the small bones.
  • Splenomegaly and Auto-Splenectomy: In early childhood, the spleen enlarges as it traps sickled cells, but repeated damage eventually causes it to scar and shrink, leaving the patient vulnerable to severe infections.

Identifying and Managing Sickle Cell Crises

A sickle cell crisis is an acute, painful episode that occurs when sickled red blood cells block small blood vessels, cutting off oxygen to tissues and organs. These crises can be triggered by infections, dehydration, cold temperatures, physical stress, or low oxygen levels.

The most common medical emergency is the vaso-occlusive crisis, characterized by severe, throbbing bone or chest pain. Other dangerous types include sequestration crises, where a large volume of blood pools in the spleen or liver causing rapid shock, and aplastic crises, where viral infections temporarily halt red blood cell production in the bone marrow.

Nursing management during a crisis focuses on four primary goals: managing pain aggressively with prescribed analgesics, restoring hydration with oral or intravenous fluids, improving tissue oxygenation, and treating any underlying infection with antibiotics.

Diagnostic Tests and Routine Laboratory Investigations

Confirming the diagnosis of sickle cell anaemia requires specialized blood testing that looks at the types of haemoglobin present in the red blood cells.

  • Haemoglobin Electrophoresis: This is the gold standard test. It separates different types of haemoglobin based on electrical charge, clearly showing whether haemoglobin S, A, or other variants are present.
  • Full Blood Count (FBC): Reveals low haemoglobin levels, low red blood cell counts, and often an elevated reticulocyte count as the bone marrow tries to replace lost cells.
  • Sickle Solubility Test: A quick screening test that mixes blood with a chemical solution to see if haemoglobin S precipitates, though it cannot distinguish between the trait and the full disease.
  • Newborn Screening Programs: Heel-prick blood samples collected shortly after birth allow early detection, enabling preventative care before symptoms appear.

Primary Health Care Prevention and Public Health Strategies

Preventing acute crises and reducing complications requires proactive primary health care measures, especially in endemic malaria regions.

Malaria is a deadly trigger for sickle cell crises because parasite-infected red blood cells cause widespread sickling. Routine public health strategies include providing long-lasting insecticidal nets, prompt malaria testing, and intermittent preventive treatment.

Routine childhood immunizations, including pneumococcal and meningococcal vaccines, are essential because patients lack normal spleen function. Additionally, daily prophylactic penicillin is prescribed to children from infancy until at least age five to prevent overwhelming bacterial sepsis. Regular nutritional support with folic acid also helps the bone marrow maintain its high rate of red blood cell production.

Holistic Nursing Care and Patient Education

Holistic nursing care addresses the physical, psychological, and social dimensions of living with a chronic, painful condition. Patients and their families often face stigma, emotional distress, and financial strain due to frequent hospital visits.

Effective patient education focuses on self-management strategies. Nurses must teach families how to recognize early warning signs of a crisis, such as mild joint pain, fever, or unusual fatigue, so they can seek care before symptoms worsen. Hydration is a core daily message; patients must learn to drink plenty of water throughout the day, especially in hot weather or during physical activity.

Nurses should also advise patients to avoid extreme cold, wear loose clothing to prevent restricted circulation, and manage stress effectively. Supporting mental health and creating a trusting, non-judgmental environment during pain management is vital for comprehensive care.

Long-Term Complications and Multi-Disciplinary Follow-Up

As individuals with sickle cell anaemia live longer lives, managing chronic, long-term organ damage becomes a central focus of care. Repeated cycles of vessel blockage and low oxygen gradually injure major organs over time.

  • Avascular Necrosis: Loss of blood supply causes bone tissue in the hips or shoulders to die, leading to chronic joint pain and mobility issues.
  • Stroke: Blocked blood vessels in the brain can cause sudden strokes, making regular transcranial Doppler ultrasound screening essential for children.
  • Chronic Kidney Disease: Damage to the tiny blood vessels in the kidneys impairs their ability to filter waste, leading to protein loss and eventual renal failure.
  • Acute Chest Syndrome: A dangerous condition marked by chest pain, fever, and lung infiltrates that mimics pneumonia and requires urgent medical support.

Comprehensive management requires a coordinated multi-disciplinary team including paediatricians, haematologists, physiotherapists, social workers, and nephrologists to monitor organ function and improve quality of life.
