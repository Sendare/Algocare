Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Sickle Cell Anaemia

Sickle cell anaemia is a genetic blood disorder characterized by the presence of abnormal haemoglobin, known as haemoglobin S (HbS), instead of normal adult haemoglobin (HbA). When deoxygenated, HbS molecules polymerize and form rigid, rod-like structures that alter the pliable biconcave disc shape of normal red blood cells into a stiff, crescent or sickle shape. This structural distortion significantly reduces the deformability of the erythrocytes, making it difficult for them to pass smoothly through narrow microvasculature. As a result, these sickled cells cause microvascular occlusion, leading to ischemia, tissue infarction, and severe pain. Furthermore, sickle cells have a drastically shortened life span of approximately 10 to 20 days, compared to the normal 120 days, which overwhelms the reticuloendothelial system and leads to chronic haemolytic anaemia. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice in Nigeria, understanding this foundational pathophysiology is vital. It explains why patients experience chronic fatigue, jaundice from excessive bilirubin breakdown, and acute episodes of agonizing pain triggered by conditions that promote deoxygenation, such as hypoxia, dehydration, infection, and cold temperatures. Recognizing how sickled erythrocytes obstruct blood flow helps nurses anticipate clinical deteriorations, prioritize prompt hydration, administer supplemental oxygen when indicated, and manage pain effectively in emergency and ward settings.

Genetic Inheritance and Carrier Screening

Sickle cell anaemia is inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, meaning an individual must inherit two abnormal copies of the haemoglobin S gene—one from each parent—to manifest the disease (HbSS). Individuals who inherit only one abnormal gene and one normal gene are carriers, known as having sickle cell trait (HbAS). Carriers are typically asymptomatic under normal physiological conditions but can pass the gene to their offspring. In regions with a high prevalence of the sickle cell gene, such as Nigeria, premarital carrier screening and genetic counseling are paramount public health interventions. Nurses play a crucial role in educating couples about inheritance risks before marriage or conception. If both parents carry the trait, there is a 25 percent chance with each pregnancy of having a child with sickle cell anaemia, a 50 percent chance of having a child who is an asymptomatic carrier, and a 25 percent chance of having a child with normal haemoglobin. Genetic counseling helps prospective parents make informed reproductive choices, encourages early testing for newborns, and dispels cultural stigmas associated with the condition. Understanding these genetic principles allows nurses to provide empathetic, culturally sensitive education and support families navigating complex reproductive health decisions.

Clinical Manifestations and Common Symptoms

The clinical manifestations of sickle cell anaemia are diverse, affecting multiple organ systems due to chronic haemolysis and vaso-occlusion. One of the earliest signs in infants is dactylitis, commonly known as hand-foot syndrome, which presents as painful swelling of the hands and feet caused by microinfarctions in the small bones. Chronic anaemia leads to persistent fatigue, generalized weakness, pallor, and scleral icterus due to elevated indirect bilirubin from red blood cell breakdown. Children may experience delayed growth, delayed puberty, and an increased susceptibility to encapsulated bacterial infections like Streptococcus pneumoniae because functional asplenia develops early in life due to repeated splenic infarctions. Vaso-occlusive episodes can cause pain in the limbs, chest, back, and abdomen. Nurses caring for these patients must remain vigilant for signs of chronic organ damage and acute exacerbations. Assessing pain characteristics, monitoring developmental milestones, evaluating skin and sclera color for jaundice, and monitoring for signs of infection are fundamental nursing responsibilities. Recognizing these common symptoms early ensures timely medical intervention and prevents life-threatening complications, improving the overall quality of life for individuals living with the condition.

Identifying and Managing Sickle Cell Crises

Sickle cell crises are acute, severe exacerbations of symptoms that require immediate medical and nursing intervention. The most common type is the vaso-occlusive crisis, triggered by factors such as dehydration, infection, hypoxia, and extreme temperatures, where sickled cells block blood vessels, causing severe ischemic pain. Another critical emergency is the splenic sequestration crisis, characterized by sudden pooling of a large volume of blood in the spleen, leading to rapid enlargement, severe anaemia, hypovolemic shock, and potential death if untreated. Aplastic crises involve a sudden drop in red blood cell production, frequently triggered by parvovirus B19 infection, resulting in profound anaemia and lethargy. Acute chest syndrome is another life-threatening crisis marked by chest pain, fever, new infiltrates on chest radiography, and respiratory distress, resembling pneumonia or pulmonary infarction. As a nurse, managing these crises demands rapid assessment, prompt intravenous fluid resuscitation to reduce blood viscosity, scheduled and breakthrough pain management using appropriate analgesics, administration of supplemental oxygen if oxygen saturation is low, and careful monitoring of vital signs. Nurses must also educate patients and caregivers on recognizing early warning signs, maintaining optimal hydration, and avoiding known crisis triggers at home.

Diagnostic Tests and Routine Laboratory Investigations

Accurate diagnosis and ongoing monitoring of sickle cell anaemia rely on specific haematological and genetic laboratory investigations. The definitive diagnostic test is Haemoglobin Electrophoresis, a laboratory technique that separates different types of haemoglobin based on their electrical charge, allowing clinicians to identify the presence of Haemoglobin S, HbA, HbF, and HbC. In newborns, high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) or isoelectric focusing is commonly utilized for early screening. Routine laboratory investigations for diagnosed patients include a Complete Blood Count (CBC), which typically reveals normocytic or macrocytic anaemia with low haemoglobin and haematocrit levels, along with elevated reticulocyte counts indicating active bone marrow compensation for haemolysis. Peripheral blood smear examinations vividly display characteristic sickled erythrocytes, target cells, and Howell-Jolly bodies, which signify hyposplenism. Renal function tests, liver function tests, and urinalysis are also performed routinely to monitor for end-organ damage. For nursing professionals, interpreting these lab values is crucial for evaluating treatment efficacy, detecting complications such as worsening anaemia or infection, and communicating effectively with the multidisciplinary healthcare team to optimize patient outcomes.

Primary Health Care Prevention and Public Health Strategies

Primary health care prevention and public health strategies are vital in reducing the burden of sickle cell anaemia, particularly in endemic areas like Nigeria. Community-based education programs focus on raising awareness about premarital counselling and genetic screening to help reduce the incidence of affected births. Public health initiatives also emphasize routine newborn screening programs, enabling early diagnosis during the first few weeks of life so that prophylactic interventions can begin immediately. Standard prophylactic measures include daily oral penicillin administration in infants to prevent severe pneumococcal infections, alongside up-to-date immunizations against pneumococcus, meningococcus, influenza, and Haemophilus influenzae type b. Folic acid supplementation is routinely prescribed to support the increased rate of erythropoiesis and prevent megaloblastic anaemia. Nurses working in primary health care settings are at the forefront of these community interventions. They conduct screening outreaches, deliver health talks at antenatal and immunisation clinics, distribute prophylactic medications, and provide supportive counseling to families. By promoting early detection and preventive health practices, nurses significantly decrease morbidity and mortality rates associated with sickle cell anaemia in the community.

Holistic Nursing Care and Patient Education

Holistic nursing care for patients with sickle cell anaemia encompasses physical, psychological, and social dimensions, tailored to the unique needs of individuals across their lifespan. Patient and caregiver education forms the cornerstone of effective self-management. Nurses must educate patients on maintaining adequate hydration by drinking plenty of fluids daily, dressing appropriately to avoid extreme cold or overheating, avoiding strenuous physical exertion that leads to hypoxia, and recognizing early signs of infection or crisis. Psychological support is equally essential, as chronic pain and frequent hospitalizations often lead to anxiety, depression, and social isolation. Nurses provide empathetic listening, encourage participation in support groups, and involve medical social workers when financial or social challenges arise. Pain management requires a non-judgmental approach, acknowledging that patients are experts in their own pain experience; nurses should utilize prescribed analgesic ladders, administer medications promptly, and incorporate non-pharmacological comfort measures such as warm compresses and relaxation techniques. By addressing the whole person rather than just the disease pathology, nurses empower patients and their families to lead fulfilling, proactive lives.

Long-Term Complications and Multi-Disciplinary Follow-Up

As individuals with sickle cell anaemia age, they are at risk of developing long-term multi-organ complications that require coordinated, multi-disciplinary follow-up care. Chronic vaso-occlusion and repeated ischemic events can lead to progressive damage in organs such as the kidneys (resulting in papillary necrosis and chronic kidney disease), the bones (leading to avascular necrosis of the femoral or humeral heads), the central nervous system (increasing the risk of silent or overt strokes), and the lungs (causing pulmonary hypertension and chronic respiratory failure). Additionally, leg ulcers and retinopathy are common chronic manifestations. Managing these complex conditions necessitates a collaborative approach involving haematologists, paediatricians or adult physicians, nephrologists, orthopaedic surgeons, ophthalmologists, and specialized nurses. Nurses act as vital coordinators within this multi-disciplinary team, ensuring that patients attend scheduled specialist clinics, monitoring adherence to complex medication regimens like hydroxyurea, tracking baseline laboratory and imaging results, and advocating for comprehensive care plans. Through vigilant long-term follow-up and proactive intervention, the healthcare team can mitigate chronic organ damage and improve overall life expectancy.
