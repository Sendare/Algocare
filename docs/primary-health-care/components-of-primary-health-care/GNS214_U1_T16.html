Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Sickle Cell Anaemia

Sickle cell anaemia is a severe hereditary blood disorder characterized by the presence of abnormal haemoglobin, known as **haemoglobin S (HbS)**. Under conditions of low oxygen tension, dehydration, or acidosis, this abnormal haemoglobin polymerizes, forming rigid rods that distort the flexible, biconcave red blood cells into a characteristic crescent or sickle shape. These structurally altered cells lose their deformability and become sticky, leading to microvascular occlusion. The consequences are twofold: impaired tissue perfusion resulting in ischemic pain and organ damage, and premature destruction of the erythrocytes, known as **haemolysis**, which triggers chronic anaemia. In many resource-limited settings across Sub-Saharan Africa, this pathology manifests early in infancy and requires keen clinical observation to differentiate from other prevalent tropical morbidities like malaria.

Genetic Inheritance and Carrier Screening

The condition follows an **autosomal recessive** inheritance pattern. An individual must inherit two abnormal haemoglobin genes—one from each parent—to manifest sickle cell anaemia (HbSS). Those who inherit only one abnormal gene are termed carriers or possess the **sickle cell trait (HbAS)**, who are typically asymptomatic but capable of passing the gene to offspring. Pre-marital and neonatal screening programs are vital public health interventions. In Nigerian clinical practice, identifying genotype compatibility before marriage helps couples make informed reproductive choices. Genetic counseling must explain the statistical probabilities of inheritance clearly, avoiding stigmatization while emphasizing that carriers do not develop the full disease.

Clinical Manifestations and Common Symptoms

Clinical features of sickle cell anaemia typically emerge after six months of life, as fetal haemoglobin is gradually replaced by adult haemoglobin S. Manifestations vary widely from mild fatigue to life-threatening complications. - **Chronic Haemolytic Anaemia**: Causes baseline pallor, jaundice from bilirubin breakdown, fatigue, and generalized weakness. - **Dactylitis**: Painful swelling of the hands and feet, often called hand-foot syndrome, frequently the earliest presentation in infants. - **Splenic Sequestration**: Sudden trapping of blood within the spleen, causing rapid enlargement, severe anaemia, and hypovolemic shock. - **Growth Retardation**: Delayed puberty and delayed linear growth due to chronically increased metabolic demands and sub-optimal tissue oxygenation.

Identifying and Managing Sickle Cell Crises

Acute crises are sudden worsening episodes that punctuate the chronic course of the disease. Recognizing them quickly prevents catastrophic morbidity. | Crisis Type | Primary Mechanism | Key Clinical Features | | --- | --- | --- | | **Vaso-Occlusive (Pain)** | Microvascular blockage by sickled cells | Severe bone, chest, or abdominal pain | | **Aplastic Crisis** | Temporary cessation of erythropoiesis, often via Parvovirus B19 | Severe lethargy, profound anaemia, absent reticulocytes | | **Acute Chest Syndrome** | Pulmonary infarction or infection | Fever, new chest pain, infiltrates on chest X-ray | Management focuses on prompt pain relief with analgesics, aggressive hydration to reduce blood viscosity, supplemental oxygen if hypoxia is present, and treating any underlying precipitating infection.

Diagnostic Tests and Routine Laboratory Investigations

Accurate diagnosis relies on laboratory tests that detect abnormal haemoglobin variants. The **Haemoglobin Electrophoresis** is the gold standard, separating different haemoglobin types based on their electrical charge and movement through a medium. Routine monitoring involves complete blood counts to assess baseline haemoglobin levels and white blood cell counts, which may indicate infection. Reticulocyte counts are vital to measure the bone marrow's rate of red blood cell production. In resource-limited settings, the **sickling test** using sodium metabisulfite can confirm the presence of HbS, though electrophoresis remains necessary for definitive typing.

Primary Health Care Prevention and Public Health Strategies

Primary prevention aims to reduce the incidence of severe disease manifestations through community-level interventions and prophylactic care. Public health campaigns focus on genetic counseling, community education, and routine newborn screening. For diagnosed children, comprehensive primary care includes daily **penicillin prophylaxis** starting early in infancy to prevent life-threatening pneumococcal infections due to functional asplenia. Keeping children updated on all routine immunizations, alongside the pneumococcal and meningococcal vaccines, is non-negotiable. Malaria chemoprophylaxis is also standard practice in endemic regions, as malarial fever is a major trigger for acute painful crises.

Holistic Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nursing care encompasses physical management, emotional support, and comprehensive education for patients and their families. Nurses must assess pain meticulously using age-appropriate scales, ensuring analgesics are administered promptly and effectively. Education centers on avoiding known crisis triggers: dehydration, sudden temperature changes, high altitudes without supplemental oxygen, and excessive physical exhaustion. Hydration is a cornerstone of daily self-management; patients must learn to recognize early signs of dehydration and maintain an adequate fluid intake. Psychosocial support is equally critical to help families cope with the chronic, unpredictable nature of the condition.

Long-Term Complications and Multi-Disciplinary Follow-Up

Chronic organ damage accumulates over decades, necessitating a multi-disciplinary care team involving haematologists, paediatricians, nephrologists, ophthalmologists, and specialized nurses. Key long-term complications include **Avascular Necrosis** of the femoral or humeral heads due to bone infarction, leading to chronic joint destruction. Chronic kidney disease frequently develops from repeated ischemic injury to the renal medulla. Neurological complications such as silent cerebral infarcts or overt strokes require monitoring with Transcranial Doppler ultrasound in children. Regular ophthalmic screenings are also necessary to catch proliferative sickle retinopathy before vision is permanently lost.
