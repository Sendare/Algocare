Hypertension

Definition and Normal Blood Pressure Ranges

Blood pressure measures the force exerted by circulating blood against the walls of the body's arteries. It is recorded using two numbers: systolic pressure, which is the pressure when the heart beats and pushes blood out, and diastolic pressure, which is the pressure when the heart rests between beats.

  • Normal Blood Pressure: Less than 120 mmHg systolic and less than 80 mmHg diastolic.
  • Elevated Blood Pressure: 120-129 mmHg systolic and less than 80 mmHg diastolic.
  • Stage 1 Hypertension: 130-139 mmHg systolic or 80-89 mmHg diastolic.
  • Stage 2 Hypertension: 140 mmHg or higher systolic, or 90 mmHg or higher diastolic.

Hypertension is diagnosed when a patient consistently has high blood pressure readings taken on two or more separate healthcare visits. A single high reading does not mean a person has hypertension, because physical activity, anxiety, or rushing to the clinic can temporarily spike blood pressure values.

Classification and Risk Factors

Hypertension is broadly divided into two main categories based on its origin. Primary hypertension, also known as essential hypertension, has no identifiable secondary cause and develops gradually over many years, often linked to genetics and lifestyle. Secondary hypertension appears suddenly and is caused by an underlying condition, such as chronic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, or endocrine disorders like Cushing's syndrome.

Risk factors play a major role in the development of primary hypertension. Non-modifiable risk factors include advancing age, a family history of high blood pressure, and genetic predisposition. Modifiable risk factors include excessive dietary sodium intake, physical inactivity, obesity, heavy alcohol consumption, and chronic psychological stress. Identifying these risk factors during patient intake helps nurses guide targeted health education.

Clinical Manifestations

Hypertension is widely known as the 'silent killer' because it typically produces no warning signs or symptoms for years. Many patients walk around with dangerously high blood pressure readings feeling completely healthy.

When symptoms do eventually appear—often only when blood pressure reaches very high levels—they can include early morning occipital headaches, dizziness, blurred vision, fatigue, and nosebleeds. Nurses must remember that waiting for symptoms to appear is a dangerous strategy; routine screening is the only reliable way to catch high blood pressure early before it damages vital organs.

Screening and Assessment

Regular blood pressure screening in primary health care settings is vital for early detection and prevention of long-term organ damage. To ensure accuracy during assessment, the nurse must follow standard clinical procedures:

  1. Patient Preparation: Ensure the patient has rested quietly for at least five minutes without consuming caffeine or smoking.
  2. Positioning: Have the patient sit comfortably with their back supported, legs uncrossed, and bare arm supported at heart level.
  3. Cuff Selection: Choose the correct cuff size; a cuff that is too small will falsely elevate the reading, while one that is too large will yield falsely low results.
  4. Measurement Technique: Inflate the cuff and deflate it slowly while listening with the stethoscope over the brachial artery to capture the first tapping sound (systolic) and the disappearance of sound (diastolic).

Non-Pharmacological Management

Lifestyle modifications are the foundational first step in managing hypertension and can sometimes reduce the need for medication in mild cases. Nurses play a crucial role in counseling patients on sustainable daily changes:

  • Dietary Approaches: Encourage the DASH diet, which emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy while restricting sodium.
  • Weight Management: Promote gradual weight loss for overweight individuals to significantly reduce vascular resistance.
  • Physical Activity: Recommend regular aerobic exercise, such as brisk walking for thirty minutes most days of the week.
  • Substance Reduction: Advise limiting alcohol consumption and completely stopping tobacco use, as smoking causes acute vasoconstriction.

Pharmacological Treatment and Nursing Care

When lifestyle changes are insufficient, healthcare providers prescribe antihypertensive medications. Common drug classes include diuretics, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and calcium channel blockers.

Nurses must monitor for adverse effects and educate patients on adherence. A common misconception among patients is that they can stop taking their medication once their blood pressure normalizes. The nurse must explain that medication controls hypertension rather than curing it. Furthermore, nurses should monitor patients for orthostatic hypotension—a sudden drop in blood pressure when standing up—especially when starting a new medication regimen.

Complications and Emergency Management

Uncontrolled chronic hypertension places excessive strain on the arterial walls and vital organs, leading to severe long-term complications. These include stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, and hypertensive retinopathy.

Occasionally, blood pressure spikes to dangerous levels rapidly, resulting in a hypertensive crisis. This is divided into urgency, where blood pressure is extremely high without acute target organ damage, and emergency, where extremely high blood pressure causes acute damage to organs like the brain, heart, or kidneys. Hypertensive emergencies require immediate hospitalization and intravenous medications to lower blood pressure in a controlled manner.
