Hypertension

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Hypertension

Hypertension, commonly known as high blood pressure, is a chronic cardiovascular condition in which the systemic arterial pressure is persistently elevated. Blood pressure is determined by two main physiological factors: cardiac output and total peripheral vascular resistance. When cardiac output increases or blood vessels constrict, resistance rises, forcing the heart to work harder to perfuse vital organs. In primary essential hypertension, the exact underlying cause remains unknown, though it is strongly linked to genetic predisposition, autonomic nervous system dysregulation, and environmental factors. Secondary hypertension, conversely, stems from an identifiable underlying pathology, such as chronic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, or endocrine disorders like Conn syndrome. Sustained high pressure damages the delicate endothelial lining of blood vessels, accelerating atherosclerosis and predisposing the patient to end-organ damage throughout the body.

Blood Pressure Classification and Risk Factors

Clinical classification of blood pressure guides diagnosis and treatment intensity. Standard guidelines categorize blood pressure into normal, elevated, stage 1 hypertension, and stage 2 hypertension based on systolic and diastolic millimetres of mercury (mmHg) measurements. - **Normal**: Systolic below 120 and diastolic below 80. - **Elevated**: Systolic 120-129 and diastolic below 80. - **Stage 1 Hypertension**: Systolic 130-139 or diastolic 80-89. - **Stage 2 Hypertension**: Systolic at least 140 or diastolic at least 90. Risk factors are categorized into non-modifiable traits, such as advancing age, family history, and genetic ancestry, and modifiable lifestyle factors, including high dietary sodium intake, sedentary habits, tobacco use, chronic psychological stress, and excessive alcohol consumption.

Clinical Manifestations and Diagnostic Assessment

Hypertension is widely recognized as the "silent killer" because many individuals remain completely asymptomatic for years while progressive vascular damage occurs. When symptoms do appear, they are usually a consequence of longstanding vascular disease or acute pressure elevations. Common complaints include occipital headaches that are typically worse in the morning, dizziness, blurred vision, palpitations, and fatigue. Assessment in primary healthcare requires accurate sphygmomanometry. The patient should rest quietly for at least five minutes with feet flat on the floor and arm supported at heart level before measurement. Diagnosis is rarely made on a single reading; serial measurements across multiple clinical encounters are necessary. Laboratory investigations such as serum electrolytes, fasting blood glucose, lipid profiles, and urinalysis help evaluate baseline renal function and identify co-existing metabolic risks.

Lifestyle Modification and Non-Pharmacological Care

Non-pharmacological interventions are the foundational first step for all hypertensive patients and serve as essential adjuncts to drug therapy. Dietary adjustments focus on adopting the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) eating plan, which emphasizes fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy products while restricting saturated fats. Sodium restriction is particularly impactful; reducing dietary salt lowers intravascular volume and peripheral resistance. Regular aerobic exercise, such as brisk walking for at least thirty minutes most days of the week, strengthens the cardiovascular system. Additionally, achieving and maintaining a healthy body mass index, limiting alcohol intake, and completely abstaining from tobacco products are crucial nursing teaching points for sustainable blood pressure control.

Pharmacological Treatment and Nursing Responsibilities

When lifestyle modifications fail to achieve target blood pressure goals, pharmacological therapy is initiated. Complications and Hypertensive Emergencies

Uncontrolled chronic hypertension exerts destructive mechanical forces on systemic vasculature, leading to severe end-organ damage over time. Major complications include stroke due to cerebral vessel rupture or thrombosis, myocardial infarction from accelerated coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease progressing to renal failure, and left ventricular hypertrophy leading to heart failure. A hypertensive crisis represents a severe escalation of blood pressure and is subdivided into urgency and emergency. Hypertensive urgency involves systolic pressures over 180 or diastolic over 120 without acute target organ damage. Hypertensive emergency involves similar extreme blood pressure readings accompanied by acute, life-threatening damage to organs such as the brain, retina, heart, or kidneys, requiring immediate parenteral drug therapy in an intensive care setting.
