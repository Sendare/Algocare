Ebola Virus Disease

Introduction and Overview of Ebola Virus Disease

Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) is a severe, often fatal illness in humans caused by the Ebola virus, a member of the *Filoviridae* family. The virus is zoonotic, with fruit bats of the *Pteropodidae* family acting as its natural reservoir hosts. Transmission to humans occurs through close contact with the blood, secretions, organs, or other bodily fluids of infected animals such as chimpanzees, gorillas, fruit bats, monkeys, forest antelope, and porcupines found dead or ill in the rainforest.

Historically, outbreaks have heavily impacted sub-Saharan Africa, with the massive West African epidemic leaving profound scars on healthcare systems, including those in Nigeria. Understanding the epidemiological profile of EVD helps nursing students contextualize the panic and structural breakdown that often accompany outbreaks. The virus attacks multiple organ systems simultaneously, causing a rapid decline that demands immediate, highly coordinated isolation and medical intervention.

Pathophysiology and Transmission Dynamics

Once inside the human body, the Ebola virus targets endothelial cells, mononuclear phagocytes, and hepatocytes. The virus triggers a massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading to a severe systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS). This inflammatory cascade damages vascular integrity, causing capillary leak, hypotension, and impaired coagulation that culminates in disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC).

Person-to-person transmission happens strictly through direct contact—via broken skin or mucous membranes—with blood or body fluids of a person who is sick with or has died from EVD. Objects like needles and syringes contaminated with these fluids also transmit the infection. Crucially, the virus is not airborne under natural conditions, though aerosol-generating medical procedures can create a localized inhalation risk if proper personal protective equipment is absent.

Clinical Manifestations and Staging

The incubation period for Ebola ranges from 2 to 21 days. The illness typically begins with an abrupt dry phase featuring non-specific symptoms that mimic malaria or typhoid fever:

  • Dry Phase: High-grade fever, severe headache, generalized myalgia, profound fatigue, and arthralgia.
  • Wet Phase: Progression to gastrointestinal symptoms including watery diarrhea, intense abdominal pain, and profuse vomiting, which rapidly induce severe dehydration.
  • Hemorrhagic Phase: Occurs in a subset of severe cases, manifested by petechiae, ecchymosis, mucosal bleeding, and oozing from venipuncture sites.
  • Convalescence or Demise: Patients either succumb to multi-organ failure and hypovolemic shock typically within the second week, or gradual clinical improvement begins around day ten.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis

Assessing a patient with suspected EVD requires strict adherence to barrier precautions before entering the clinical evaluation zone. The nurse must obtain a thorough epidemiological history alongside the clinical review, specifically screening for travel to active outbreak zones, contact with a known case, or participation in unsafe funeral rites within the past three weeks.

Laboratory confirmation is achieved through Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) assay on blood samples, or antigen-capture enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) testing. Because routine phlebotomy in early EVD carries an extreme risk of needlestick transmission and sample spillage, collection protocols demand double-gloving, face shields, and dedicated phlebotomy teams operating under strict supervision.

Supportive Medical and Nursing Management

There is no guaranteed cure for EVD, though targeted monoclonal antibody therapies have improved survival rates significantly. Standard nursing and medical care remains intensely supportive, focusing on correcting severe fluid and electrolyte imbalances caused by profuse vomiting and diarrhea.

Intravenous fluid resuscitation with balanced crystalloids is standard, though oral rehydration salts are utilized if the patient is stable and conscious. Nurses closely monitor strict intake and output charts, manage electrolyte replacement (especially potassium and magnesium), control fever with paracetamol while avoiding NSAIDs like ibuprofen due to bleeding risks, and administer antiemetics and antidiarrheals. Nutritional support, psychological comfort, and aggressive management of secondary bacterial infections round out the core supportive interventions.

Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) in Clinical Settings

Infection Prevention and Control protocols form the absolute backbone of stopping transmission within healthcare facilities. Healthcare workers must wear full Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) covering every inch of skin and mucous membranes, including impermeable coveralls, hoods, goggles, face shields, double gloves, and rubber boots.

Strict adherence to safe doffing sequences is vital, as self-contamination during equipment removal accounts for many nosocomial infections among healthcare staff. Hand hygiene with soap and water or alcohol-based hand rub must be performed religiously between every patient contact. Furthermore, all medical waste, contaminated linens, and body waste must be managed through secure incineration or chemical disinfection protocols to eliminate environmental reservoirs.

Community Surveillance and Public Health Response

Controlling an Ebola outbreak extends far beyond hospital walls into the surrounding communities. Contact tracing is a pillar of public health response, requiring health teams to identify, list, and monitor every person who has had contact with an infected individual for 21 days from their last exposure date.

Community engagement and culturally sensitive communication are essential in preventing resistance to control measures. In many settings, traditional burial practices involving the washing and touching of corpses represent high-risk transmission events. Public health nurses work alongside local leaders to establish safe and dignified burial teams that respect cultural sensitivities while neutralizing infectious corpses through secure containment and chemical disinfection.

Complications, Survivorship, and Post-Ebola Syndrome

Surviving acute EVD marks the beginning of a complex recovery journey. Many survivors experience a constellation of lingering physical and psychological difficulties collectively termed Post-Ebola Syndrome (PES). Common manifestations include chronic joint pain (arthralgia), profound fatigue, muscular weakness, headaches, blurred vision, and psychosocial challenges such as severe stigma, depression, and anxiety.

Furthermore, the virus can persist in immunologically privileged sites within the human body for extended periods. Viable virus has been isolated from semen, ocular fluid, and the central nervous system long after acute clearance from the blood, creating a potential risk for sexual transmission or late-onset relapse that requires ongoing survivor counseling and follow-up care.
