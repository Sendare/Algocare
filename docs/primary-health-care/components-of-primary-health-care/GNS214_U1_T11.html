Ebola Virus Disease

Introduction and Overview of Ebola Virus Disease

Ebola Virus Disease (EVD) is a severe, often fatal illness in humans caused by the Ebolavirus, a member of the *Filoviridae* family. The virus is zoonotic, meaning it originates in animals, with fruit bats of the Pteropodidae family considered the natural hosts. When it crosses over into human populations, it triggers intense outbreaks characterized by rapid human-to-human transmission. Understanding the historical context and baseline behavior of this pathogen helps healthcare workers reduce panic and deliver rational, science-based care during epidemics.

Pathophysiology and Transmission Dynamics

The Ebola virus primarily targets the human immune system, specifically monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Once inside, it suppresses the body's early interferon response, allowing the virus to replicate unchecked and spread through the lymphatic system to the liver, spleen, and lymph nodes.

Transmission occurs through direct contact with the blood, secretions, organs, or other bodily fluids of infected people, as well as surfaces and materials contaminated with these fluids.

  • Direct Contact: Broken skin or mucous membranes touching infected body fluids.
  • Fomites: Contaminated needles, bedding, or clothing.
  • Postmortem Transmission: Direct handling of the body of someone who died from Ebola during traditional burial rites.

Clinical Manifestations and Staging

The incubation period for Ebola ranges from 2 to 21 days. The illness typically begins abruptly with dry symptoms, progressing over days to wet, gastrointestinal and hemorrhagic phases.

PhaseClinical Features
Early Phase (Days 1-3)Sudden fever, severe headache, intense fatigue, muscle pain, and sore throat.
Gastrointestinal Phase (Days 4-6)Watery diarrhea, severe abdominal pain, nausea, and persistent vomiting.
Advanced Phase (Day 7 onward)Neurological confusion, unexplained hemorrhaging (bleeding from puncture sites, gums), multiorgan failure, and shock.

Nursing Assessment and Triage

Early identification is the single most effective tool to stop hospital outbreaks. Nurses working in primary health settings must maintain a high index of suspicion. Triage protocols require screening every arriving patient for travel history, contact with sick individuals, and sudden unexplained fever before they enter general waiting areas.

When assessing a suspected case, minimize physical contact, wear appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE), and immediately isolate the patient in a designated holding area while awaiting confirmatory laboratory PCR testing. Avoid invasive procedures unless absolutely necessary to prevent accidental needlesticks and splashes.

Primary Health Care Management and Supportive Treatment

There is no guaranteed cure for Ebola, though targeted monoclonal antibody therapies have improved survival rates significantly when given early. Primary nursing care is entirely supportive, focusing on maintaining hemodynamic stability, correcting severe electrolyte imbalances, and managing distressing symptoms.

  • Fluid Resuscitation: Aggressive oral or intravenous rehydration to replace massive fluid losses from vomiting and diarrhea.
  • Symptom Relief: Administering antipyretics for fever and antiemetics for nausea, while monitoring urine output closely to catch acute kidney injury early.
  • Nutritional Support: Providing high-protein, easily digestible liquid meals or enteral nutrition as soon as the patient can tolerate feeding.

Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) in Primary Health Settings

Strict adherence to IPC protocols protects healthcare workers from nosocomial infection. Standard precautions must be upgraded to rigorous contact and droplet precautions for every patient presenting with fever of unknown origin in endemic regions.

Healthcare workers must don full personal protective equipment (PPE)—including coveralls, waterproof aprons, double gloves, boots, and face shields—before entering isolation zones. Proper doffing (removal) of PPE is a high-risk procedure that requires a trained observer to prevent self-contamination. Additionally, environmental surfaces must be decontaminated frequently using chlorine-based solutions.

Community Surveillance, Contact Tracing, and Public Health Response

Stopping an Ebola outbreak requires active community engagement beyond clinic walls. Public health nurses often lead contact tracing teams to identify everyone who had physical interaction with a confirmed case. Contacts are monitored daily for fever and symptoms for the full 21-day incubation period.

Building trust with local leaders is essential. When communities fear health workers, families hide sick relatives, which accelerates hidden transmission. Transparent communication, respectful burial practices, and involving local influencers help ensure rapid reporting and early case isolation.

Complications, Survivorship, and Post-Ebola Syndrome

Patients who recover from Ebola face physical and psychological hurdles long after leaving the treatment center. Many survivors experience Post-Ebola Syndrome, a collection of chronic symptoms that can include severe joint pain, extreme fatigue, chronic headaches, and eye inflammation such as uveitis, which can lead to vision loss if untreated.

Furthermore, the virus can persist in immune-privileged sites like the testes, eyes, and central nervous system. Male survivors can transmit the virus through semen for months after recovery, requiring ongoing counseling, safe sex education, and support groups to combat social stigma.
