Leprosy

Introduction and Definition of Leprosy

Leprosy, historically known as Hansen's disease, is a chronic granulomatous infection primarily targeting the peripheral nerves, skin, upper respiratory tract mucosa, and eyes. Despite centuries of social stigma and fear, it has a low level of infectivity and is fully curable. The disease progresses slowly, with an incubation period that can span several years, masking the early stages of infection. In tropical regions like Nigeria, endemic pockets still require vigilant community surveillance and early case detection to prevent long-term physical deformities.

Causative Agent and Mode of Transmission

  • Causative Organism: Mycobacterium leprae, an obligate intracellular, acid-fast bacillus that cannot be cultivated on standard cell-free artificial laboratory media.
  • Reservoir and Transmission: Humans are the primary reservoir. Transmission occurs mainly through prolonged, close contact via droplets from the nose and mouth of untreated, multibacillary cases.
  • Susceptibility: The vast majority of human beings possess natural immunity against the bacillus, meaning casual contact does not transmit the disease.

Classification and Clinical Types of Leprosy

Clinical classification is vital for determining treatment regimens and prognosis. The Ridley-Jopling five-tier scale ranges from tuberculoid to lepromatous, but for operational field purposes, the World Health Organization classifies the disease based on skin lesion counts:

Signs, Symptoms, and Clinical Assessment

Assessment centers on identifying hypopigmented or erythematous skin patches that exhibit a distinct loss of thermal, pain, and tactile sensation. Palpation of peripheral nerve trunks—such as the ulnar, median, lateral popliteal, and posterior tibial nerves—often reveals localized thickening and tenderness. Nurses must test sensory functions systematically using fine touch, pinpricks, and warm or cool test tubes, comparing affected areas to healthy skin.

Complications and Associated Disabilities

Nerve damage leads to irreversible sensory deficits, muscle weakness, and paralysis if left unchecked. Impaired sensation in the hands and feet creates high vulnerability to unnoticed burns, cuts, and pressure ulcers, frequently resulting in secondary infections and auto-amputation of digits. Ocular complications, including lagophthalmos and corneal ulceration, arise from facial nerve involvement and can cause permanent blindness.

Medical Management and Multi-Drug Therapy (MDT)

Monotherapy is strictly contraindicated due to the high risk of drug resistance. Multi-Drug Therapy (MDT), standardized by the World Health Organization, combines rifampicin, clofazimine, and dapsone. This combination successfully kills the bacilli, stops transmission, and prevents relapse. Paucibacillary cases receive a shorter course, while multibacillary regimens are extended. Health workers must directly observe monthly doses of rifampicin to ensure adherence.

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Nursing interventions focus on self-care education, ulcer prevention, and psychological rehabilitation. Patients must learn to inspect numb hands and feet daily using mirrors, soak dry skin, apply protective emollients, and wear well-fitted footwear. Nurses also coordinate physical therapy to maintain joint mobility and provide empathetic counseling to reduce internalised shame and encourage community reintegration.

Primary Health Care and Community Prevention Strategies

Community-level control relies on active case finding, contact tracing, and health education to dispel myths surrounding the disease. Integrating leprosy services into general primary health care clinics reduces the stigma associated with specialized leprosaria. Public awareness campaigns ensure that community members recognize early skin symptoms and seek prompt diagnosis, stopping transmission chains early.
