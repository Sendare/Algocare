Leprosy

Introduction and Definition of Leprosy

Leprosy, also known as Hansen's disease, is a chronic, progressive infectious disease primarily affecting the peripheral nerves and the skin. It is caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium leprae. Throughout history, the disease has carried a heavy social stigma due to the visible disfigurements it can cause if left untreated. Despite this fear, leprosy is curable, and early detection completely prevents long-term physical impairments.

Causative Agent and Transmission

The causative organism is Mycobacterium leprae, an acid-fast, rod-shaped bacillus that multiplies very slowly. The exact mechanism of transmission is not fully proven, but the general scientific consensus points toward prolonged, close contact via respiratory droplets from untreated patients.

  • Incubation Period: Unusually long, typically ranging from 3 to 5 years, but can span up to 20 years.
  • Infectivity: Only untreated multibacillary patients shed the bacteria into the environment.
  • Susceptibility: Most humans have a natural immunity; only a small percentage of exposed individuals actually develop the disease.

Classification and Clinical Types

Leprosy is broadly classified based on the patient's immune response and the number of skin lesions present. This guides treatment length and drug combinations.

  • Paucibacillary (PB): Features 1 to 5 skin lesions with no bacteria found in skin smears. The patient's cell-mediated immunity is relatively strong.
  • Multibacillary (MB): Features more than 5 lesions, symmetric skin involvement, and positive skin smears. The immune response is weak, allowing widespread bacterial growth.

Signs, Symptoms, and Clinical Assessment

Clinical assessment focuses on finding skin changes and nerve damage. The earliest sign is often a pale or reddish skin patch that has lost normal sensation.

  • Skin Manifestations: Hypopigmented patches, nodules, plaques, or generalized thickening of the skin.
  • Nerve Involvement: Enlarged, tender peripheral nerves, particularly the ulnar, median, or common peroneal nerves.
  • Sensory Loss: Inability to feel light touch, pain, or temperature over the affected skin areas, increasing the risk of unnoticed injuries.

Complications and Associated Disabilities

Disabilities in leprosy do not stem directly from the bacteria eating away flesh, but rather from nerve damage and loss of sensation. When patients cannot feel pain, they repeatedly injure their hands and feet without realizing it, leading to chronic ulcers and bone absorption.

  • Primary Impairments: Direct nerve damage leading to muscle weakness, clawed fingers, foot drop, and lagophthalmos (inability to close eyelids).
  • Secondary Impairments: Secondary infections, tissue necrosis, contractures, and blindness resulting from corneal exposure.

Medical Management and Multi-Drug Therapy

Leprosy is treated using a combination of medications known as Multi-Drug Therapy (MDT). Monotherapy is strictly avoided because the bacterium develops drug resistance very quickly.

--------->
Paucibacillary (PB)Rifampicin and Dapsone6 months
Multibacillary (MB)Rifampicin, Dapsone, and Clofazimine12 to 24 months

MDT kills the bacteria, halts transmission within days of starting treatment, and is provided free of charge through public health systems globally.

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Nursing care goes beyond administering medications; it involves comprehensive physical and psychological support. Nurses teach self-care techniques to protect anesthetic hands and feet, such as soaking feet in warm water, applying oil to dry skin, and wearing protective footwear.

Rehabilitation focuses on restoring function through physiotherapy, reconstructive surgery for claw hands, and counseling to help patients reintegrate into their families and communities without fear of rejection.

Primary Health Care and Community Prevention

Community control of leprosy relies on early case detection, prompt treatment, and health education to dispel myths. Public health nurses play a vital role in screening household contacts of diagnosed patients since they are at a higher risk of contracting the infection.

  • Contact Tracing: Regular check-ups for family members living in the same household.
  • Stigma Reduction: Educating the public that leprosy is transmitted like a common cold, is not a curse, and is fully curable with free medications.
