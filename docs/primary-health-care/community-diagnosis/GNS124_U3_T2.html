Types of Health Needs and Community Diagnosis

Introduction to Health Needs in Primary Health Care

Primary health care relies heavily on understanding what communities require to achieve optimal well-being. A health need represents the gap between a current health status and a desired, achievable standard. In public health practice across Nigeria, identifying these gaps ensures that limited resources are directed where they will create the greatest impact. Recognizing that health needs differ from simple consumer wants is a foundational skill for community health nurses. Wants are driven by personal preferences or demands, whereas needs are defined by professional standards of health and disease prevention. Distinguishing between them prevents the misallocation of scarce medical supplies and personnel in rural and urban health clinics alike.

Classification of Health Needs: Felt, Expressed, Normative, and Comparative

Public health planners categorize requirements into four distinct types to structure interventions effectively:

  • Felt needs: What individuals feel or perceive as their own health problems, often shaped by personal experience and cultural background.
  • Expressed needs: Felt needs that are turned into action, such as a patient actually visiting a primary health center for treatment.
  • Normative needs: Deficiencies defined by health professionals or experts based on established scientific thresholds and guidelines.
  • Comparative needs: Disparities identified by comparing the health services or status of one community against another group with better access.

Concept and Importance of Community Diagnosis

A community diagnosis is a comprehensive assessment of the state of health of a defined population, taking into account socio-economic, physical, and environmental determinants. It mirrors an individual clinical diagnosis, but instead of focusing on a single patient, the target is an entire village, town, or municipal district. Through this process, nurses identify endemic diseases, nutritional deficiencies, and environmental hazards like contaminated water sources. Without a proper community diagnosis, public health programs risk treating symptoms rather than root causes, leading to wasted effort and persistent community illness.

Steps in Conducting a Community Diagnosis

Carrying out a systematic community diagnosis follows a structured sequence that moves from preparation to action:

  1. Community entry and mobilization: Engaging traditional leaders, youth groups, and stakeholders to secure cooperation and trust.
  2. Data collection: Gathering both qualitative and quantitative information on health indicators, demographics, and infrastructure.
  3. Data analysis and interpretation: Processing raw figures to identify major health problems, vulnerable groups, and causal factors.
  4. Community diagnosis report writing: Synthesizing findings into a clear document to share with health authorities and community leaders.
  5. Feedback to the community: Presenting results back to the populace to validate findings and foster a sense of shared ownership.

Data Collection Methods and Community Profiling

Building an accurate community profile requires a mix of quantitative and qualitative data-gathering tools. Surveys and structured questionnaires capture statistical metrics like immunization coverage rates and birth statistics. Meanwhile, focus group discussions and key informant interviews reveal cultural beliefs, health-seeking behaviors, and hidden barriers to care. Observational windshielf surveys—where nurses drive or walk through a neighborhood—offer immediate insights into housing conditions, waste management, and drainage systems. Combining these diverse methods ensures that the resulting profile reflects both hard numbers and the lived human reality of the population.

Identifying and Prioritizing Community Health Problems

Once raw data is compiled, nurses often face a overwhelming list of deficits, from poor sanitation to high maternal mortality. Because funds and personnel are finite, prioritizing issues is an absolute necessity. Tools like the Hanlon Method or simple matrix scoring help rank problems based on explicit criteria: the magnitude of the condition, its seriousness or severity, the feasibility of solving it, and the community's own perceived urgency. Involving community representatives in this ranking process guarantees that interventions align with local values rather than external assumptions.

Planning and Implementing Community-Based Interventions

Effective planning translates prioritized health problems into concrete, measurable objectives and actionable strategies. A well-designed intervention plan outlines specific activities, assigns clear responsibilities to team members, establishes a realistic timeline, and identifies necessary material resources. During implementation, community health nurses act as facilitators, educators, and coordinators. Mobilizing local volunteers and collaborating with local government councils increases the reach and long-term sustainability of immunization drives, health education workshops, or sanitation campaigns.

Monitoring and Evaluation of Community Health Programs

Monitoring and evaluation provide the feedback loop needed to determine if a health intervention is succeeding. Monitoring is an ongoing, day-to-day review of project activities to ensure tasks are being executed according to plan. Evaluation, conversely, is a periodic, comprehensive assessment measuring whether the program achieved its ultimate objectives and long-term health outcomes. By tracking indicators such as vaccine uptake percentages or reduced malaria incidence, nurses can adjust strategies mid-course, account for donor investments, and apply lessons learned to future community health initiatives.
