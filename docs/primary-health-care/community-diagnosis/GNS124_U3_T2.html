Types of Health Needs and Community Diagnosis

Understanding Health Needs in the Community

A health need represents the gap between a community's current health status and a desired, healthier state. In public health and primary healthcare, nurses must recognize that health is not merely the absence of disease, but a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being. When working with communities, healthcare providers look beyond individual patients to assess the collective needs of families, occupational groups, and entire villages or towns. Understanding these needs allows nurses to allocate limited resources effectively and design programs that truly matter to the people they serve.

Classification of Health Needs: Felt, Expressed, Normative, and Comparative

Public health planners classify health needs into four distinct categories to understand them from different perspectives:

  • Felt needs: What people say they feel or want. For example, community members might state they need more maternal health talks because they worry about childbirth.
  • Expressed needs: Felt needs that are turned into action. If those same community members actually walk to the clinic and demand or use maternal services, the need becomes expressed.
  • Normative needs: Defined by health professionals or experts based on established standards. A doctor or nurse might test water samples and declare that the community needs a safe borehole, regardless of whether residents are complaining.
  • Comparative needs: Identified by comparing the services received by one group with those received by another. If Town A has a functioning immunization post but neighboring Town B does not, Town B has a comparative need.

Concept and Purpose of Community Diagnosis

A community diagnosis is a comprehensive assessment of the health status of a community in relation to its social, economic, physical, and environmental context. Just as a clinical diagnosis helps a nurse treat an individual patient, a community diagnosis helps health workers understand the root causes of disease outbreaks, nutritional deficiencies, and health disparities in a population. It pinpoints vulnerable groups, identifies available local resources, and provides the baseline data needed to plan relevant interventions.

Steps in Conducting a Community Diagnosis

Carrying out a systematic community diagnosis follows a logical sequence to ensure accuracy and community ownership:

  1. Pre-survey phase: Securing administrative clearance, mobilizing stakeholders, and forming the health team.
  2. Designing the methodology: Choosing data collection tools, defining target populations, and mapping the area.
  3. Data collection: Gathering primary and secondary data through interviews, observations, and records.
  4. Data collation and analysis: Organizing raw data, calculating frequencies and rates, and identifying trends.
  5. Validation: Presenting findings back to the community leaders and members to confirm accuracy.
  6. Report writing and dissemination: Publishing the final diagnosis and sharing it with stakeholders for action.

Data Collection Methods and Community Profiling

Gathering reliable information requires a mix of quantitative and qualitative methods. Nurses use questionnaires and structured interviews to gather numerical data on disease prevalence and household size. Focus group discussions and key informant interviews with village elders, religious leaders, and teachers provide deeper insight into cultural beliefs and local attitudes toward health services. Additionally, secondary data from existing clinic registers, census reports, and school attendance records help build a complete profile of the community's infrastructure and demographic makeup.

Identifying and Prioritizing Community Health Problems

A community survey often uncovers multiple health issues—ranging from poor sanitation and malaria to malnutrition and lack of schools. Because resources are always limited, nurses cannot tackle everything at once. Prioritization requires objective criteria such as the magnitude of the problem (how many people are affected), the severity or urgency (how dangerous it is), the feasibility of solving it with local resources, and the community's own expressed concern. Using tools like the Hanlon method or simple ranking matrices helps nurses and community leaders agree on which issue to address first.

Planning and Implementing Community-Based Interventions

Once the top priority problem is selected, community health planning begins. Nurses work hand-in-hand with community development committees to set clear objectives, outline specific activities, assign responsibilities, and establish timelines. Implementation relies heavily on community participation rather than top-down imposition. For instance, if the priority is reducing guinea worm disease, the plan might include training local youth to distribute water filters, organizing community clean-up days for water sources, and holding health education rallies.

Monitoring and Evaluation of Community Health Programs

Monitoring and evaluation ensure that community health interventions stay on track and achieve their goals. Monitoring is an ongoing, day-to-day review of project activities to check if supplies are arriving and attendance is steady. Evaluation is a periodic assessment that measures the overall impact and outcome of the program—asking whether the intervention actually reduced disease rates or improved health knowledge. By sharing these evaluation results with the community, nurses foster transparency, celebrate successes, and learn valuable lessons for future public health projects.
