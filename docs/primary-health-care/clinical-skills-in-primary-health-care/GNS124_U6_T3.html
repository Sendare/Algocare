Physical Examination

Introduction to Physical Examination in Primary Health Care

Physical examination forms the bedrock of clinical assessment in primary health care settings across Nigeria. When a patient presents to a primary health clinic, the structured collection of objective data helps distinguish between various differential diagnoses before laboratory investigations are available. Basic assessment skills allow clinicians to evaluate baseline health status, monitor disease progression, and evaluate the effectiveness of initiated treatments. Mastering these foundational techniques ensures that subtle clinical deviations are detected early, preventing simple conditions from deteriorating into medical emergencies in resource-constrained environments.

Preparation of the Patient and Environment

A reliable physical examination requires careful preparation of both the physical surroundings and the patient's psychological state. The examination room must offer adequate lighting, thermal comfort, and strict visual and auditory privacy—an aspect often challenging in busy Nigerian public health facilities. Clinicians should explain every step of the procedure to alleviate anxiety and secure informed cooperation. The patient must be draped appropriately, exposing only the body region currently under examination to preserve dignity and minimize embarrassment.

Standard Universal Precautions and Infection Control

Infection prevention is paramount during any patient encounter. Standard precautions apply to all patients regardless of their diagnosed or suspected infection status. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective intervention to interrupt the chain of transmission. Clean gloves must be worn whenever touching blood, body fluids, secretions, excretions, and non-intact skin. Proper biomedical waste segregation—disposing of sharps in puncture-resistant safety boxes and cotton waste in appropriate colored bins—protects both healthcare workers and the community.

Basic Techniques of Physical Assessment

  • Inspection: Concentrated visual observation of the patient's body, noting symmetry, color, movement, and obvious abnormalities.
  • Palpation: Using the sense of touch to evaluate texture, temperature, moisture, swelling, tenderness, and pulsations.
  • Percussion: Tapping the body surface to produce vibrations and sound waves, helping determine organ density, borders, and fluid accumulation.
  • Auscultation: Listening to internal body sounds, primarily using a stethoscope to assess heart, lung, and bowel sounds.

Vital Signs Measurement and Anthropometric Assessment

Vital signs provide immediate quantitative data regarding homeostatic stability. These parameters include body temperature, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure, often accompanied by oxygen saturation as a fifth vital sign. Accurate measurement requires calibrated equipment and standardized patient positioning. Anthropometric measurements—such as height, weight, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), and Body Mass Index (BMI)—complement vital signs by revealing nutritional status and growth trajectories, especially critical in pediatric and maternal care programs across Nigeria.

Systemic Examination: Head-to-Toe Approach

A systematic head-to-toe approach ensures that no anatomical region is overlooked during a comprehensive physical examination. The sequence typically begins at the head, assessing the skull, eyes, ears, nose, and throat, before progressing downwards through the neck, chest, cardiovascular system, abdomen, and musculoskeletal system, finishing with the extremities and neurological reflexes. Maintaining this consistent structural flow prevents omissions and helps the examiner correlate regional findings into a coherent clinical picture.

Documenting and Reporting Physical Examination Findings

Accurate documentation bridges the gap between assessment and ongoing clinical management. All physical findings must be recorded promptly using clear, objective, and professional language, avoiding vague terms like 'normal' without supporting descriptive data. In Nigerian clinical practice, both paper-based nursing care plans and electronic health records require entries to be legible, timed, and dated with the clinician's signature. Abnormal findings must be reported immediately to the attending physician or senior nurse to initiate timely interventions.
