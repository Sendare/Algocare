Physical Examination

Introduction to Physical Examination in Primary Health Care

Physical examination is a foundational clinical skill utilized by nurses and other healthcare professionals in primary health care settings across Nigeria. It involves the systematic collection of objective data through direct physical observation and examination of a patient's body systems. In primary health care, physical examinations are critical for early disease detection, health promotion, continuous monitoring of chronic conditions, and making prompt referral decisions when abnormalities are identified beyond the scope of primary care. As a nursing student, mastering physical examination techniques empowers you to validate subjective complaints reported by patients during history-taking and provides baseline physiological data. This process relies heavily on good interpersonal communication, cultural sensitivity, and clinical competence. Because primary health care serves diverse populations in both urban and rural Nigerian communities, nurses must adapt their examination approaches to respect local cultural norms while maintaining clinical thoroughness. Understanding how to conduct a proper physical assessment ensures that patients receive holistic, safe, and evidence-based care right from their first point of contact with the healthcare system.

Preparation of the Patient and Environment

Preparing both the patient and the examination environment is a vital prerequisite for an accurate and comfortable physical assessment. The examination environment must be warm, well-lit, quiet, and ensure complete privacy to maintain patient dignity and confidentiality. Adequate lighting is crucial for inspecting skin changes, rashes, and subtle color variations, which is especially important when assessing individuals with diverse skin tones. Preparing the patient involves establishing a strong nurse-patient therapeutic relationship, explaining every step of the procedure clearly, addressing any anxieties, and obtaining informed consent. The nurse must instruct the patient to empty their bladder before the examination to promote comfort and facilitate abdominal palpation. Positioning the patient correctly according to the body system being assessed—such as the dorsal recumbent position for abdominal checks or the sitting position for head and neck assessments—is equally critical. Furthermore, ensuring that necessary equipment like stethoscopes, thermometers, tape measures, and penlights are clean, functional, and readily accessible prevents unnecessary delays and maintains an organized workflow. Proper preparation ultimately fosters trust, reduces patient anxiety, and yields more reliable clinical findings.

Standard Universal Precautions and Infection Control

Standard universal precautions and infection control protocols are non-negotiable standards of practice for nurses performing physical examinations. These practices are designed to protect both the healthcare provider and the patient from the transmission of infectious agents, including bloodborne pathogens like HIV and Hepatitis B, which remain significant public health concerns in Nigeria. Universal precautions dictate that every patient's blood, body fluids, secretions, and non-intact skin must be treated as potentially infectious. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective intervention in preventing hospital-acquired and community-acquired infections; nurses must perform hand hygiene before and after direct patient contact, after removing gloves, and after touching contaminated items. Personal protective equipment (PPE), such as clean non-sterile gloves, must be worn whenever there is an anticipated risk of contact with bodily fluids, mucous membranes, or broken skin. Additionally, proper handling and disposal of sharp instruments, such as needles used for injections or blood draws, prevent accidental needle-stick injuries. Decontaminating reusable diagnostic equipment—like stethoscopes, blood pressure cuffs, and thermometer probes—between patients using appropriate disinfectant wipes breaks the chain of infection in busy primary health care clinics.

Basic Techniques of Physical Assessment: Inspection, Palpation, Percussion, and Auscultation

The physical assessment relies on four fundamental, sequential techniques: inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. The only exception to this standard sequence is when examining the abdomen, where auscultation precedes palpation and percussion to avoid altering bowel sounds through physical manipulation. Inspection involves concentrated, deliberate observation of the patient's body, noting symmetry, color, size, shape, and abnormal movements. Palpation uses the sense of touch through the hands and fingers to evaluate texture, temperature, moisture, swelling, tenderness, pulsations, and organ masses. Light palpation is used first to detect surface abnormalities, followed by deeper palpation when necessary. Percussion involves tapping the body surface with the fingertips to produce vibrations and sound waves, which help determine the density, size, and borders of underlying organs, as well as whether tissue is fluid-filled, air-filled, or solid. Auscultation is the act of listening to sounds produced within the body, primarily using a stethoscope. It requires a quiet environment and skill in distinguishing pitch, intensity, duration, and quality of sounds generated by the cardiovascular, respiratory, and gastrointestinal systems. Mastering these four techniques equips nursing students to gather comprehensive diagnostic clues during clinical rotations.

Systemic Examination: Head-to-Toe Approach

The systemic examination utilizes a head-to-toe approach to ensure a thorough, organized, and efficient physical assessment that misses no body region. This structured method minimizes the need for frequent position changes by the patient and standardizes documentation. The assessment typically begins at the head, evaluating the skull, scalp, facial symmetry, eyes, ears, nose, and mouth for abnormalities, lesions, or sensory impairments. Moving downward, the nurse inspects and palpates the neck for lymph node enlargement, thyroid gland status, and carotid pulse characteristics. Next is the thorax and lungs, where inspection of respiratory effort is followed by palpation for tactile fremitus, percussion for lung resonance, and auscultation for breath sounds and adventitious noises like crackles or wheezes. The cardiovascular assessment involves identifying the apical impulse and auscultating heart sounds, specifically noting S1, S2, and any murmurs or abnormal rhythms across designated anatomical valve areas. The abdominal examination follows, utilizing inspection, auscultation, percussion, and palpation to assess bowel activity, organomegaly, and tenderness. Finally, the examination of the musculoskeletal and neurological systems evaluates muscle tone, joint mobility, reflexes, cranial nerve function, and sensory status. Employing this systematic sequence ensures comprehensive clinical data collection across all body systems.

Vital Signs Measurement and Anthropometric Assessment

Vital signs measurement and anthropometric assessment are core components of baseline clinical data collection. Vital signs include temperature, pulse rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, and pain assessment, often referred to as the fifth vital sign. These parameters reflect critical physiological functions and can provide early indicators of clinical deterioration or acute illness. Nurses must measure vital signs accurately using calibrated equipment, such as a sphygmomanometer and stethoscope for blood pressure, and thermometer devices for body temperature. Oxygen saturation is also routinely monitored in many primary care settings. Anthropometric measurements quantify physical dimensions of the body and include weight, height, body mass index (BMI), mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), and head circumference in infants. In primary health care in Nigeria, MUAC is a vital anthropometric tool used for rapid nutritional screening in children and pregnant women to detect acute malnutrition. Accurately recording and interpreting these measurements allows nurses to track growth trends in pediatric patients, identify obesity or malnutrition, and monitor response to medical or nutritional interventions over time.

Documenting and Reporting Physical Examination Findings

Documenting and reporting physical examination findings accurately is a professional and legal responsibility for nursing students and registered nurses. Documentation creates a permanent, legally binding medical record that tracks the patient's health status, guides ongoing clinical decision-making, and ensures effective continuity of care among multidisciplinary healthcare teams. All findings must be recorded promptly after the assessment while details remain fresh in the nurse's memory. Documentation should be objective, clear, concise, and descriptive, avoiding vague terminology such as 'normal' or 'fine' when specific descriptive measurements or characteristics can be documented instead. Nurses must distinguish between subjective data (what the patient states) and objective data (what the nurse observes, measures, or detects during physical examination techniques). In addition to written documentation, critical abnormal findings—such as severely elevated blood pressure, respiratory distress, or acute signs of shock—must be reported immediately to the supervising physician or senior nurse using structured communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). Timely reporting ensures that emergency interventions are initiated without delay, safeguarding patient outcomes in primary health care settings.
