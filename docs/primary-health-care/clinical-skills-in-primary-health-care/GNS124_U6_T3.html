Physical Examination

Introduction to Physical Examination in Primary Health Care

A physical examination is a systematic head-to-toe evaluation of a patient's body to detect both normal and abnormal clinical findings. In primary health care settings, it serves as a baseline for clinical decision-making, helping nurses identify actual or potential health problems early. This process relies heavily on clinical observation, objective data collection, and therapeutic communication to build a comprehensive picture of the patient's well-being. By combining subjective data from the nursing history with objective findings from the physical exam, nurses can formulate accurate nursing diagnoses and plan effective individualized care.

Preparation of the Patient and Environment

Before touching a patient, a nurse must prepare both the person and the surrounding environment to ensure accuracy, comfort, and safety. A proper physical examination room must be warm, well-lit, quiet, and private to reduce patient anxiety.

  • Physical Comfort: Ensure the examination table is at a comfortable working height and covered with clean paper or linen.
  • Patient Privacy: Draw curtains, close doors, and provide proper draping to expose only the body part currently under examination.
  • Psychological Preparation: Explain every step of the procedure clearly, answer questions honestly, and obtain informed consent before starting.

Standard Universal Precautions and Infection Control

Infection control is paramount during any physical examination to protect both the patient and the healthcare provider from cross-contamination. Nurses must treat all body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes as potentially infectious. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective way to prevent the spread of microorganisms. Always wash hands or use alcohol-based hand rub before and after direct patient contact. Additionally, wear clean gloves whenever there is a risk of touching blood, bodily fluids, or open wounds, and discard used equipment properly in designated bins.

Basic Techniques of Physical Assessment

Physical assessment relies on four core techniques performed in a specific sequence, except during an abdominal exam where auscultation precedes palpation to prevent altering bowel sounds.

  1. Inspection: The process of deliberate, focused observation using the unaided eye and sometimes a light source.
  2. Palpation: Using the sense of touch to assess texture, temperature, moisture, swelling, and tenderness.
  3. Percussion: Tapping the body surface with the fingers to produce vibrations and evaluate the density of underlying organs.
  4. Auscultation: Listening to sounds produced within the body, typically using a stethoscope, such as heart, lung, and bowel sounds.

Vital Signs Measurement and Anthropometric Assessment

Vital signs provide quantitative baseline data regarding a patient's core physiological functions. They include body temperature, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure, often accompanied by oxygen saturation as the fifth vital sign. Alongside vitals, anthropometric measurements evaluate body composition and nutritional status. These measurements include height, weight, body mass index (BMI), and mid-upper arm circumference, which is especially useful in community health nursing and pediatric assessments. Accurate measurement and recording of these parameters help detect acute clinical deterioration or chronic nutritional imbalances early.

Systemic Examination: Head-to-Toe Approach

The head-to-toe approach is a structured method for conducting a comprehensive physical examination. Starting at the head ensures that no body system is overlooked. The nurse systematically assesses the head and neck, eyes and ears, thorax and lungs, cardiovascular system, abdomen, extremities, and skin. This orderly progression minimizes the number of position changes required of the patient and ensures a thorough, logical flow. Any localized abnormality discovered during this sweep warrants a more focused, in-depth regional assessment.

Documenting and Reporting Physical Examination Findings

Accurate documentation turns observations into permanent, legally binding medical records. Nurses must record findings objectively, avoiding vague terms like 'normal' or 'fine' when precise descriptions are possible. Use clear measurements, standard anatomical landmarks, and descriptive terminology for skin lesions, wounds, and drainage. Document immediately after the assessment while details remain fresh in mind. Furthermore, any critical findings—such as severe hypertension, respiratory distress, or acute pain—must be reported verbally to the attending physician or senior nurse right away.
