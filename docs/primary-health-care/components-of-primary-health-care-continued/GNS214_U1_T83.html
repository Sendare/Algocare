Immunity

Introduction to Immunity and Basic Concepts

Immunity is the body's physiological capability to recognize foreign substances and mount a defense to protect itself from disease. This defense network relies on specialized cells, tissues, and organs working in unison. A primary function of this system is to distinguish between self—the body's own healthy cells—and non-self, which includes invading pathogens like bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. When this recognition fails, the system may mistakenly attack its own tissues, leading to autoimmune disorders. Understanding these baseline concepts is vital for nurses, as it forms the foundation for grasping how vaccines work and why certain infections cause severe illness while others pass unnoticed.

Innate versus Adaptive Immunity

Human defense mechanisms are broadly divided into two main categories: innate and adaptive immunity.

  • Innate Immunity: This is the non-specific defense system you are born with. It acts as the first line of defense, including physical barriers like the skin and mucous membranes, as well as chemical barriers like stomach acid and phagocytic white blood cells that engulf any foreign invader immediately.
  • Adaptive Immunity: This is acquired over time through exposure to specific pathogens. It is slower to respond during a first encounter, but it creates immunological memory, allowing the body to mount a faster, targeted attack if the same pathogen invades again.

A common clinical misconception is that innate immunity cannot improve over time; while it lacks memory, its components can become more reactive during ongoing inflammation.

Types of Acquired Immunity: Active and Passive

Acquired immunity describes protection that develops throughout a person's lifetime and is categorized by how the body acquires the protective components.

  • Active Immunity: Occurs when the body's own immune system is stimulated to produce antibodies and T-cells. This provides long-lasting protection because memory cells are formed.
  • Passive Immunity: Occurs when ready-made antibodies are transferred from an outside source into the body. This provides immediate protection, but it is temporary because no memory cells are generated by the recipient.

Natural versus Artificial Immunity Pathways

Both active and passive immunity can be acquired through either natural or artificial pathways, creating four distinct combinations that nurses must understand for patient education.

  1. Natural Active: Developing an infection like measles and subsequently recovering, leaving permanent resistance.
  2. Natural Passive: Transfer of maternal antibodies to an infant across the placenta or through breast milk.
  3. Artificial Active: Receiving a vaccination containing weakened or killed antigens to stimulate antibody production without causing the full disease.
  4. Artificial Passive: Receiving an injection of immune globulins, such as tetanus antitoxin or rabies immunoglobulin, for immediate post-exposure treatment.

The Role of Antigens and Antibodies

The adaptive immune response relies heavily on the interaction between antigens and antibodies. An antigen is any substance—such as a protein on a bacteria cell wall or virus surface—that the body identifies as foreign and triggers an immune response. In response, specialized white blood cells called B-lymphocytes mature into plasma cells and produce antibodies, which are Y-shaped proteins. Each antibody is uniquely shaped to lock onto a specific antigen, neutralizing it or marking it for destruction by other immune cells. Nurses often monitor antibody levels, known as titers, to determine if a patient has adequate protection against specific diseases like hepatitis B.

Herd Immunity and Community Protection

Herd immunity, also known as community immunity, occurs when a large portion of a population becomes immune to a specific infectious disease, either through vaccination or prior infections. This high level of immunity makes the spread of disease from person to person unlikely. As a result, the whole community becomes protected—including those who cannot be vaccinated, such as newborns, pregnant women, or individuals with compromised immune systems. Achieving herd immunity is a primary goal of public health vaccination programs. If vaccination rates drop in a community, herd immunity breaks down, leading to preventable outbreaks of diseases like measles and polio.

Factors Influencing Immune Response in Primary Health Care

A patient's ability to mount an effective immune response is not uniform and can be influenced by multiple everyday factors encountered in primary health care settings. Nutrition plays a vital role; protein and micronutrient deficiencies impair antibody production and white blood cell function. Age is another critical factor, as both the very young with immature immune systems and older adults experiencing immunosenescence are at higher risk for severe infections. Chronic conditions such as diabetes, stress, fatigue, and certain medications like corticosteroids also suppress immune efficiency. Nurses must assess these lifestyle and clinical factors during health education to help patients optimize their natural defenses.
