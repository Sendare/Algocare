Immunity

Introduction to Immunity and Basic Concepts

Immunity is the physiological capability of the human body to recognize foreign substances, neutralize them, and protect itself against disease-causing microorganisms. This defense network relies heavily on the immune system, which coordinates cells, tissues, and organs to preserve internal balance, or homeostasis. When pathogens like bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites breach physical barriers such as the skin and mucous membranes, internal defense mechanisms mobilize immediately. A failure in this recognition process can lead to infections, autoimmune conditions, or hypersensitivity reactions, making a firm grasp of immunological principles essential for clinical nursing practice.

Innate versus Adaptive Immunity

Defense mechanisms are broadly categorized into two branches: innate and adaptive immunity.

  • Innate immunity: Present from birth, this system provides rapid, non-specific responses to any invading pathogen. It includes physical barriers, phagocytic cells like macrophages, and chemical mediators such as lysozyme.
  • Adaptive immunity: This branch develops over time through exposure to specific pathogens. It is characterized by specificity and immunological memory, mounting a targeted attack via T-lymphocytes and B-lymphocytes and remembering the invader for faster clearance during subsequent exposures.

Types of Acquired Immunity: Active and Passive

Acquired immunity describes protection that is gained during an individual's lifetime and is split into two modes based on how the body acquires the protective agents.

  • Active immunity: Triggered when the individual's own immune system encounters an antigen and manufactures specific antibodies and memory cells. This type takes days or weeks to develop but offers long-lasting protection.
  • Passive immunity: Acquired when pre-formed antibodies are transferred directly into the body from another source, such as maternal antibodies crossing the placenta. This confers immediate protection, but because the recipient's own immune system is not stimulated, the protection fades after a few months as the antibodies degrade.

Natural versus Artificial Immunity Pathways

Immunity can also be classified by how the exposure to the antigen or antibody occurred, intersecting with the active and passive divisions.

  • Natural pathways: Involve everyday life experiences, such as contracting an infection (natural active) or an infant ingesting breast milk (natural passive).
  • Artificial pathways: Involve deliberate medical interventions, such as receiving a vaccination containing attenuated or killed pathogens to stimulate antibody production (artificial active), or receiving an intravenous injection of pre-formed immunoglobulins like tetanus antitoxin following a dirty wound (artificial passive).

The Role of Antigens and Antibodies

The adaptive immune response hinges on the interaction between antigens and antibodies. An antigen is any foreign molecule—often a protein on the surface of a bacterium or virus—that triggers an immune response. In response, specialized white blood cells called B-lymphocytes differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete antibodies (immunoglobulins). These Y-shaped proteins bind specifically to target antigens, neutralizing toxins, marking pathogens for destruction by phagocytes, or activating the complement cascade. Understanding this lock-and-key specificity explains why a specific antibody protects against only one particular strain of pathogen.

Herd Immunity and Community Protection

When a high percentage of a population becomes immune to a contagious disease—either through vaccination or prior infections—herd immunity is established. This community-level protection drastically reduces the probability that an infected individual will encounter a susceptible person, effectively halting transmission chains. Herd immunity is especially critical in protecting vulnerable individuals who cannot receive vaccines, such as newborns, immunocompromised patients, or those with severe allergies. Maintaining high immunization coverage across communities is therefore a cornerstone of public health nursing strategies.

Factors Influencing Immune Response in Primary Health Care

Clinical outcomes depend heavily on variables that strengthen or suppress a patient's immune function. In primary health care settings across Nigeria, nurses must assess factors such as malnutrition, chronic illnesses like diabetes, extreme ages, and chronic stress, all of which impair lymphocyte proliferation and antibody production. Additionally, environmental exposures and adherence to immunization schedules dictate community vulnerability. Recognizing these determinants enables community health nurses to tailor nutritional support, health education, and vaccination campaigns effectively.
