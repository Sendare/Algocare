Diseases Preventable by Immunization

Introduction to Vaccine-Preventable Diseases

Vaccine-preventable diseases are infectious illnesses that can be significantly reduced or completely avoided through the timely administration of safe and effective immunizations. In regions like Nigeria, where certain pathogenic burdens remain a public health concern, these diseases contribute heavily to childhood morbidity and mortality if left unchecked. Immunization works by stimulating the host immune system to produce specific antibodies without causing the actual disease, creating immunological memory that protects against future exposure.

From a community health nursing perspective, understanding these diseases goes beyond treating symptoms; it involves recognizing transmission routes, vulnerable populations, and herd immunity thresholds. When a high enough percentage of a population is vaccinated, the spread of the contagious agent slows down drastically, protecting infants and immunocompromised individuals who cannot receive certain vaccines. Nurses serve as the primary educators and administrators in bridging the gap between available biomedical interventions and community uptake.

The Expanded Programme on Immunization and Schedule

The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), launched globally by the World Health Organization and adapted nationally, provides a structured framework for delivering routine vaccines to infants and pregnant women. In Nigeria, this schedule is integrated into primary health care delivery to ensure accessibility regardless of geography.

  • At birth: Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) for tuberculosis and the first dose of oral polio vaccine (OPV 0).
  • 6 weeks: Second dose of oral polio vaccine, pentavalent vaccine (Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b), and pneumococcal conjugate vaccine.
  • 10 weeks: Third round of oral polio, pentavalent, and pneumococcal doses.
  • 14 weeks: Final infant doses of oral polio, inactivated polio vaccine (IPV), pentavalent, and pneumococcal vaccines.
  • 9 to 15 months: Measles vaccine and yellow fever immunization.

Tuberculosis and Poliomyelitis

Tuberculosis and poliomyelitis represent two major viral and bacterial targets of childhood immunization programs.

  • Tuberculosis: Caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, this chronic airborne infection primarily affects the lungs. In infants, it can progress rapidly into severe forms like tuberculous meningitis. The BCG vaccine is given intradermally at birth to protect young children from these disseminated forms, leaving a characteristic scar.
  • Poliomyelitis: A highly contagious viral infection transmitted via the fecal-oral route that invades the nervous system and can cause irreversible paralysis within hours. Both the Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV) and the Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV) are utilized to interrupt transmission and eradicate the wild poliovirus.

Diphtheria, Pertussis, and Tetanus

The pentavalent vaccine combines protection against five distinct pathogens, three of which are diphtheria, pertussis, and tetanus.

  • Diphtheria: Caused by *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*, this bacterial infection produces a thick grey membrane in the throat that can obstruct the airway and release systemic toxins affecting the heart and nerves.
  • Pertussis: Commonly known as whooping cough, this bacterial infection causes severe, uncontrollable coughing fits that make it difficult to breathe, posing a fatal threat to young neonates.
  • Tetanus: Caused by *Clostridium tetani*, spores entering through broken skin produce a potent neurotoxin leading to painful muscle spasms and lockjaw. Neonatal tetanus remains a critical concern linked to unhygienic umbilical cord care during delivery.

Nurses must monitor children for mild side effects like low-grade fever and localized soreness following pentavalent administration, reassuring parents that these reactions indicate normal immune response activation.

Measles, Yellow Fever, and Hepatitis B

Measles, yellow fever, and hepatitis b target major organ systems and require specific immunization timing to ensure lifelong or durable protection.

  • Measles: A highly contagious viral infection characterized by a high fever, generalized maculopapular rash, and Koplik spots in the mouth. Complications include severe diarrhea, pneumonia, and blindness.
  • Yellow Fever: A viral hemorrhagic disease transmitted by infected mosquitoes, causing jaundice, fever, and potential liver failure. The yellow fever vaccine is typically administered around 9 to 12 months of age.
  • Hepatitis B: A blood-borne and body-fluid-transmitted virus that attacks the liver, leading to chronic infection, cirrhosis, or hepatocellular carcinoma. Administering the birth dose within 24 hours of delivery is critical for preventing perinatal transmission from an infected mother.

Meningococcal Meningitis and Haemophilus Influenzae Type b

Bacterial infections affecting the meninges and respiratory system pose severe epidemic risks, particularly in sub-Saharan regions prone to seasonal outbreaks.

  • Meningococcal Meningitis: Caused by *Neisseria meningitidis*, this infection inflames the protective membranes covering the brain and spinal cord. Symptoms include sudden high fever, stiff neck, and photophobia. Crowded living conditions can accelerate community transmission.
  • Haemophilus Influenzae Type b (Hib): A bacterium responsible for severe childhood illnesses, including pneumonia, epiglottitis, and bacterial meningitis. Hib is a key component of the pentavalent vaccine schedule.

Healthcare providers must maintain a high index of suspicion during dry seasons when meningitis rates spike, educating communities on early symptom recognition and seeking prompt medical care.

Community Surveillance and Cold Chain Management

Vaccines are biological products sensitive to environmental changes, meaning their potency depends entirely on maintaining an unbroken cold chain—a system of storage and transport equipment that keeps vaccines at recommended temperatures from the manufacturer to the point of administration.

  • Temperature Monitoring: Vaccine refrigerators must be monitored daily using standard thermometers. Vaccines like OPV tolerate freezing, whereas vaccines containing adjuvants, such as pentavalent or tetanus, can be permanently damaged if frozen.
  • Surveillance: Nurses play an active role in disease surveillance by reporting suspected cases of vaccine-preventable illnesses to local public health authorities. Prompt reporting allows rapid containment measures, outbreak investigations, and mass vaccination campaigns.

Nursing Roles in Vaccine Promotion and Client Education

Nurses are uniquely positioned to address vaccine hesitancy, dispel cultural myths, and advocate for high immunization coverage within communities. Effective communication involves listening respectfully to caregiver concerns, providing transparent information regarding minor side effects, and emphasizing the severe risks associated with preventable illnesses.

Beyond the clinic walls, community health nurses conduct outreach programs, track defaulters who miss scheduled doses, and collaborate with local leaders to build trust. Ensuring every child completes their immunization timeline requires persistent follow-up, empathetic counseling, and active engagement with families at every healthcare touchpoint.
