Diseases Preventable by Immunization

Introduction to Vaccine-Preventable Diseases in Nigeria

Vaccine-preventable diseases remain a significant public health challenge across Nigeria, disproportionately affecting infants and children under five years of age. These conditions are caused by specific pathogens—bacteria or viruses—that can spread rapidly in communities with suboptimal immunization coverage. The burden is heavily felt in resource-limited settings where malnutrition, overcrowding, and delayed healthcare-seeking behaviors worsen clinical outcomes. For the nursing professional, understanding the pathophysiology and transmission routes of these illnesses is foundational to stopping outbreaks at the primary healthcare level. Herd immunity plays a protective role here; when a high enough percentage of a population is vaccinated, the spread of contagious agents from person to person becomes highly unlikely, safeguarding vulnerable infants who are too young to receive their own inoculations.

The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) and National Schedule

The Expanded Programme on Immunization is a global strategy launched by the World Health Organization to make life-saving vaccines universally available. In Nigeria, the National Programme on Immunization outlines a strict calendar of visits from birth through the first year of life.

  • Birth: Bacillus Calmette-Guérin and oral polio vaccine dose zero.
  • Six Weeks: First doses of pentavalent vaccine, oral polio vaccine, pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, and rotavirus vaccine.
  • Ten Weeks: Second doses of pentavalent, oral polio, pneumococcal conjugate, and rotavirus vaccines.
  • Fourteen Weeks: Third doses of pentavalent and oral polio vaccines, along with inactivated polio vaccine and the first dose of measles vaccine.
  • Nine Months: Second dose of measles vaccine, yellow fever vaccine, and meningitis vaccine depending on regional risk.

Tuberculosis and Poliomyelitis

Tuberculosis is a chronic bacterial infection caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily targeting the pulmonary system in adults but capable of causing devastating disseminated disease like tuberculous meningitis in infants. Prevention relies on the intradermal administration of the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin vaccine, which leaves a characteristic scar on the left deltoid. Poliomyelitis is a highly contagious viral illness transmitted via the fecal-oral route that invades the central nervous system, leading to irreversible acute flaccid paralysis. While Nigeria has made historic strides in interrupting wild poliovirus transmission, surveillance for acute flaccid paralysis remains a core competency for community nurses to detect imported strains or vaccine-derived outbreaks immediately.

Diphtheria, Pertussis, and Tetanus

The pentavalent vaccine protects against five distinct pathogens, three of which are diphtheria, pertussis, and tetanus. Diphtheria is caused by *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*, producing an adherent gray pseudomembrane in the pharynx that can obstruct the airway, alongside systemic exotoxins that damage the myocardium. Pertussis, or whooping cough, is driven by *Bordetella pertussis*, characterized by paroxysmal coughing fits ending in a high-pitched inspiratory whoop, which frequently causes apnea in neonates. Tetanus stems from *Clostridium tetani* spores entering through contaminated skin breaks; its neurotoxin triggers painful muscle rigidity, trismus, and spasms. Nurses must monitor for local swelling or mild fever following pentavalent administration while assuring caregivers that these reactions are normal immune responses.

Measles, Yellow Fever, and Viral Hepatitis

Measles is an extremely contagious viral disease marked by high fever, cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, and a characteristic descending maculopapular rash, often preceded by pathognomonic Koplik spots inside the cheeks. Complications include severe diarrhea, pneumonia, and encephalitis. Yellow fever is an arboviral hemorrhagic disease transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, presenting with jaundice, fever, and potential renal failure. Hepatitis B targets the liver through contact with infected blood or bodily fluids, risking chronic cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Administering the birth dose of the hepatitis B vaccine within twenty-four hours of delivery is critical for preventing perinatal transmission from infected mothers.

Meningococcal Meningitis and Haemophilus Influenzae Type b

Bacterial meningitis and invasive respiratory infections pose severe risks to young children, particularly in the African meningitis belt where seasonal outbreaks occur. Neisseria meningitidis causes meningococcal meningitis, a rapidly progressive infection presenting with high fever, neck stiffness, photophobia, and a non-blanching purpuric rash. Haemophilus influenzae type b is a bacterial culprit behind epiglottitis, pneumonia, and meningitis. Vaccination against both pathogens has drastically reduced childhood mortality and neurological sequelae such as deafness and cognitive impairment. Nurses assessing febrile infants must remain vigilant for meningeal signs, as prompt antibiotic therapy alongside preventive vaccination campaigns remains life-saving.

Community Surveillance and Cold Chain Management

Maintaining vaccine potency from the central medical store down to rural primary health clinics relies entirely on the cold chain—an unbroken system of storage and transport equipment operating within temperatures of +2°C to +8°C for most vaccines. Vaccines like the oral polio vaccine can withstand freezing, while freeze-sensitive vaccines such as pentavalent and tetanus toxoid are permanently ruined if exposed to sub-zero temperatures. Nurses must check vaccine vial monitors before administration to verify potency. Concurrently, community disease surveillance requires frontline health workers to report suspected cases of acute flaccid paralysis, measles, and yellow fever instantly to public health authorities to contain potential epidemics.

Nursing Roles in Vaccine Promotion and Client Education

Nurses act as trusted educators and advocates within Nigerian communities, bridging the gap between healthcare facilities and local populations. Overcoming vaccine hesitancy requires addressing cultural misconceptions, religious rumors, and fear of side effects through empathetic dialogue and evidence-based explanations. Healthcare providers must conduct rigorous health education talks during antenatal and immunization clinics, emphasizing completion of the full schedule rather than just receiving the first dose. Tracking defaulters through community outreach ensures high-risk children do not fall out of the system, ultimately sustaining community-wide protection against preventable diseases.
