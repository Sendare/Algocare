Diseases Preventable by Immunization

Introduction to Vaccine-Preventable Diseases in Nigeria

Vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) represent a significant public health challenge in Nigeria, contributing considerably to childhood morbidity and mortality. These are infectious diseases for which an effective prophylactic vaccine exists and is recommended nationally. In the Nigerian healthcare context, encountering conditions like measles, pertussis, and poliomyelitis remains a reality due to gaps in routine immunization coverage, geographical barriers, and socioeconomic factors. As nursing students and future frontline healthcare providers in Nigeria, understanding the pathophysiology, transmission dynamics, and clinical manifestations of these diseases is crucial. Vaccines work by stimulating the host immune system to produce memory B and T cells without causing the actual disease, thereby conferring active immunity. When a high percentage of the population is vaccinated, herd immunity is achieved, which protects vulnerable, unvaccinated individuals, including newborns and immunocompromised patients. In resource-limited settings, endemic VPDs strain healthcare infrastructure and push families into deeper poverty due to catastrophic out-of-pocket spending on treatments. Nurses must recognize that disease prevention is vastly more cost-effective and life-saving than disease treatment. By mastering the principles of vaccine immunology and epidemiology, Nigerian nurses can better advocate for immunization uptake, debunk cultural myths, and participate actively in eradication and elimination campaigns spearheaded by the Federal Ministry of Health and the World Health Organization. Ultimately, reducing the burden of vaccine-preventable diseases is a cornerstone of achieving Sustainable Development Goal 3 in Nigeria.

The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) and National Schedule

The Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) was launched globally by the World Health Organization in 1974 and subsequently in Nigeria to protect children against common disabling and fatal infectious diseases. In Nigeria, the National Programme on Immunization (NPI), now integrated into the Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA), coordinates the scheduling and distribution of routine vaccines. The Nigerian routine immunization schedule outlines the specific ages at which infants and pregnant women must receive life-saving antigens. At birth, newborns receive the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine against tuberculosis and the oral polio vaccine (OPV zero dose). As the infant grows, visits at 6, 10, and 14 weeks are scheduled for pentavalent vaccine (Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b), pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV), rotavirus vaccine, and additional doses of oral and inactivated polio vaccines. Furthermore, the schedule incorporates the measles vaccine and yellow fever vaccine administered at 9 months of age, alongside meningitis vaccines depending on regional epidemiological risks. Understanding this schedule is mandatory for nursing students, as missed appointments lead to prolonged susceptibility windows. Nurses are responsible for reviewing child health cards, administering vaccines via correct routes, documenting doses accurately, and counseling caregivers on return dates. Adhering to the national immunization schedule is the single most effective strategy to safeguard the Nigerian child population from debilitating infectious morbidities.

Tuberculosis and Poliomyelitis

Tuberculosis (TB) and poliomyelitis are two major public health concerns targeted by childhood immunization programs in Nigeria. Tuberculosis, caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily affects the lungs (pulmonary TB) but can manifest extra-pulmonary forms like meningitis in children. The Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine is given intradermally at birth to protect infants against severe, disseminated forms of TB, such as tuberculous meningitis and miliary TB. Though BCG does not completely prevent primary infection, it significantly reduces childhood mortality. Poliomyelitis is a highly contagious viral infection caused by the poliovirus, which attacks the central nervous system and can cause irreversible paralysis within hours. Transmission occurs via the fecal-oral route or through contaminated water and food. Nigeria historically struggled as a polio-endemic nation, but through intensive vaccination campaigns using Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV) and Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV), the country attained wild poliovirus-free status in 2020. However, circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (cVDPV) outbreaks still occur, necessitating continued vigilance, high routine coverage, and synchronized vaccination campaigns. Nurses play a critical role in administering these vaccines correctly, identifying acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) as part of surveillance, and educating communities on proper sanitation, hygiene, and the absolute necessity of completing all polio vaccine doses.

Diphtheria, Pertussis, and Tetanus (DPT)

Diphtheria, pertussis, and tetanus are severe bacterial infections historically grouped together under the DPT vaccine, now delivered in Nigeria via the pentavalent vaccine formulation. Diphtheria is caused by Corynebacterium diphtheriae, producing an exotoxin that forms a characteristic greyish pseudomembrane in the pharynx, potentially causing airway obstruction and toxic myocarditis. Pertussis, commonly known as whooping cough, is caused by Bordetella pertussis and is characterized by severe, paroxysmal coughing fits ending in a high-pitched 'whoop,' frequently causing hypoxia, apnea, and pneumonia in infants. Tetanus is caused by Clostridium tetani, an anaerobic spore-forming bacterium found in soil; its neurotoxin (tetanospasmin) causes painful muscle spasms, lockjaw (trismus), and autonomic instability. Tetanus can affect neonates (tetanus neonatorum) through unhygienic umbilical cord cutting practices, as well as children and adults through contaminated skin wounds. The pentavalent vaccine combines antigens against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b, administered at 6, 10, and 14 weeks of age. Additionally, pregnant women receive Tetanus-containing Toxoid (TdT) vaccines during antenatal visits to protect both the mother and prevent neonatal tetanus. Nurses must ensure complete series completion, monitor for local adverse events following immunization (AEFI) such as mild fever and swelling, and provide clear discharge instructions on comfort measures.

Measles, Yellow Fever, and Hepatitis B

Measles, yellow fever, and hepatitis B represent major viral threats to child and public health in Nigeria. Measles is an extremely contagious viral illness spread via respiratory droplets, marked by a prodrome of fever, cough, coryza, and conjunctivitis, followed by Koplik spots on the buccal mucosa and a generalized maculopapular rash. Complications include severe diarrhea, otitis media, pneumonia, encephalitis, and corneal ulceration leading to blindness, especially in malnourished children. The measles vaccine is administered at 9 months, with a second dose introduced in many regions to boost seroconversion rates. Yellow fever is a viral hemorrhagic fever transmitted by infected Aedes mosquitoes. It presents with fever, jaundice (hence 'yellow'), myalgia, and potential hepatic and renal failure. The yellow fever vaccine is also given at 9 months and confers lifelong immunity in most recipients. Hepatitis B is a blood-borne and sexually transmitted virus that attacks the liver, leading to chronic infection, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Mother-to-child (vertical) transmission during birth is a major route in endemic areas, making the timely birth dose of the hepatitis B vaccine and subsequent pentavalent doses vital. Nurses must emphasize the importance of these vaccines, ensure proper administration technique (subcutaneous for measles and yellow fever, intramuscular for hepatitis B), and educate caregivers on recognizing potential complications.

Meningococcal Meningitis and Haemophilus Influenzae Type b

Meningococcal meningitis and Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) are formidable bacterial pathogens responsible for severe invasive diseases such as meningitis, sepsis, and epiglottitis in Nigerian children. Meningococcal meningitis, caused by Neisseria meningitidis, is an acute inflammation of the meninges characterized by sudden high fever, severe headache, neck stiffness (nuchal rigidity), photophobia, and petechial or purpuric rashes. Nigeria lies within the African 'meningitis belt,' experiencing periodic dry-season epidemics (harmattan season) driven by specific serogroups like A, C, W, and recently Y and X. Conjugate meningococcal vaccines are deployed during routine immunization and reactive mass vaccination campaigns. Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) is another encapsulated bacterium that causes devastating bacterial meningitis, pneumonia, and life-threatening epiglottitis in infants and young children under five years old. Hib is incorporated into the pentavalent vaccine, dramatically reducing the incidence of invasive Hib disease since its nationwide adoption. Nurses must be highly skilled in performing physical assessments to detect early signs of meningeal irritation, such as positive Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs. Furthermore, nurses play a pivotal role in educating communities about overcrowding ventilation during harmattan, promoting early reporting of febrile illnesses, and ensuring vaccine cold chain integrity to protect these thermolabile biological products.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of Target Diseases

Clinical assessment and identification of vaccine-preventable diseases require nurses to possess sharp observational skills, thorough knowledge of history-taking, and familiarity with diagnostic criteria. When a child presents to a Nigerian primary healthcare clinic or hospital with fever, rash, cough, respiratory distress, or neurological changes, the nurse must rapidly triage the patient while maintaining standard and droplet infection control precautions. Identifying target diseases involves recognizing distinct clinical syndromes: the paroxysmal cough of pertussis, the pseudomembrane of diphtheria, the generalized maculopapular rash of measles, the paralysis of poliomyelitis, or the nuchal rigidity and petechial rash of meningitis. Nurses must routinely inquire about the child's immunization history using the home-based health record (immunization card) to determine if doses were missed, delayed, or completely omitted. Physical examination techniques must be executed gently yet thoroughly, inspecting the oropharynx, checking skin turgor and hydration, evaluating respiratory effort, and assessing neurological status. Early recognition prevents complications, guides prompt isolation and medical treatment, and ensures mandatory disease notification to public health authorities for rapid outbreak response. Furthermore, clinical assessment serves as an educational touchpoint where nurses can counsel hesitant or uninformed parents on the direct link between unimmunized status and severe disease presentation.

Community Surveillance and Cold Chain Management

Community surveillance and cold chain management are the twin pillars supporting the success of immunization programs in Nigeria. Community surveillance involves the systematic collection, analysis, and interpretation of health data to detect disease outbreaks and monitor disease eradication. Nurses participate actively in disease surveillance by reporting cases of acute flaccid paralysis, suspected measles, neonatal tetanus, and meningitis to local government area (LGA) disease surveillance and notification officers (DSNOs). Without robust surveillance, public health authorities cannot allocate resources or mount targeted vaccination campaigns. Concurrently, cold chain management ensures that vaccines are stored, transported, and handled within recommended temperature ranges (typically +2°C to +8°C for most refrigerated vaccines, and freezing temperatures for oral polio and measles vaccines stored in freezers) from the point of manufacture to the point of administration. In Nigeria, erratic electricity supply and difficult terrain pose severe threats to the cold chain, necessitating the use of solar-powered refrigerators, cold boxes, vaccine carriers, and ice packs. Nurses are legally and professionally responsible for monitoring and recording daily refrigerator temperatures, practicing the 'first-to-expire, first-out' (FEFO) principle, and discarding vaccines exposed to heat stockouts or temperature excursions (the shake test can help check for frozen DPT/pentavalent vaccines). A broken cold chain renders vaccines impotent, leaving children falsely assumed to be immune.

Nursing Roles in Vaccine Promotion and Client Education

Nurses play a multifaceted, indispensable role in vaccine promotion, client education, and overcoming vaccine hesitancy across Nigerian communities. Vaccine hesitancy—driven by religious misconceptions, cultural myths, misinformation, and fear of side effects—remains a major barrier to achieving optimal immunization coverage. Nurses act as trusted community health educators who must engage mothers, fathers, traditional rulers, and religious leaders in open, respectful dialogue. Effective client education involves explaining the benefits of immunization in simple, locally understood language, addressing safety concerns transparently, and clarifying that mild side effects like low-grade fever, soreness at the injection site, or mild irritability are normal physiological responses indicating the immune system is reacting to the vaccine. Furthermore, nurses must utilize every clinical encounter—such as antenatal clinics, postnatal visits, pediatric ward admissions, and out-patient consultations—as an opportunity to screen immunization cards and counsel caregivers. Community outreach programs, home visits, and health talks at market squares and churches are powerful avenues for nurses to reach defaulters and mobile populations. By building strong therapeutic nurse-client relationships and demonstrating unwavering competence, Nigerian nurses can foster positive health-seeking behaviors, dispel dangerous myths, and ensure that every child is fully protected against vaccine-preventable diseases.
