Intra-Natal Care

Introduction and Objectives of Intra-Natal Care

Intra-natal care encompasses the continuous clinical monitoring and supportive management provided to a woman and her fetus from the onset of true labour until the complete expulsion of the placenta. The primary goal is achieving a healthy maternal and neonatal outcome with minimal intervention, preserving the dignity and safety of the woman throughout the birth process. Intra-natal services must anticipate deviations from normal physiological patterns, ensuring that potential complications like postpartum haemorrhage or fetal distress are identified and managed before they threaten life. In many resource-constrained primary health centres across Nigeria, skilled birth attendants rely on systematic risk assessment and timely escalation protocols to bridge the gap between community deliveries and comprehensive emergency obstetric care facilities.

Anatomy and Physiology of Normal Labour

Normal labour is driven by the coordinated interplay of the 'powers', the 'passenger', and the 'passage'.

  • Powers: Involuntary uterine contractions work alongside voluntary maternal pushing efforts to efface and dilate the cervix while propelling the fetus downward.
  • Passenger: The fetus, specifically its head size, presentation, position, and attitude, must adapt to the birth canal.
  • Passage: The maternal bony pelvis and soft tissues provide the structural pathway through which the fetus must navigate via cardinal movements including engagement, descent, flexion, internal rotation, extension, restitution, and external rotation.

The Four Stages of Labour and Their Management

Labour progresses through four distinct physiological phases, each demanding specific monitoring priorities from the midwife.

  1. First Stage: Begins with true labour contractions and ends with full cervical dilation (10 cm). It is divided into latent and active phases.
  2. Second Stage: Spans from full cervical dilation to the birth of the baby, characterized by irresistible maternal urge to push.
  3. Third Stage: Extends from the birth of the newborn until the complete expulsion of the placenta and fetal membranes.
  4. Fourth Stage: Covers the immediate two hours postpartum, a critical window for monitoring uterine involution and preventing primary postpartum haemorrhage.

Assessment and Monitoring of Mother and Fetus

Continuous clinical evaluation ensures that maternal physiological adaptation remains within safe parameters while fetal well-being is preserved. Maternal vital signs—blood pressure, pulse rate, and temperature—are tracked regularly to detect early signs of dehydration, exhaustion, infection, or pre-eclampsia. Fetal heart rate monitoring is performed using a Pinard stethoscope or a Doppler ultrasound device, particularly immediately after a uterine contraction to catch late decelerations. Abdominal palpation confirms ongoing fetal descent and position, while vaginal examinations evaluate cervical change, membrane status, and the degree of caput or molding when clinically indicated.

The Partograph: Principles and Utilization

The partograph is a pictorial, graphical overview of labour that serves as an early warning system to prevent prolonged and obstructed labour. It tracks cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, contraction frequency, and maternal condition against elapsed time. Action and alert lines on the cervical dilation graph guide clinical decision-making; when progress crosses the alert line into the action line, timely interventions or facility transfer must be initiated. Regular plotting prevents both unnecessary surgical interventions and dangerous delays in managing labour dystocia, making it an indispensable tool in Nigerian maternity units.

Aseptic Techniques and Clean Delivery Practices

Infection prevention during childbirth protects both the puerperal mother and the neonate from severe sepsis. Midwives must adhere strictly to standard precautions, including thorough hand hygiene, the wearing of sterile gloves during vaginal examinations and the second stage of labour, and the use of sterile drapes. The 'clean delivery' practices emphasize the clean hands, clean surface, clean blade, clean cord tie, and clean cord stump principle, which dramatically reduces neonatal tetanus and puerperal sepsis in community and low-resource settings. Antiseptic solutions are applied to the perineum thoughtfully to reduce microbial colonization without disrupting normal vaginal flora.

Immediate Newborn Care and Resuscitation

The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life requires rapid assessment and prompt supportive interventions. Immediately following birth, the newborn is dried thoroughly, placed in skin-to-skin contact with the mother, and assessed for vigorous breathing and tone. Routine care includes delayed cord clamping, eye prophylaxis, and vitamin K administration to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn. If the infant fails to breathe spontaneously, initial steps of resuscitation—warmth maintenance, airway clearing if obstructed, and tactile stimulation—must be initiated within the golden minute, followed by bag-valve-mask ventilation with room air if heart rate remains low or apnea persists.

Identification and Management of Intra-Partum Complications

Intra-partum complications require swift recognition and structured emergency management to safeguard maternal and fetal survival.

  • Postpartum Haemorrhage (PPH): Managed through active management of the third stage of labour (AMTSL) using uterotonics like oxytocin, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage.
  • Fetal Distress: Addressed by maternal repositioning, high-flow oxygen administration, stopping oxytocin infusion if running, and preparing for expedited delivery.
  • Shoulder Dystocia: Requires maneuvers such as McRoberts positioning and suprapubic pressure rather than excessive traction on the fetal head.

Documentation, Referral, and Post-Delivery Handover

Comprehensive documentation concludes every intra-natal encounter, ensuring legal accountability and continuity of nursing care. The birth record must accurately capture the duration of each labour stage, interventions performed, maternal vital signs, newborn Apgar scores, and medication administration times. When complications exceed the facility's capacity, a structured referral using standardized communication tools ensures receiving units are prepared. The postnatal handover to the ward nurse highlights critical risk factors, such as blood loss volume or risk of postpartum pre-eclampsia, securing safe transition into the postpartum period.
