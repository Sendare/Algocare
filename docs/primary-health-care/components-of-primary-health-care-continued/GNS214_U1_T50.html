Intra-Natal Care

Introduction and Objectives of Intra-Natal Care

Intra-natal care, commonly known as intrapartum care, encompasses the specialized care and clinical management provided to a pregnant woman and her fetus during labor and childbirth. As nursing students preparing for professional practice in Nigeria, understanding this phase is crucial for reducing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, which remain significant public health concerns in the region. The primary objectives of intra-natal care are to ensure the birth of a healthy baby while safeguarding the life, health, and psychological well-being of the mother. Key goals include providing continuous emotional and physical support, monitoring the progress of labor meticulously, identifying any deviations from the normal physiological process early, and executing timely, evidence-based interventions. Furthermore, intra-natal care emphasizes infection prevention, promotion of natural delivery processes when safe, and prompt preparation for emergency obstetric interventions such as cesarean sections or instrumental deliveries if complications arise. By maintaining vigilance, adhering to national clinical protocols, and practicing compassionate patient-centered care, nurses play a pivotal role in achieving positive birth outcomes and fostering a reassuring birthing experience for every mother.

Anatomy and Physiology of Normal Labour

Labour is a physiological process characterized by coordinated, rhythmic uterine contractions that result in the effacement and dilation of the cervix, ultimately expelling the fetus and placenta. Understanding the anatomy of the female pelvis and the fetal skull is essential for nursing students to comprehend the mechanism of labor. The true pelvis, consisting of the pelvic inlet, cavity, and outlet, represents the bony canal through which the fetus must pass. The fetal skull features key sutures and fontanelles (such as the anterior and posterior fontanelles) that allow molding—the overlapping of cranial bones—to facilitate passage through the birth canal. Physiology of labor is driven by hormonal shifts, including a rise in estrogen and prostaglandins combined with a decrease in progesterone, which heighten uterine sensitivity. Oxytocin receptors also increase significantly in the myometrium, enhancing uterine contractility. The mechanism of labor describes the cardinal movements the fetus must undergo to adapt its smallest diameter to the pelvic dimensions. These movements include engagement, descent, flexion, internal rotation, extension, restitution, and external rotation, followed by expulsion. Nurses must monitor these physiological adaptations to differentiate normal progress from mechanical or functional dystocia.

The Four Stages of Labour and Their Management

Labour is clinically divided into four distinct stages, each requiring specific nursing assessments and management strategies. The first stage begins with the onset of true labor contractions and ends with full cervical dilation (10 cm). It is subdivided into latent and active phases, where nurses monitor contraction frequency, duration, intensity, and cervical progress. Pain management, maternal hydration, and psychological reassurance are critical here. The second stage spans from full cervical dilation to the complete expulsion of the fetus. Nursing care focuses on encouraging effective maternal pushing during contractions, monitoring fetal heart rate closely, providing perineal support, and preparing for spontaneous or assisted delivery. The third stage extends from the birth of the baby until the complete delivery of the placenta and membranes. Active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL)—which includes prophylactic uterotonic administration (e.g., oxytocin), controlled cord traction, and uterine massage—is standard practice to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. The fourth stage encompasses the first two hours immediately following placental delivery. During this recovery phase, the nurse closely monitors maternal vital signs, uterine tone, lochial discharge, and bladder distension to detect early signs of postpartum hemorrhage or cardiovascular instability while facilitating early mother-infant bonding.

Assessment and Monitoring of Mother and Fetus

Comprehensive assessment and continuous monitoring of both the mother and fetus are vital components of intra-natal care that ensure safety and early detection of complications. For the mother, baseline vital signs including blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiration rate must be checked regularly. Monitoring hydration status, urine output, and emotional well-being is equally important. Uterine contractions are evaluated for frequency, strength, and duration, while vaginal examinations assess cervical dilation, effacement, membrane status, and fetal station. Fetal well-being is monitored primarily through intermittent auscultation of the fetal heart rate (FHR) using a Pinard stethoscope or a Doppler ultrasound device, or through continuous electronic fetal monitoring (cardiotocography) when indicated. FHR monitoring helps identify signs of fetal hypoxia or distress, such as abnormal baselines, late decelerations, or severe variable decelerations. Nurses must document all findings promptly and recognize that any sudden alteration in maternal vital signs or non-reassuring fetal heart patterns warrants immediate reporting to the attending obstetrician or midwife for timely intervention.

The Partograph: Principles and Utilization

The partograph is a pictorial, graphical overview of labor progress and maternal-fetal condition, recommended by the World Health Organization for use during the active phase of labor. It serves as an early-warning system to prevent prolonged and obstructed labor, uterine rupture, and postpartum hemorrhage. The partograph tracks several key parameters, including fetal heart rate, amniotic fluid status, molding, cervical dilation, descent of the fetal head, uterine contractions, oxytocin administration, drugs given, and maternal vital signs (blood pressure, pulse, and temperature). A crucial feature of the partograph is the alert line and action line. When cervical dilation plots to the right of the alert line, it indicates potentially prolonged labor, prompting increased vigilance and transfer to a comprehensive care facility if necessary. If the plot crosses the action line, immediate intervention is required to manage dystocia or deliver the baby. Nursing students must master accurate plotting and interpretation of the partograph to facilitate timely, life-saving clinical decisions.

Aseptic Techniques and Clean Delivery Practices

Maintaining strict aseptic techniques and clean delivery practices is fundamental to preventing puerperal sepsis, neonatal tetanus, and other infectious morbidities associated with childbirth. Nurses must adhere to standard precautions, including proper hand hygiene before and after patient contact, wearing sterile gloves during vaginal examinations and delivery, and utilizing sterile drapes to create a clean field. The 'clean birth' practices recommended by global health bodies focus on clean hands, clean surfaces, clean cord care, and clean perineum. Instruments utilized for amniotomy, cord clamping, and perineal repair must be thoroughly sterilized or autoclaved. Additionally, nurses should minimize the number of unnecessary vaginal examinations to reduce the risk of ascending pathogens from the lower genital tract. Environmental cleanliness in the labor and delivery ward, proper disposal of infectious waste in color-coded bins, and safe sharps handling are integral components of infection prevention protocols. By diligently practicing asepsis, nurses protect both the mother and the newborn from preventable hospital-acquired infections.

Immediate Newborn Care and Resuscitation

Immediate newborn care begins the moment the baby is born and focuses on facilitating the transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life. The nurse must immediately dry the infant with a clean, warm towel to stimulate breathing and prevent hypothermia, simultaneously assessing the baby's color, tone, and cry. The Apgar score is assessed at 1 and 5 minutes post-delivery to evaluate heart rate, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability, and color. Most newborns transition smoothly with routine care, which includes delayed cord clamping, skin-to-skin contact, and initiating early breastfeeding. However, a small percentage of newborns require resuscitation. The nurse must be skilled in basic neonatal resuscitation steps: providing warmth, clearing the airway gently if obstructed, initiating positive pressure ventilation (PPV) with a bag-valve-mask device if the infant is apneic or has a heart rate below 100 beats per minute, and monitoring heart rate response. Administering eye prophylaxis (e.g., tetracycline eye ointment) and Vitamin K injection to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn are also standard immediate care protocols.

Identification and Management of Intra-Partum Complications

Intra-partum complications can arise unexpectedly and threaten the lives of both mother and fetus, requiring rapid identification and decisive management. Common complications include postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), obstructed labor, shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse, and uterine rupture. PPH, often caused by uterine atony, is managed through fundal massage, uterotonic drugs, and bimanual compression. Obstructed labor, frequently resulting from cephalopelvic disproportion, presents with severe molding, caput succedaneum, and maternal exhaustion; it requires emergency cesarean section. Shoulder dystocia occurs when the fetal anterior shoulder impacts against the maternal pubic symphysis; nurses must immediately execute maneuvers such as McRoberts maneuver and suprapubic pressure. Cord prolapse is an obstetric emergency where the umbilical cord slips ahead of the presenting part, compromising fetal blood supply; the nurse must manually elevate the presenting part off the cord and position the mother in a knee-chest or exaggerated Sims position while preparing for immediate delivery. Recognizing warning signs and coordinating emergency multidisciplinary responses are vital competencies for safe nursing practice.

Documentation, Referral, and Post-Delivery Handover

Accurate documentation, prompt referral, and structured post-delivery handover are essential pillars of safe obstetric care that ensure continuity and accountability. Nurses must document all observations, clinical interventions, medication administrations, and the exact times events occur in the patient's records and partograph. Clear records protect healthcare providers legally and provide crucial clinical data for subsequent caregivers. When complications exceed the capacity of the primary health center, timely referral is critical. The nurse must stabilize the mother and baby, compile a comprehensive referral summary, ensure accompanying medical personnel are available, and communicate effectively with the receiving facility. Post-delivery handover follows structured communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to relay vital information to the postpartum ward team. Key handover details include the type of delivery, estimated blood loss, maternal vital sign trends, infant status, and any administered medications. Diligent documentation and handovers minimize communication errors and optimize maternal-neonatal recovery outcomes.
