Intra-Natal Care

Introduction and Objectives of Intra-Natal Care

Intra-natal care refers to the specialized support, monitoring, and medical management provided to a woman and her unborn child throughout the process of labor and childbirth. The primary goal is achieving a safe delivery for both mother and baby while preventing, detecting, and managing any complications early. Nurses play a crucial role by providing continuous physical care, emotional reassurance, and clinical observation during this vulnerable period. Ensuring a positive birth experience requires respecting the cultural preferences and dignity of the woman while maintaining strict adherence to clinical standards.

Anatomy and Physiology of Normal Labour

Normal labor is driven by the interaction of the passenger, the passage, and the power.

  • The Passenger: The fetus, whose size, lie, presentation, and attitude affect how easily it moves through the pelvis.
  • The Passage: The maternal bony pelvis and soft tissues of the birth canal.
  • The Power: Uterine contractions and maternal pushing efforts that expel the fetus.

As labor progresses, coordinated uterine contractions cause the cervix to efface (thin out) and dilate (open), while the presenting part of the fetus descends down the birth canal through cardinal movements such as engagement, descent, flexion, internal rotation, extension, and restitution.

The Four Stages of Labour and Their Management

Labor is divided into four distinct clinical stages, each requiring specific nursing actions:

  1. First Stage: From the onset of true labor contractions to full cervical dilation (10 cm). Managed through vital sign checks, encouraging hydration, and monitoring contraction frequency.
  2. Second Stage: From full cervical dilation to the birth of the baby. Managed by guiding maternal pushing efforts and preparing for safe delivery.
  3. Third Stage: From the birth of the baby until the delivery of the placenta. Managed by active management of the third stage to prevent postpartum hemorrhage.
  4. Fourth Stage: The immediate postpartum period lasting about two hours after placental delivery. Managed by frequent monitoring of uterine tone, bleeding, and maternal vital signs.

Assessment and Monitoring of Mother and Fetus

Continuous and systematic assessment ensures early detection of distress or complications. Maternal monitoring includes tracking blood pressure, pulse, temperature, urine output, and contraction strength and frequency. Fetal well-being is evaluated primarily through intermittent auscultation of the fetal heart rate using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler device, or continuous electronic fetal monitoring where available. A normal fetal baseline heart rate ranges between 110 and 160 beats per minute. Any persistent deceleration or acceleration abnormalities demand immediate clinical intervention.

The Partograph: Principles and Utilization

The partograph is a graphical tool used to record maternal and fetal status alongside labor progress. It provides an early warning system to identify prolonged labor and facilitates prompt decision-making or referral. Key components plotted on the partograph include cervical dilation, descent of the head, contraction patterns, fetal heart rate, and maternal vital signs.

  • Alert Line: A line starting at 4 cm dilation representing the slowest rate of normal cervical dilation.
  • Action Line: A line parallel to and four hours to the right of the alert line; crossing this line indicates that management decisions, such as augmentation or referral, must be made.

Aseptic Techniques and Clean Delivery Practices

Infection prevention is paramount during intra-natal care to protect both mother and newborn from puerperal sepsis and neonatal infections. Healthcare providers must practice strict hand hygiene, wear sterile gloves during vaginal examinations and delivery, and ensure all instruments are properly sterilized. The 'clean delivery' practices emphasize clean hands, a clean surface for delivery, clean perineum, clean cord cutting instruments, and a clean tie for the umbilical cord. Minimizing unnecessary vaginal examinations further reduces the risk of introducing ascending pathogens into the uterine cavity.

Immediate Newborn Care and Resuscitation

Immediately after birth, the newborn requires rapid assessment and routine care. Essential steps include drying the baby thoroughly with a clean towel, assessing breathing and color, establishing skin-to-skin contact, and initiating early breastfeeding. Delayed cord clamping allows for optimal placental transfusion, improving iron stores in the infant.

If a newborn fails to cry or breathe spontaneously, immediate resuscitation must begin following the ABC steps: Airway positioning, Breathing support (ventilation with a bag and mask), and Circulation assessment.

Identification and Management of Intra-Partum Complications

Intra-partum complications can escalate rapidly and threaten the lives of both mother and child. Common emergencies include postpartum hemorrhage, shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse, and fetal distress. Nurses must recognize early warning signs, such as abnormal fetal heart patterns, excessive bleeding before delivery, or failure of progress. Immediate actions involve calling for assistance, positioning the mother correctly (such as the McRoberts maneuver for shoulder dystocia or knee-chest position for cord prolapse), and initiating emergency protocols.

Documentation, Referral, and Post-Delivery Handover

Accurate, timely, and concise documentation is a legal and professional necessity in intra-natal care. Every clinical observation, medication administered, and intervention performed must be recorded immediately. When complications exceed the capacity of the primary care facility, prompt and organized referral is required, accompanied by a clear referral note detailing care given so far. A structured handover between shifts or departments ensures continuity of care and highlights ongoing monitoring needs for the mother and newborn during the postpartum recovery phase.
