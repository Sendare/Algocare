Care of the Newborn

Immediate Care at Birth and Apgar Scoring

When a baby is delivered, the very first priority is establishing a clear airway and encouraging spontaneous breathing. Midwives and nurses immediately dry the infant with warm towels to stimulate crying and prevent rapid heat loss through evaporation. While caring for the newborn, the clinical team performs the Apgar score assessment at precisely one minute and five minutes after birth. This scoring system evaluates five distinct physiological parameters: Appearance, Pulse, Grimace, Activity, and Respiration. Each category is assigned a score from zero to two, yielding a total score out of ten.

  1. Appearance (Color): Evaluates central cyanosis versus generalized pink skin tones.
  2. Pulse (Heart rate): Assessed via auscultation of the chest or palpation of the umbilical cord base.
  3. Grimace (Reflex irritability): Response to tactile stimulation like a gentle foot flick.
  4. Activity (Muscle tone): Degree of flexion and resistance to extension in the extremities.
  5. Respiration (Respiratory effort): Strength of crying and rate of breathing.

A score of seven to ten indicates good transition, whereas lower scores demand varying levels of resuscitation.

Physiological Adaptations of the Newborn

Transitioning from the warm, fluid-filled uterine environment to extrauterine life requires massive, rapid adjustments across multiple organ systems. The respiratory system takes over gas exchange as fluid clears from the alveoli, driven by the first deep breaths and pulmonary blood vessel relaxation. Simultaneously, the cardiovascular system undergoes a dramatic shift with the functional closure of fetal shunts, including the ductus arteriosus, foramen ovale, and ductus venosus. These closures redirect blood flow entirely through the lungs and liver, establishing adult-like circulation patterns.

Metabolically, newborns must maintain stable blood glucose levels using stored glycogen while transitioning to intermittent breast milk feeds. Hepatic function also matures slowly, which often leads to physiological jaundice as immature liver enzymes struggle to process bilirubin produced from breaking down fetal red blood cells.

Routine Newborn Assessment and Physical Examination

A systematic physical examination establishes a baseline of health and rules out congenital anomalies. Nurses inspect the head for molding, caput succedaneum, or cephalhematoma, checking the fontanelles for size and tension. Anterior fontanelles should feel soft and flat, while a sunken fontanelle signals dehydration and a bulging one suggests increased intracranial pressure.

Skin assessments look for birthmarks, milia, and vernix caseosa. The spine is palpated from the neck down to the sacrum to ensure there are no dimples, tufts of hair, or spina bifida occulta. Genitalia are inspected for normal development, and the anus is checked to confirm patency by noting the passage of the first meconium stool.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Hypothermia

Newborns are exceptionally vulnerable to cold stress due to a high surface-area-to-mass ratio, thin skin layers, and an inability to shiver effectively. Instead of shivering, infants generate heat through non-shivering thermogenesis, which relies on metabolizing specialized brown adipose tissue. When a baby loses heat, core body temperature drops, leading to increased oxygen consumption, pulmonary vasoconstriction, and metabolic acidosis.

Nurses prevent hypothermia by keeping the delivery room warm, placing babies skin-to-skin on the mother's chest, using radiant warmers, and delaying the first bath until thermal stability is achieved for at least several hours.

Cord Care and Prevention of Infection

The umbilical cord stump represents an open wound and a direct entry point for systemic bacterial pathogens like tetanus. Standard clinical practice focuses on keeping the cord stump clean, dry, and exposed to air to encourage natural mummification and separation. Nurses instruct mothers to fold diapers below the level of the umbilicus to avoid moisture accumulation from urine.

Antiseptic solutions are used selectively based on local public health guidelines, but dry cord care remains globally recommended in resource-rich settings. Parents must monitor for warning signs of omphalitis, which includes periumbilical redness, foul-smelling purulent discharge, and localized warmth.

Initiation and Promotion of Breastfeeding

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life maximizes oxytocin release in the mother and provides the infant with nutrient-dense colostrum. Colostrum acts as a natural laxative and delivers high concentrations of secretory IgA antibodies to line the gut mucosa. Nurses assist mothers with proper positioning and a deep latch to prevent nipple trauma and ensure effective milk transfer.

Frequent on-demand feeding stimulates prolactin production and establishes a robust milk supply. Healthcare providers monitor infant weight loss, ensuring a drop does not exceed ten percent of birth weight by day four or five.

Immunization and Prophylactic Medications in the Neonatal Period

Prophylactic interventions immediately after birth protect newborns from severe, preventable infections. Vitamin K is administered intramuscularly to every newborn to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn, since their immature liver cannot yet synthesize clotting factors without it. Additionally, prophylactic eye ointment, such as tetracycline or erythromycin, is instilled into both conjunctival sacs to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum caused by gonococcal or chlamydial exposure during vaginal birth.

Routine vaccinations given before hospital discharge typically include the birth dose of the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin vaccine against tuberculosis and the first dose of the hepatitis B vaccine.

Identifying Common Neonatal Complications and Danger Signs

Nurses and parents must remain vigilant for subtle indicators of serious illness in neonates, whose conditions can deteriorate rapidly. Danger signs include persistent fever or hypothermia, lethargy, poor feeding, and respiratory distress evidenced by nasal flaring, chest retractions, and grunting.

Jaundice appearing within the first twenty-four hours of life is always pathological and requires urgent investigation. Recognizing these red flags allows for prompt referral, diagnostic workup, and life-saving intervention before irreversible shock or sepsis occurs.
