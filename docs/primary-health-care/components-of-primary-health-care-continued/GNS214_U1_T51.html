Care of the Newborn

Immediate Care at Birth and Apgar Scoring

The immediate care of the newborn at birth focuses on establishing and maintaining an open airway, supporting spontaneous respirations, and maintaining body temperature. Upon delivery of the infant, the nurse immediately dries the baby with a warm towel while simultaneously assessing the newborn's vigor and need for resuscitation. Suctioning of the airway with a bulb syringe is performed only if secretions obstruct the airway, beginning with the mouth and then the nostrils. The Apgar score is a rapid and standardized method used to evaluate the physical condition of the newborn at exactly one minute and five minutes after birth. It assesses five clinical parameters: Appearance (color), Pulse (heart rate), Grimace (reflex irritability), Activity (muscle tone), and Respiration (respiratory effort). Each parameter is assigned a score of 0, 1, or 2, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 10. A total score of 7 to 10 indicates good condition requiring routine care, 4 to 6 indicates moderate depression requiring stimulation and oxygen support, and 0 to 3 indicates severe distress requiring immediate resuscitation. In Nigerian clinical practice, documenting the 1-minute and 5-minute Apgar scores accurately in the labor ward register and newborn records is critical for identifying birth asphyxia early, facilitating prompt pediatric referral, and guiding continuous nursing monitoring during the transition period.

Physiological Adaptations of the Newborn

Transitioning from intrauterine to extrauterine life requires profound physiological adaptations across multiple body systems. The respiratory system undergoes the most dramatic change as fluid-filled alveoli are cleared and replaced with air, triggered by mechanical chest compression during vaginal birth, thermal changes, and mild asphyxia. Pulmonary vascular resistance drops significantly, leading to increased pulmonary blood flow, functional closure of the foramen ovale, and eventual closure of the ductus arteriosus and ductus venosus. Thermoregulation is initially challenged by a large surface-area-to-mass ratio and thin subcutaneous fat layer, requiring non-shivering thermogenesis via brown adipose tissue metabolism. The gastrointestinal tract begins processing feeds, though meconium—a dark, sticky, odorless substance—must be passed within the first 24 to 48 hours of life. Renal function matures gradually, with initial concentrated urine output expected followed by adequate voiding frequency. Hematologically, fetal hemoglobin transitions to adult hemoglobin, and physiological jaundice may appear due to increased red blood cell turnover and immature hepatic glucuronyl transferase activity. For student nurses, recognizing these normal physiological milestones ensures that benign transition signs are not confused with pathological conditions, allowing for effective parental reassurance and timely identification of abnormal deviations.

Routine Newborn Assessment and Physical Examination

A comprehensive routine newborn assessment and physical examination is conducted shortly after admission to the nursery or postnatal ward to establish a baseline and detect congenital anomalies. The examination follows a systematic head-to-toe approach, beginning with general appearance, posture, cry, and skin color (noting normal findings such as vernix caseosa, lanugo, milia, and physiologic erythema toxicum, as well as abnormal findings like cyanosis or pallor). Head assessment includes measuring head circumference, inspecting fontanelles (anterior should be soft and flat, posterior triangular), and checking for molding, caput succedaneum, or cephalohematoma. The nurse assesses the eyes for symmetry and red reflex, the mouth for an intact palate and sucking reflex, and the neck for range of motion and masses. The chest is auscultated for clear breath sounds and regular heart rate and rhythm, while the abdomen is palpated for soft texture, umbilical cord vessel count (two arteries, one vein), and bowel sounds. Extremities are evaluated for equal movement, symmetrical creases, developmental dysplasia of the hips using the Barlow and Ortolani maneuvers, and digit number. Neurological assessment checks primitive reflexes including Moro, rooting, sucking, grasp, and stepping reflexes. Documenting these findings clearly provides essential health data and reassures parents of their baby's wellbeing.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Hypothermia

Thermoregulation is a critical nursing priority because newborns are highly susceptible to rapid heat loss and subsequent hypothermia, which can lead to metabolic acidosis, hypoglycemia, and respiratory distress. Heat is lost from the newborn's body via four main mechanisms: conduction (direct contact with cold surfaces), convection (cool air currents), evaporation (moisture evaporating from the wet skin), and radiation (transfer of heat to cooler solid objects not in direct contact). To prevent hypothermia, the nurse must immediately dry the infant thoroughly at birth, place the baby skin-to-skin on the mother's chest, cover the infant's head with a cap, and maintain a warm, draft-free room environment. Radiant warmers are used during resuscitation and initial procedures. Bathing should be delayed until the newborn's core body temperature has stabilized within the normal range of 36.5°C to 37.5°C, typically for at least 6 to 24 hours. Regular axillary temperature monitoring is an essential nursing responsibility in the postnatal ward to detect early signs of cold stress, such as lethargy, poor feeding, cool mottled skin, and hypoglycemia, ensuring timely rewarming interventions are implemented.

Cord Care and Prevention of Infection

Proper cord care is essential to prevent omphalitis and systemic neonatal sepsis, which remain significant causes of neonatal morbidity and mortality in developing settings. The umbilical cord stump undergoes ischemic necrosis and natural mummification, typically separating within 7 to 14 days. Standard evidence-based nursing care emphasizes keeping the cord stump clean and dry. In many low-resource and high-risk communities across Nigeria, the application of 4% chlorhexidine digluconate gel to the umbilical stump daily during the first week of life is strongly recommended by public health guidelines to significantly reduce bacterial colonization and severe omphalitis. Nurses must educate mothers and caregivers to avoid applying traditional substances such as saliva, palm oil, toothpaste, herbal mixtures, or dirty bandages to the cord, as these introduce pathogens and tetanus spores. The diaper should be folded below the level of the umbilicus to allow air circulation and prevent moisture retention. The nurse must teach parents how to inspect the cord daily for danger signs, including foul-smelling discharge, pus, persistent bleeding, and spreading redness or warmth of the periumbilical skin, requiring immediate healthcare facility reporting.

Initiation and Promotion of Breastfeeding

Initiation and promotion of breastfeeding is a fundamental cornerstone of neonatal care, providing optimal nutritional, immunological, and psychological benefits. Exclusive breastfeeding should be initiated within the first hour of life, known as the golden hour, capitalizing on the newborn's high alertness and strong sucking reflex. Rooming-in is actively encouraged to facilitate demand feeding whenever the infant shows early hunger cues, such as rooting, sucking motions, and stirring. Nurses play a vital coaching role by assisting mothers with correct positioning and effective attachment (latch). A good latch requires the baby's mouth to cover a large portion of the areola beneath the nipple, with lips flanged outward and the chin pressed firmly against the breast. Effective breastfeeding prevents complications such as nipple soreness, engorgement, and insufficient milk transfer. Colostrum, the thick yellowish initial milk, is exceptionally rich in immunoglobulins, proteins, and white blood cells, serving as the newborn's first immunization. In contexts where maternal infections like HIV require specific management, nurses provide evidence-based counseling on infant feeding options following national protocols while offering compassionate, stigma-free support.

Immunization and Prophylactic Medications in the Neonatal Period

Routine immunizations and prophylactic medications are administered during the immediate neonatal period to protect newborns against infectious diseases and hemorrhagic disorders. Vitamin K (phytomenadione) is administered intramuscularly as a single prophylactic dose (typically 1 mg for term infants) shortly after birth to prevent Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding (VKDB), a potentially fatal hemorrhagic condition due to the newborn's sterile gut and immature liver clotting factors. Ophthalmic prophylaxis, such as 1% tetracycline eye ointment, is instilled into both conjunctival sacs within an hour of birth to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum caused by maternal gonococcal or chlamydial infections acquired during passage through the birth canal. Additionally, the expanded program on immunization (EPI) guidelines in Nigeria require administering the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine against tuberculosis, the oral polio vaccine (OPV zero dose), and the first dose of Hepatitis B vaccine before discharge or within the first two weeks of life. Nurses are responsible for verifying consent, administering correct dosages via appropriate routes, accurately documenting all given medications and vaccines in the child health card, and educating parents on vaccine schedules and mild side effects.

Identifying Common Neonatal Complications and Danger Signs

Early identification of common neonatal complications and clinical danger signs is vital for reducing neonatal mortality and ensuring prompt emergency intervention. Student nurses must educate mothers, families, and healthcare staff to recognize warning signs that indicate serious systemic illness. Key danger signs in the newborn include severe jaundice appearing within the first 24 hours of life or extending to the palms and soles, lethargy or unconsciousness, inability to feed or persistent vomiting, convulsions or abnormal twitching, severe hypothermia or high fever, fast breathing (respiratory rate greater than 60 breaths per minute), severe chest indrawing, grunting, and signs of local or systemic infection such as pus draining from the umbilicus or eyes. Common neonatal complications like birth asphyxia, neonatal sepsis, neonatal jaundice, hypothermia, and neonatal hypoglycemia require immediate stabilization, oxygen therapy, thermoregulation, intravenous access, blood glucose checks, and urgent referral to a higher-level neonatal intensive care unit when necessary. Vigilant nursing assessment, effective communication, and prompt triage save lives during the vulnerable neonatal period.
