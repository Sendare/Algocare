Care of the Newborn

Immediate Care at Birth and Apgar Scoring

The birth of a newborn initiates a critical transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life, requiring immediate, systematic evaluation. The first priority is establishing a patent airway and ensuring adequate respiration, often achieved by gentle bulb suctioning of the mouth and then the nose if excessive secretions are present. The Apgar score provides a rapid assessment of the infant's clinical status at 1 and 5 minutes post-delivery. This tool evaluates five distinct physiological parameters: heart rate, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability, and color. Each parameter is assigned a score of 0, 1, or 2, yielding a total possible score of 10. A score of 7 to 10 indicates good transition, 4 to 6 signifies moderate depression requiring intervention, and 0 to 3 demands immediate resuscitation. In busy Nigerian maternity units, assigning the 1-minute Apgar score while simultaneously drying the infant with a warm towel helps prevent rapid heat loss without delaying essential documentation.

Physiological Adaptations of the Newborn

Transitioning from placental support to independent survival triggers profound cardiovascular and respiratory shifts. The first breath expands fluid-filled alveoli, drastically reducing pulmonary vascular resistance and closing temporary fetal shunts such as the ductus arteriosus, foramen ovale, and ductus venosus. Concurrently, the thermoregulatory and renal systems assume responsibility for homeostasis, though hepatic immaturity frequently leads to transient physiological jaundice during the first week of life. Understanding these baseline shifts helps clinicians differentiate normal transitional phenomena from pathological distress.

Routine Newborn Assessment and Physical Examination

A thorough physical examination confirms normal anatomical development and unmasks congenital abnormalities. Assessment proceeds in a head-to-toe sequence, noting vital signs, head circumference, skin color, and symmetry of movement. Key anatomical features include assessing the anterior and posterior fontanelles, inspecting the palate for clefts, and checking femoral pulses to rule out coarctation of the aorta.

  • Head: Evaluate fontanelle size and tension; check for caput succedaneum or cephalhematoma.
  • Chest: Observe respiratory symmetry, breath sounds, and apical pulse location.
  • Abdomen: Inspect umbilical cord stump for vessels and palpate for masses.
  • Extremities: Count digits, check clavicles for fractures, and perform Ortolani's maneuver for hip stability.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Hypothermia

Newborns are exceptionally vulnerable to cold stress due to a high surface area-to-body mass ratio, thin subcutaneous fat layers, and an inability to shiver effectively. Instead, infants generate heat through the metabolism of specialized brown adipose tissue. Unchecked hypothermia triggers profound metabolic complications, including hypoglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and hypoxia.

Preventing heat loss requires an understanding of four physical mechanisms:

  • Evaporation: Loss of heat when amniotic fluid on the skin converts to vapor; mitigated by thorough drying immediately at birth.
  • Conduction: Direct transfer of heat to cooler objects; prevented by pre-warming radiant warmers, scales, and stethoscopes.
  • Convection: Heat loss via surrounding cool air currents; managed by keeping delivery rooms draft-free.
  • Radiation: Transfer of heat to cooler solid surfaces not in direct contact; reduced by placing cribs away from cold windows and exterior walls.

Cord Care and Prevention of Infection

The umbilical stump represents an open wound and a direct portal of entry for systemic pathogens, making proper hygiene essential to prevent omphalitis. Modern clinical guidelines advocate for clean, dry care, leaving the stump exposed to air or loosely covered by clean clothing rather than applying tight, occlusive bandages. Traditional practices involving the application of saliva, herbal concoctions, palm oil, or toothpaste to the cord must be actively discouraged through health education, as these substances significantly increase the risk of neonatal tetanus and severe bacterial sepsis. Caregivers should be instructed to wash their hands thoroughly before touching the area and to watch for persistent oozing, foul odors, or surrounding skin erythema.

Initiation and Promotion of Breastfeeding

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life maximizes neonatal survival by providing nutrient-dense colostrum, which delivers concentrated immunoglobulins and protective antibodies. Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months provides optimal nutrition, immunological defense, and fosters maternal-infant bonding. Healthcare providers play a pivotal role in assessing infant latch, ensuring proper positioning, and educating mothers on recognizing early feeding cues like rooting and hand-to-mouth movements rather than waiting until vigorous crying begins.

Immunization and Prophylactic Medications in the Neonatal Period

Prophylactic interventions administered immediately after birth shield the newborn from severe, preventable infections. Ophthalmic ointment, such as 1% tetracycline, is instilled into both conjunctival sacs to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum caused by maternal gonococcal or chlamydial exposure. An intramuscular injection of vitamin K is given to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn due to immature clotting factor synthesis. Additionally, the birth dose of the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin vaccine and the oral polio vaccine are standard components of neonatal immunization protocols in high-burden settings.

Identifying Common Neonatal Complications and Danger Signs

Early recognition of neonatal danger signs is vital for preventing mortality and ensuring prompt referral to specialized pediatric care. Nurses must educate mothers and birth attendants to observe for subtle clinical indicators of severe systemic illness. Key warning indicators include refusal of feeds, persistent hypothermia or fever, lethargy, respiratory distress marked by grunting or chest retractions, and seizures. Any infant exhibiting these signs warrants immediate clinical escalation rather than delayed observation.
