Concept of Safe Motherhood

Introduction and Definition of Safe Motherhood

Safe motherhood is a crucial concept in public health and nursing, referring to the strategies, interventions, and care provided to women during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period to ensure optimal health and survival for both mother and child. The primary goal is to reduce maternal morbidity and mortality rates, which remain disproportionately high in developing nations like Nigeria. Safe motherhood is not merely the absence of disease or complications, but a holistic state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being throughout the childbearing cycle. Nurses and midwives play a foundational role in promoting this concept by ensuring that every pregnant woman has access to quality, respectful, and evidence-based maternity care. By addressing the root causes of maternal death—such as hemorrhage, sepsis, unsafe abortion, hypertensive disorders, and obstructed labor—healthcare providers can safeguard the health of families and communities. Understanding this definition helps nursing students recognize that maternal health is a fundamental human right and a core indicator of a functioning health system.

Historical Background and Global Initiatives

The global movement for safe motherhood began gaining formal international recognition in the late 1980s, primarily marked by the landmark Safe Motherhood Initiative launched in 1987 in Nairobi, Kenya. Before this initiative, maternal mortality was largely a hidden tragedy, accepted as an inevitable risk of childbearing, especially in low-income regions. The 1987 conference brought global attention to the millions of women suffering from pregnancy-related complications and death. Following Nairobi, several major global milestones shaped the landscape of maternal health, including the International Conference on Population and Development (ICPD) in Cairo in 1994, the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) in 2000, and subsequently the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by 2015. SDG target 3.1 specifically aims to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio to less than 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030. For Nigerian nursing students, understanding this historical evolution highlights how international commitments translate into national health policies, resource mobilization, and targeted clinical guidelines designed to protect childbearing women worldwide.

Core Pillars and Components of Safe Motherhood

Safe motherhood is built upon a comprehensive framework of core pillars and components designed to address every phase of reproductive life. Traditionally, these pillars include family planning, antenatal care, obstetric care (specifically clean and safe delivery, and emergency obstetric care), and postnatal care. Family planning acts as the first pillar by preventing unwanted pregnancies, spacing births appropriately, and protecting women from the risks associated with high-parity or closely spaced pregnancies. Antenatal care ensures early detection and management of complications such as pre-eclampsia and gestational anemia. Clean and safe delivery guarantees that birth is attended by skilled birth attendants utilizing aseptic techniques to prevent puerperal sepsis and neonatal tetanus. Postnatal care addresses the vulnerable period immediately following birth when a large percentage of maternal and neonatal deaths occur. Additional components incorporated into modern frameworks include safe abortion care where legally permitted, management of reproductive tract infections, and addressing adolescent sexual and reproductive health. Nurses and midwives must integrate all these pillars into daily practice to achieve comprehensive maternal wellness.

The Three Delays Model in Maternal Mortality

The Three Delays Model, developed by Thaddeus and Maine, is an essential conceptual framework used by healthcare professionals to understand why women die from obstetric emergencies. The model categorizes delays in seeking and receiving life-saving care into three distinct phases. The First Delay is the delay in deciding to seek care, which is often influenced by factors such as lack of financial resources, low status of women, lack of awareness of danger signs, and cultural beliefs. The Second Delay is the delay in reaching a healthcare facility, driven by poor road infrastructure, geographical remoteness, lack of transportation, and high transit costs, which are particularly prevalent in rural Nigeria. The Third Delay is the delay in receiving adequate and appropriate care once the facility is reached, caused by factors such as staff shortages, stock-outs of essential drugs and blood products, power outages, and poor staff attitudes. By identifying which delay contributes most to maternal mortality in a given community, nurses, midwives, and healthcare planners can design targeted interventions to bridge these gaps and save lives.

Antenatal, Intrapartum, and Postnatal Care Services

Continuity of care across the antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal periods is the bedrock of safe motherhood practice. Antenatal care (ANC) provides a window of opportunity to monitor fetal development, administer preventive interventions like intermittent preventive treatment for malaria (IPTp) and tetanus toxoid vaccination, and educate mothers on danger signs. Current WHO guidelines recommend a minimum of eight ANC contacts to reduce perinatal mortality and improve the woman's experience of care. Intrapartum care focuses on monitoring labor progress using the partograph, ensuring clean delivery, providing emotional support, and conducting active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. Postnatal care (PNC) involves closely monitoring the mother and newborn during the critical first 42 days after delivery, with early checks within the first 24 hours. PNC visits assess uterine involution, lochia, perineal healing, breastfeeding establishment, and psychological well-being, screening specifically for postpartum depression. Nurses and midwives must document findings meticulously and provide prompt referrals when deviations from normal physiological recovery are detected.

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC)

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC) is a vital strategy designed to manage direct obstetric complications that cannot be predicted or prevented. EmONC facilities are categorized into Basic (BEmONC) and Comprehensive (CEmONC) levels based on the specific signal functions they perform. BEmONC facilities provide seven signal functions: administration of parenteral antibiotics, uterotonic drugs (such as oxytocin), and anticonvulsants (magnesium sulfate), manual removal of the placenta, removal of retained products of conception (using manual vacuum aspiration), assisted vaginal delivery, and basic neonatal resuscitation. CEmONC facilities provide all seven BEmONC functions plus two additional life-saving signal functions: blood transfusion services and emergency cesarean sections. In resource-constrained settings like Nigeria, ensuring the geographical distribution and functional readiness of EmONC facilities is paramount for lowering maternal and neonatal mortality. Nurses and midwives working in maternity units must be fully proficient in performing basic signal functions, recognizing when a patient requires escalation to CEmONC care, and participating in emergency drills.

Role of the Nurse and Midwife in Primary Health Care Settings

Nurses and midwives form the backbone of primary health care (PHC) delivery systems in Nigeria, serving as the first point of formal medical contact for pregnant women in rural and semi-urban communities. Their roles encompass clinical, educational, administrative, and advocacy functions. Clinically, they provide comprehensive antenatal care, conduct normal deliveries, manage immediate postpartum care, provide family planning services, and administer routine immunizations. Educatively, they empower women and families by teaching nutrition during pregnancy, recognizing obstetric danger signs, promoting exclusive breastfeeding, and advocating for birth preparedness and complication readiness. Administratively, PHC nurses manage health facility supplies, maintain cold-chain integrity for vaccines, and compile accurate health management information system (HMIS) reports. Furthermore, they act as advocates by bridging the gap between Western medicine and local communities, combating harmful traditional practices, and fostering respectful maternity care. By building a trusting rapport with patients, nurses and midwives significantly improve healthcare utilization and health-seeking behaviors.

Community Participation and Intersectoral Collaboration

Achieving safe motherhood requires active community participation and robust intersectoral collaboration because maternal health is influenced by multi-sectoral determinants such as poverty, education, gender inequality, and infrastructure. Community participation involves engaging local leaders, traditional rulers, religious groups, and women's associations in planning, executing, and evaluating maternal health programs. When communities take ownership of health initiatives, they help dismantle cultural barriers, encourage facility-based deliveries, and establish community-led emergency transport schemes to tackle the Second Delay. Intersectoral collaboration brings together various government and non-governmental sectors—such as health, education, transport, water resources, and finance—to address the social determinants of health. For example, the Ministry of Works must collaborate with health authorities to construct motorable roads, while the education sector works to improve girl-child education, which directly correlates with better health-seeking behaviors later in life. Nursing students must appreciate that clinical interventions alone are insufficient without a supportive community and multi-sectoral partnerships.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and Challenges in Nigeria

Monitoring, evaluation, and addressing systemic challenges are critical for tracking progress toward safe motherhood goals in Nigeria. Monitoring involves the routine tracking of key indicators—such as maternal mortality ratio, skilled birth attendance rate, and ANC coverage—using data collection tools within the National Health Management Information System (NHMIS). Evaluation assesses the long-term impact and effectiveness of implemented maternal health policies and interventions. Despite various national policies, Nigeria continues to face formidable challenges, including inadequate funding for the health sector, brain drain (migration of skilled nurses and doctors abroad), out-of-pocket health expenditures, poor infrastructure, and insecurity in certain regions that limits access to remote health facilities. Addressing these challenges requires sustained political will, strengthening primary health care governance, implementing universal health coverage schemes (such as the Basic Health Care Provision Fund), and improving data quality for evidence-based decision-making. Nursing students must understand these systemic hurdles to effectively advocate for health system reforms and deliver resilient care.
