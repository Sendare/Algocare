Post-Natal Care

Introduction and Definition of Post-Natal Care

Post-natal care, often called the puerperium, begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six weeks, or forty-two days. This critical period is marked by profound anatomical, physiological, and psychological transitions as the mother's body returns to its pre-pregnant state while adapting to the demands of newborn care. Despite the high concentration of maternal mortality and morbidity during this window, post-natal care has historically suffered from low attendance compared to antenatal services. In Nigerian clinical practice, bridging this gap requires structured outreach and emphasizing that the care addresses both maternal recovery and infant wellbeing simultaneously.

Objectives and Timing of Post-Natal Visits

Structured timing ensures early detection of life-threatening complications and provides continuous support for infant feeding. The World Health Organization recommends a specific schedule for routine contacts:

  • First Contact: Within 24 hours of birth, vital for catching early postpartum hemorrhage and assessing immediate stabilization.
  • Second Contact: Between days 3 and 7, aligning with the physiological peak of breast engorgement and neonatal jaundice screening.
  • Third Contact: Around 6 weeks post-partum for a comprehensive physical, psychological, and contraceptive review.

Primary objectives include monitoring lochia, supporting exclusive breastfeeding, evaluating perineal wound healing, and screening for postpartum mood disorders.

Physiological Changes and Puerperium Involution

Involution involves the rapid shrinking of the uterus to its non-pregnant size, dropping by roughly one to two centimeters per day. By the tenth day, the fundus should no longer be palpable abdominally. Concurrently, the uterine lining sheds through lochia: lochia rubra (red, days 1-3), lochia serosa (pinkish-brown, days 4-10), and lochia alba (whitish-yellow, days 11-21). Failure of the uterus to contract properly leads to subinvolution, frequently caused by retained placental fragments or infection. Cervical changes also occur, with the external os permanently altering from a dimple to a transverse slit.

Comprehensive Post-Natal Maternal Assessment

A systematic head-to-toe examination uncovers hidden morbidities. Assess vital signs meticulously, keeping in mind that a slight temperature elevation within the first 24 hours may relate to dehydration, while fever after 48 hours points toward puerperal sepsis. Inspect the breasts for engorgement, cracked nipples, or early signs of mastitis. Palpate the abdomen to confirm uterine firmness and tenderness. Examine the perineum for episiotomy or tear integrity, checking for hematoma formation, excessive edema, or foul-smelling discharge. Finally, inspect the lower extremities for unilateral swelling, warmth, and calf pain indicative of deep vein thrombosis.

Newborn Assessment and Routine Care

Immediate neonatal evaluation begins with the Apgar score at one and five minutes, followed by a thorough physical examination to rule out congenital anomalies. Maintain thermal neutrality through skin-to-skin contact to prevent hypothermia, which rapidly leads to hypoglycemia and metabolic acidosis. Encourage early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life to harness the immunological benefits of colostrum. Perform routine prophylactic interventions, including administering Vitamin K to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn and applying tetracycline eye ointment to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum.

Identification and Management of Post-Partum Complications

Post-partum hemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal mortality, defined as blood loss exceeding 500 mL following vaginal delivery or 1000 mL following a Caesarean section. The commonest cause is uterine atony. Nurses must master emergency protocols: bimanual uterine compression, administering uterotonics like oxytocin or misoprostol, and monitoring fluid balance via an indwelling catheter. Beyond hemorrhage, screen for hypertensive disorders evolving into eclampsia, thromboembolic events, and mental health challenges such as postpartum depression, distinguishing it from transient postpartum blues.

Post-Natal Nutrition, Hygiene, and Self-Care

Lactation places high metabolic demands on the mother, requiring an increase in daily caloric intake and nutrient-dense foods rich in protein, iron, calcium, and vitamins. In resource-constrained settings, local food demonstrations help mothers optimize nutrition using affordable staples. Personal hygiene practices prevent infection: frequent perineal washing with clean water from front to back, regular pad changes, and avoiding the insertion of traditional concoctions or herbs into the vagina. Adequate rest is vital, though challenging; family involvement should be encouraged to share household burdens.

Family Planning and Post-Partum Sexual Health

Resumption of sexual intercourse and child spacing requires sensitive counseling. Ovulation can occur before the return of menses, making unintended pregnancy a risk during the puerperium. Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM) serves as a highly effective temporary contraceptive method if criteria are met: exclusive breastfeeding, infant under six months, and amenorrhea. For hormonal methods, progestin-only pills, injectables, or implants are preferred during breastfeeding as they do not suppress milk supply, unlike estrogen-containing options. Barrier methods offer dual protection against pregnancy and sexually transmitted infections.

Community Integration and Primary Health Care Services

Effective post-natal care extends beyond hospital walls into the community through primary health care structures, home visits, and community health extension workers. Linking facility-based care with traditional birth attendants and community leaders ensures high-risk mothers do not fall through the cracks. Outreach programs facilitate immunization tracking, nutritional monitoring, and early identification of danger signs. Empowering communities with health education fosters a supportive environment that reduces maternal and neonatal mortality across rural and urban settings alike.
