Oral Rehydration Therapy

Introduction to Diarrhoeal Diseases and Dehydration in Children

Diarrhoeal disease remains a leading cause of childhood morbidity and mortality, particularly in developing regions. Frequent watery stools lead to a rapid depletion of body fluids and essential electrolytes. When fluid output exceeds intake, a child quickly slips into dehydration. The primary danger of acute gastroenteritis is not the infection itself, but the resulting hypovolemic shock caused by electrolyte and water loss.

Dehydration robs the body of circulating volume, leading to reduced tissue perfusion, organ failure, and eventually death if fluid losses are not aggressively replaced. Recognizing the early warning signs allows nurses and caregivers to intervene before emergency resuscitation becomes necessary.

Definition and Principles of Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)

Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) is a simple, highly effective method of replacing fluids and salts lost through diarrhoea by drinking a specially prepared solution. Rather than relying on intravenous lines, ORT harnesses the body's natural intestinal absorption mechanisms to pull water back into the bloodstream.

The core principle of ORT relies on the gut's ability to absorb sodium and water even during severe infection, provided glucose is present. This breakthrough discovery transformed cholera and gastroenteritis management, shifting the mainstay of treatment from costly hospital procedures to affordable home-based care.

The Physiology of Glucose-Coupled Sodium Transport

  • Sodium-Glucose Linked Transporter (SGLT1): A specialized protein in the brush border of the small intestine that simultaneously transports one sodium ion and one glucose molecule across the cell membrane.
  • Solvent Drag: As sodium and glucose are actively pulled into the intestinal cells, water passively follows down an osmotic gradient into the extracellular space and bloodstream.
  • Unaltered Cholera Secretion: Even when cholera toxin causes massive water secretion into the gut lumen, the SGLT1 transporter remains fully functional and unaffected.

Types and Composition of Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS)

Standard Oral Rehydration Salts come in pre-measured sachets designed to be dissolved in a specific volume of clean water. Over the decades, the World Health Organization optimized the formula to reduce stool output and vomiting.

Sodium90 mmol/L75 mmol/L
Glucose111 mmol/L75 mmol/L
Total Osmolality311 mOsm/L245 mOsm/L

Low-osmolality ORS is the current global standard because its lower salt and sugar concentration prevents hypertonic dehydration and reduces the need for unplanned intravenous infusions.

Assessment of Dehydration Severity in Clinical Practice

Clinical assessment determines how much fluid a child needs and where treatment should take place. Nurses evaluate patients using established guidelines divided into three categories: No Dehydration, Some Dehydration, and Severe Dehydration.

  • No Dehydration: Alert, normal eyes, moist mouth, drinks normally, skin pinch goes back instantly.
  • Some Dehydration: Restless or irritable, sunken eyes, dry mouth, drinks eagerly with thirst, skin pinch goes back slowly (less than two seconds).
  • Severe Dehydration: Lethargic or unconscious, very sunken and dry eyes, drinks poorly or unable to drink, skin pinch goes back very slowly (longer than two seconds).

Administration and Preparation of ORS Solutions

Proper preparation is vital. Mixing an entire ORS sachet into the exact recommended volume of water—usually one litre or a locally specified smaller volume—ensures the correct electrolyte balance. Using too little water creates a hypertonic solution that worsens diarrhoea, while too much water dilutes the essential salts.

During the acute phase, give frequent small sips using a cup and spoon rather than a feeding bottle, which is difficult to sterilize. If the child vomits, wait ten minutes, then resume feeding slowly. Breastfed infants should continue nursing on demand throughout the rehydration process.

Complementary Micronutrient Therapy: Zinc Supplementation

World Health Organization guidelines recommend giving daily zinc supplements to children with diarrhoea for 10 to 14 days. Zinc is not just a vitamin; it plays a critical therapeutic role in restoring mucosal integrity, boosting immune function, and reducing the duration and severity of the current episode.

Furthermore, routine zinc supplementation during diarrhoeal illness significantly lowers the risk of subsequent episodes in the following two to three months. Nurses must educate parents to complete the full 14-day course even after the diarrhoea stops.

Primary Health Care and Community Education on ORT

Community education is the cornerstone of preventing childhood mortality from dehydration. Mothers and primary caregivers must be taught to recognize early signs of dehydration and start ORT at the very first loose stool before visiting a clinic.

Nurses working in primary health care settings play a vital role in demonstrating correct ORS mixing techniques during immunization visits and antenatal classes. Dispelling local myths—such as withholding food or fluids to 'rest the bowel'—saves countless young lives through early, proactive community intervention.
