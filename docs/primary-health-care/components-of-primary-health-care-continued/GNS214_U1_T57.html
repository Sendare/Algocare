Oral Rehydration Therapy

Introduction to Diarrhoeal Diseases and Dehydration in Children

Diarrhoeal disease remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among under-five children in Nigeria and other resource-limited settings. The primary danger of acute gastroenteritis is not the stool frequency itself, but the rapid depletion of extracellular fluid and essential electrolytes. When a child loses large amounts of water and sodium through watery stools and vomiting, hypovolemic shock can develop within hours if replacement therapy is delayed. Understanding this fluid shift is critical for preventing catastrophic outcomes in community health centres and pediatric wards.

Definition and Principles of Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)

Oral Rehydration Therapy encompasses both the administration of standard oral rehydration salts and the continued feeding of the child during an episode of acute diarrhea. The core principle relies on using the intact absorptive capacity of the intact small intestine to transport water and electrolytes into the bloodstream, bypassing the need for intravenous access in most cases. This simple yet profound medical intervention saves millions of lives globally by utilizing physiological mechanisms that remain functional even during severe secretory diarrhea.

The Physiology of Glucose-Coupled Sodium Transport

The scientific backbone of oral rehydration is the sodium-glucose cotransporter-1 (SGLT-1) located in the brush border of the proximal small intestine. For every single molecule of glucose actively transported across the luminal membrane, one sodium ion is simultaneously carried across. This cotransport mechanism operates independently of the cyclic AMP pathways that cause pathological chloride and water secretion in cholera and other secretory diarrheas. As glucose and sodium move intracellularly, an osmotic gradient is created, drawing water passively along with them into the intercellular spaces and ultimately into the vascular system.

Types and Composition of Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS)

Over the decades, the formulation of Oral Rehydration Salts has evolved to optimize clinical safety and efficacy. The World Health Organization introduced reduced-osmolarity ORS to lower the risk of hypernatremia and reduce stool output and vomiting episodes compared to older, higher-osmolarity preparations. - **Sodium chloride**: Provides essential sodium and chloride ions to correct extracellular deficits. - **Glucose anhydrous**: Serves as the vital substrate for sodium-glucose cotransport. - **Potassium chloride**: Replaces intracellular potassium lost through frequent loose stools. - **Trisodium citrate dihydrate**: Acts as a buffer to correct metabolic acidosis resulting from base loss.

Assessment of Dehydration Severity in Clinical Practice

Accurate clinical classification directs the appropriate treatment plan, categorized into no dehydration, some dehydration, and severe dehydration. Administration and Preparation of ORS Solutions

Proper reconstitution and administration are vital for ORS effectiveness. A common clinical error in home management is improper dilution—using too little water creates a hypertonic solution that worsens diarrhea, while too much water delays rehydration. The contents of an entire ORS sachet must be mixed into the exact volume of clean, safe water specified on the packet, typically one liter or a measured smaller volume for smaller sachets. Once prepared, the solution should be administered frequently in small sips using a clean cup rather than a feeding bottle, which is difficult to sterilize and increases infection risks.

Complementary Micronutrient Therapy: Zinc Supplementation

Zinc supplementation is a vital adjunct to ORS in the management of acute childhood diarrhea. Clinical trials demonstrate that daily administration of zinc for ten to fourteen days significantly reduces the duration and severity of the current diarrheal episode, while also decreasing the likelihood of subsequent episodes in the following two to three months. Zinc restores mucosal integrity, enhances immune defenses, and boosts the regeneration of brush border enzymes. Standard protocols recommend 20 mg per day for children over six months of age, and 10 mg per day for infants under six months.

Primary Health Care and Community Education on ORT

Empowering mothers and community caregivers with knowledge is the most effective defense against diarrheal mortality. Primary health care nurses play a pivotal role in demonstrating correct ORS preparation during antenatal and immunization clinics. Community education must emphasize that diarrhea requires increased fluid intake rather than fluid restriction, and that foods should never be withheld. By demystifying ORT and ensuring local availability of sachets and zinc tablets, community health initiatives successfully bridge the gap between clinical facilities and rural homes.
