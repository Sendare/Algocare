Route of Transmission

Introduction to Route of Transmission

The route of transmission refers to the specific mechanism by which an infectious agent moves from a reservoir or source to a susceptible host. Understanding this pathway is central to infection control, as it dictates where and how barrier interventions must be deployed. In the chain of infection, the transmission phase follows the portal of exit and precedes the portal of entry. Pathogens rely on various physical environments, vectors, or contact mediums to bridge the spatial gap between hosts. Without a viable transmission route, even the most virulent pathogen cannot sustain an outbreak within a population.

Direct Contact Transmission

Direct contact transmission occurs through physical touch between an infected or colonized individual and a susceptible host. This requires bodily surface-to-surface contact and physical transfer of microorganisms. Common examples include touching, kissing, sexual intercourse, or direct contact with wound drainage and other infectious body fluids. In Nigerian clinical settings, scabies, herpes simplex, and multi-resistant organism spread on hands are frequently encountered manifestations of this mechanism. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective intervention to break this specific link.

Indirect Contact and Fomite Transmission

Indirect contact transmission involves the transfer of an infectious agent through an intermediate object or person.

  • Fomites: Inanimate objects such as stethoscopes, thermometers, door handles, writing pens, and bed linens that harbor pathogens.
  • Unwashed hands: Healthcare workers or visitors acting as mechanical bridges between patients without touching each other directly.
  • Shared equipment: Blood pressure cuffs or glucometers used across multiple patients without intervening disinfection.

Droplet and Airborne Transmission

Respiratory transmission routes are divided based on particle size and behavior in the air.

Vector-Borne and Zoonotic Transmission

Vector-borne transmission involves living organisms—most commonly arthropods like mosquitoes, ticks, fleas, and flies—that transfer infectious agents to humans. Vectors can transmit pathogens mechanically by carrying them on their body parts, or biologically where the pathogen multiplies or develops inside the vector before injection via saliva. Zoonotic transmission encompasses diseases naturally transmitted from vertebrate animals to humans, such as Lassa fever spread by multimammate rats or rabies transmitted through dog bites. In many parts of West Africa, environmental changes and rural-urban migration increase human exposure to these animal and insect reservoirs.

Common Vehicle Transmission: Food, Water, and Blood

Common vehicle transmission occurs when a single contaminated source spreads an infectious agent to multiple hosts simultaneously. Waterborne transmission accounts for massive outbreaks of cholera and typhoid fever when sewage contaminates municipal supplies or shallow wells. Foodborne transmission involves pathogens like Salmonella or hepatitis A ingested via improperly handled, stored, or cooked foodstuffs. Bloodborne transmission occurs when contaminated blood or body fluids enter the vascular system of a susceptible host through unsafe injections, unscreened transfusions, or needle-stick injuries. Protecting blood supplies and ensuring safe water sanitation are foundational public health safeguards against these routes.

Primary Health Care Strategies for Interrupting Transmission

Interrupting transmission routes is the most cost-effective approach to controlling infectious diseases at the primary health care level. Nursing interventions focus on breaking specific chains through rigorous hygiene, environmental sanitation, immunization, and isolation protocols.

  • Hand hygiene: Washing with soap and water or using alcohol-based rubs to stop contact and fomite spread.
  • Standard and transmission-based precautions: Applying barrier nursing principles based on the identified route.
  • Safe waste management: Properly segregating and disposing of sharp needles and infectious clinical waste.
  • Community health education: Promoting clean drinking water, bed net usage, and proper food hygiene.
