National Programme on Immunization

Introduction and Objectives of the National Programme on Immunization (NPI)

The National Programme on Immunization (NPI) is a government-led health initiative designed to protect children and pregnant women from vaccine-preventable diseases. Originally established as the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) by the World Health Organization, Nigeria adopted and scaled up the framework to combat high rates of childhood mortality. The core goal is to interrupt the transmission of infectious agents by achieving high population immunity.

  • Reduction of Child Mortality: Lowering death rates caused by killer diseases like measles, polio, and pertussis.
  • Eradication and Elimination: Targeting specific pathogens, such as the poliovirus and neonatal tetanus, for permanent removal from the population.
  • Equitable Access: Ensuring that children in remote rural villages and urban slums receive the exact same protection.

The Nigerian Routine Immunization Schedule

The routine immunization schedule outlines the exact ages when a child must receive specific vaccines to build lasting immunity. Nurses must follow this timeline closely because delaying doses leaves the infant vulnerable during their most susceptible months.

  1. At Birth: BCG to protect against tuberculosis, and Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV zero) to guard against polio.
  2. 6 Weeks: First doses of pentavalent vaccine (Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b), pneumococcal conjugate vaccine, rotavirus, and OPV.
  3. 10 Weeks: Second doses of pentavalent, pneumococcal, rotavirus, and OPV vaccines.
  4. 14 Weeks: Third doses of pentavalent, pneumococcal, rotavirus, OPV, and the inactivated polio vaccine (IPV).
  5. 9 Months to 15 Months: Measles vaccine, yellow fever vaccine, and meningitis vaccines as guided by local epidemiological risks.

Cold Chain System and Vaccine Management

Vaccines are biological products that lose their potency when exposed to improper temperatures. The cold chain is an uninterrupted system of storage and transport facilities that maintains vaccines within recommended temperature ranges from the manufacturer to the point of administration.

  • Freezer Compartment (-15°C to -25°C): Used strictly for storing oral polio and measles vaccines.
  • Refrigerator Compartment (+2°C to +8°C): Used for pentavalent, tetanus toxoid, fully liquid vaccines, and diluents.
  • Vaccine Vial Monitors (VVM): Small label indicators that change color when heat exposure has exceeded safe limits, telling the nurse whether a vial is still safe to use.

Role of the Nurse in Vaccine Administration and Client Education

Nurses play a central role in translating national immunization policies into community-level protection. Beyond calculating doses and injecting solutions safely using sterile technique, the nurse is an educator. Mothers and caregivers often arrive with anxieties, fears of side effects, or cultural reservations.

The nurse must explain the benefits of each jab, outline expected mild reactions like low-grade fever or local soreness, and provide clear instructions on return dates. Clear documentation in the child health card is non-negotiable, as it creates an accurate lifelong medical record and guides future healthcare providers.

Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI) and Monitoring

While vaccines are exceptionally safe, untoward medical incidents can sometimes occur after administration. An Adverse Event Following Immunization (AEFI) is any untoward medical occurrence which follows immunization and does not necessarily have a causal relationship with the usage of the vaccine.

Nurses must categorize AEFIs correctly: minor reactions like local swelling resolve on their own, whereas severe events like anaphylaxis or seizures require immediate clinical intervention and mandatory reporting to public health authorities for investigation.

Challenges to Coverage and Strategies for Improvement

Despite ongoing efforts, achieving universal immunization coverage in Nigeria faces persistent obstacles. Geographic barriers leave remote rural settlements unreached, while cultural myths and vaccine hesitancy undermine community participation. Insecurity in certain zones also disrupts supply chains and prevents health workers from reaching target populations.

To overcome these hurdles, the health sector employs outreach immunization sessions, engages local traditional and religious leaders as advocates, and implements mobile health clinics to bridge the gap between primary health centers and vulnerable households.
