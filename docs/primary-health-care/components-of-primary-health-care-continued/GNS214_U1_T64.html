National Programme on Immunization

Introduction and Evolution of the National Programme on Immunization in Nigeria

The **National Programme on Immunization (NPI)** represents Nigeria's organized framework for protecting children and women against vaccine-preventable diseases. Originally established as the Expanded Programme on Immunization in 1979 under the auspices of the World Health Organization, the initiative underwent structural transformations, eventually operating as an autonomous agency before being integrated into the **National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA)**. This integration ensures that immunization services are delivered alongside comprehensive primary healthcare at the grassroots level. The primary mandate focuses on reducing morbidity and mortality rates associated with childhood killer diseases such as poliomyelitis, measles, yellow fever, tuberculosis, diphtheria, pertussis, and tetanus through systematic vaccination delivery.

Goals, Objectives, and Core Principles of Routine Immunization

The overarching goal of the NPI is to achieve universal immunization coverage, ensuring that every eligible child completes the mandatory schedule before their first birthday. Strategic objectives focus on interrupting wild poliovirus transmission, eliminating maternal and neonatal tetanus, achieving high coverage for measles-containing vaccines, and introducing new antigens based on epidemiological profiles. The foundational principles guiding operations include equity in access, community ownership, integration with other maternal-child health interventions, and accountability at all tiers of government. Achieving these targets requires consistent political will, sustained funding, and active participation from traditional and religious leaders, particularly in hard-to-reach rural settlements across Nigeria.

The Nigerian Routine Immunization Schedule and Vaccine Types

The routine immunization schedule outlines specific timelines for administering various antigens to protect infants and women of childbearing age. Vaccines are categorized based on their composition into live-attenuated and inactivated preparations, each requiring strict adherence to age limits and spacing intervals. - **Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG)**: Administered at birth or first contact to protect against severe forms of childhood tuberculosis. - **Oral Polio Vaccine (OPV)**: Given at birth (OPV 0) and at 6, 10, and 14 weeks to induce mucosal immunity against poliomyelitis. - **Pentavalent Vaccine**: A combination vaccine targeting diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, hepatitis B, and Haemophilus influenzae type b, administered at 6, 10, and 14 weeks. - **Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine (PCV)**: Protects against invasive pneumococcal infections, given in three doses at 6, 10, and 14 weeks. - **Rotavirus Vaccine**: Oral drops given at 6 and 10 weeks to prevent severe infantile gastroenteritis. - **Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV)**: Administered intramuscularly or intradermally at 14 weeks alongside the final doses of OPV and pentavalent. - **Measles and Yellow Fever Vaccines**: Administered at 9 months and 12-15 months respectively to confer long-lasting viral immunity. - **Tetanus-Diphtheria (Td)**: Given to pregnant women during antenatal visits to prevent neonatal tetanus.

The Cold Chain System and Vaccine Management

The **cold chain** is a temperature-controlled supply chain network designed to ensure vaccines retain their potency from the point of manufacture down to the point of administration. Role of the Nurse in Vaccine Administration and Client Education

Nurses play a pivotal role in the execution of the NPI through direct service delivery, community mobilization, and cold chain maintenance. Clinical responsibilities begin with proper vaccine handling, checking the **Vaccine Vial Monitor (VVM)**, verifying expiration dates, and observing the correct route of administration—such as intradermal for BCG, intramuscular for pentavalent, and oral drops for polio and rotavirus. Beyond technical administration, nurses serve as educators who dispel cultural myths, explain potential mild side effects, and emphasize the importance of completing the full immunization schedule. They maintain accurate records using home-based records (child health cards) and facility registers to track defaulters and implement effective defaulter-tracing mechanisms.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and Adverse Events Following Immunization

Monitoring and evaluation within the immunization program involve systematic tracking of coverage rates, dropout rates between antigens (such as Pentavalent 1 versus Pentavalent 3), and stock levels. High dropout rates signal operational bottlenecks or poor client retention. Concurrently, pharmacovigilance requires active surveillance of **Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI)**. AEFIs are categorized into vaccine product-related reactions, program error-related reactions, coincidence events, injection reactions, and anxiety-related events. Nurses must promptly investigate, manage, and report serious AEFIs to appropriate regulatory bodies like NAFDAC to maintain public trust and ensure vaccine safety.

Operational Challenges and Strategies for Improving Coverage

Despite sustained interventions, immunization coverage in Nigeria faces persistent systemic hurdles. Geographic barriers, hard-to-reach riverine and mountainous terrains, insecure conflict zones, and infrastructural deficits in power supply hamper vaccine distribution. Socio-cultural factors, religious misconceptions, misinformation, and caregiver fatigue also contribute to missed opportunities and low turnout. To bridge these gaps, strategies such as **Outreach Services**, **Accelerated Immunization Activities**, mobile clinics, task-shifting policies, and improved community engagement are deployed. Strengthening routine immunization through integrated primary health care renewal remains the cornerstone for achieving sustainable herd immunity across all geopolitical zones.
