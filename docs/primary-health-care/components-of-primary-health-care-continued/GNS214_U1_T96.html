Airborne Infections

Introduction and Definition of Airborne Infections

Airborne infections are illnesses spread through microscopic droplet nuclei suspended in the air over long distances and time periods. When an infected person coughs, sneezes, laughs, or speaks, they expel respiratory secretions. While larger droplets quickly drop to the ground, tiny particles dry out and remain suspended, allowing them to drift on air currents across rooms or even entire building corridors.

What distinguishes airborne transmission from droplet transmission is both particle size and duration. Droplet nuclei are generally less than 5 micrometers in diameter, small enough to bypass the upper respiratory tract defenses and penetrate deep into the pulmonary alveoli. In resource-constrained clinical settings across Nigeria, overcrowded waiting areas and poorly ventilated wards significantly increase the risk of nosocomial airborne spread, making a solid understanding of these pathogens essential for every healthcare worker.

Pathophysiology and Modes of Transmission

The journey of an airborne pathogen begins in the respiratory tract of a primary host. Once inhaled by a susceptible secondary host, these microscopic agents bypass mechanical filters like nasal hairs and mucus, settling deep within the lung parenchyma. Here, local immune cells attempt to engulf the invader, triggering an inflammatory cascade that often leads to characteristic tissue damage, such as the formation of tubercles in tuberculosis or cellular necrosis in measles.

  • Inhalation: The primary entry route, where suspended droplet nuclei are drawn directly into the lower respiratory tract during normal breathing.
  • Aerosolization procedures: Medical interventions like endotracheal intubation, bronchoscopy, or nebulizer therapy that mechanically generate high concentrations of infectious aerosols.
  • Host susceptibility: Factors such as malnutrition, HIV co-infection, and chronic stress that lower cellular immunity and determine whether exposure progresses to active disease.

Common Airborne Diseases in Clinical Practice

Several major pathogens drive the burden of airborne illness globally and locally. Recognizing their distinct presentations helps guide prompt isolation and nursing interventions.

  • Tuberculosis (TB): Caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, characterized by a chronic cough lasting over two weeks, night sweats, and unexplained weight loss.
  • Measles (Rubeola): A highly contagious viral infection marked by a generalized maculopapular rash, high fever, and pathognomonic Koplik spots on the buccal mucosa.
  • Chickenpox (Varicella): Caused by the varicella-zoster virus, presenting with an itchy vesicular rash that appears in successive crops across the body.
  • Influenza: While often spread by droplets, certain strains and aerosol-generating contexts contribute to widespread airborne influenza epidemics.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

Assessing a patient with a suspected airborne infection requires balancing clinical thoroughness with strict personal protection. The nurse begins by evaluating respiratory status, noting tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, cyanosis, and asymmetrical chest expansion. Vital signs often reveal a low-grade or spiking fever, tachycardia, and reduced oxygen saturation levels measured via pulse oximetry.

Beyond the respiratory system, a comprehensive assessment covers nutritional status and fatigue levels, since chronic airborne conditions like tuberculosis exact a heavy metabolic toll. Nurses must document the exact duration of symptoms, past exposure history, and any previous treatment regimens. In busy Nigerian outpatient clinics, performing this rapid triage while ensuring the patient wears a surgical mask immediately prevents ongoing transmission in the waiting hall.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Confirming an airborne infection relies on a combination of microbiological assays and imaging studies. For tuberculosis, sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli and rapid molecular tests like GeneXpert are standard frontline diagnostics. These tests identify both the presence of the bacillus and potential rifampicin resistance.

Chest radiography remains a cornerstone tool, often revealing upper lobe infiltrates, cavitary lesions, or hilar lymphadenopathy in pulmonary TB. For viral infections like measles or chickenpox, diagnosis is predominantly clinical, supported by serological antibody testing when necessary. Nurses play a pivotal role in ensuring sputum samples are collected correctly—instructing the patient on deep coughing from the chest rather than simple saliva spitting—to avoid false-negative laboratory results.

Nursing Care and Clinical Management

Managing hospitalized patients with airborne infections centers on rigorous isolation protocols and supportive symptom relief. Patients must be placed in an Airborne Infection Isolation Room (AIIR) featuring negative pressure ventilation, which ensures air flows inward when doors open, preventing pathogens from escaping into hospital corridors. When an AIIR is unavailable, cohorting patients with the same confirmed infection is an accepted alternative.

Nurses monitor respiratory function, administer prescribed antimicrobial or antiviral therapies, and manage antipyretics for fever control. Nutritional support is equally critical, as many chronic pulmonary infections lead to severe wasting. Nurses collaborate with dietitians to provide high-protein, high-calorie meals that bolster the patient's immune recovery and support tissue healing.

Infection Prevention and Control in Primary Healthcare

Primary healthcare clinics in Nigeria often face structural challenges such as limited electricity, lack of negative pressure rooms, and high patient volumes. In these environments, administrative and environmental controls form the first line of defense. Administrative controls include prompt triage, immediate separation of coughing clients, and enforcing cough etiquette. Environmental controls rely on maximizing natural ventilation by keeping windows and doors wide open to ensure high air exchange rates.

Personal protective equipment (PPE) remains vital. While surgical masks protect patients from provider droplets, healthcare workers caring for airborne cases must wear properly fitted N95 or particulate respirators that form a tight seal over the nose and mouth. Regular fit-testing and correct disposal techniques prevent occupational acquisition of infections like multidrug-resistant tuberculosis.

Public Health Education and Community Engagement

Stopping the spread of airborne infections extends far beyond hospital walls; it requires active community participation and public health advocacy. Nurses serve as educators, teaching families about the importance of cough etiquette—such as covering the mouth and nose with a flexed elbow or tissue rather than bare hands. Emphasizing adherence to long treatment regimens, particularly for tuberculosis, prevents relapse and the development of drug-resistant strains.

Community outreach involves debunking local stigmas associated with chronic coughs and infectious diseases, which often discourage individuals from seeking early care. By partnering with local community leaders, religious groups, and market associations in Nigeria, nurses can promote early screening, vaccination campaigns for vaccine-preventable airborne illnesses like measles, and the creation of well-ventilated living spaces.
