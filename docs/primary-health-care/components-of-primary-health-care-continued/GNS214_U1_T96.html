Airborne Infections

Understanding Airborne Infections and Transmission

Airborne infections are illnesses caused by pathogens that spread through the air via tiny droplets or dust particles. When an infected person coughs, sneezes, talks, or even breathes, they release microscopic droplets into the environment. Unlike larger droplets that quickly fall to the ground, these smaller particles—often called droplet nuclei—can remain suspended in the air for hours and travel long distances on air currents.

  • Tuberculosis (TB): A chronic bacterial infection primarily affecting the lungs, spread when infectious particles are inhaled.
  • Measles: A highly contagious viral infection that stays in the air long after the infected person has left the room.
  • Chickenpox: A viral illness causing an itchy blister-like rash, spread through both airborne droplets and direct contact with blister fluid.

Understanding this mechanism is vital because standard surgical masks are often insufficient to block these tiny particles, necessitating specialized respiratory protection in clinical settings.

Common Airborne Pathogens Prevalent in Nigeria

Healthcare professionals practicing in Nigeria frequently encounter specific airborne diseases that pose significant public health challenges. Tuberculosis remains a leading infectious killer nationwide, exacerbated by crowded living conditions and co-infection with HIV. Measles outbreaks still occur periodically, especially in communities with low routine immunization coverage among children. Additionally, emerging respiratory viruses continue to threaten vulnerable populations, requiring constant vigilance from nursing staff in both rural clinics and large tertiary hospitals.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

Assessing a patient with a suspected airborne infection requires careful observation of respiratory symptoms and systemic signs. Common indicators include a persistent cough lasting more than two weeks, unexplained weight loss, night sweats, low-grade afternoon fevers, and hemoptysis in advanced cases of pulmonary tuberculosis. Nurses must perform a thorough respiratory assessment, noting chest pain, shortness of breath, and abnormal breath sounds like crackles or wheezes. A detailed travel and social history is equally critical to identify potential exposure sources.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Confirming an airborne infection relies on specific laboratory tests and imaging studies. For tuberculosis, sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli and rapid molecular tests like GeneXpert are standard. Chest radiography is essential to visualize lung infiltrates, cavitations, or lymphadenopathy. Nurses play a crucial role in educating patients on how to produce a proper deep-lung sputum sample rather than mere saliva, ensuring accurate diagnostic results.

Airborne Precautions and Nursing Management

Managing patients with confirmed or suspected airborne infections requires strict adherence to transmission-based precautions.

  • Negative Pressure Rooms: Place the patient in a single room with negative air pressure to prevent infectious air from escaping into hallways.
  • Respiratory Protection: Healthcare workers must wear fit-tested N95 or higher respirators before entering the room.
  • Patient Transport: Limit patient movement outside the room; if transport is necessary, the patient must wear a surgical mask to contain secretions.
  • Medication Adherence: Support patients through long treatment regimens, especially for drug-resistant strains of tuberculosis, through directly observed therapy.

Infection Prevention and Control in Primary Healthcare

In many Nigerian primary healthcare settings, sophisticated negative pressure isolation rooms are unavailable. Nurses must therefore rely on administrative and environmental controls to minimize transmission. This includes ensuring optimal natural ventilation by keeping windows and doors wide open, implementing triage systems that quickly identify and separate coughing patients upon arrival, and promoting cough etiquette. Environmental hygiene, sunlight exposure of patient bedding, and staff training remain cost-effective pillars of safety.

Public Health Education and Community Engagement

Community engagement is essential to overcome the social stigma often associated with airborne diseases like tuberculosis. Nurses act as educators, dispelling myths that attribute airborne illnesses to witchcraft or curses. Public health campaigns focus on teaching proper cough etiquette—such as covering the mouth and nose with a tissue or the inner elbow—and emphasizing the importance of completing full medication courses. Empowering community leaders helps foster supportive environments where patients seek early diagnosis without fear.
