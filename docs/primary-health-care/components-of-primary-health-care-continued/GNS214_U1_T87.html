Communicable Disease

Introduction to Communicable Diseases

Communicable diseases are illnesses caused by specific infectious agents or their toxic products that can be transmitted from an infected person, animal, or reservoir to a susceptible host. This transmission can occur directly or indirectly through an intermediate plant or animal host, vector, or inanimate environment. In clinical practice across Nigeria, managing these conditions forms a substantial portion of daily workload, ranging from acute viral outbreaks like Lassa fever to endemic parasitic infections such as malaria. Understanding how these pathogens establish themselves and spread allows healthcare providers to break transmission cycles and protect vulnerable populations.

Key Epidemiological Terms and Concepts

Epidemiology relies on specific vocabulary to describe the behavior of diseases within populations. Grasping these terms ensures clear communication among healthcare teams during health emergencies. - **Endemic**: The constant presence or usual prevalence of a disease or infectious agent within a given geographic area or population group. - **Epidemic**: An unexpected surge in the number of cases of a disease well above what is normally expected in that population. - **Pandemic**: An epidemic that has spread over several countries or continents, affecting a large number of people. - **Incidence**: The number of new cases of a specific disease diagnosed during a specified time interval. - **Prevalence**: The total number of established cases, both old and new, in a given population at a specific point in time.

The Chain of Infection

Infection transmission follows a continuous, sequential link of six distinct components. If any single link is broken, the infectious process is halted. 1. **Infectious Agent**: The pathogenic microorganism, such as a bacterium, virus, fungus, or parasite. 2. **Reservoir**: The natural habitat where the pathogen lives, grows, and multiplies, which can include humans, animals, soil, or water. 3. **Portal of Exit**: The path through which the pathogen leaves the source reservoir, such as respiratory secretions, blood, or gastrointestinal tracts. 4. **Mode of Transmission**: The method by which the microorganism travels from the reservoir to the susceptible host. 5. **Portal of Entry**: The site through which the infectious agent enters the new host, such as broken skin, mucous membranes, or the respiratory tract. 6. **Susceptible Host**: An individual who lacks sufficient resistance or immunity to ward off the invading pathogen.

Modes of Transmission

Pathogens utilize various routes to navigate from an infected source to a new host. Recognizing these pathways dictates the specific barrier nursing precautions required in clinical wards. - **Direct Contact**: Physical transfer of microorganisms between an infected person and a susceptible host, such as touching, kissing, or sexual intercourse. - **Indirect Contact**: Transfer of an infectious agent through an intermediate object or inanimate surface, known as a fomite, like contaminated instruments, door handles, or bedding. - **Droplet Transmission**: Spread via large respiratory particles generated by coughing, sneezing, or talking, which travel short distances, typically under one meter, before depositing on mucosal surfaces. - **Airborne Transmission**: Dispersal of droplet nuclei or dust particles containing infectious agents that remain suspended in the air for prolonged periods and travel long distances. - **Vector-borne Transmission**: Transfer of pathogens via living intermediate carriers, most notably mosquitoes, ticks, or fleas.

Common Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

Healthcare professionals practicing in Nigeria encounter a distinct epidemiological profile characterized by both endemic tropical infections and emerging viral outbreaks. Malaria, caused by Plasmodium species and transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes, remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly among children under five and pregnant women. Lassa fever, an acute viral hemorrhagic fever zoonotically linked to the multimammate rat, presents seasonal outbreaks that demand strict barrier nursing. Cholera outbreaks occur periodically, driven by compromised sanitation and unsafe drinking water sources, causing rapid dehydration through profuse watery diarrhea. Additionally, vaccine-preventable diseases like measles and routine childhood infections require persistent immunization campaigns to maintain community-level protection.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

Accurate clinical assessment forms the foundation of effective communicable disease management. Nurses must perform thorough health histories, noting recent travel, occupational exposures, and contact with sick individuals. Physical examination focuses on identifying hallmark signs such as rashes, lymphadenopathy, jaundice, and respiratory distress. Temperature charting is vital to observe fever patterns, which often offer diagnostic clues. Laboratory investigations, including rapid diagnostic tests, microscopy for blood smears, and microbiological cultures, confirm the causative agent. Prompt identification prevents diagnostic delay, mitigates complications, and ensures timely notification of public health authorities.

Prevention and Control Strategies

Controlling communicable diseases requires a multi-layered approach combining community interventions and clinical protocols. Immunization programs build active immunity, protecting both vaccinated individuals and creating herd immunity for the wider population. Environmental sanitation, including proper waste disposal, vector control through insecticide-treated nets, and access to potable water, eliminates environmental reservoirs. In clinical environments, standard and transmission-based precautions protect staff and patients alike. Case management, involving prompt isolation, effective pharmacotherapy, and rigorous contact tracing, stops ongoing community spread during outbreaks.

The Role of the Nurse in Primary Health Care

Nurses occupy a central position in primary health care settings, acting as frontline educators, care coordinators, and advocates. In Nigerian primary health clinics, nurses lead community outreach programs, deliver routine immunizations, and provide health education on hygiene and nutrition. They play a pivotal role in surveillance by identifying unusual disease patterns and reporting them promptly to health authorities. By fostering trusting relationships within communities, nurses dispel harmful health myths, encourage early health-seeking behavior, and empower individuals to take charge of their personal and collective wellbeing.
