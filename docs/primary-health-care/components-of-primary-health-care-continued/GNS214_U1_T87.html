Communicable Disease

Introduction to Communicable Diseases

A communicable disease is an illness caused by a specific infectious agent or its toxic products that can be transmitted from one person, animal, or reservoir to a susceptible host. These conditions remain a major public health concern, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where they strain healthcare systems and limit socio-economic development. Understanding how these diseases start, spread, and stop is a core duty for every nurse working in community or hospital settings.

Not all infectious diseases are communicable. For example, tetanus is infectious because it is caused by bacteria entering a wound, but it cannot spread from person to person. True communicable diseases require an unbroken chain of transmission to survive within a population. Recognizing this distinction helps nurses prioritize isolation precautions and community education effectively.

The Chain of Infection

The spread of any communicable disease follows a sequential link known as the chain of infection. If any single link in this chain is broken, the spread of the disease stops.

  1. Infectious Agent: Pathogens like bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites.
  2. Reservoir: The natural habitat where the pathogen lives and multiplies, such as humans, animals, water, or soil.
  3. Portal of Exit: The path by which the pathogen leaves the reservoir, including respiratory secretions, blood, or gastrointestinal tract.
  4. Mode of Transmission: How the pathogen travels to a new host, whether through direct contact, air, vectors, or vehicles.
  5. Portal of Entry: The route the pathogen uses to enter a new host, such as broken skin, mucous membranes, or the respiratory tract.
  6. Susceptible Host: A person who lacks immunity or resistance to ward off the invading pathogen.

Modes of Transmission

Pathogens use various routes to move from a source to a new victim. Knowing these pathways guides nurses in selecting proper personal protective equipment and barrier nursing techniques.

  • Direct Transmission: Physical contact between an infected person and a susceptible host, such as touching, kissing, or sexual intercourse.
  • Indirect Transmission: Transfer of the infectious agent through a secondary object or medium, such as contaminated medical instruments, door handles, bed linens, or shared cups (fomites).
  • Airborne Transmission: Inhalation of droplet nuclei or dust particles that remain suspended in the air for long periods, common in tuberculosis and measles.
  • Vector-Borne Transmission: Transfer through living creatures like mosquitoes, ticks, or fleas, which carry the pathogen from one host to another, as seen with malaria.
  • Vehicle-Borne Transmission: Spread through contaminated food, water, or biological products.

Common Communicable Diseases in Nigeria

Healthcare practice in Nigeria involves managing several endemic and epidemic-prone communicable diseases.

  • Malaria: Caused by *Plasmodium* parasites and transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes, leading to high fever, chills, and severe anemia if untreated.
  • Cholera: An acute diarrheal infection caused by *Vibrio cholerae* bacteria, typically spread through contaminated water in areas with poor sanitation, causing rapid dehydration.
  • Lassa Fever: A viral hemorrhagic fever transmitted by infected multimammate rats through food or contact with household items contaminated with rat urine or feces.
  • Tuberculosis (TB): A chronic airborne bacterial infection affecting the lungs, requiring long-term combination drug therapy to cure.

Public health campaigns in local communities focus heavily on environmental sanitation, insecticide-treated net usage, and safe drinking water to curb these burdens.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

Assessing a patient with a suspected communicable disease requires a careful health history alongside physical examination. Nurses must evaluate travel history, occupation, vaccination status, and recent contact with sick individuals. Common clinical indicators include fever, rashes, lymphadenopathy, respiratory distress, and gastrointestinal disturbances.

Laboratory investigations confirm the clinical diagnosis. These tests include blood smears for malaria parasites, sputum microscopy for acid-fast bacilli in tuberculosis, stool cultures, and specific serological rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs). Early and accurate diagnosis ensures timely treatment, limits complications, and prompts immediate epidemiological reporting to health authorities when dealing with quarantinable diseases.

Prevention and Control Strategies

Controlling the spread of communicable diseases relies on a combination of primary and secondary preventive measures tailored to the community and healthcare facility.

  • Immunization: Vaccines build active immunity against vaccine-preventable diseases like measles, polio, and yellow fever.
  • Environmental Sanitation: Ensuring clean water supply, proper sewage disposal, and vector control programs.
  • Isolation and Quarantine: Separating infected individuals from healthy people to stop transmission during outbreaks.
  • Chemoprophylaxis: Administering preventive medications to high-risk groups, such as anti-malarials for pregnant women.

Nurses play a frontline role in delivering these interventions, educating families, and tracking compliance with public health guidelines.

The Role of the Nurse in Primary Health Care

Nurses are the backbone of primary health care delivery, acting as educators, caregivers, advocates, and epidemiological monitors. In the community, community health nurses organize health-talk sessions, administer routine vaccinations, and conduct contact tracing during outbreaks.

In acute care settings, nurses enforce strict infection prevention and control (IPC) protocols, including proper hand hygiene, safe biomedical waste disposal, and the correct use of personal protective equipment. By maintaining accurate records and reporting notifiable diseases promptly to public health departments, nurses help prevent localized infections from escalating into national epidemics.
