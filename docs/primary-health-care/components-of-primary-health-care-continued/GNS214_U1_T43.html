Maternal and Child Health Services

Introduction and Objectives of Maternal and Child Health Services

Maternal and Child Health (MCH) services form the bedrock of primary healthcare delivery, focusing on the most vulnerable groups in any population: mothers, infants, and children. The primary goal is to reduce preventable morbidity and mortality by providing comprehensive healthcare from conception through adolescence.

  • Preventive Care: Spotting potential health issues before they become life-threatening emergencies.
  • Promotive Care: Educating families on nutrition, hygiene, and safe parenting practices.
  • Curative Care: Treating common childhood illnesses and managing pregnancy-related complications promptly.
  • Rehabilitative Care: Supporting children and mothers to recover fully from chronic illness or severe malnutrition.

Adequate MCH programming looks beyond just physical survival. It addresses social determinants of health, empowers women economically and educationally, and builds resilient community structures capable of sustaining health interventions long after external programs end.

Antenatal Care: Monitoring Pregnancy and Preventing Complications

Antenatal Care (ANC) gives healthcare providers a vital window to monitor the physiological adaptation of pregnancy and protect both maternal and fetal well-being. Regular visits allow nurses to screen for high-risk conditions such as gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, and severe anemia.

Routine ANC interventions include:

  • Administering intermittent preventive treatment for malaria during pregnancy (IPTp-SP).
  • Providing routine iron and folic acid supplementation to prevent neural tube defects and maternal anemia.
  • Checking maternal blood pressure and urine protein at every visit to catch pre-eclampsia early.
  • Providing tetanus toxoid immunization to prevent neonatal tetanus.
  • Offering voluntary counseling and testing for HIV to prevent mother-to-child transmission.

A common misconception is that ANC is only necessary when a pregnant woman feels sick. Nurses must emphasize that regular visits are mandatory precisely when a woman feels well, as conditions like rising blood pressure often present with no early warning signs.

Intrapartum and Postnatal Care for Mother and Newborn

The transition from pregnancy to birth and the immediate postpartum period carries the highest statistical risk for maternal and neonatal mortality. Intrapartum care requires skilled birth attendance to manage normal labor and rapidly identify complications such as obstructed labor, uterine rupture, and postpartum hemorrhage.

Immediately following delivery, the postnatal period begins. The first twenty-four hours are critical for monitoring lochia, uterine involution, and maternal vital signs. For the newborn, immediate care involves drying, skin-to-skin contact, initiating breastfeeding within the first hour, and administering prophylactic eye ointment and vitamin K.

Postnatal visits continue at specific intervals (day 3, day 7, week 6) to evaluate maternal psychological adjustment, support exclusive breastfeeding, and assess infant growth and healing of the umbilical cord stump.

Child Health Services: Growth Monitoring and Developmental Milestones

Child health services use regular tracking tools to ensure that infants and toddlers achieve expected physical and mental capacities. Growth monitoring involves regular weighing and length or height measurements plotted on standard growth charts, such as those provided by the World Health Organization.

Monitoring helps nurses detect faltering growth long before severe malnutrition becomes visible. Alongside physical growth, nurses assess developmental milestones across several domains:

  • Gross Motor: Rolling over, sitting unsupported, crawling, and walking.
  • Fine Motor: Grasping objects, transferring toys from hand to hand, and self-feeding.
  • Language and Social: Cooing, babbling, responding to names, and smiling in social interactions.

Delayed milestones serve as an early red flag for underlying neurological, nutritional, or systemic medical conditions, allowing for early referral and intervention.

Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI) in Nigeria

The Expanded Programme on Immunization is a cornerstone preventive strategy designed to protect infants and young children from vaccine-preventable diseases that historically caused high rates of disability and death. In Nigeria, the routine immunization schedule covers tuberculosis, poliomyelitis, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b, measles, yellow fever, and meningitis.

Nurses play a critical role in managing the cold chain—the temperature-controlled supply chain required to keep vaccines potent from central storage down to remote primary health centers. A break in the cold chain renders vaccines useless, even if they are administered on schedule.

Community mobilization is equally important. Nurses must address vaccine hesitancy, clear up myths, and educate caregivers about mild expected side effects like low-grade fever or local soreness at the injection site.

Integrated Management of Childhood Illnesses (IMCI)

The Integrated Management of Childhood Illnesses strategy, developed by the World Health Organization and UNICEF, shifts healthcare workers away from treating single isolated symptoms toward a holistic assessment of the sick child. Children brought to primary clinics often present with overlapping symptoms such as fever, cough, and diarrhea.

IMCI rests on three main pillars:

  1. Improving Case Management Skills: Enhancing the clinical skills of healthcare workers using standardized assessment guidelines.
  2. Strengthening Health Systems: Ensuring essential drugs, equipment, and supportive supervision are available.
  3. Improving Family and Community Practices: Educating caregivers on home management, nutrition, and recognizing when to seek urgent care.

Using the IMCI algorithm, nurses systematically check for general danger signs (inability to drink or breastfeed, vomiting everything, convulsions, lethargy or unconsciousness) and classify illnesses using a color-coded system that guides immediate referral or treatment.

Nutrition and Family Planning Integration within MCH

Nutrition and family planning are deeply intertwined with maternal and child health outcomes. Malnutrition in pregnancy leads to low birth weight, which compromises infant survival and long-term cognitive development. Integrating nutrition education—focusing on exclusive breastfeeding for six months, timely introduction of nutrient-dense complementary foods, and micronutrient supplementation—breaks the intergenerational cycle of malnutrition.

Similarly, family planning allows women to space their pregnancies adequately. Closely spaced pregnancies (less than twenty-four months apart) deplete maternal nutritional reserves and increase the risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal mortality. Nurses play a pivotal role in offering counseling on various contraceptive methods, debunking myths, and helping couples choose methods that align with their reproductive life plans.

Challenges and Community Strategies in Primary Health Care Delivery

Delivering effective MCH services at the primary health care level in resource-constrained settings presents persistent hurdles. Common barriers include geographic isolation, poor transport infrastructure, stock-outs of essential medicines, cultural practices that discourage facility-based deliveries, and shortages of skilled healthcare personnel.

To bridge these gaps, successful programs rely heavily on community-based strategies:

  • Community Health Workers (CHWs): Training local volunteers to conduct household visits, promote hygiene, identify danger signs, and refer clients to clinics.
  • Traditional Birth Attendant (TBA) Engagement: Partnering with TBAs to educate them on recognizing complications and encouraging prompt referral rather than attempting high-risk home deliveries.
  • Community Dialogue: Engaging religious and community leaders to shift cultural norms around healthcare utilization and immunization.

Nurses must act as facilitators, building trust with local communities to ensure that healthcare interventions are culturally acceptable and sustainable.
