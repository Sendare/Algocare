Prevention of Maternal Newborn and Infant Morbidity and Mortality

Introduction and Core Statistical Indicators in Nigeria

Maternal, newborn, and infant morbidity and mortality remain critical public health challenges, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. Nigeria carries a disproportionately high burden of these preventable tragedies. Examining the baseline metrics helps healthcare providers gauge the severity of the crisis and measure the impact of interventions.

  • Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR): The number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days of termination.
  • Neonatal Mortality Rate (NMR): The probability of dying within the first 28 days of life per 1,000 live births.
  • Infant Mortality Rate (IMR): The probability of a child dying before reaching their first birthday per 1,000 live births.
  • Under-Five Mortality Rate: The probability of dying between birth and exactly five years of age per 1,000 live births.

Major Direct and Indirect Causes of Death

Preventing maternal and child loss requires a firm grasp of the underlying pathologies. Causes are typically categorized as direct obstetric complications or indirect medical conditions exacerbated by pregnancy.

  • Postpartum Haemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding following delivery, representing the leading direct cause of maternal death locally.
  • Eclampsia and Severe Preeclampsia: Pregnancy-induced hypertension leading to seizures, stroke, or multi-organ failure.
  • Sepsis: Severe systemic infection originating from the genital tract post-delivery or unsafe abortion procedures.
  • Birth Asphyxia: Inability of the newborn to initiate and sustain adequate breathing at birth, leading to hypoxic-ischemic injury.
  • Prematurity and Low Birth Weight: Babies born before 37 completed weeks of gestation struggle with thermal regulation and respiratory immaturity.

The Three Delays Model

Clinical interventions alone cannot solve mortality if systemic barriers delay access to care. The three delays model explains why women and infants fail to reach lifesaving health facilities in time.

  1. First Delay (Decision to Seek Care): Rooted in low socioeconomic status, lack of education, cultural preferences, or failure to recognize danger signs.
  2. Second Delay (Identifying and Reaching Care): Caused by poor road infrastructure, long distances to health centers, and a lack of reliable transportation or emergency funds.
  3. Third Delay (Receiving Adequate Treatment): Occurs inside health facilities due to severe shortages of trained personnel, stockouts of essential drugs, erratic electricity, or poor staff attitude.

Antenatal and Intrapartum Care Interventions

Quality antenatal care (ANC) and skilled attendance during labor form the bedrock of prevention. Current global guidelines recommend at least eight ANC contacts to ensure adequate monitoring, risk identification, and prompt treatment.

During pregnancy, nurses administer tetanus toxoid vaccination, screen for asymptomatic bacteriuria, distribute insecticide-treated nets for malaria prevention, and provide intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp). Intrapartum care relies heavily on the active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) using uterotonics like oxytocin to prevent PPH. Continuous monitoring using the partograph helps detect prolonged labor early before uterine rupture or fetal distress occurs.

Essential Newborn Care and Immunization

The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life requires systematic support to prevent hypothermia, infection, and respiratory failure. Essential newborn care includes immediate and thorough drying, skin-to-skin contact, delayed cord clamping, and the initiation of exclusive breastfeeding within the first hour of life.

Prophylactic interventions such as applying chlorhexidine gel to the umbilical cord stump and administering tetracycline eye ointment protect against omphalitis and ophthalmia neonatorum. Immunization programs safeguard infants against vaccine-preventable diseases such as tuberculosis, poliomyelitis, measles, and diphtheria, establishing durable population immunity.

Nutrition, Health Education, and Community Strategies

Community engagement bridges the gap between formal health institutions and vulnerable populations. Empowering mothers with knowledge regarding nutrition, danger signs, and birth preparedness alters health-seeking behaviors.

Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months provides optimal nutrition and passive antibodies. Nutritional supplementation with iron, folate, and calcium mitigates anemia and hypertensive disorders. Community-based health workers play a vital role in identifying danger signs early, facilitating emergency referrals, and ensuring that grassroots populations access care before complications become irreversible.
