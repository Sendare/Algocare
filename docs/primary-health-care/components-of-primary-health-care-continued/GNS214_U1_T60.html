Prevention of Maternal Newborn and Infant Morbidity and Mortality

Introduction to Maternal, Newborn, and Infant Morbidity and Mortality

Maternal and child health remains a core foundation of public health nursing. Understanding the scope of morbidity (illness and complications) and mortality (death) helps nurses design targeted interventions. Maternal mortality refers to the death of a woman while pregnant or within forty-two days of termination of pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy. Newborn mortality covers deaths during the first twenty-eight days of life, while infant mortality spans the entire first year.

Every statistic represents an individual, a family, and a community deeply impacted by a preventable loss. When mothers and babies survive and thrive, entire communities grow stronger. As healthcare professionals, our goal goes far beyond merely keeping patients alive; we strive to ensure a high quality of life free from long-term disability.

Epidemiology and Key Statistical Indicators in Nigeria

Nigeria carries a heavy burden of maternal and child ill health on the global stage. Epidemiological indicators provide the raw data required to measure how well health systems are performing.

  • Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR): Expressed as the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.
  • Neonatal Mortality Rate (NMR): Expressed as the number of deaths during the first 28 days of life per 1,000 live births.
  • Infant Mortality Rate (IMR): Expressed as the number of deaths in children under one year of age per 1,000 live births.
  • Under-Five Mortality Rate: Measures the probability of dying between birth and exactly five years of age per 1,000 live births.

Disparities often exist between rural and urban settings, driven by poverty, distance to health facilities, and cultural practices. A nurse must interpret these local indicators to plan effective community sensitization programs.

Major Direct and Indirect Causes of Maternal and Newborn Deaths

Preventing deaths requires a clear understanding of what kills mothers and babies. Most maternal deaths stem from direct obstetric complications that can strike suddenly.

  1. Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Severe bleeding after delivery remains the leading cause of maternal mortality.
  2. Hypertensive Disorders: Conditions like preeclampsia and eclampsia can lead to seizures, stroke, and multi-organ failure.
  3. Sepsis: Severe infections following childbirth or unsafe abortion.
  4. Unsafe Abortions: Performed in unhygienic conditions by unqualified persons.

For newborns and infants, the primary killers include birth asphyxia (failure to initiate and sustain breathing at birth), neonatal sepsis, and complications of prematurity such as respiratory distress syndrome. Indirect causes like malaria, severe anemia, and malnutrition further worsen outcomes for both mother and child.

Antenatal, Intrapartum, and Postnatal Care Interventions

Quality care across the maternity cycle forms a continuous safety net. Antenatal care (ANC) allows nurses to monitor fetal development, screen for infections like HIV and syphilis, administer tetanus toxoid, and provide routine iron and folic acid supplementation.

During the intrapartum period, monitoring labor using the partograph helps detect prolonged labor early before uterine rupture or fetal distress occurs. Skilled birth attendance ensures clean delivery practices and active management of the third stage of labor to prevent bleeding. The postnatal period is notoriously neglected, yet many maternal and neonatal deaths occur during the first 48 hours post-delivery. Routine checks during this window catch early signs of infection, bleeding, and feeding difficulties.

Essential Newborn Care and Immunization Programs

Immediate newborn care practices dictate whether an infant survives the vulnerable neonatal phase. Every baby needs immediate thermal care through drying and skin-to-skin contact to prevent hypothermia. Immediate initiation of exclusive breastfeeding within the first hour provides vital colostrum, which acts as the baby's first immunization.

Avoiding harmful traditional cord practices prevents neonatal tetanus and severe umbilical infections. Standard newborn prophylaxis includes applying eye antimicrobial ointment to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum and administering vitamin K to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn. Immunization programs build upon this foundation by protecting infants against vaccine-preventable diseases like polio, measles, tuberculosis, and pertussis.

Nutrition, Family Planning, and Health Education

Empowering women through knowledge and proper nutrition transforms family health outcomes. Maternal malnutrition contributes heavily to low birth weight, stunting, and maternal exhaustion. Nurses must educate pregnant women on balanced diets rich in local protein sources, green leafy vegetables, and micronutrients.

Family planning serves as a powerful preventative tool. Allowing adequate spacing between pregnancies—at least two to three years—allows a mother's body to recover its nutrient stores, drastically lowering the risk of premature birth, low birth weight, and maternal mortality. Health education sessions in clinics and communities bridge the gap between clinical science and everyday household practices.

Community-Based Strategies and Referral Systems

Hospitals alone cannot solve the crisis of maternal and infant mortality. Many deaths occur at home because families cannot recognize danger signs or afford transport to health facilities. Community-based strategies engage local leaders, traditional birth attendants, and women's groups to change health-seeking behavior.

Developing functional emergency referral systems is vital. When a primary health clinic encounters a complicated case like obstructed labor or severe preeclampsia, rapid transport to a comprehensive facility must occur without bureaucratic delays. Establishing community emergency transport schemes and clear communication channels between traditional settings and modern hospitals saves lives when every minute counts.
