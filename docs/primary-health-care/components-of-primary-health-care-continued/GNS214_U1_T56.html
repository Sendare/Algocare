Control of Diarrhoeal Diseases

Understanding Diarrhoeal Diseases and Pathophysiology

Diarrhoea remains a major public health concern, especially among children under five years of age. It is clinically defined as the passage of three or more loose or watery stools within a twenty-four-hour period.

At the tissue level, fluid movement in the intestines goes off balance. Normally, the gut absorbs water and nutrients while keeping waste solid. During a disease state, this balance shifts through two primary mechanisms:

  • Secretory diarrhoea: Pathogens stimulate the intestinal cells to pump massive amounts of water and electrolytes directly into the gut lumen, overwhelming normal reabsorption.
  • Osmotic or watery diarrhoea: Damaged mucosal cells lose their surface area and digestive enzymes, leaving unabsorbed substances in the gut that draw water out of the body by osmosis.

When fluid loss outpaces intake, rapid depletion of water and vital minerals follows, putting the patient at immediate risk of severe shock and metabolic acidosis.

Common Causes and Primary Risk Factors

In primary health care settings, infectious agents are the leading drivers of acute watery or bloody diarrhoea. Rotavirus is the most common viral culprit among infants, while bacterial pathogens like *Escherichia coli* and *Shigella* species frequently cause inflammatory or dysenteric illness. Parasites such as *Giardia* contribute to persistent forms lasting longer than fourteen days.

Transmission typically follows the fecal-oral route, fueled by several environmental and social risk factors:

  1. Unsafe drinking water: Consuming contaminated surface water or poorly stored well water.
  2. Poor sanitation: Lack of proper latrines leading to environmental contamination.
  3. Inadequate hygiene: Infrequent handwashing with soap after using the toilet or before feeding children.
  4. Early cessation of breastfeeding: Removing passive maternal immunity too soon leaves infants vulnerable to gut infections.
  5. Malnutrition: Vitamin A deficiency and protein-energy malnutrition weaken the mucosal immune barrier of the gut.

Clinical Assessment and Dehydration Grading

Evaluating a child with acute diarrhoea requires a systematic physical assessment to grade dehydration status. Nurses must look past the frequency of stools and focus on physiological signs of water shortage.

Assessing the skin pinch is done by pinching the skin of the abdomen for one second and releasing; if it takes longer than two seconds to flatten, fluid deficit is significant.

Oral Rehydration Therapy and Zinc Supplementation

Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) is the cornerstone of treating mild to moderate dehydration. Standard Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) contain a precise balance of glucose and electrolytes. The presence of glucose activates sodium-glucose cotransporters in the small intestine, driving water absorption even when secretory diarrhea is active.

Alongside ORS, standard clinical protocol mandates daily zinc supplementation for ten to fourteen days:

  • Dosing: Infants under six months receive 10 mg daily; children over six months receive 20 mg daily.
  • Benefits: Zinc reduces the duration and severity of the current episode, improves intestinal mucosal regeneration, and lowers the risk of subsequent diarrhoeal episodes over the following two to three months.

Nurses should demonstrate how to mix ORS in clean water to mothers, emphasizing that the solution must be freshly prepared daily and discarded after twenty-four hours.

Nutritional Care and Continued Feeding

A common misconception in managing childhood diarrhoea is that the gut needs to rest by withholding food. Starvation actually worsens mucosal atrophy, prolongs recovery, and worsens nutritional status.

Breastfeeding should continue on demand throughout the illness. For non-breastfed children, regular nutrient-rich foods should resume as soon as rehydration is complete. Small, frequent meals given six times a day encourage intake despite reduced appetite.

Foods should be soft, easily digestible, and rich in energy and micronutrients. Porridges mixed with legume flour, mashed yam, or mashed bananas serve as good recovery foods. After diarrhoea stops, extra meals should continue for at least two weeks to help the child catch up on missed growth.

Intravenous Fluid Therapy and Hospital Referral

Oral rehydration is not always sufficient. When a child presents with severe dehydration, shock, or intractable vomiting that prevents oral intake, immediate intravenous (IV) fluid resuscitation is required.

Ringers Lactate or Normal Saline solution is infused rapidly to restore blood volume and correct electrolyte imbalances. Nurses must monitor infusion rates closely to prevent fluid overload, tracking vital signs and urine output every thirty minutes.

Immediate hospital referral is also indicated if any of the following danger signs are present:

  • Blood in the stool, suggesting dysentery or invasive bacterial disease.
  • Persistent vomiting that prevents keeping any oral fluids down.
  • High fever that does not respond to antipyretics.
  • Signs of severe malnutrition like severe wasting or bilateral pitting edema.

Prevention Strategies and Community Education

Sustainable control of diarrhoeal diseases depends on proactive community health education and environmental sanitation. Nurses serve as educators in primary care clinics, teaching families how to break the cycle of infection.

Core prevention messages focus on five key interventions:

  1. Safe water management: Boiling drinking water or using chlorine treatment when community supplies are suspect.
  2. Hand hygiene: Washing hands with soap and water before preparing food, before eating, and after using the latrine or changing diapers.
  3. Sanitary waste disposal: Using proper latrines and keeping human waste away from water sources and play areas.
  4. Food safety: Keeping cooked food covered, avoiding raw or unwashed produce, and reheating leftovers thoroughly.
  5. Immunization: Ensuring children receive rotavirus and measles vaccines according to the national immunization schedule.
