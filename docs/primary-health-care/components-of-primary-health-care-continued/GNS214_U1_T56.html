Control of Diarrhoeal Diseases

Introduction and Classification of Diarrhoeal Diseases

Diarrhoea remains a leading contributor to childhood morbidity and mortality across developing regions, including Nigeria. Clinically, it is defined as the passage of three or more loose or liquid stools per day, or more frequently than is normal for the individual. Understanding the duration and clinical pattern dictates the management approach in primary health care settings.

  • Acute watery diarrhoea: Lasts for several hours or days, posing an immediate threat through rapid fluid loss and hypovolaemic shock.
  • Acute bloody diarrhoea (dysentery): Characterized by visible red blood in the stool, typically indicating intestinal wall invasion, mucosal damage, and systemic inflammation.
  • Persistent diarrhoea: Episodes that last for 14 days or longer, frequently accompanied by significant nutritional depletion and chronic malabsorption.

Pathophysiology and Mechanisms of Fluid Loss

Fluid loss in acute diarrhoeal episodes generally occurs through secretory or osmotic mechanisms. In secretory diarrhoea, enterotoxins stimulate active anion secretion into the intestinal lumen, dragging water and electrolytes along without structural mucosal destruction. Conversely, osmotic diarrhoea results from unabsorbed dietary solutes drawing water osmotically into the bowel lumen, commonly seen in carbohydrate malabsorption during persistent illness.

Electrolyte depletion runs parallel to water loss. Sodium and potassium are heavily stripped through profuse watery evacuations, while bicarbonate loss leads directly to metabolic acidosis. This underlying biochemical disruption accounts for the characteristic lethargy, rapid breathing, and sunken fontanelles observed in severe dehydration.

Etiology and Primary Health Care Risk Factors

Infectious agents dominate the etiology of childhood diarrhoea, spread primarily through the faecal-oral route. Rotavirus is the leading viral trigger for severe dehydrating gastroenteritis among infants. Bacterial pathogens such as Escherichia coli strains, Shigella species, and Salmonella account for substantial burdens of both watery and bloody episodes, while protozoal agents like Giardia lamblia frequently contribute to prolonged illness.

Transmission dynamics are heavily influenced by environmental and socio-economic factors common in resource-constrained communities:

  • Unsafe drinking water sources and inadequate household water storage.
  • Poor sanitation facilities and open defecation practices.
  • Absence of handwashing with soap after defecation and before food handling.
  • Early cessation of exclusive breastfeeding and introduction of contaminated weaning foods.

Oral Rehydration Therapy and Zinc Supplementation

Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) remains the cornerstone of modern diarrhoea management, relying on sodium-glucose cotransporter coupling in the small intestine. Even when secretory toxins prompt net fluid secretion, this co-transport mechanism remains fully functional, allowing water and sodium absorption to continue as long as glucose is present in the lumen.

Low-osmolarity Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS) solution reduces stool output, vomiting frequency, and the need for intravenous infusions compared to older, higher-osmolarity formulations. Alongside ORS, elemental zinc administration for 10 to 14 days is clinically mandatory. Zinc shortens the duration of the current episode, reduces stool severity, and bolsters immune function to prevent recurrent diarrhoeal episodes over the subsequent two to three months.

Nutritional Care and Continued Feeding

Starving a child with diarrhoea is an outdated practice that worsens nutritional compromise, mucosal atrophy, and systemic stunting. Nutritional management focuses on maintaining adequate caloric and nutrient intake throughout the acute phase and aggressively catching up during convalescence.

Breastfeeding must be continued on demand throughout the illness without interruption. For older infants and young children, regular nutrient-dense family foods should be offered in small, frequent meals. Foods rich in potassium, such as mashed bananas and tender vegetables, help replenish lost intracellular electrolytes, while adequate dietary energy intake ensures rapid regeneration of damaged intestinal villi.

Indications for Intravenous Therapy and Hospital Referral

While oral rehydration succeeds in the vast majority of cases, specific clinical scenarios demand immediate escalation to intravenous fluid therapy and urgent hospital transfer. Primary health care workers must recognize these red flags promptly to prevent fatal hypovolaemic shock.

  • Severe dehydration where the child cannot drink or is persistently vomiting all oral fluids.
  • Suspected severe systemic infection, severe acute malnutrition, or clinical signs of acute abdomen.
  • Failure of oral rehydration therapy after a designated trial period or worsening clinical status during rehydration.
  • Presence of profuse, continuous watery stool output exceeding the intake capacity of the child.

Prevention Strategies and Public Health Education

Controlling diarrhoeal disease at a population level requires targeted public health interventions that address the root environmental and behavioral drivers of transmission. Community health education plays a pivotal role in empowering caregivers to adopt protective hygiene practices.

Key preventive measures include expanding access to safely managed drinking water and promoting household water treatment such as boiling or chlorination. Sanitation infrastructure improvements must be paired with sustained hygiene promotion campaigns emphasizing proper handwashing with soap at critical times—specifically after toilet use, cleaning a child's bottom, and before preparing food. Furthermore, scaling up rotavirus and measles vaccination programs directly reduces the incidence of severe viral enteritis and immune-mediated complications.
