Pre-Natal Care

Introduction and Objectives of Pre-Natal Care

Pre-natal care, commonly referred to as antenatal care (ANC), encompasses the comprehensive medical and nursing supervision given to pregnant women. The primary goal is to monitor the physiological progression of gestation and promote the health of both mother and fetus. Through scheduled clinical visits, healthcare providers detect deviations from normal pregnancy early, offer preventive treatments like tetanus toxoid immunization and intermittent preventive treatment for malaria, and provide health education.

Key objectives within the primary health care setting include:

  • Risk identification: Screening for pre-existing or pregnancy-induced medical conditions.
  • Health promotion: Educating mothers on nutrition, hygiene, and danger signs.
  • Psychological preparation: Counseling the mother for childbirth and newborn care.

Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

Gestation induces profound anatomical and physiological adaptations across nearly every body system. Understanding these normal variants helps nurses distinguish benign symptoms from true pathology.

  • Cardiovascular system: Blood volume increases by up to 40-50% to meet placental demands, leading to a physiological decrease in blood pressure during the second trimester and a functionally elevated heart rate.
  • Hematological system: Plasma volume expands faster than red blood cell mass, resulting in physiological anemia of pregnancy.
  • Gastrointestinal system: Progesterone relaxes smooth muscles, slowing gastric motility and causing constipation, heartburn, and delayed emptying.
  • Renal system: Glomerular filtration rate increases significantly, which frequently leads to normal gestational frequency of urination.

Initial and Routine Antenatal Assessments

The booking visit—the first antenatal contact—establishes a baseline for the pregnancy. Nurses take a detailed obstetric, medical, and social history, and calculate the estimated due date (EDD) using Naegele's rule. Routine visits subsequently monitor maternal weight, blood pressure, urinalysis for protein and sugar, and fetal well-being.

Fetal assessment includes measuring symphysis-fundal height in centimeters after 20 weeks of gestation to track intrauterine growth, auscultating fetal heart tones, and evaluating fetal presentation during the third trimester. Routine screening tests also check for blood group, rhesus factor, hemoglobin levels, and infections such as HIV, syphilis, and hepatitis B.

Nutritional Requirements and Health Promotion

Expectant mothers require targeted nutritional counseling to support fetal development and maintain maternal reserves. Energy intake must increase moderately, with specific emphasis on micronutrients. Folic acid supplementation in the first trimester prevents neural tube defects, while iron and folate tablets prevent severe maternal anemia.

Health promotion during ANC covers personal hygiene, adequate rest, avoidance of teratogenic substances like alcohol and tobacco, and safe insecticide-treated net usage to prevent malaria. Nurses must also debunk cultural food taboos that restrict nutritious local foods such as eggs, meat, and fish during pregnancy.

Identification and Management of Antenatal Complications

Nurses must remain vigilant for danger signs that indicate life-threatening complications. Prompt recognition allows timely referral from primary health centers to secondary or tertiary facilities.

Common danger signs requiring immediate action:

  • Severe vaginal bleeding: Indicates conditions like placenta previa or placental abruption.
  • Persistent severe headache and blurred vision: Suggests gestational hypertension or pre-eclampsia.
  • Fever and chills: Points toward infections such as malaria or urinary tract infections.
  • Absence of fetal movements: Indicates potential fetal distress or intrauterine fetal demise.

Birth Preparedness and Complication Readiness

Birth preparedness and complication readiness (BPCR) is a strategy implemented during ANC to reduce delays in obtaining care. It ensures that every pregnant woman has a concrete plan for her delivery and knows what to do if an emergency arises.

Core components of a birth plan include:

  • Identifying the intended place of delivery and a skilled birth attendant.
  • Saving money for transport, hospital fees, and medications.
  • Arranging for blood donors in case of postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Identifying a reliable mode of emergency transportation.
  • Recognizing key danger signs of labor and obstetric emergencies.
