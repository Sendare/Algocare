Pre-Natal Care

Introduction and Objectives of Pre-Natal Care

Pre-natal care, also known as antenatal care (ANC), refers to the specialized medical, nutritional, and emotional support provided to a pregnant woman from conception until the onset of labor. Within primary health care systems, ANC serves as a vital preventive strategy rather than a reactive treatment. The primary goal is to monitor the normal physiological progression of pregnancy while screening for any deviations that could threaten the health of the mother or the developing fetus.

Regular clinical contact allows midwives and doctors to detect silent conditions like gestational hypertension or asymptomatic urinary tract infections before they become life-threatening emergencies. Beyond physical screening, these visits offer an invaluable platform for health education, preparing parents for childbirth, and building a trusting relationship between the healthcare team and the community.

Normal Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

Pregnancy induces profound systemic adaptations across almost every organ system to sustain the growing fetus. Understanding these shifts helps nurses distinguish between normal discomforts and pathological warning signs.

  • Cardiovascular System: Blood volume increases by up to 40-50% to supply the uteroplacental unit, causing a physiological drop in blood pressure during the second trimester and an elevated resting heart rate.
  • Respiratory System: Elevated progesterone levels stimulate the respiratory center, increasing tidal volume and causing mild, normal shortness of breath.
  • Renal System: GFR increases significantly due to increased renal blood flow, which frequently leads to daytime frequency of urination.
  • Gastrointestinal System: Smooth muscle relaxation caused by progesterone slows gastric motility and bowel transit time, commonly resulting in constipation and heartburn.

Initial and Routine Antenatal Assessments

The first antenatal visit is the most comprehensive, establishing a baseline for the entire pregnancy. It includes a detailed medical, surgical, and obstetric history, alongside a thorough physical examination and baseline laboratory investigations such as blood grouping, genotype, hemoglobin estimation, and screening for infections like HIV and syphilis.

Subsequent routine visits follow a scheduled timeline—typically monthly until 28 weeks, fortnightly until 36 weeks, and weekly thereafter. During every routine contact, nurses check critical parameters:

  1. Blood Pressure: To screen for pregnancy-induced hypertension or pre-eclampsia.
  2. Urinalysis: To test for protein and glucose, indicating renal stress or gestational diabetes.
  3. Fundal Height Measurement: To track fetal growth trajectory in centimeters from the pubic symphysis.
  4. Fetal Heart Rate Auscultation: To confirm fetal viability using a fetoscope or Doppler device.

Nutritional Requirements and Health Promotion

Nutritional demands rise noticeably during pregnancy to support maternal tissue growth and fetal development. Nurses must educate mothers on balanced diets rich in energy, protein, and micronutrients.

Iron supplementation is routinely prescribed to prevent iron-deficiency anemia, compounded by the physiological hemodilution of pregnancy. Folic acid is essential, especially in the periconceptional period and first trimester, to prevent neural tube defects like spina bifida. Calcium is vital for fetal skeletal development and protecting maternal bone density.

Beyond diet, health promotion includes encouraging adequate rest, safe physical activity, and strict avoidance of teratogenic substances such as alcohol, tobacco, and unprescribed medications. Insecticide-treated mosquito nets are also heavily promoted in malaria-endemic regions to prevent maternal infection and low birth weight.

Identification and Management of Antenatal Complications

While most pregnancies proceed smoothly, nurses must remain vigilant for warning signs that signal obstetric emergencies. Early recognition saves lives.

  • Vaginal Bleeding: May indicate spontaneous abortion, placenta previa, or placental abruption.
  • Severe Headaches and Visual Disturbances: Classic indicators of impending eclampsia driven by severe hypertension.
  • Fever and Chills: Often point toward malaria, urinary tract infections, or chorioamnionitis.
  • Decreased Fetal Movements: A critical red flag signaling fetal distress or hypoxia.

When these danger signs appear, immediate triage, stabilization, and rapid referral to a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility are required. Nurses must never dismiss a mother's subjective complaint of reduced fetal movement.

Birth Preparedness and Complication Readiness

Birth preparedness and complication readiness (BPCR) is a strategy designed to reduce delays in obtaining skilled obstetric care. Every pregnant woman and her family must create a clear plan before the estimated date of delivery.

The plan must identify the intended place of delivery, a skilled birth attendant, and a reliable means of emergency transport. Families should also arrange for emergency funds and identify a compatible blood donor in case postpartum hemorrhage occurs. Nurses guide couples through these preparations during routine counseling sessions, ensuring that cultural preferences are respected while prioritizing clinical safety.
