Pre-Natal Care

Introduction and Objectives of Pre-Natal Care

Pre-natal care, commonly referred to as antenatal care (ANC), represents the comprehensive medical and nursing supervision given to an expectant mother throughout her pregnancy. The primary goal is monitoring physiological progression, safeguarding maternal and fetal well-being, and ensuring a safe delivery. Within primary health care settings across Nigeria, ANC serves as the bedrock for preventive obstetrics. Clinics utilize these scheduled contacts to screen for asymptomatic conditions like bacteriuria, gestational hypertension, and anemia, which could otherwise escalate into life-threatening emergencies if left unchecked.

Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

Pregnancy induces profound systemic adaptations across every organ system to accommodate the developing fetus. - **Cardiovascular System**: Plasma volume increases by nearly 50%, surpassing the rise in red blood cell production. This physiological hemodilution frequently results in a functional gestational anemia. - **Respiratory System**: Progesterone drives an increased tidal volume and minute ventilation, often leading to a subjective maternal sensation of mild dyspnea. - **Gastrointestinal System**: Smooth muscle relaxation mediated by elevated progesterone slows gastric emptying and bowel motility, predisposing the woman to constipation and heartburn. - **Renal System**: GFR and renal plasma flow increase significantly, causing a lower baseline serum creatinine and frequent nocturnal urination.

Initial and Routine Antenatal Assessments

The initial antenatal visit establishes the baseline health profile of the mother. It involves a detailed obstetric history, a complete physical examination, and baseline laboratory investigations including blood grouping, genotype, VDRL for syphilis, hepatitis screening, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) counseling and testing. Subsequent routine visits focus on sequential monitoring parameters: 1. **Weight Tracking**: Sudden upward spikes may indicate pre-eclampsia edema, while failure to gain weight can point to intrauterine growth restriction. 2. **Blood Pressure Measurement**: Essential for detecting pregnancy-induced hypertension early. 3. **Urinalysis**: Dipstick testing checks for protein, indicating renal compromise, and glucose, signaling gestational diabetes. 4. **Abdominal Examination**: Performed using Leopold's maneuvers from the second trimester onward to assess fetal lie, presentation, and fundal height growth.

Nutritional Requirements and Health Promotion

Expectant mothers require targeted nutritional counseling to support fetal tissue growth and prevent maternal depletion. Iron and folic acid supplementation is routinely commenced at booking to prevent neural tube defects and iron-deficiency anemia. Calcium supplementation is prioritized where dietary intake is low to mitigate hypertensive disorders. Health promotion extends beyond diet. Nurses must provide guidance on safe hygiene practices, adequate rest, vector-borne disease prevention—such as the distribution and consistent use of insecticide-treated bed nets for malaria prevention in endemic regions like Nigeria—and strict avoidance of teratogenic substances, including alcohol, unprescribed native concoctions, and tobacco.

Identification and Management of Antenatal Complications

Nurses must maintain high clinical vigilance to recognize red-flag symptoms during antenatal visits. Vaginal bleeding at any stage of pregnancy can indicate placenta previa, placental abruption, or spontaneous miscarriage. Severe headaches accompanied by visual disturbances and epigastric pain strongly point toward severe pre-eclampsia. Additionally, sudden fever or painful urination suggests urinary tract infections that require immediate antibiotic therapy to prevent preterm labor. Prompt triage, stabilization, and timely referral to a secondary or tertiary facility remain critical nursing actions when these complications arise.

Birth Preparedness and Complication Readiness

Every antenatal contact must incorporate birth preparedness and complication readiness (BPCR) planning. This strategy equips the family to make proactive arrangements before the onset of labor, minimizing delays in reaching skilled care. Key elements include identifying the intended place of delivery, selecting a skilled birth attendant, arranging reliable transportation, securing emergency funds, and identifying a suitable blood donor when feasible. Nurses also educate the woman and her support system on recognizing the true danger signs of labor, ensuring prompt action if complications occur.
