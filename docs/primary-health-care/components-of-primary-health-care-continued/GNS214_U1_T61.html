Immunization Against Major Communicable Diseases

Introduction to Immunization and Primary Health Care

Immunization stands as one of the most cost-effective public health interventions globally, forming a core pillar of Primary Health Care (PHC). By introducing a weakened or inactivated antigen into the body, healthcare providers stimulate the immune system to produce antibodies without causing the full-blown disease. This proactive defense protects both the individual and the wider community through herd immunity, which occurs when a high percentage of a population is vaccinated, making the spread of infectious disease from person to person unlikely. In many resource-limited settings, delivering these life-saving vaccines through community-based PHC clinics ensures that even remote rural populations gain access to essential disease prevention. Nurses serve as the primary educators and administrators in these settings, bridging the gap between scientific prevention and community trust.

Basic Immunology Concepts: Active and Passive Immunity

Understanding how the body builds defense mechanisms helps nurses explain the value of vaccination to hesitant parents. Immunity generally falls into two distinct categories based on how the body acquires its protective response:

  • Active Immunity: Developed when the body's immune system is stimulated to produce its own antibodies in response to an antigen. This can happen naturally through surviving an actual infection, or artificially through vaccination. Active immunity typically takes time to develop but offers long-lasting protection due to immunological memory.
  • Passive Immunity: Acquired when ready-made antibodies are transferred directly into the body from another source. Examples include maternal antibodies passing through the placenta or breast milk to an infant, or receiving therapeutic immunoglobulin injections. While passive immunity provides immediate protection, it fades away after a few weeks or months because the body does not create its own memory cells.

The National Immunization Schedule in Nigeria

Nigeria's national immunization schedule outlines the specific timeline for administering routine vaccines to protect infants against major childhood killers. Following this schedule closely ensures that children receive optimal protection at ages when their immune systems can respond effectively and when they are most vulnerable to infection.

  1. At Birth: Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) for tuberculosis, Oral Polio Vaccine dose zero (OPV 0), and Hepatitis B dose zero.
  2. 6 Weeks: Pentavalent vaccine (Diphtheria, Pertussis, Tetanus, Hepatitis B, Haemophilus influenzae type b) first dose, Pneumococcal Conjugate Vaccine (PCV 1), Rotavirus vaccine first dose, and OPV 1.
  3. 10 Weeks: Pentavalent vaccine second dose, PCV 2, Rotavirus vaccine second dose, and OPV 2.
  4. 14 Weeks: Pentavalent vaccine third dose, PCV 3, Inactivated Polio Vaccine (IPV), and OPV 3.
  5. 9 Months: Measles vaccine first dose, Yellow Fever vaccine, and Meningitis vaccine where applicable.

Target Major Communicable Diseases and Vaccine-Preventable Illnesses

The Expanded Programme on Immunization targets specific infectious diseases that historically caused high rates of infant mortality and lifelong disability across developing nations.

  • Tuberculosis: A bacterial infection attacking the lungs, prevented by the intradermal BCG vaccine given at birth.
  • Poliomyelitis: A viral infection causing irreversible paralysis, targeted by both oral and injectable polio vaccines.
  • Diphtheria, Pertussis, and Tetanus: Severe bacterial infections addressed collectively through the pentavalent combination vaccine. Pertussis causes violent coughing fits, while tetanus leads to painful muscle spasms.
  • Measles: A highly contagious viral illness characterized by high fever, rash, and severe respiratory complications, preventable by a live-attenuated vaccine given at nine months.
  • Yellow Fever: A viral hemorrhagic fever transmitted by mosquitoes, controlled through a single-dose subcutaneous vaccine.

Nurses must recognize the clinical presentations of these illnesses to educate caregivers and quickly identify suspected cases during outbreak investigations.

Vaccine Handling, Storage, and the Cold Chain System

Vaccines are biological products sensitive to heat, light, and freezing. The cold chain system is an uninterrupted system of storage and transport that maintains vaccines within recommended temperature ranges from the manufacturer to the point of administration. If the cold chain breaks, antigens degrade, losing their potency and leaving the recipient unprotected.

  • Freezer Storage (-15°C to -25°C): Used for Oral Polio Vaccine and Measles vaccine.
  • Refrigerator Storage (+2°C to +8°C): Used for Pentavalent, PCV, Hepatitis B, and tetanus toxoid. These vaccines must never be allowed to freeze, as freezing destroys their adjuvants.
  • Vaccine Vial Monitors (VVMs): Small label indicators attached to vaccine vials that change color when exposed to cumulative heat over time, helping nurses decide if a vial is still safe to use.

Proper management requires twice-daily temperature monitoring of all cold room equipment, refrigerators, and vaccine carriers.

Administration Techniques and Safety Protocols

Correct administration technique ensures that vaccines are delivered into the right tissue layer to provoke an optimal immune response while minimizing local adverse effects. Nurses must verify the vaccine type, check expiry dates, inspect the VVM, and confirm the correct route for every injection.

  • Intradermal (ID): Used for the BCG vaccine, typically given on the right upper arm, creating a shallow wheal.
  • Intramuscular (IM): Used for Pentavalent, PCV, and Yellow Fever vaccines, injected into the anterolateral thigh for infants or the deltoid muscle for older children.
  • Subcutaneous (SC): Used for Measles and Meningitis vaccines, administered into the fatty tissue.
  • Oral: Used for OPV and Rotavirus drops.

Strict adherence to aseptic technique and safe waste disposal—such as using auto-disable syringes and discarding them immediately into puncture-proof safety boxes—prevents needle-stick injuries and the transmission of blood-borne pathogens.

Adverse Events Following Immunization (AEFI): Surveillance and Management

While vaccines are remarkably safe, minor side effects can occur as the immune system responds. An Adverse Event Following Immunization (AEFI) is any untoward medical occurrence that happens after vaccination, which may or may not be caused by the vaccine product itself.

AEFIs are categorized into five groups: vaccine product-related reactions, quality defect-related reactions, immunization error-related reactions, immunization anxiety-related reactions, and coincidental events. Common minor reactions include mild fever, soreness, and slight swelling at the injection site, which resolve on their own within a couple of days. Serious adverse events, such as severe allergic reactions or anaphylaxis, require immediate emergency management with epinephrine, airway support, and rapid referral. Effective AEFI surveillance protects public confidence by investigating and addressing safety concerns transparently.

Community Mobilization, Health Education, and Overcoming Vaccine Hesitancy

Vaccine hesitancy—the reluctance or refusal to vaccinate despite the availability of services—poses a major threat to disease eradication efforts. Rumors, cultural misconceptions, and religious beliefs often fuel this hesitation within communities. Nurses play a vital frontline role in building trust through respectful communication, active listening, and targeted health education. Engaging local leaders, traditional rulers, and religious figures helps dispel myths and encourages community ownership of immunization programs. Outreach sessions, house-to-house campaigns, and clear explanations during antenatal visits ensure that caregivers understand both the benefits of vaccines and the risks of preventable diseases.
