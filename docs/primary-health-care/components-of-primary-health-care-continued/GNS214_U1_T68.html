Sterilization of Instruments

Introduction and Pre-requisites: Decontamination and Cleaning

Before any medical tool can be sterilized, it must be thoroughly cleaned. Sterilization destroys all micro-organisms, but it cannot work effectively if organic matter like blood, tissue, or mucus is left on the instrument. Dried organic debris acts as a protective shield for bacteria and viruses, hiding them from heat or chemicals. Therefore, processing used instruments follows a strict sequence: decontamination, cleaning, rinsing, drying, and finally, sterilization.

  1. Decontamination: Soaking used instruments in a chlorine solution (typically 0.5% sodium hypochlorite) for 10 minutes immediately after use. This step inactivates blood-borne pathogens like HIV and Hepatitis B to protect staff during handling.
  2. Manual Cleaning: Scrubbing the instruments in warm water and detergent using a soft brush. Brushing must be done under the water surface to prevent splashing infectious droplets into the air.
  3. Rinsing and Drying: Rinsing off all soap residue with clean water and drying the items completely. Wet instruments can dilute chemical solutions or cause rust during heat processing.

Physical Methods of Sterilization: Steam Under Pressure

The gold standard for medical and surgical instrument sterilization in healthcare settings is the autoclave. An autoclave uses moist heat in the form of pressurized steam to destroy all forms of microbial life, including tough bacterial spores. Steam under pressure is superior to boiling water because pressure raises the temperature of the steam well above the normal boiling point of 100 degrees Celsius.

Standard operating parameters for a gravity displacement autoclave typically require a temperature of 121 degrees Celsius at 15 pounds per square inch of pressure for 15 to 30 minutes, depending on whether the items are wrapped or unwrapped. Common student misconception: steaming for a longer time at a lower temperature does not achieve sterilization. The high temperature driven by pressure is what denatures microbial proteins rapidly. The loading of the autoclave chamber is equally critical; packs must never be overcrowded, because steam must freely circulate around and through every item to ensure contact with all surfaces.

Dry Heat and Chemical Sterilization Alternatives

While steam is preferred for most metal items, certain instruments and materials require alternative methods.

  • Dry Heat Sterilization: Utilizes a hot air oven operating at higher temperatures (typically 160 degrees Celsius for 2 hours or 180 degrees Celsius for 1 hour). It is ideal for sharp instruments because it does not dull cutting edges, and for anhydrous powders or oils that steam cannot penetrate. However, it requires longer exposure times because dry heat transfers energy much slower than moist steam.
  • Chemical Sterilization: Involves soaking heat-sensitive items in liquid chemical sterilants like activated glutaraldehyde (2%) or hydrogen peroxide. This method is often called cold sterilization. It requires prolonged soaking times (often 8 to 10 hours) to achieve true sterilization. Items processed chemically must be rinsed thoroughly with sterile water before use on a patient to remove toxic chemical residues that can cause tissue irritation.

Monitoring, Quality Control, and Packaging

Running a sterilizer does not guarantee that instruments are sterile; quality control measures must verify the process every time. There are three main types of monitoring:

  1. Mechanical Monitors: The gauges, dials, and digital printouts on the machine itself that show time, temperature, and pressure during the cycle.
  2. Chemical Indicators: Special tape, strips, or indicator lines on packaging that change color when exposed to specific temperatures or steam. These confirm that the package was exposed to heat, but they do not prove that micro-organisms are dead.
  3. Biological Indicators: The only true confirmation of sterilization. They contain harmless bacterial spores (such as *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* for steam autoclaves) that are exceptionally heat resistant. If the spores are killed during the cycle, the biological test passes.

Proper packaging using porous materials like medical-grade paper or cotton wrappers allows steam to enter while blocking bacteria during storage. Sterile packages must be stored in clean, dry, closed cabinets away from moisture and dust.

Infection Control and Preventing Contamination

Maintaining sterility is a continuous chain of infection control practices. A single break in technique renders a sterile instrument contaminated, putting the patient at risk of surgical site infections. Nurses must practice aseptic technique when opening packs, ensuring that sterile forceps touch only sterile surfaces and that sterile fields are never left unattended.

When handling wrapped sterile items, inspect the package integrity before opening. Any package that is torn, wet, dropped on the floor, or past its expiration date is considered contaminated and must be re-processed. Expiration of sterility is event-related rather than time-related; a pack remains sterile as long as the packaging barrier remains intact, dry, and undamaged.
