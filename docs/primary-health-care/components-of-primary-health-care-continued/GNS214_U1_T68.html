Sterilization of Instruments

Introduction and Pre-requisites: Decontamination and Cleaning

Before any instrument enters an autoclave or chemical bath, it must undergo meticulous decontamination and cleaning. Skipping these foundational steps ensures that residual protein, blood, and bioburden shield microorganisms from sterilizing agents.

  • Decontamination: Soaking used instruments in a 0.5% chlorine solution for 10 minutes immediately after use to neutralize blood-borne pathogens like HIV and Hepatitis B.
  • Cleaning: Scrubbing instruments manually with soap, water, and a soft brush to remove physical debris, followed by thorough rinsing and drying.

Never use hot water for initial cleaning of bloody instruments, as heat coagulates proteins, baking them onto the metal surfaces and making subsequent sterilization ineffective.

Physical and Chemical Methods of Sterilization

Sterilization destroys all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial endospores. Selection of the method depends on the heat and moisture tolerance of the medical devices.

  • Physical Methods: Utilize thermal energy. Steam under pressure is the gold standard for most surgical metals and linen packs. Dry heat suits sharp instruments and anhydrous powders.
  • Chemical Methods: Employ liquid or gaseous agents for heat-sensitive equipment like plastic endoscopes and certain rubber goods. Common agents include activated glutaraldehyde, hydrogen peroxide gas plasma, and ethylene oxide gas.

Exam pitfall: Chemical soaking is often called 'cold sterilization' by clinical staff, but microbiologically it is high-level disinfection unless items remain submerged for extended hours to achieve true spore destruction.

Operation and Maintenance of the Autoclave

The steam autoclave uses pressure to elevate water boiling points, achieving temperatures capable of destroying all pathogens rapidly. Standard operating parameters typically require 121°C (250°F) at 15 pounds per square inch (psi) of pressure for 15 to 30 minutes, depending on pack density.

Proper loading is critical for success. Instruments must be arranged loosely to allow steam to circulate freely around every item. Overloading traps air pockets, creating cold spots where steam fails to penetrate. Door seals must be inspected regularly for cracks, and chamber drains must be cleared of sediment weekly to prevent pressure fluctuations.

Dry Heat Sterilization and Boiling Water Techniques

When steam or modern autoclaves are unavailable in rural primary health posts, alternative thermal methods are occasionally utilized, though each carries distinct operational limitations.

  • Dry Heat (Hot Air Ovens): Operates at higher temperatures (e.g., 160°C for 2 hours or 170°C for 1 hour). Because dry heat transfers thermal energy slowly, items take longer to process. It does not rust carbon-steel instruments, making it suitable for sharp cutting edges.
  • Boiling Water: Submerging items in rolling water for 20 minutes kills vegetative bacteria and most viruses, but it does NOT reliably destroy bacterial endospores or certain hepatitis viruses. Therefore, boiling is classified as high-level disinfection rather than true sterilization.

Monitoring and Quality Control of Sterilization

Running an autoclave does not guarantee sterility; continuous verification is required. Quality control relies on three distinct types of indicators:

  1. Mechanical Indicators: Gauges, digital displays, and printouts showing time, temperature, and pressure during the cycle.
  2. Chemical Indicators: Tape, strips, or special labels containing ink that changes color when exposed to specific temperatures or steam conditions. They confirm the pack was processed, but not necessarily that sterility was achieved.
  3. Biological Indicators: Vials or strips containing heat-resistant bacterial spores (such as *Geobacillus stearothermophilus*). These provide the ultimate proof of sterilization capability because if the cycle kills the hardy spores, all other pathogens are dead.

Packaging, Storing, and Maintaining Sterile Integrity

Sterilizing an instrument is pointless if contamination occurs before use. Packaging materials—such as medical-grade paper, woven muslin wraps, or specialized pouches—must allow sterilizing agents to penetrate while blocking airborne microbes afterward.

Sterility is event-related rather than time-related. A pack remains sterile indefinitely unless the protective barrier is compromised. Factors that compromise sterility include moisture, tears, dust accumulation, or broken seals. Store sterile supplies in clean, dry, closed cabinets away from sinks, exterior walls, and high-traffic floors.
