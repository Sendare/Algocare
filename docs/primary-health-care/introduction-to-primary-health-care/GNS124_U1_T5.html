Principles of Primary Health Care

Historical Background and Evolution of Primary Health Care

Before the late 1970s, global health strategies largely focused on centralized, hospital-based, and disease-specific curative models. This approach left rural and impoverished populations without adequate care. Recognizing this gross inequity, the World Health Organization and UNICEF convened a historic international conference in 1978 in Alma-Ata, Kazakhstan. The resulting Declaration of Alma-Ata established Primary Health Care as the key to achieving health for all by the year 2000. It shifted the paradigm from mere absence of disease to holistic well-being, emphasizing social justice, community self-reliance, and basic human rights in health service delivery.

Core Definition and Philosophy

Primary Health Care is not merely a level of care, but a foundational approach to health systems organization. According to the World Health Organization, it is essential health care based on practical, scientifically sound, and socially acceptable methods and technology. These services are made universally accessible to individuals and families in the community through their full participation. The philosophy rests squarely on social justice, equity, and self-determination, treating health as a fundamental human right rather than a commercial commodity.

Equitable Distribution and Universal Coverage

Equitable distribution means that health services must be shared equally by all segments of the population, regardless of wealth, gender, or geographic location. In many settings, urban elites enjoy well-funded medical centers while rural communities lack basic dispensaries. Universal coverage ensures that everyone who needs care receives it, without facing financial hardship. Achieving this requires deliberate resource allocation, bringing services closer to where people live and work, and dismantling physical, financial, and cultural barriers to health access.

Community Participation and Empowerment

Sustainable health programs cannot be imposed from the outside; they require active involvement from the target population. Community participation transforms individuals from passive recipients of care into active drivers of their own health outcomes. Communities must be involved in identifying their primary health needs, planning interventions, implementing programs, and evaluating results. Empowerment builds local capacity, ensuring that community members gain the confidence, skills, and resources to sustain health initiatives long after external funding or personnel depart.

Intersectoral Collaboration

Health is influenced by many factors outside the traditional medical sector, including education, agriculture, housing, sanitation, and economic policies. Therefore, intersectoral collaboration is vital. Ministries of health cannot achieve optimal population health working in isolation. Collaboration brings together agriculture to ensure food security, education to promote health literacy, public works to provide clean water, and local government to improve sanitation. Recognizing that social determinants of health drive disease patterns makes cross-sectoral partnerships mandatory.

Appropriate Technology and Cost-Effectiveness

Advanced medical technology is often prohibitively expensive, difficult to maintain, and unsuited for rural or low-resource environments. Appropriate technology refers to methods, procedures, equipment, and facilities that are scientifically sound, adaptable to local needs, and maintainable with available community resources. It emphasizes cost-effectiveness, ensuring that limited health budgets achieve the greatest possible impact. Examples include the use of oral rehydration salts for diarrhea, growth monitoring charts, and solar-powered vaccine refrigerators in remote health posts.

Essential Components of Primary Health Care

The Alma-Ata Declaration outlined eight essential components required to meet the basic health needs of a population. These services form the bedrock of grassroots health systems worldwide.

  • Education: Health education concerning prevailing health problems and methods of preventing them.
  • Promotion of Food Supply: Proper nutrition and an adequate supply of safe food.
  • Water and Sanitation: An adequate supply of safe drinking water and basic sanitation.
  • Maternal and Child Health: Family planning and reproductive health services.
  • Immunization: Protection against major infectious childhood diseases.
  • Prevention and Control: Local endemic disease prevention and management.
  • Appropriate Treatment: Proper treatment of common diseases and minor injuries.
  • Essential Drugs: Provision of essential, affordable pharmaceutical supplies.

Application in Nigerian Nursing Practice

In Nigeria, Primary Health Care is delivered primarily through a network of primary health centers at the Local Government Area level. Nurses and midwives play pivotal leadership roles in these facilities, functioning not only as clinicians but also as health educators, administrators, and community mobilizers. When conducting antenatal care, home visits, or immunization drives, Nigerian nurses apply PHC principles by collaborating with local chiefs, training village health workers, and using local languages for health education. Recognizing common public health challenges—such as malaria, malnutrition, and vaccine hesitancy—enables nurses to deliver culturally safe, impactful care.
