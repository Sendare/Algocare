Development Committees in Primary Health Care Services in Nigeria

Introduction to Primary Health Care Development Committees in Nigeria

Primary Health Care (PHC) in Nigeria is built on the philosophy of community participation, as enshrined in the Bamako Initiative and the National Health Policy. To move healthcare delivery from a top-down approach to a community-owned model, a structured system of development committees was established across various tiers of government. These committees serve as a vital bridge between health service providers and the grassroots population, ensuring that community needs directly influence health planning, resource mobilization, and decision-making.

Village Development Committee (VDC)

The **Village Development Committee (VDC)** operates at the absolute grassroots level, representing a cluster of villages or a specific community. Comprising local leaders, traditional rulers, women's group representatives, and youth leaders, the VDC identifies local health priorities and mobilizes community resources. Key functions include: - **Health Mobilization**: Encouraging community members to utilize immunization services, antenatal care, and environmental sanitation programs. - **Resource Contribution**: Organizing communal labor for clearing paths to health posts or building waiting sheds. - **Feedback Mechanism**: Reporting outbreaks of communicable diseases or community dissatisfaction with health services to higher authorities.

Health Facility Development Committee (HFDC)

While the VDC focuses on the wider community, the **Health Facility Development Committee (HFDC)** is specifically attached to a designated Primary Health Care facility, such as a Comprehensive Health Centre or Basic Health Post. The officer-in-charge of the facility works hand-in-hand with the HFDC chairperson to manage day-to-day facility challenges. Common responsibilities of the HFDC include maintaining the physical infrastructure of the clinic, ensuring security for health workers living in rural quarters, managing revenue generated from user fees (where applicable), and resolving petty grievances between patients and clinical staff. In many Nigerian states, an active HFDC is a mandatory prerequisite for receiving decentralized drug revolving funds.

District and Local Government Area Development Committees

Moving upward through the primary healthcare administrative structure, the **District Development Committee (DDC)** coordinates activities across multiple villages and health facilities within a defined district. It synthesizes reports from various VDCs and HFDCs before forwarding them to the local level. At the Local Government Area (LGA) tier, the **Local Government Area Primary Health Care Development Committee (LGPHCDC)** holds immense executive power. Chaired by the LGA Chairman or the PHC Coordinator, this committee oversees all health programs within the council area, manages personnel deployment, coordinates cold chain maintenance, and ensures integration between vertical disease control programs and routine primary care.

State and National Policy Implementation Committees

At the macro level, the **State Implementation Committee (SIC)** and the **National Policy Implementation Committee** ensure alignment between grassroots activities and national health goals. The State Primary Health Care Development Agency (SPHCDA) anchors the state committee, which provides technical support, budget allocations, and capacity building for LGA committees. Meanwhile, the national committee formulates broad guidelines, manages donor partner interventions, and evaluates the overall impact of primary health care delivery across Nigeria's thirty-six states and the Federal Capital Territory.

Challenges and Strategies for Effective Committee Operations

Despite their well-designed structure, primary health care development committees in Nigeria frequently encounter operational bottlenecks. Common challenges include political interference, lack of consistent funding for committee meetings, elite capture where powerful individuals hijack decisions, and high illiteracy rates hindering proper record-keeping. To overcome these hurdles, nursing and public health leaders advocate for continuous capacity building, transparent financial tracking, regular supportive supervision from state monitors, and strict adherence to inclusive selection processes that empower ordinary community members.
