Functions and Responsibilities of the Nurse in the Primary Health Care Setting

Introduction to Primary Health Care and Nursing Roles

Primary Health Care represents the first level of contact individuals, families, and communities have with the national health system. In Nigeria, the PHC system forms the bedrock of the entire healthcare delivery structure, bringing essential health services as close as possible to where people live and work. Within this setup, the nurse is often the most visible and accessible health worker, particularly in rural and semi-urban health posts and clinics.

Nursing practice at this level extends far beyond treating simple ailments. The nurse functions as a clinician, an educator, a counselor, and a community leader all at once. Because specialist doctors and advanced diagnostic equipment are rarely available in primary care settings, the community health nurse must rely heavily on clinical judgment, basic assessment tools, and a deep understanding of local health determinants. Success in this environment requires respecting community values, communicating in local languages, and building genuine trust with local leaders and families.

Core Principles of PHC Guiding Nursing Practice

  • Equity: Health services must be shared equally among all people, ensuring that vulnerable and marginalized groups get care regardless of their ability to pay.
  • Community Participation: Healthcare cannot be forced upon a community; residents must be actively involved in planning, implementing, and evaluating their own health programs.
  • Health Promotion: Emphasis is placed on keeping people healthy through lifestyle changes, sanitation, and education, rather than merely treating illness after it occurs.
  • Appropriate Technology: Medical methods, equipment, and procedures must be scientifically sound, adaptable to local cultural values, and affordable for the community to maintain.

Clinical and Curative Nursing Duties in the PHC Clinic

Every day in a primary health care clinic begins with managing common, everyday ailments. Nurses assess, diagnose, and treat minor illnesses using standardized standing orders and treatment protocols. Common presentations include uncomplicated malaria, acute respiratory infections, simple skin infections, and mild watery diarrhea.

Effective curative care at this level relies on accurate triage. The nurse must quickly identify patients who are stable enough for routine treatment versus those requiring urgent referral to a general hospital. A common clinical mistake is failing to recognize early signs of severe dehydration in children or respiratory distress, treating them as routine cases instead of initiating emergency stabilization and prompt transport.

Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health Services

Safeguarding the health of mothers and children forms a massive portion of the primary care workload. Antenatal care clinics allow nurses to monitor pregnancies, detect hypertensive disorders early, provide iron and folate supplementation, and administer tetanus toxoid vaccines. Nurses also conduct normal deliveries in well-equipped primary health centers and provide immediate newborn care, including drying, warming, and initiating early breastfeeding.

Postnatal visits are equally vital for checking uterine involution, monitoring lochia, and assessing infant growth and development milestones. Immunization schedules are tracked meticulously during these visits. Health education during maternal clinics empowers women with knowledge regarding birth spacing, nutrition, and recognizing danger signs during pregnancy.

Disease Surveillance, Immunization, and Preventive Care

Preventing disease outbreaks is far more cost-effective than managing them after spread occurs. PHC nurses act as frontline detectives through disease surveillance, keeping a watchful eye out for priority diseases like measles, yellow fever, cholera, and acute flaccid paralysis. Any unusual spike in symptoms must be reported immediately to local health authorities to trigger a rapid public health response.

Routine immunization is another pillar of preventive nursing in Nigeria. Nurses manage cold chain storage to keep vaccines potent, organize outreach sessions to reach remote settlements, and educate hesitant caregivers. Health education on safe drinking water, proper waste disposal, and vector control further shields the community from preventable infectious diseases.

Community Mobilization, Health Education, and Advocacy

A successful PHC nurse engages the community beyond the walls of the clinic building. Community mobilization involves gathering local leaders, youth groups, and women's associations to discuss prevailing health challenges and organize collective action, such as clearing stagnant gutters or building local health posts.

Health education must be tailored to the cultural context and literacy level of the audience. Using local folklore, drama, songs, and visual aids helps communicate critical messages about nutrition, hygiene, and disease prevention. Advocacy involves speaking up on behalf of vulnerable community members to local government councils and traditional rulers to secure resources for clean water supplies, better roads to health facilities, and health insurance enrollment.

Leadership, Management, and Interprofessional Collaboration

Managing a primary health care facility involves administrative duties alongside clinical tasks. The nurse often acts as the facility manager, overseeing junior community health extension workers, managing drug inventories, maintaining facility records, and ensuring proper waste disposal. Effective leadership requires delegating tasks based on staff competency and maintaining staff morale despite difficult working conditions.

Collaboration is equally essential. PHC nurses work alongside community health extension workers, environmental health officers, traditional birth attendants, and local volunteer groups. Building strong working relationships with these diverse groups ensures a coordinated approach to community health, reducing duplication of effort and closing service gaps.

Challenges and Ethical Considerations for the PHC Nurse

Working in primary health care in Nigeria comes with significant operational hurdles. Nurses frequently face power outages, poor road infrastructure, lack of running water, delayed salaries, and severe shortages of essential medications and supplies. These constraints can lead to professional burnout and moral distress when nurses know what care a patient needs but lack the resources to provide it.

Ethical dilemmas also arise daily. Nurses must navigate patient confidentiality in tight-knit rural communities where gossip travels fast, respect patient autonomy when cultural beliefs clash with medical advice, and practice within their legal scope without abandoning patients. Maintaining professional integrity and compassion despite these systemic hardships remains one of the greatest triumphs of the primary health care nurse.
