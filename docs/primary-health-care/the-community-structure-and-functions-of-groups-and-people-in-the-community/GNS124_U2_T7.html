Functions and Responsibilities of the Nurse in the Primary Health Care Setting

Introduction to Primary Health Care (PHC) and the Nurse's Role

Primary Health Care (PHC) serves as the bedrock of the Nigerian health system, acting as the first level of contact individuals, families, and communities have with the national health service. In this foundational setting, the nurse functions not merely as a care provider, but as a community leader, educator, and frontline clinician. Nursing practice within Nigerian PHC aims to bring health care as close as possible to where people live and work, embodying the philosophy of health for all. The nurse operating at a Primary Health Care center must possess a diverse set of competencies ranging from acute clinical assessment to public health planning. Because PHC addresses the majority of a population's health needs throughout their lifespan, the nurse must navigate resource-limited environments while maintaining high standards of compassionate care. Responsibilities include assessing community health trends, delivering preventive interventions, managing common ailments, and referring complex cases to secondary or tertiary facilities. Understanding the broad scope of PHC empowers Nigerian nursing students to appreciate their vital position in national development, disease prevention, and health promotion. Ultimately, the PHC nurse bridges the gap between formal medical institutions and grassroots communities, ensuring that vulnerable populations receive continuous, culturally appropriate, and equitable health services.

Core Principles of PHC Guiding Nursing Practice in Nigeria

Nursing practice within the Nigerian Primary Health Care system is heavily anchored on core principles established by international declarations and adapted locally by the National Primary Health Care Development Agency (NPHCDA). These principles include equitable distribution of health resources, community participation, intersectoral coordination, appropriate technology, and a strong emphasis on health promotion and disease prevention. For the Nigerian nurse, equity means that essential health services must be accessible to all segments of the population regardless of socioeconomic status, ethnic background, or geographical location, paying special attention to rural and underserved communities. Community participation requires the nurse to actively involve local leaders, women's groups, and youth organizations in planning and implementing health programs, ensuring local ownership and sustainability. Appropriate technology guides the nurse to utilize affordable, scientifically sound, and culturally acceptable tools and methods for diagnosis and treatment. Intersectoral collaboration involves working alongside agriculture, education, water resources, and local government authorities to address the social determinants of health. By integrating these core principles into daily clinical and community routines, the nurse fosters an environment where health is recognized not as a privilege, but as a fundamental human right for every Nigerian citizen.

Clinical and Curative Nursing Duties in the PHC Clinic

Clinical and curative duties form a significant portion of the daily responsibilities of a nurse working in a PHC clinic in Nigeria. Patients presenting to the clinic often exhibit common tropical and communicable diseases such as malaria, upper respiratory tract infections, gastroenteritis, uncomplicated urinary tract infections, and minor trauma. The nurse utilizes standardized protocols, such as the Integrated Management of Childhood Illness (IMCI) and standard treatment guidelines, to conduct thorough physical assessments, take accurate medical histories, and provide prompt diagnosis and treatment. In settings where medical doctors are scarce or absent, PHC nurses are legally and professionally empowered to prescribe basic medications, dress wounds, administer intravenous or oral rehydration therapy, and monitor vital signs. Critical to this clinical role is the identification of danger signs that indicate severe illness requiring immediate referral to a secondary health facility. The nurse must maintain accurate clinical documentation, practice strict infection prevention and control measures, and manage clinic pharmacy supplies responsibly. Furthermore, the nurse uses every clinical encounter as an opportunity to provide individualized health counseling, ensuring that patients understand their treatment regimens, medication adherence, and follow-up schedules. Through competent clinical care, the PHC nurse builds community trust and reduces the burden on higher-level hospitals.

Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health Services

Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health (MNCH) services are paramount components of primary health care delivery in Nigeria, aiming to drastically reduce high maternal and infant mortality rates. The PHC nurse provides comprehensive antenatal care (ANC), which includes monitoring fetal development, screening for gestational hypertension and gestational diabetes, administering tetanus toxoid immunization, providing intermittent preventive treatment for malaria in pregnancy (IPTp), and supplying routine hematinics. During labor and delivery (where facilities permit), the nurse conducts clean and safe deliveries, performs active management of the third stage of labor to prevent postpartum hemorrhage, and provides immediate newborn care, including resuscitation when necessary. Postnatal care involves assessing the mother for postpartum complications, supporting exclusive breastfeeding, and monitoring infant growth and development milestones. The nurse also delivers comprehensive family planning counseling and services, offering various contraceptive options to help spacing births and protect maternal health. Immunization tracking, nutritional assessments, and the management of childhood malnutrition are routine responsibilities within child welfare clinics. By maintaining an empathetic, educational, and vigilant approach during these critical life stages, the PHC nurse directly safeguards the health of future generations.

Disease Surveillance, Immunization, and Preventive Care

Disease surveillance, immunization, and preventive care form the shield that protects Nigerian communities from epidemics and vaccine-preventable diseases. Under the Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) strategy, the PHC nurse is responsible for detecting, recording, and immediately reporting priority epidemic-prone diseases such as measles, cholera, yellow fever, and acute flaccid paralysis (AFP) to local government health authorities. Surveillance requires constant vigilance during daily clinic consultations and community outreach activities. Immunization services, delivered through routine clinics and outreach campaigns (such as polio eradication drives), are coordinated by the nurse to ensure high vaccine coverage. This involves maintaining the cold chain system to preserve vaccine potency, safely administering antigens, and educating caregivers on potential side effects and return dates. Beyond immunization, preventive care encompasses water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) education, screening for non-communicable diseases like hypertension and diabetes, and promoting safe dietary practices. The nurse also implements vector control measures, such as distributing insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) for malaria prevention. Through proactive disease surveillance and robust preventive health measures, the PHC nurse mitigates outbreaks before they overwhelm the entire health system.

Community Mobilization, Health Education, and Advocacy

Community mobilization, health education, and advocacy are powerful tools utilized by the PHC nurse to transform passive recipients of health care into active agents of change. Health education in the PHC setting is tailored to address local health beliefs, cultural practices, and prevailing morbidities, utilizing local languages and participatory methods such as market talks, home visits, and drama groups. Topics range from personal hygiene, nutrition, and family planning to the importance of completing immunization schedules. Community mobilization involves partnering with traditional rulers, religious leaders, youth groups, and women's associations to garner support for public health initiatives, such as building community health posts or organizing clean-up campaigns. Advocacy is directed at local government councils, policy makers, and stakeholders to secure adequate funding, procure essential drugs, and improve infrastructural amenities like electricity and potable water in the health facility's catchment area. By empowering communities with accurate health information and giving them a voice in health matters, the nurse fosters health literacy and self-reliance. This educational and collaborative approach ensures that health interventions outlast short-term project cycles and become embedded in community lifestyle and culture.

Leadership, Management, and Interprofessional Collaboration in PHC

Effective leadership, management, and interprofessional collaboration are indispensable competencies for the PHC nurse, who often coordinates day-to-day operations at the health facility. As a manager, the nurse oversees facility inventory, manages vaccine and drug stocks, supervises community health extension workers (CHEWs) and junior staff, ensures proper waste management, and maintains statistical records and health management information systems (HMIS) reports. Leadership entails inspiring staff, resolving conflicts equitably, fostering teamwork, and maintaining high morale despite challenging work environments. Furthermore, primary health care delivery is rarely a solitary endeavor; it requires seamless interprofessional collaboration among nurses, medical officers (where available), pharmacists, environmental health officers, laboratory technicians, and community volunteers. The nurse coordinates team meetings, delegates tasks according to individual scope of practice, and ensures clear communication channels to prevent gaps in patient care. Strong leadership and collaborative teamwork optimize resource utilization, reduce medical errors, and guarantee that patients receive holistic, coordinated, and continuous care across all service delivery points within the primary health care system.

Challenges and Ethical Considerations for the PHC Nurse in Nigeria

Working as a Primary Health Care nurse in Nigeria comes with a unique array of operational challenges and complex ethical considerations. Common challenges include severe infrastructural deficits, erratic power supply for cold chains, inadequate medical supplies, insecurity in rural regions, understaffing, and delayed remuneration, all of which can lead to occupational burnout. Ethically, nurses often encounter difficult dilemmas, such as balancing patient autonomy with cultural practices that oppose modern medical treatments (e.g., refusing blood transfusions or facility-based deliveries), managing confidentiality in tightly-knit rural communities, and dealing with scarce resources that force difficult triage decisions. The PHC nurse must navigate these situations by strictly adhering to the nursing code of ethics, upholding patient dignity, practicing cultural sensitivity without compromising safety, and advocating for social justice. Maintaining professional boundaries, obtaining informed consent despite language barriers, and reporting unsafe practices are vital ethical duties. By recognizing and preparing for these challenges during training, Nigerian nursing students can develop resilience, ethical fortitude, and problem-solving skills necessary to thrive and uphold professional integrity in primary health care delivery.
