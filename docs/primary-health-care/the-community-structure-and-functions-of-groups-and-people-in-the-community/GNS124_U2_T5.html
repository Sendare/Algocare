Roles of Primary Health Care Workers

Introduction to Primary Health Care Workforce in Nigeria

Primary health care (PHC) serves as the bedrock of the health system in Nigeria, bringing essential services directly to the grassroots. The workforce is diverse, bridging the gap between tertiary medical centers and rural communities where health facilities are often scarce. Every cadre within this system—from doctors to local volunteers—has specific training and statutory boundaries that dictate their daily duties.

Working at this level requires immense resourcefulness. Unlike urban teaching hospitals equipped with advanced technology, PHC workers frequently rely on clinical judgment, basic tools, and community mobilization. Understanding each worker's role prevents professional overlap and ensures patients receive safe, timely care.

Community Health Officers (CHOs) and Nurses

Community Health Officers (CHOs) and public health nurses form the administrative and clinical backbone of primary health centers. CHOs supervise the junior health workers, manage clinic operations, and handle complex primary care cases that exceed the scope of community health extension workers.

Public health nurses focus heavily on preventive care. Their daily duties include:

  • Immunization: Organizing and administering vaccines against childhood killer diseases.
  • Maternal tracking: Monitoring pregnant women and identifying high-risk pregnancies early.
  • Health education: Teaching communities about sanitation, nutrition, and disease prevention.

Midwives and Maternal Care

Midwives are the champions of safe motherhood in primary health care settings. In Nigeria, where maternal and neonatal mortality rates remain a public health concern, skilled birth attendants are vital. Midwives manage normal pregnancies, conduct deliveries, and provide immediate postnatal care to both mother and newborn.

A common misconception is that midwives only assist during active labor. In reality, their care spans the entire continuum: antenatal health talks, screening for complications like pre-eclampsia, facilitating immediate skin-to-skin contact after birth, and providing family planning counseling before discharge.

Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs)

Community Health Extension Workers, comprising both Senior (CHEWs) and Junior (JCHEWs) cadres, are specifically trained to bridge the gap between the health facility and the community. They form the largest percentage of paid workers in primary health centers.

  • Senior CHEWs: Capable of diagnosing and treating common ailments like malaria, managing minor injuries, providing family planning services, and conducting routine antenatal checks.
  • Junior CHEWs (JCHEWs): Work under direct supervision, focusing on basic home-visiting, health education, recording vital statistics, and treating very minor ailments under protocol.

Village Health Workers and Traditional Birth Attendants

At the most localized level, primary health care relies on volunteer village health workers (VHWs) and traditional birth attendants (TBAs). VHWs are chosen by their own communities to promote healthy behaviors, distribute basic first-aid supplies, and report disease outbreaks to the nearest health facility.

Traditional birth attendants have long assisted with deliveries in rural areas due to cultural preferences and distance from formal clinics. Modern primary health care strategy focuses on training TBAs to recognize danger signs and promptly refer laboring women to health facilities, rather than attempting high-risk deliveries at home.

Interprofessional Collaboration and Referral Systems

No single health worker can manage every community health need alone. Effective primary health care demands seamless interprofessional teamwork. A village health worker identifies a sick child, a CHEW provides initial malaria treatment, but if severe complications arise, the patient must be escalated up the system.

A functional referral system prevents bottlenecks and saves lives. PHC workers must recognize their professional limits and initiate prompt referrals to general hospitals or tertiary centers when clinical findings exceed primary care protocols. Clear communication and respectful collaboration among all cadres guarantee that patients experience continuity of care.
