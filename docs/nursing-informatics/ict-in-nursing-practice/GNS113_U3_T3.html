Workplace Ergonomics and Electronics Use

Introduction to Workplace Ergonomics in Nursing

Workplace ergonomics in nursing involves designing the healthcare environment, tools, and tasks to fit the physiological and psychological capabilities of the nursing workforce. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where nurse-to-patient ratios are often high and resources can be constrained, physical demands are exceptionally heavy. Nurses frequently perform manual patient transfers, push heavy medication trolleys, and spend prolonged hours documenting patient care on electronic health record (EHR) terminals. Ergonomics aims to reduce physical and mental strain, minimize the incidence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs), and enhance overall productivity and patient safety. By understanding the principles of ergonomics, nursing students and professionals can critically evaluate their clinical workspaces, advocate for safer equipment, and adopt posture-conscious work habits. Implementing ergonomic interventions not only protects the individual nurse from career-ending injuries but also ensures sustainable, high-quality healthcare delivery across diverse clinical environments in Nigeria, from bustling urban teaching hospitals to rural primary health care centers.

Anatomical and Physiological Impacts of Poor Posture

Poor posture during clinical duties and electronic documentation has immediate and long-term consequences on the anatomical and physiological structures of the human body. When a nurse slouches, cranes their neck forward to view a computer screen, or twists while lifting, abnormal biomechanical loads are placed on the spinal vertebrae, intervertebral discs, ligaments, and supporting musculature. Physiologically, sustained static postures restrict local blood circulation to muscles, leading to a rapid accumulation of lactic acid, localized muscle fatigue, microtrauma, and chronic pain. Over time, these repetitive strains can cause structural changes such as disc herniation, accelerated spinal degeneration, and chronic joint dysfunction. Furthermore, poor posture can negatively impact respiratory function by compressing the thoracic cavity, reducing lung expansion, and inducing fatigue that impairs cognitive focus and clinical decision-making. Recognizing these biomechanical and physiological impacts is essential for nursing students, as it highlights why maintaining neutral spinal alignments and utilizing proper body mechanics during every shift are vital for long-term health and professional longevity.

Musculoskeletal Disorders Common Among Healthcare Workers

Work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs) represent a major occupational health challenge for healthcare workers, particularly nurses. Common conditions include lower back pain, tension neck syndrome, rotator cuff tendinitis, carpal tunnel syndrome, and epicondylitis (tennis elbow). Lower back pain is frequently triggered by heavy patient lifting, bending, and twisting, while upper extremity disorders like carpal tunnel syndrome are often exacerbated by repetitive keyboard and mouse use during electronic health record charting. Nurses in Nigeria are particularly vulnerable due to infrastructural challenges, such as a lack of mechanical lift aids, adjustable beds, and ergonomic workstations, which force reliance on manual labor. Symptoms typically manifest gradually as localized aching, numbness, tingling, or stiffness at the end of a shift, which can progress to chronic, debilitating pain if unaddressed. Early recognition of WMSD symptoms, prompt reporting, and adherence to preventive ergonomic practices are crucial competencies for student nurses entering the clinical workforce to prevent permanent disability.

Ergonomic Setup for Computers and Electronic Health Record Terminals

As hospitals and clinics transition toward electronic health records (EHRs), nurses spend substantial portions of their shifts interacting with computers and terminals. Creating an ergonomic setup for these workstations is vital for preventing strain. The monitor should be positioned directly in front of the user, with the top of the screen at or slightly below eye level, and placed about an arm's length away to prevent neck flexion or extension. The keyboard and mouse should be located at a height that allows the elbows to be bent at a comfortable 90-to-100-degree angle, with wrists kept in a neutral, straight position rather than bent upward or downward. The chair must provide adequate lumbar support, and its height should be adjusted so that the nurse's feet rest flat on the floor, or on a footrest, with thighs parallel to the floor. Additionally, frequently used items—such as pens, documentation sheets, and barcode scanners—should be kept within easy reach to prevent excessive twisting or overreaching during fast-paced charting tasks.

Safe Handling and Use of Mobile Medical Devices

The proliferation of mobile medical devices—such as tablets, smartphones, barcode medication administration (BCMA) scanners, and mobile computing carts (COWs)—has revolutionized nursing workflow but introduced unique ergonomic hazards. Mobile devices are frequently held for prolonged periods in awkward hand grips, leading to hand fatigue, De Quervain's tenosynovitis, and neck strain caused by downward gazing ("text neck"). When pushing mobile carts, nurses must ensure the cart wheels are well-maintained, avoid overloading the cart with heavy supplies, and push rather than pull the cart whenever possible to maintain clear forward vision and use larger muscle groups. When using handheld tablets or smartphones for point-of-care documentation, nurses should support the device with both hands or use protective casing with integrated stands to minimize static muscle tension in the fingers and wrists. Furthermore, screens should be kept clean and free from smudges to prevent squinting, and lighting should be adjusted to minimize distracting glare while walking through busy clinical wards.

Visual Ergonomics and Prevention of Eye Strain from Screens

Visual ergonomics is a critical yet frequently overlooked component of nursing health, particularly given the shift toward digital charting and continuous monitor surveillance. Staring at electronic screens for hours can cause Computer Vision Syndrome (CVS) or digital eye strain, characterized by symptoms such as eye fatigue, dryness, blurred vision, headaches, and neck pain. To prevent eye strain, nurses should apply the 20-20-20 rule: every 20 minutes, look at an object at least 20 feet away for at least 20 seconds to relax the ciliary muscles of the eyes. Workstation lighting should also be optimized by positioning computer screens perpendicular to windows to avoid harsh glare from sunlight or overhead fluorescent lighting. Adjusting screen brightness to match the ambient room lighting, increasing font sizes for easier readability, and consciously blinking more frequently to maintain corneal lubrication are practical visual hygiene habits that protect ocular health during demanding shifts.

Practical Stretching and Micro-Break Strategies in Clinical Shifts

Clinical nursing shifts are often fast-paced and unpredictable, making formal, prolonged exercise breaks difficult to schedule. However, incorporating practical stretching and micro-break strategies into daily routines can significantly mitigate the physical toll of static postures and heavy lifting. Micro-breaks are short, 30-to-60-second pauses taken every hour during documentation tasks to stand up, roll the shoulders backward, gently stretch the neck from side to side, and flex the wrists and fingers. These brief interludes restore local blood flow, relieve muscle tension, and reset posture before fatigue sets in. In addition, nurses can perform dynamic stretches during shift handovers or while waiting for medication dispensing systems, targeting major muscle groups in the back, legs, and shoulders. Nursing students should cultivate these self-care habits early in their training, recognizing that taking a minute to stretch is not a waste of time, but a necessary investment in safety, alertness, and injury prevention.

Organizational Policies and Implementing Ergonomic Improvements

While individual ergonomic practices are essential, ensuring long-term health and safety requires robust organizational policies and institutional commitment. Healthcare facility management, nursing leadership, and occupational health departments in Nigeria must collaborate to establish comprehensive ergonomic programs. This includes conducting regular workstation assessments, investing in adjustable furniture, providing mechanical patient transfer devices, and ensuring proper maintenance of mobile computing carts. Furthermore, institutions should implement mandatory training on safe patient handling and electronics use during nursing orientation and continuous professional development (CPD) programs. Creating a supportive reporting culture where nurses can report faulty equipment or early musculoskeletal symptoms without fear of reprisal is equally important. When organizational policies actively prioritize ergonomics, absenteeism decreases, nurse retention improves, and patient care quality is safeguarded across the entire healthcare system.
