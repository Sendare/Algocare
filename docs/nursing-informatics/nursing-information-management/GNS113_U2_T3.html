Types of Electronic Health Records

Introduction to Electronic Health Records

An Electronic Health Record (EHR) is a digital version of a patient's paper chart. It contains medical history, diagnoses, medications, treatment plans, immunization dates, allergies, radiology images, and laboratory test results. Unlike old paper records, EHRs are designed to be shared across different healthcare settings, allowing multiple authorized providers to access real-time, patient-centered information instantly and securely.

For nurses, moving away from paper means fewer errors due to messy handwriting and faster access to critical vital signs or drug orders. The ultimate goal of any EHR system is to support safe, high-quality care by making accurate data available right at the bedside.

Acute Care versus Ambulatory EHR Systems

Healthcare environments vary widely, so electronic systems must match the clinical setting. Acute care EHRs are built for hospitals and emergency departments. They focus heavily on round-the-clock monitoring, rapid medication administration, surgical checklists, and minute-by-minute vital sign tracking.

In contrast, ambulatory EHRs serve outpatient clinics, physician offices, and community health centers. Their design prioritizes scheduled appointments, long-term chronic disease tracking, routine preventive screenings, and prescription refill histories. A nurse working in a busy surgical ward uses a very different screen layout and workflow than a nurse working in a maternal health clinic.

Enterprise and Specialty EHR Solutions

Large healthcare networks often use an Enterprise EHR. This single, massive software system connects every department across multiple hospitals and clinics. A patient can visit an urgent care center in one city, be admitted to a regional hospital in another, and see a specialist downtown, while all their doctors and nurses view the exact same integrated record.

However, some medical fields need customized tools. Specialty-specific EHRs are tailored for unique practices like pediatrics, behavioral health, or dentistry. For instance, a behavioral health EHR includes specialized templates for mental status exams and strict privacy safeguards, while a pediatric EHR tracks growth percentiles and immunization schedules closely.

Personal Health Records and Patient Portals

  • Patient Portals: Secure, online websites connected to the hospital's EHR that allow patients to view lab results, request medication refills, and message their care team.
  • Personal Health Records (PHRs): Standalone health logs managed entirely by the patient, often stored on a smartphone or personal computer, where they track their own blood pressure, diet, and symptoms.

Both tools encourage active patient participation. Nurses frequently teach patients how to navigate these digital tools so they can manage their chronic conditions better at home.

Infrastructure: Cloud-Based versus On-Premise EHR

The underlying technology powering an EHR affects reliability and cost. On-premise systems store all data on physical servers housed inside the hospital building. The facility's own IT department maintains the hardware and software.

Cloud-based systems store data on remote, secure servers managed by an external vendor, accessed via the internet. Cloud systems often allow faster updates and easier remote access for mobile nurses, but they depend entirely on stable internet connections and robust cybersecurity measures.

Interoperability and Nursing Implications

Interoperability is the ability of different health information systems to communicate and exchange data accurately. When systems lack interoperability, a nurse in an emergency room cannot see records from a clinic just down the street, creating dangerous information gaps.

For nurses, seamless EHR types and good interoperability mean less time hunting down old paper charts or calling clinics for lab results. Accurate, shared data directly improves patient safety by preventing duplicate testing, catching drug allergies early, and ensuring continuity of care during patient transfers.
