Uterine Fibroids

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Uterine Fibroids

Uterine fibroids, scientifically termed **leiomyomas** or **myomas**, represent the most frequent benign monoclonal tumors arising from the smooth muscle cells of the myometrium, interspersed with an abundant extracellular matrix. Though non-cancerous, they heavily impact gynecological health. Hormonal stimulation acts as a primary catalyst; both estrogen and progesterone heavily promote cellular proliferation within the myometrium during reproductive years. Consequently, these growths typically regress following menopause when ovarian hormone production declines. Nigerian clinical settings frequently record presentations among women of African descent at a younger average age, accompanied by larger tumor burdens that demand vigilant monitoring.

Anatomical Classifications of Myomas

The anatomical location of a fibroid dictates its clinical presentation and the specific surgical approach required for removal. Classification relies heavily on the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) sub-mucosal to sub-serosal system. - **Intramural**: Tumors located entirely within the muscular wall of the uterus, representing the most common variant. - **Submucosal**: Growths situated just beneath the endometrium, often protruding directly into the uterine cavity and causing heavy bleeding. - **Subserosal**: Fibroids expanding outward beneath the outer peritoneal covering of the uterus, sometimes developing on a stalk known as pedunculated. - **Cervical**: Rare instances where the myoma develops within the cervical tissue rather than the uterine corpus.

Etiology and Risk Factors

The exact trigger for the initial genetic mutation in a single myometrium smooth muscle cell remains under investigation, but several established risk factors accelerate tumor development. - **Genetics**: A positive family history significantly increases susceptibility, indicating a strong hereditary predisposition. - **Race**: Women of African descent experience a disproportionately higher incidence, earlier onset, and faster growth rate. - **Age**: Risk increases steadily throughout the reproductive years, peaking during the fourth and fifth decades of life. - **Reproductive History**: Nulliparity and early menarche expose tissues to prolonged, uninterrupted cycles of estrogen and progesterone stimulation.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Presentation

Many women harbor fibroids asymptomatically, discovering them incidentally during routine pelvic scans. When symptoms do appear, they vary widely according to tumor size, number, and precise anatomical location. - **Menorrhagia**: Excessively heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding, frequently leading to iron deficiency anemia. - **Pelvic Pressure**: A sensation of heaviness, lower back pain, or bloating caused by physical mass effect on neighboring pelvic organs. - **Urinary Frequency**: Compressive pressure exerted on the bladder wall, diminishing its functional capacity. - **Dysmenorrhea**: Severe menstrual cramping resulting from intense uterine contractions attempting to expel submucosal lesions.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Nursing Assessment

Confirming a diagnosis of uterine fibroids requires a systematic clinical approach combining thorough history-taking, physical examination, and advanced imaging. Abdominal and transvaginal **pelvic ultrasound** serves as the primary, highly accessible diagnostic imaging tool, delineating the size, number, and location of myomas. Magnetic resonance imaging provides superior anatomical mapping when surgical planning is complex. During the nursing assessment, evaluating the patient's hemoglobin and hematocrit levels is critical, as chronic heavy bleeding frequently induces iron-deficiency anemia, manifesting as pallor, fatigue, and tachycardia.

Medical and Surgical Management Strategies

Management strategies range from conservative pharmacological therapy to definitive surgical intervention, tailored to the patient's age, symptom severity, and desire for future fertility. Medical options aim to reduce bleeding and shrink tumor volume. **Gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonists** induce a temporary hypoestrogenic state, shrinking the myomas prior to surgery. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and tranexamic acid help control heavy bleeding during menses. Surgical choices depend on reproductive goals. **Myomectomy** preserves the uterus and is favored by women wishing to conceive. Conversely, **hysterectomy** remains the definitive, permanent cure for symptomatic fibroids when childbearing is complete, performed via abdominal, laparoscopic, or vaginal routes.

Complications and Impact on Fertility

While many fibroids remain harmless, certain types and sizes lead to significant physical complications. Submucosal and large intramural lesions distort the endometrial cavity, impairing embryo implantation and contributing to **infertility** or recurrent pregnancy loss. Obstetric complications during pregnancy include placental abruption, fetal malpresentation, and preterm labor due to limited intrauterine space. Furthermore, acute degeneration can occur when a rapidly growing fibroid outgrows its blood supply, triggering severe localized ischemic pain, low-grade fever, and localized tenderness that requires careful differential diagnosis.
