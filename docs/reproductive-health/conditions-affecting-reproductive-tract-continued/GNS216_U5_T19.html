Uterine Abnormalities

Embryology and Congenital Uterine Malformations

Congenital uterine anomalies arise from defects during embryogenesis, specifically involving the **müllerian ducts** (paramesonephric ducts). Normally, these paired ducts undergo a three-phase process: organogenesis, fusion, and septal resorption. Disruption at any stage leads to distinct structural variations classified by the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) system. - **Agenesis/Hypoplasia (Class I)**: Complete or partial failure of müllerian duct development, resulting in vaginal and uterine absence, clinically known as Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser syndrome. - **Unicornuate Uterus (Class II)**: Arrested development of one duct while the contralateral side develops normally, producing a banana-shaped single-horned uterine cavity. - **Uterine Didelphys (Class III)**: Complete failure of fusion between the two müllerian ducts, resulting in dual uteri, dual cervices, and often a longitudinal vaginal septum. - **Bicornuate Uterus (Class IV)**: Partial fusion of the upper müllerian ducts, creating a heart-shaped uterine cavity with a deep fundal indentation. - **Septate Uterus (Class V)**: Complete fusion of the ducts with failure of the central septum to resorb, dividing the cavity symmetrically while maintaining a normal external contour. - **Arcuate Uterus (Class VI)**: Near-complete resorption of the internal septum, leaving a mild, broad indentation at the uterine fundus. In Nigerian gynecological practice, congenital anomalies frequently present late, often only diagnosed during investigations for primary amenorrhea, recurrent pregnancy loss, or infertility.

Acquired Structural Abnormalities

Unlike congenital malformations present from birth, acquired structural abnormalities develop over time due to hormonal influences, infections, surgical trauma, or neoplastic growth. These alterations modify the normal endometrial cavity or myometrial architecture, disrupting reproductive and menstrual function. - **Uterine Fibroids (Leiomyomas)**: Benign monoclonal tumors of smooth muscle cells categorized by anatomical location: **submucosal** (encroaching on the cavity), **intramural** (within the myometrial wall), and **subserosal** (projecting outward). - **Endometrial Polyps**: Hyperplastic overgrowths of endometrial glands and stroma forming projections into the uterine cavity, often driven by estrogen stimulation. - **Adenomyosis**: The presence of ectopic endometrial glands and stroma deep within the myometrium, causing diffuse uterine enlargement and hypertrophic muscular changes. - **Intrauterine Adhesions (Asherman Syndrome)**: Fibrous scar bands bridging the uterine walls, typically resulting from trauma to the basal endometrial layer via postpartum curettage or intrauterine surgery. Uterine fibroids are exceptionally prevalent among women of African descent, frequently presenting with heavy menstrual bleeding and bulk-related pelvic pressure symptoms.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

The clinical presentation of uterine abnormalities depends on whether the lesion alters endometrial surface area, compromises myometrial contractility, or obstructs outflow tracts. Distortions of the endometrial cavity, particularly submucosal fibroids or polyps, increase the vascular surface area and disrupt normal local hemostasis, leading to **menorrhagia** (heavy menstrual bleeding) or intermenstrual spotting. Myometrial lesions like adenomyosis or large intramural fibroids prevent effective uterine muscle fiber contraction during menstruation, causing severe **dysmenorrhea** and pelvic pressure. Obstructive anomalies, such as a transverse vaginal septum or obstructed uterine horn, trap menstrual blood, triggering cyclical pelvic pain without external bleeding (**cryptomenorrhea**). In pregnancy, structural constraints prevent normal expansion or placental implantation, manifesting clinically as recurrent pregnancy loss, fetal malpresentation, or preterm labor.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging Techniques

Diagnosing uterine abnormalities requires a combination of clinical assessment and advanced imaging modalities to map the exact location, size, and architectural impact of the lesion. - **Transvaginal Ultrasonography (TVUS)**: The primary screening tool for assessing myometrial texture, endometrial thickness, and identifying fibroids, polyps, or adenomyosis. - **Saline Infusion Sonohysterography (SIS)**: Instillation of sterile saline into the uterine cavity during ultrasound to enhance visualization of intracavitary lesions like submucosal fibroids or adhesions. - **Hysterosalpingography (HSG)**: A fluoroscopic procedure using radiopaque contrast to outline the uterine cavity and assess fallopian tube patency, crucial in infertility evaluations. - **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)**: The gold standard for mapping complex congenital malformations and differentiating leiomyomas from adenomyosis when ultrasound is inconclusive. - **Hysteroscopy**: Direct visualization of the uterine cavity using a fiberoptic scope, allowing simultaneous diagnostic confirmation and operative intervention.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management strategies for uterine abnormalities are guided by symptom severity, patient age, and future reproductive desires. Asymptomatic abnormalities often require only routine surveillance. Medical management focuses on controlling bleeding and pain using **nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)**, **tranexamic acid**, and **hormonal therapy** such as combined oral contraceptives, progestins, or gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists to reduce fibroid volume or suppress endometrial proliferation. Surgical intervention is reserved for patients with intractable symptoms or reproductive failure. **Hysteroscopic resection** removes intracavitary polyps, submucosal fibroids, or intrauterine adhesions. **Myomectomy** preserves the uterus and fertility by excising fibroids via laparoscopy, laparotomy, or hysteroscopy. **Hysterectomy** serves as the definitive surgical treatment for benign disease when childbearing is complete.

Impact on Fertility and Pregnancy Outcomes

Uterine abnormalities significantly influence reproductive success, acting as mechanical barriers to conception or successful gestation. Intracavitary lesions like large submucosal fibroids, endometrial polyps, and severe intrauterine adhesions act as natural intrauterine contraceptive devices, impairing embryo implantation. Congenital anomalies restrict uterine growth and distension; a unicornuate or bicornuate uterus limits fetal expansion, frequently resulting in **intrauterine growth restriction**, **malpresentation** (such as breech presentation), and a high rate of **preterm delivery**. Furthermore, a incompetent or structurally rigid cervix associated with müllerian defects predisposes patients to mid-trimester pregnancy loss and cervical insufficiency.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nursing management for patients with uterine abnormalities centers on holistic care, symptom monitoring, and facilitating informed decision-making throughout diagnostic and therapeutic journeys. Nurses monitor vital signs and assess blood loss in patients presenting with severe menorrhagia, watching closely for signs of iron-deficiency anemia such as pallor, fatigue, and tachycardia. Preoperative and postoperative care for surgical procedures like myomectomy or hysteroscopy involves pain management, monitoring for hemorrhage or visceral injury, and enforcing early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis. Education focuses on explaining diagnostic imaging procedures, clarifying the difference between benign fibroids and malignant neoplasms to alleviate anxiety, and counseling women on how structural abnormalities may influence future contraceptive choices and pregnancy planning.
