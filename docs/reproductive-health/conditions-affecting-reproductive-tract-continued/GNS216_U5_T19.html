Uterine Abnormalities

Introduction and Congenital Uterine Malformations

Uterine abnormalities can be present from birth due to improper development in the womb, or they can develop later in life from various causes. Congenital uterine malformations occur when the Müllerian ducts (paramesonephric ducts), which form the female reproductive tract during embryonic life, fail to develop, fuse properly, or reabsorb the dividing wall between them.

Common types of congenital anomalies include:

  • Uterine Didelphys: Complete failure of duct fusion, resulting in two separate uterine bodies and sometimes two cervices or vaginas.
  • Bicornuate Uterus: Partial fusion of the upper ducts, creating a heart-shaped uterus with two distinct horns.
  • Septate Uterus: Normal outer contour of the uterus, but the internal dividing wall (septum) fails to dissolve, splitting the cavity.
  • Unicornuate Uterus: Only one Müllerian duct develops, forming a banana-shaped uterus with a single fallopian tube.
  • Agenesis (Mayer-Rokitansky-Küster-Hauser syndrome): Complete failure of the uterus and upper vagina to develop.

Many women with these structural variations live normal lives without knowing they have them until they try to conceive. However, others face severe reproductive challenges depending on the specific defect.

Acquired Structural Abnormalities

Unlike congenital malformations, acquired uterine abnormalities develop after normal embryogenesis. These structural changes often disrupt the normal lining or muscular wall of the uterine cavity, leading to bleeding changes or fertility issues.

  1. Uterine Fibroids (Leiomyomas): Benign smooth muscle tumors growing in the wall of the uterus. They can sit inside the cavity (submucosal), within the wall (intramural), or on the outer surface (subserosal).
  2. Endometrial Polyps: Overgrowths of cells in the inner lining (endometrium) of the uterus that attach by a stalk.
  3. Uterine Adhesions (Asherman's Syndrome): Scar tissue inside the uterine cavity, usually caused by past surgeries like dilation and curettage (D&C) or severe uterine infections.
  4. Adenomyosis: A condition where the inner lining tissue breaks through the muscular wall of the uterus, making the organ boggy, enlarged, and painful.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

The physical presence of a uterine abnormality alters the normal anatomy and physiology of the pelvis. When the endometrial cavity is distorted by fibroids, polyps, or a septum, the normal shedding and repair of the uterine lining during menstruation is disrupted.

Patients frequently report heavy, prolonged menstrual bleeding, medically termed menorrhagia, which can lead to iron-deficiency anemia. If scars or fibroids block the lower uterine segment or cervix, menstrual blood may become trapped, causing severe cramping. Structural defects also interfere with the normal contractions of the myometrium, impairing the ability of a fertilized egg to implant securely or causing the expanding uterus to irritate surrounding pelvic organs, resulting in chronic pelvic pressure and painful intercourse.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging Techniques

Accurate diagnosis requires visualizing both the outside contour of the uterus and the inside of the cavity. Clinicians use a step-by-step approach starting with non-invasive imaging.

  • Transvaginal Ultrasound (TVUS): The primary screening tool. It clearly shows the thickness of the endometrium, identifies fibroids, and outlines the overall shape of the uterus.
  • Hysterosalpingography (HSG): An X-ray procedure where contrast dye is injected through the cervix to outline the shape of the uterine cavity and check if the fallopian tubes are open.
  • Saline Infusion Sonohysterography (SIS): Sterile saline is placed inside the cavity during an ultrasound to create a high-contrast view of polyps or small submucosal fibroids.
  • Hysteroscopy: A small lighted camera is inserted through the cervix directly into the uterus, allowing the doctor to see inside and take a small tissue sample at the same time.

Impact on Fertility and Pregnancy Outcomes

Uterine abnormalities can profoundly affect a woman's reproductive journey. While many women achieve healthy pregnancies without intervention, certain structural flaws create mechanical barriers to conception and normal fetal growth.

A distorted uterine cavity prevents a fertilized egg from finding a smooth, well-vascularized surface for implantation. If pregnancy does occur, abnormalities like a septate or bicornuate uterus reduce the available space for the growing fetus, frequently leading to recurrent pregnancy loss in the first or second trimester, or preterm labor. Furthermore, fibroids or abnormal uterine walls can cause the placenta to implant improperly, increasing the risk of abnormal bleeding or malpresentation at birth.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment depends entirely on the type of abnormality, the severity of symptoms, and the patient's desire for future children. Asymptomatic abnormalities usually require no treatment at all.

  • Medical Therapy: Hormonal medications such as oral contraceptive pills, progestins, or GnRH agonists are used to control heavy bleeding and shrink fibroids temporarily, but they do not cure structural defects.
  • Hysteroscopic Surgery: Minimally invasive procedures where instruments are passed through the cervix to remove polyps, fibroids inside the cavity, or cut away a uterine septum.
  • Laparoscopic or Open Surgery: Performed for large fibroids on the outer wall (myomectomy) or to repair severe structural defects like a bicornuate uterus (metioplasty), though this is done rarely.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nurses play a vital role in supporting patients dealing with uterine abnormalities, especially because these conditions often touch on sensitive topics of fertility, body image, and chronic pain.

Care begins with a thorough health history assessing menstrual patterns, pain levels, and pregnancy goals. Nurses provide emotional support for patients experiencing grief from pregnancy losses or anxiety regarding surgical procedures. Educational efforts focus on explaining diagnostic tests, preparing patients for pre- and post-operative routines, and teaching them to monitor for warning signs like excessive bleeding or fever after surgery. Ensuring the patient understands their specific diagnosis helps empower them to make informed choices about their reproductive health.
