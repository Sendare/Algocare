Ovarian Cancer

Anatomy and Overview of Ovarian Cancer

The ovaries are a pair of small, almond-shaped organs located in the lower pelvis, one on each side of the uterus. Their primary job is to produce eggs for reproduction and to release female hormones like estrogen and progesterone. Ovarian cancer begins when abnormal cells in these tissues start to grow out of control, forming a tumor. Because the ovaries sit deep within the pelvic cavity, these early tumors can grow quite large without pressing against other organs, which is a major reason why the disease is rarely caught in its earliest stages.

Types and Pathophysiology of Ovarian Tumors

Ovarian cancer is not just one disease; it is categorized into three main types based on the specific cell of origin within the ovary:

  • Epithelial ovarian cancer: This is the most common form, accounting for about 90% of cases. It arises from the surface epithelium, the thin layer of tissue covering the outside of the ovaries.
  • Stromal tumors: These develop in the structural hormone-producing cells of the ovary and often present with abnormal hormone production, such as excess estrogen.
  • Germ cell tumors: These originate from the cells that develop into eggs. They are rare and occur mostly in younger women and adolescents.

Risk Factors and Etiology

Several factors increase a woman's chance of developing ovarian cancer. Genetics play a massive role, particularly inherited mutations in the BRCA1 or BRCA2 genes. Other strong risk factors include a family history of ovarian or breast cancer, increasing age, and never having given birth, known as nulliparity. Conversely, factors that reduce the total number of lifetime ovulations—such as long-term use of oral contraceptive pills, multiple pregnancies, and breastfeeding—tend to offer a protective effect against epithelial ovarian cancer.

Clinical Manifestations

Early ovarian cancer is notoriously silent, but as the tumor grows or spreads, vague symptoms emerge. A common clinical misconception is that ovarian cancer causes sharp, severe pelvic pain right away. Instead, patients typically report persistent, subtle gastrointestinal and pelvic complaints.

Key warning signs include:

  • Bloating or a swollen abdomen
  • Feeling full quickly when eating
  • Pelvic or abdominal pain
  • Urinary urgency or frequency

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosing ovarian cancer requires a combination of clinical exams and imaging. A physical examination includes a thorough bimanual pelvic exam to check for adnexal masses. Imaging studies like a transvaginal ultrasound or a pelvic CT scan help visualize the size and texture of the ovaries. Blood tests often include checking for the CA-125 tumor marker, though this marker can also be elevated in benign conditions like endometriosis or fibroids, meaning it is not a standalone screening tool for the general public.

Staging and Grading

Once ovarian cancer is diagnosed, staging determines how far the disease has spread throughout the body, while grading describes how abnormal the cancer cells look under a microscope. Staging is typically surgical, relying on findings during an exploratory laparotomy or laparoscopy. Stage I is confined strictly to the ovaries, Stage II involves one or both ovaries with pelvic extension, Stage III spreads beyond the pelvis into the abdomen or retroperitoneal lymph nodes, and Stage IV indicates distant metastasis, such as to the liver parenchyma or pleural cavity.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management of ovarian cancer usually involves a combination of aggressive surgery and chemotherapy. The primary surgical goal is optimal cytoreduction, often called debulking, where the surgeon removes as much visible tumor tissue as possible from the abdomen and pelvis. This often includes a total hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, omentectomy, and removal of involved lymph nodes. Following surgery, platinum-based chemotherapy regimens, such as carboplatin combined with paclitaxel, are typically administered to destroy remaining microscopic cancer cells.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nursing care for a patient with ovarian cancer centers on managing surgical recovery, alleviating chemotherapy side effects, and offering holistic support. Nurses must monitor for post-operative complications like hemorrhage, infection, or bowel obstruction. During chemotherapy, care shifts toward managing nausea, fatigue, hair loss, and the risk of neutropenia. Nurses also play a vital role in educating patients and their families about signs of infection, medication schedules, and nutritional support to maintain strength during treatment.
