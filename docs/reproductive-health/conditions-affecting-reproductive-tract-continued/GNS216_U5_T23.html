Cancer of the Uterus

Introduction and Overview of Uterine Cancer

Uterine cancer refers to any malignancy that arises from the tissues of the uterus. The vast majority of these cancers develop in the inner lining, known as the endometrium. Endometrial cancer is one of the most common gynecological malignancies, particularly affecting women after menopause. Early detection usually leads to a very favorable prognosis because the disease often produces symptoms before it spreads far beyond the pelvic cavity.

Types and Classifications of Uterine Cancer

  • Endometrial Adenocarcinoma: Accounts for over 80% of all uterine cancers, originating in the glandular cells of the endometrial lining.
  • Uterine Sarcoma: A much rarer and more aggressive form that develops in the muscles or supporting connective tissues of the myometrium.
  • Type I Tumors: Generally estrogen-dependent, low-grade, and carry a more favorable prognosis.
  • Type II Tumors: Estrogen-independent, high-grade, tend to be more aggressive, and include serous and clear cell carcinomas.

Risk Factors and Etiology

The development of uterine cancer is strongly linked to prolonged exposure to estrogen without the balancing effect of progesterone. Factors that increase this hormonal exposure include obesity, tamoxifen use, late menopause, and nulliparity. Diabetes mellitus and hypertension also independently raise a woman's risk. Conversely, factors that reduce estrogen exposure or shed the endometrium regularly, such as combination oral contraceptives, tend to be protective.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

The hallmark symptom of uterine cancer is abnormal uterine bleeding, particularly any postmenopausal bleeding. Even a small spot of blood after periods have stopped completely warrants an immediate workup. Other signs may include persistent pelvic pain, cramping, or abnormal watery, blood-tinged vaginal discharge. During assessment, nurses must take a detailed menstrual and medication history.

Diagnostic Investigations

Diagnosing uterine cancer involves visualizing the tissue and obtaining a sample for pathology. Transvaginal ultrasound is often the first imaging step to measure endometrial thickness. Final confirmation requires an endometrial biopsy or hysteroscopy with dilation and curettage (D&C) to examine cellular changes under a microscope.

Medical and Surgical Management

Total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy is the primary treatment for most stages of uterine cancer. This surgical removal of the uterus, fallopian tubes, and ovaries may be followed by radiation therapy or chemotherapy if the cancer has spread or carries high-risk features. Hormone therapy can also be used for advanced or recurrent disease in select patients.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nursing care focuses on surgical recovery, pain management, monitoring for complications like hemorrhage or infection, and providing emotional support. Patient education should cover activity restrictions, signs of wound infection, and managing side effects of adjuvant therapies. Nurses also provide a compassionate space for patients to discuss body image concerns following a hysterectomy.
