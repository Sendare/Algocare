Ovarian Cyst

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Ovarian Cysts

An ovarian cyst is a fluid-filled sac or pocket located on or within an ovary. The female reproductive system relies heavily on two ovaries, which store eggs and produce the hormones estrogen and progesterone. Most ovarian cysts form naturally during the menstrual cycle and are entirely harmless. These are called functional cysts. They develop when a follicle fails to rupture and release an egg, or when a follicle seals back up after releasing its egg and swells with fluid.

While the vast majority of these structures disappear on their own within a few months without medical intervention, understanding the underlying mechanisms helps nurses ease patient anxiety. A common clinical misconception is that every pelvic mass on an ovary is cancerous; however, simple functional cysts are benign. Hormonal fluctuations, early menstruation, or fertility treatments can increase the likelihood of these fluid collections forming.

Types of Ovarian Cysts

Ovarian cysts are broadly categorized into functional types and pathological types. Recognizing the specific type helps guide treatment.

  • Follicular cyst: Occurs when the ovarian follicle fails to rupture and release an ovum during ovulation, continuing to grow instead.
  • Corpus luteum cyst: Develops when the follicular space seals after releasing an egg and accumulates fluid or blood inside.
  • Dermoid cyst (Teratoma): A benign tumor containing embryonic tissue types, which can include hair, skin, or teeth.
  • Cystadenoma: Develops from ovarian surface cells and is filled with a watery or mucous material.
  • Endometrioma: Formed when endometrial-like tissue grows outside the uterus and attaches to the ovary.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Many women with ovarian cysts experience no symptoms at all, discovering the mass purely by chance during a routine pelvic ultrasound. When symptoms do appear, they typically stem from the size of the cyst, rapid growth, or bleeding into the sac.

Patients often report a dull, heavy ache in the lower abdomen or pelvis. They may feel a sense of pressure or fullness in the abdomen, along with bloating. Pain during sexual intercourse, medically known as dyspareunia, can also occur if the cyst is positioned near the cul-de-sac. Bowel and bladder habits might change if a large mass presses against the rectum or urinary bladder, leading to frequent urination or constipation.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Nursing Assessment

A thorough diagnostic workup is essential to determine the nature of an ovarian mass and rule out malignancy or surgical emergencies. Nurses play a vital role in preparing patients for these diagnostic procedures and gathering a detailed health history.

A pelvic examination allows the healthcare provider to feel for any swelling or lumps on the ovaries. Transvaginal or pelvic ultrasound serves as the gold standard imaging tool, providing clear pictures of the cyst's size, shape, and internal composition—whether it is fluid-filled or solid. Blood tests, such as CA-125, may be ordered, especially in postmenopausal women, to screen for ovarian cancer, though elevated levels can also occur with benign conditions like endometriosis or fibroids.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management strategies range from watchful waiting to surgical removal, depending on the patient's age, symptom severity, cyst size, and ultrasound appearance.

For premenopausal women with small, simple cysts, conservative management is standard. This involves regular follow-up scans over two to three months to ensure the cyst resolves spontaneously. Hormonal contraceptives, such as combination birth control pills, are sometimes prescribed to prevent new functional cysts from forming, though they do not shrink existing ones.

Surgical intervention becomes necessary if the cyst is large, causes persistent pain, grows over several cycles, or has suspicious features on imaging. A laparoscopic cystectomy removes only the cyst while preserving the healthy ovarian tissue, which is vital for fertility. In more severe cases, an oophorectomy may be performed to remove the entire ovary.

Potential Complications and Emergency Care

Although most ovarian cysts are harmless and resolve independently, certain situations demand immediate medical attention. Two primary complications require emergency surgical evaluation: ovarian torsion and cyst rupture.

Ovarian torsion occurs when a heavy cyst causes the ovary to twist around its supporting ligaments. This twisting cuts off the blood supply to the ovary, causing sudden, excruciating lower abdominal pain, often accompanied by nausea and vomiting. If not treated quickly, the ovarian tissue will die. Cyst rupture happens when the wall of the cyst breaks open, spilling its contents into the pelvic cavity. While minor ruptures cause mild discomfort, a major rupture can cause sudden, sharp pain and internal bleeding that leads to hemodynamic instability.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with ovarian cysts focuses on pain management, emotional reassurance, and preparing the patient for diagnostics or surgery. Nurses must assess pain levels regularly using a standardized pain scale and administer prescribed analgesics promptly.

Discharge teaching is a critical component of recovery, especially for patients managed conservatively or post-surgery. The nurse should instruct the patient to track their menstrual cycles and report any abnormal bleeding or worsening pain immediately. Patients must be taught the warning signs of complications, such as a fever, dizziness, or severe abdominal pain that does not respond to medication. Providing clear guidance helps reduce anxiety and ensures timely intervention if an emergency arises.
