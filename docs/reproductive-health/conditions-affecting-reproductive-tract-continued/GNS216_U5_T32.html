Ovarian Cyst

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Ovarian Cysts

An ovarian cyst is a fluid-filled sac or pouch that develops within or on the surface of an ovary. The female reproductive system features two almond-shaped ovaries responsible for producing ova and secreting sex hormones like estrogen and progesterone. During each menstrual cycle, follicles mature inside the ovarian cortex to release an egg. When these follicles fail to rupture or reabsorb fluid properly after release, fluid accumulates, forming a cyst. Most cysts are benign, asymptomatic, and resolve spontaneously without medical intervention. However, understanding their structural classification helps differentiate normal cyclic changes from abnormal neoplastic growths that require active clinical management.

Classification of Functional and Pathological Cysts

Ovarian cysts are broadly categorized into two main groups based on their origin and hormonal dependence:

  • Follicular Cyst: Occurs when the ovarian follicle fails to rupture during ovulation and continues to grow, collecting fluid inside.
  • Corpus Luteum Cyst: Forms when the corpus luteum seals after releasing an egg and accumulates fluid or blood within its center.
  • Dermoid Cyst (Teratoma): A benign germ cell tumor containing embryonic tissue types such as hair, skin, teeth, and sebum.
  • Endometrioma: A cyst formed when endometrial-like tissue implants and grows within the ovaries, often filled with dark, old blood.
  • Cystadenoma: Benign epithelial tumors that develop from the outer surface cells of the ovary, containing watery or mucous fluid.

Clinical Manifestations and Diagnostic Evaluation

Many small ovarian cysts remain silent, discovered incidentally during routine pelvic examinations or ultrasound imaging. When symptoms do appear, they typically stem from cyst enlargement, pressure on adjacent pelvic organs, or hemorrhage. Common clinical signs include dull pelvic aching, lower back pain, dyspareunia, and urinary frequency due to bladder compression.

Diagnostic confirmation relies on a combination of clinical history and imaging. A bimanual pelvic examination may reveal an adnexal mass. Pelvic ultrasonography—particularly transvaginal ultrasound—is the gold standard for visualizing cyst architecture, size, and composition (solid versus cystic). Serum markers such as CA-125 may be ordered, especially in postmenopausal women, to help rule out malignancy, though elevated levels can also occur with benign conditions like endometriosis or pelvic inflammatory disease.

Medical and Surgical Management Strategies

Management approaches vary according to cyst size, patient age, menopausal status, and reported symptoms. Functional cysts often resolve independently over two to three menstrual cycles; watchful waiting with serial ultrasound monitoring is standard for small, asymptomatic lesions. Oral contraceptive pills may be prescribed to suppress ovulation and prevent new functional cyst formation, though they do not shrink existing cysts.

Surgical intervention becomes necessary when cysts persist, cause severe pain, grow larger than seven centimeters, or present features suggestive of malignancy. Laparoscopy is preferred for benign cystectomy, preserving ovarian function, whereas laparotomy or oophorectomy may be indicated for large masses or postmenopausal women where cancer risk is elevated.

Complications: Ovarian Torsion and Cyst Rupture

While many ovarian cysts remain benign, acute complications can precipitate surgical emergencies. Ovarian torsion occurs when a cyst causes the ovary to twist upon its vascular pedicle, obstructing arterial blood flow and leading rapidly to ischemic necrosis. This presents as sudden, severe, unilateral lower abdominal pain frequently accompanied by nausea and vomiting.

Cyst rupture involves the bursting of the cyst wall, releasing its contents into the peritoneal cavity. This can cause acute, sharp pelvic pain. While small functional cysts spilling serous fluid cause mild, self-limiting irritation, the rupture of a dermoid or hemorrhagic cyst can lead to significant internal bleeding and peritoneal inflammation, requiring prompt hemodynamic assessment and potential surgical hemostasis.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with ovarian cysts focuses on pain management, psychological support, and thorough patient education. Nurses assess pain intensity using standard rating scales and administer prescribed analgesics promptly. Monitoring vital signs helps detect early indicators of internal hemorrhage or infection, particularly in postoperative settings.

Education empowers patients to manage their health proactively. Nurses must instruct patients on recognizing red-flag symptoms that necessitate immediate medical evaluation, such as sudden severe pelvic pain, dizziness, or fainting. Patients managed conservatively should understand the importance of keeping follow-up ultrasound appointments. Preoperative and postoperative instructions should clearly explain activity restrictions, wound care, and signs of surgical site infection.
