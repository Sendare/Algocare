Endometritis

Introduction and Definition of Endometritis

Endometritis is an infection or inflammation of the endometrium, which is the inner lining of the uterus. It often happens after childbirth, but it can also occur outside of pregnancy due to infections, sexually transmitted organisms, or gynecological procedures like inserting an intrauterine device. When bacteria ascend from the lower genital tract through the cervix, they colonize and infect the uterine lining. While mild cases resolve quickly with proper treatment, ignoring the condition can allow the infection to spread deeper into the pelvic organs, leading to serious systemic illness.

Etiology and Common Risk Factors

The main trigger for endometritis is bacterial invasion. Following childbirth, especially after a prolonged labor or a cesarean section, the risk spikes significantly because the placental site leaves a large open wound inside the uterus. Other risk factors include frequent vaginal examinations during labor, prolonged rupture of membranes, and retained products of conception after delivery or miscarriage. Non-obstetric cases are often linked to pelvic inflammatory disease caused by organisms like Chlamydia trachomatis or Neisseria gonorrhoeae, as well as minor surgical procedures that breach the cervical barrier.

Pathophysiology and Classification

Endometritis is generally categorized into two forms based on how the disease develops and progresses:

  • Acute Endometritis: Usually starts suddenly, often right after childbirth or a miscarriage. It is typically caused by bacterial infections like group B Streptococcus or mixed anaerobes. The tissue shows heavy infiltration of neutrophils, acute inflammation, and localized edema.
  • Chronic Endometritis: Develops slowly and lasts longer. It is characterized by the presence of plasma cells within the endometrial stroma. Common culprits include persistent bacterial infections, intrauterine devices, or chronic pelvic inflammatory disease. Patients may experience subtle, ongoing symptoms rather than sudden, severe illness.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

The clinical picture of endometritis varies depending on whether the infection is acute or chronic. In acute postpartum cases, patients present with a cluster of distinct signs that require prompt medical attention.

  • Lower abdominal pain: Cramping or tenderness in the lower pelvic region.
  • Fever and chills: Systemic signs of infection, often accompanied by a rapid heart rate.
  • Abnormal vaginal discharge: Foul-smelling, purulent, or heavy lochia in postpartum mothers.
  • Uterine tenderness: The uterus feels soft, boggy, and painful when pressed during a physical exam.
  • Irregular bleeding: Spotting or heavy bleeding outside of normal menstrual patterns, especially common in chronic cases.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Nursing Assessment

Diagnosing endometritis relies heavily on a thorough physical assessment and a detailed patient history. Nurses play a crucial role by measuring vital signs to catch early signs of sepsis, such as a high fever and tachycardia. A pelvic examination will reveal uterine tenderness and abnormal discharge. Doctors often order a complete blood count, which typically shows an elevated white blood cell count. Endometrial cultures or biopsies may be performed to identify the exact bacteria responsible, ensuring the patient receives the correct targeted antibiotic therapy.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Care

The cornerstone of medical treatment for endometritis is prompt antibiotic therapy to eradicate the infection and prevent complications. Broad-spectrum antibiotics, such as a combination of clindamycin and gentamicin, are commonly started right away intravenously, especially in postpartum infections involving multiple types of bacteria. Treatment usually continues until the patient has been fever-free for at least 24 to 48 hours. If retained products of conception are found inside the uterus, a minor surgical procedure called a dilatation and curettage may be necessary to clear out the remaining tissue.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Nurses provide comprehensive care to promote healing and monitor for worsening conditions. Essential nursing interventions include tracking vital signs every four hours, administering prescribed antibiotics on schedule, encouraging deep breathing, and managing pain with analgesics. Nurses also educate patients on proper perineal hygiene, teaching them to wipe from front to back and change sanitary pads frequently. It is vital to instruct patients to finish their entire course of antibiotics even if they start feeling better, to prevent the infection from returning.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

Most patients recover fully when endometritis is diagnosed and treated early. However, delayed or inadequate treatment can lead to severe complications that threaten a patient's health and future fertility. Potential complications include pelvic abscesses, peritonitis, spread of infection into the bloodstream, and chronic pelvic pain. In severe postpartum cases, scarring of the uterine cavity can occur, a condition known as Asherman syndrome, which may cause future menstrual abnormalities or infertility.
