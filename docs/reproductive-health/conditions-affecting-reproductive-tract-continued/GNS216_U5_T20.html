Endometritis

Introduction and Definition of Endometritis

Endometritis is an inflammatory condition affecting the endometrium, the inner mucosal lining of the uterus. While pelvic infections can arise from various gynecological procedures, the vast majority of clinical cases are directly linked to postpartum bacterial invasion following delivery—frequently termed puerperal sepsis. In busy obstetric wards across Nigeria, recognizing this condition early prevents progression to severe pelvic peritonitis or septic shock. The inflammation primarily stems from ascending polymicrobial flora originating in the lower genital tract, taking advantage of the traumatized placental implantation site after childbirth.

Etiology and Risk Factors

The microbial agents responsible for endometritis are usually polymicrobial, involving a mix of aerobic and anaerobic bacteria. Common culprits include group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, and various Bacteroides species.

  • Prolonged rupture of membranes: Increases the window for vaginal flora to ascend into the sterile uterine cavity.
  • Cesarean delivery: Represents the single greatest surgical risk factor compared to spontaneous vaginal delivery.
  • Multiple digital vaginal examinations: Each exam introduces bacteria higher into the birth canal during labor.
  • Internal fetal monitoring: Breaks the mucosal barrier of the scalp, creating an entry point that can coincide with uterine contamination.

Pathophysiology and Classification

Endometritis manifests in two distinct clinical forms based on duration and underlying etiology: acute and chronic.

Acute endometritis is characterized by a dense neutrophilic infiltration of the endometrial stroma, almost exclusively associated with postpartum infection or retained products of conception following abortion or delivery. Bacteria ascend from the vagina, colonize the necrotic decidual tissue, and trigger a robust local inflammatory response.

Chronic endometritis features a lymphoplasmacytic infiltrate—specifically plasma cells—within the endometrial stroma. This form is rarely linked to pregnancy; instead, it stems from persistent subclinical infections such as pelvic inflammatory disease, intrauterine devices, or pathogens like Chlamydia trachomatis and Mycobacterium tuberculosis.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

The clinical picture of acute endometritis typically develops within the first few days following delivery. Recognizing these classic signs ensures timely intervention before bacteremia occurs.

  • Fever: Often the earliest and most reliable indicator of infection, frequently accompanied by chills.
  • Uterine tenderness: Elicited readily upon abdominal palpation of the fundus during postpartum checks.
  • Lochia changes: The normal postpartum discharge turns foul-smelling, purulent, or unusually profuse.
  • Lower abdominal pain: Cramping or persistent pelvic discomfort that exceeds normal post-delivery involution pains.
  • Subinvolution: The uterus fails to shrink at the expected postpartum rate, remaining boggy and large.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Nursing Assessment

Confirming endometritis relies heavily on careful physical assessment alongside targeted laboratory investigations. During the postpartum assessment, palpate the uterine fundus gently, noting its tone, height, and any elicited tenderness. Inspect the character, color, and odor of the lochia.

Laboratory tests usually show a marked leukocytosis with a left shift. Endometrial cultures are rarely obtained via transcervical swab due to contamination from vaginal flora, but blood cultures are essential if the patient spikes a high fever to rule out sepsis. Pelvic ultrasound may be ordered to visualize the uterine cavity for retained products of conception, which can perpetuate the infection if left untreated.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Prompt initiation of broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy forms the cornerstone of treatment to halt the spread of infection to the myometrium, peritoneum, or bloodstream. The standard regimen typically involves combination therapy to cover both aerobic and anaerobic organisms—most commonly clindamycin combined with an aminoglycoside such as gentamicin.

If retained products of conception are visualized on ultrasound, surgical intervention via dilation and curettage or vacuum aspiration becomes necessary alongside antibiotic coverage. Antibiotic administration usually continues intravenously until the patient has been afebrile for at least 24 to 48 hours, eliminating the need for oral step-down therapy in most uncomplicated cases.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care focuses on monitoring vital signs, managing pain, and supporting the mother's recovery and infant bonding. Assess temperature every four hours while febrile, and monitor lochia amount and characteristics closely. Administer prescribed analgesics and antibiotics on schedule, remaining vigilant for adverse drug reactions.

Encourage early ambulation to promote venous return and prevent thromboembolism, a risk compounded by infection and postpartum status. Teach the mother proper perineal hygiene, emphasizing front-to-back wiping and frequent pad changes. Ensure she understands the importance of completing any prescribed oral medications and recognizing warning signs such as worsening pelvic pain or returning fever.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

While most cases resolve rapidly with appropriate antibiotic therapy, failure to treat endometritis promptly can lead to severe, life-threatening complications. Infection can spread beyond the endometrium to involve the entire myometrium (metritis), the pelvic peritoneum (pelvic peritonitis), or the pelvic veins, leading to septic pelvic thrombophlebitis.

Bacteremia and septic shock represent critical systemic emergencies. Chronic untreated endometritis can result in intrauterine adhesions, known as Asherman syndrome, which may cause future infertility or recurrent pregnancy loss. With early detection and appropriate antimicrobial stewardship, the overall prognosis for acute postpartum endometritis remains highly favorable.
