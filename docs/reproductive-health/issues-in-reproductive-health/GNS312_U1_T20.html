Male Causes of Infertility

Introduction and Physiology of Male Infertility

Male infertility refers to a man's inability to achieve a pregnancy in a fertile female partner after at least one year of unprotected sexual intercourse. In Nigerian clinical practice, childbearing holds immense cultural and social significance, making fertility challenges a source of profound psychological and marital distress. Historically, societal pressure tends to place the entire burden of barrenness on the woman. However, modern urological and gynecological evaluations show that male factor issues contribute wholly or partially to nearly half of all infertility cases.

Normal male reproductive physiology depends on a well-coordinated axis involving the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and the testes. The hypothalamus secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which stimulates the anterior pituitary to release luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). LH acts on Leydig cells within the testes to produce testosterone, while FSH acts alongside testosterone on Sertoli cells to support spermatogenesis—the continuous biological process of developing mature spermatozoa from primitive germ cells. Disruptions at any level of this hormonal axis or within the testicular tissue will impair sperm production or function.

Pre-Testicular and Hormonal Causes

Pre-testicular causes stem from systemic endocrine or hormonal abnormalities that originate outside the testes. These conditions disrupt the central nervous system control over the reproductive system, leading to inadequate stimulation of sperm production. Common pre-testicular etiologies include:

  • Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism: A deficiency in hypothalamic GnRH production, which can be congenital, such as in Kallmann syndrome, or acquired through pituitary tumors, hyperprolactinemia, or severe systemic illnesses.
  • Exogenous Androgen Use: The misuse of anabolic steroids or testosterone replacement therapy. This creates a negative feedback loop that shuts down pituitary LH and FSH release, effectively halting endogenous sperm production.
  • Thyroid Disorders: Both hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism disrupt normal metabolic balance and alter sex hormone-binding globulin levels, impairing overall reproductive function.

Testicular Causes and Genetic Factors

Testicular causes involve structural damage, genetic abnormalities, or acquired insults directly affecting the parenchyma of the testes, preventing normal spermatogenesis despite normal hormonal signaling.

  • Varicoceles: Abnormal dilation of the pampiniform venous plexus surrounding the testicle. This is the most common reversible cause of male infertility, thought to raise scrotal temperature and increase oxidative stress.
  • Genetic Anomalies: Conditions such as Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY karyotype) lead to small, firm testes and primary testicular failure. Microdeletions on the Y chromosome (azoospermia factor regions) also severely impact sperm generation.
  • Cryptorchidism: Undescended testes during infancy result in prolonged exposure to higher intra-abdominal core body temperatures, causing irreversible damage to germinal epithelium if uncorrected early.
  • Infections and Trauma: Post-pubertal mumps orchitis, severe testicular torsion, or physical trauma can cause localized necrosis and permanent scarring of the seminiferous tubules.

Post-Testicular Causes: Obstructions and Infections

Post-testicular factors occur when sperm are successfully produced within the testes, but physical blockages, structural defects, or functional disorders prevent their normal transit, maturation, or ejaculation.

  • Anatomical Obstructions: Blockages can occur anywhere along the excretory tract, including the epididymis, vas deferens, or ejaculatory ducts. These can be congenital, such as in cystic fibrosis-related congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens (CBAVD), or acquired following pelvic surgeries or hernia repairs.
  • Genitourinary Infections: Chronic or untreated infections of the prostate, seminal vesicles, or urethra—such as gonorrhea, chlamydia, or tuberculosis—cause scarring and strictures in the delicate ductal system. Leukocytes from active infections also generate high levels of reactive oxygen species that damage sperm membranes.
  • Ejaculatory Dysfunction: Conditions like retrograde ejaculation, where semen enters the urinary bladder instead of exiting through the urethral meatus, often resulting from diabetic neuropathy, pelvic nerve damage, or certain antihypertensive medications.

Lifestyle, Environmental, and Occupational Risks

Many cases of male subfertility are influenced by modifiable lifestyle choices, environmental toxins, and occupational hazards that induce oxidative stress and disrupt endocrine pathways.

  • Heat Exposure: Chronic scrotal hyperthermia from frequent hot baths, working with laptops directly on the laps, tight undergarments, or sedentary professions like long-distance driving impairs spermatogenesis.
  • Substance Use: Heavy alcohol consumption, cigarette smoking, and the use of recreational drugs such as marijuana or opioids lower testosterone levels, reduce sperm motility, and increase morphological abnormalities.
  • Occupational Toxins: Prolonged exposure to heavy metals like lead, pesticide chemicals, organic solvents, and ionizing radiation damages developing germ cells.
  • Obesity: Excess adipose tissue increases the peripheral conversion of testosterone to estrogen via the aromatase enzyme, creating a hormonal imbalance that depresses gonadotropin release.

Nursing Assessment and Semen Analysis

A thorough nursing assessment provides the foundation for identifying underlying etiologies and preparing the patient for specialized diagnostics. Nurses take a comprehensive sexual, medical, surgical, and occupational history while offering a non-judgmental environment to alleviate anxiety.

Medical, Surgical, and Assisted Reproduction Management

Management strategies for male infertility depend on identifying the specific root cause and tailoring interventions accordingly. Medical therapies include antibiotics for confirmed genitourinary infections, hormonal treatments such as clomiphene citrate or gonadotropin injections for hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, and antioxidant supplements to reduce oxidative cellular damage.

Surgical interventions are often curative for anatomical issues. A varicocelectomy repairs dilated veins to restore healthy scrotal temperatures. Microsurgical reconstructions, such as a vasovasostomy or vasoepididymostomy, can reverse previous vasectomies or correct blockages. When natural conception remains unachieved, Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART) offers viable pathways. Procedures like intrauterine insemination (IUI), in vitro fertilization (IVF), and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)—where a single healthy sperm is injected directly into an oocyte—allow men with severe oligospermia or obstructive azoospermia to father biological children.

Psychosocial Support and Nursing Interventions

Infertility carries a heavy emotional toll, particularly in cultures where family continuity is paramount. Men frequently experience feelings of inadequacy, guilt, anxiety, and depression, often masked by anger or withdrawal due to societal stigmas surrounding masculinity and virility.

Nurses play a critical role in providing holistic care. They create safe, confidential spaces for couples to express their fears without judgment. Interventions include offering accurate health education to clear up misconceptions, facilitating open communication between partners, and referring patients to professional counseling or support groups. By addressing the psychological dimensions alongside clinical treatments, nurses help patients navigate their fertility journey with dignity and resilience.
