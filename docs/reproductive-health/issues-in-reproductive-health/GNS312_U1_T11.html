Burden of Early and Forced Marriage

Introduction and Prevalence

Early and forced marriage refers to any formal marriage or informal union where one or both parties are under eighteen years of age, or where consent is not freely given due to coercion, threats, or immaturity. While global frameworks heavily target this human rights violation, the burden remains disproportionately high in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia. Within Nigeria, regional disparities are pronounced; the practice is significantly more prevalent in the Northwest and Northeast zones compared to the Southern regions. This geographic variance stems from deeply entrenched patriarchal structures, religious misinterpretations, and localized customary laws that supersede statutory protections like the Child Rights Act.

Socioeconomic Drivers and Root Causes

The persistence of early and forced marriage is rarely driven by a single factor, operating instead as a complex web of economic vulnerability and traditional norms. - **Poverty**: Families often view marrying off young daughters as a survival strategy to reduce household financial burdens or to secure bride price resources. - **Gender Inequality**: Deep-seated beliefs that value boys over girls relegate females to domestic roles, framing education as an unnecessary investment. - **Insecure Environments**: During conflicts or humanitarian crises, families resort to child marriage to protect girls from sexual violence or as a desperate coping mechanism. - **Religious and Cultural Misconceptions**: Misinterpretations of religious texts and adherence to customary practices perpetuate the idea that early union preserves family honor and female chastity.

Physical and Reproductive Health Consequences

Adolescent bodies are biologically unprepared for coitus, pregnancy, and childbirth. The anatomical immaturity of the pelvis frequently results in obstructed labor, which is a major precursor to **obstetric fistula**—a devastating condition causing continuous incontinence and severe social stigmatization. Furthermore, pregnancy-induced hypertension, severe anemia, and puerperal sepsis occur at significantly higher rates among minors compared to mature women. Infants born to adolescent mothers also face elevated risks of premature birth, low birth weight, and neonatal mortality due to competing nutritional demands between the growing mother and the fetus.

Psychological and Emotional Toll

The psychological impact of early and forced marriage begins with sudden social isolation and the abrupt termination of childhood play and education. Cut off from peers, young brides frequently experience chronic anxiety, depression, and complex post-traumatic stress disorder. Power dynamics heavily favor older spouses, leaving these minors powerless against psychological intimidation, domestic violence, and coerced sexual encounters. The absence of emotional maturity to process these stressors often manifests as somatic complaints, self-harm ideation, and profound feelings of hopelessness.

Educational and Economic Impact on Communities

Early marriage acts as a permanent barrier to female education, effectively cutting short academic trajectories at the primary or junior secondary level. Without literacy, vocational skills, and critical thinking capabilities, these girls are locked out of the formal labor market. This lack of earning capacity traps families in intergenerational cycles of poverty. Communities that fail to educate and economically empower young females experience depressed GDP growth, sluggish health outcomes, and slower human capital development because maternal education is directly correlated with child survival and community well-being.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of At-Risk Minors

Nurses working in community health centers, antenatal clinics, and emergency departments occupy a frontline position for identifying victims or potential victims of early marriage. Healthcare professionals must remain vigilant for subtle warning signs, such as an older male partner speaking on behalf of a young female patient, hesitation when answering direct questions, or physical signs of abuse and early pregnancy. During clinical interactions, the nurse should create a private, non-judgmental space away from accompanying family members. Asking direct, compassionate screening questions allows the minor to disclose coercion, paving the way for targeted psychosocial and legal interventions.

Interventions, Advocacy, and Preventive Strategies

Addressing early and forced marriage requires multi-sectoral collaboration involving healthcare workers, legal advocates, traditional leaders, and policymakers. Nurses play a vital role in community advocacy by educating parents, religious figures, and youth about the adverse health consequences of underage unions. Clinical interventions must include compassionate trauma-informed care, confidential reproductive health counseling, and direct linkages to social welfare agencies or legal aid organizations. Furthermore, supporting legislative enforcement of child protection laws, such as the Child Rights Act, ensures that perpetrators face legal consequences while vulnerable girls are provided with safe shelter and educational reintegration pathways.
