Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Introduction and Definition of Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation, commonly referred to as FGM, encompasses all procedures involving the partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. It is recognized globally by major health organizations as a grave violation of the human rights, bodily integrity, and health of women and girls. Most girls undergo the procedure between infancy and adolescence, usually without their informed consent. Because it is deeply rooted in traditions, myths about purity, and community acceptance, understanding FGM requires looking beyond the physical act to the intense social pressures driving families to conform.

Classification and Types of FGM

The World Health Organization classifies FGM into four distinct types to standardize clinical documentation and research:

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV (Other): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Cultural Drivers and Context in Nigeria

In Nigeria, FGM persists due to a complex web of cultural norms, beliefs about female chastity, and community acceptance. Many communities believe that the procedure ensures marital fidelity, curbs sexual desire, or preserves hygiene and religious purity. It is frequently seen as a rite of passage into womanhood. Families often fear that an uncut girl will face social ostracization, ridicule, or be deemed unmarriageable. Addressing these drivers requires community education and dialogue rather than simple condemnation, as practicing communities are often acting out of a desire to protect their daughters' social standing.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Consequences

The physical complications of FGM span a lifetime, beginning immediately at the time of cutting and extending well into old age.

TimelineCommon Health Complications
ImmediateSevere hemorrhage, intense pain, shock, acute urine retention, and local infection.
Long-TermChronic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, scarring, keloids, and painful sexual intercourse.
ObstetricProlonged labor, increased risk of caesarean section, obstetric fistula, and severe perineal tears.

Infibulated women (Type III) face the most severe obstetric risks because scar tissue must often be surgically cut open during childbirth to allow delivery, only to be re-stitched afterward in many traditional settings.

Psychosexual and Psychological Impacts

Beyond physical trauma, survivors often experience profound psychological and psychosexual distress. The violation of bodily autonomy during childhood or adolescence can trigger post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic anxiety, and depression. Psychosexual complications are particularly prevalent due to nerve damage and scar tissue formation. Survivors frequently report loss of sexual pleasure, dyspareunia (painful intercourse), and psychological distress related to intimacy. Many women suffer in silence due to cultural taboos surrounding female sexuality, making compassionate, non-judgmental mental health support vital during clinical interactions.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Nurses must be vigilant in identifying signs of FGM during routine physical assessments, antenatal bookings, and pediatric examinations. A comprehensive nursing assessment includes a gentle, trauma-informed physical examination of the perineal area while maintaining patient dignity and privacy. Nurses should ask open-ended questions about reproductive health history without judgment. Clinical identification allows healthcare providers to anticipate complications, offer timely referrals, and protect younger female siblings who may be at imminent risk of undergoing the practice.

Medical Management and Care for Complications

Managing the health complications of FGM requires a multidisciplinary healthcare team, including nurses, midwives, gynecologists, and mental health specialists. Acute injuries require immediate surgical repair, infection control with antibiotics, and pain management. For women with Type III infibulation who are pregnant, a deinfibulation (surgical opening of the scar tissue) is often performed either antenatally or during labor to allow safe delivery. Long-term care focuses on scar tissue management, treatment of chronic infections, psychological counseling, and sexual health rehabilitation.

Legal Frameworks and the Nurse's Advocacy Role

Legal frameworks exist to protect women and girls from FGM, including the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act in Nigeria, which criminalizes the practice and imposes penalties on perpetrators. Nurses play a frontline advocacy role in eradication efforts. Beyond treating physical ailments, nurses educate communities, advocate for strict enforcement of child protection laws, and mentor younger health professionals on ethical reporting and compassionate care. By standing as patient advocates, nurses help dismantle the silence surrounding FGM and contribute to its eventual elimination.
