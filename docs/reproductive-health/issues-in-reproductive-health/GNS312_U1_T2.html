Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Introduction and Definition of Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Female Genital Mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Globally recognized as a violation of human rights, the practice inflicts severe physical and psychological harm on women and girls. In Nigeria, prevalence varies significantly across geopolitical zones, deeply rooted in patriarchal structures, traditional beliefs surrounding female purity, and socio-cultural conformity. The World Health Organization classifies the practice into distinct types, each carrying specific health burdens that demand informed, culturally sensitive nursing intervention.

Classification and Types of FGM

The World Health Organization categorizes FGM into four major types to standardize clinical documentation and research: - **Type I (Clitoridectomy)**: Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce. - **Type II (Excision)**: Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. - **Type III (Infibulation)**: Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or labia majora, with or without excision of the clitoris. - **Type IV (Other)**: All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Cultural and Social Context and the Nigerian Landscape

Understanding why families continue to practice FGM requires examining deeply entrenched social norms. Communities often view the practice as a rite of passage, a preservation of chastity, a prerequisite for marriage, and a measure of hygiene or aesthetic appeal. In Nigeria, prevalence rates are historically higher in the Southern zones compared to the North, though variations exist within specific ethnic groups. Rural areas often rely on traditional birth attendants or local practitioners who use unsterilized instruments like blades, knives, or broken glass, compounding the physical danger.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Consequences

The physical complications of FGM span across acute trauma and chronic, lifelong morbidity. Immediate risks include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, neurogenic shock, acute urine retention, and localized or systemic infections such as tetanus and sepsis. Psychosexual and Psychological Impacts on Survivors

Beyond physical scars, survivors frequently endure profound psychological distress and sexual dysfunction. The trauma of the procedure, often performed during childhood or adolescence without consent, can trigger post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic anxiety, depression, and persistent mistrust of healthcare providers. Psychosexual consequences include dyspareunia, anorgasmia, and reduced sexual desire resulting from nerve damage and scar tissue formation. Nurses must approach sexual health complaints with empathy, recognizing the deep emotional overlay associated with these intimate traumas.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Clinical assessment requires a high index of suspicion, gentleness, and an unhurried, trauma-informed approach. During routine admission, antenatal booking, or gynecological examinations, nurses may uncover evidence of FGM. Assessment involves taking a comprehensive health history, observing for perineal scarring or structural absence during physical inspection, and screening for chronic pain, urinary symptoms, or psychological distress. Establishing a trusting therapeutic alliance is vital, as survivors may feel profound shame, anxiety, or fear of judgment when discussing their genital anatomy.

Medical Management and Care for Complications

Medical management focuses on treating acute emergencies, alleviating chronic symptoms, and providing specialized obstetric support. Acute management of fresh trauma or hemorrhage requires surgical repair under anesthesia, administration of analgesics, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and tetanus prophylaxis. For infibulated women presenting in labor, a defibulation (surgical opening of the fused labia) is frequently necessary to allow safe delivery and prevent severe lacerations. Psychological counseling, pelvic floor physiotherapy, and referral to specialized multidisciplinary clinics form the cornerstone of long-term rehabilitation.

Legal Frameworks and the Nurse's Role in Eradication

Eradicating FGM requires robust legislative frameworks coupled with community-level advocacy. In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act, enacted federally and domesticated in many states, criminalizes FGM, imposing penalties on practitioners and sponsors. Nurses play a pivotal role beyond bedside clinical care; they function as educators, community advocates, and frontline change agents. By engaging traditional rulers, educating pregnant women during antenatal visits, and challenging harmful myths, nurses contribute significantly to the global movement to abandon this practice.
