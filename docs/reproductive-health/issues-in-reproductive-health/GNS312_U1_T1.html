Overview of Harmful Traditional Practices

Introduction to Harmful Traditional Practices in Reproductive Health

Harmful traditional practices (HTPs) significantly impact reproductive health across various regions, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. As nursing students in Nigeria, understanding these practices is vital because you will encounter patients suffering from their physical, psychological, and social consequences. HTPs are deeply ingrained cultural, religious, or traditional customs that violate the fundamental human rights, bodily integrity, and health of women and girls. Reproductive health encompasses physical, mental, and social well-being in all matters relating to the reproductive system, making HTPs a direct assault on this state of health. Common examples include female genital mutilation, child marriage, harmful nutritional taboos during pregnancy, and distressing widowhood rites. These practices are often perpetuated under the guise of preserving cultural identity, promoting chastity, or ensuring marital fidelity. However, they frequently result in severe short-term complications such as hemorrhage, severe pain, and psychological trauma, as well as long-term consequences like chronic reproductive tract infections, obstetric fistula, infertility, and even maternal mortality. Recognizing these practices as major public health concerns allows nurses to move beyond mere clinical treatment to holistic care and advocacy. Addressing HTPs requires a comprehensive approach that combines culturally sensitive healthcare delivery, community education, and adherence to protective legal frameworks. By understanding the breadth of HTPs, nursing professionals can better protect vulnerable populations and champion reproductive justice within Nigerian healthcare settings.

Socio-Cultural Drivers and Underlying Causes

The persistence of harmful traditional practices (HTPs) is largely driven by complex socio-cultural factors that vary across different ethnic groups in Nigeria. Patriarchy is a central underlying cause, where male dominance dictates societal norms, restricting women's autonomy over their bodies and reproductive choices. Gender inequality reinforces the belief that women and girls are subordinate, making it easier to subject them to painful or restrictive cultural rituals without their consent. Deeply rooted community traditions and intergenerational transmission also play massive roles; elders pass down these practices, and deviation from them often results in social ostracization, stigmatization, or the labeling of individuals as outcasts. Economic factors intertwine with these cultural drivers, as poverty can push families to view daughters as economic commodities through child marriage or bride price collection. Furthermore, misconceptions and myths—such as the false belief that uncircumcised women are promiscuous or that certain nutritious foods will cause birth defects—sustain these behaviors over generations. Religion is sometimes misinterpreted or misquoted to justify practices like female genital mutilation or harsh widowhood rites, even when religious texts do not mandate them. As healthcare professionals, understanding these socio-cultural drivers is crucial because you cannot effectively change behavior or provide adequate counseling without addressing the root beliefs that motivate patients and their communities. Nursing interventions must therefore incorporate culturally competent health education that challenges harmful myths respectfully while empowering women and engaging community leaders.

Female Genital Mutilation: Types, Procedure, and Health Impact

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), also known as female genital cutting, involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury to female genital organs for non-medical reasons. The World Health Organization classifies FGM into four main types: Type I (clitoridectomy, partial or total removal of the clitoris), Type II (excisions, removal of the clitoris and labia minora with or without excision of the labia majora), Type III (infibulation, narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia), and Type IV (all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, scraping, or cauterization). Procedures are typically performed on young girls by traditional practitioners using unsterilized blades, knives, or broken glass without anesthesia. The immediate health impacts include severe pain, excessive bleeding (hemorrhage), shock, urine retention, acute infections, and sometimes death. Long-term physical complications are extensive, including chronic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, dyspareunia (painful sexual intercourse), keloid formation, menstrual difficulties, and severe obstetric complications during childbirth such as prolonged labor, perineal tears, and increased risk of neonatal mortality. Psychologically, survivors often suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic anxiety, depression, and sexual dysfunction. Nurses must be clinically adept at identifying FGM complications during pelvic examinations and providing sensitive, non-judgmental care to affected women.

Child Marriage and Early Adolescent Pregnancy

Child marriage—defined as any formal marriage or informal union where one or both parties are under 18 years of age—is a pervasive harmful practice in parts of Nigeria, heavily intertwined with early adolescent pregnancy. This practice severely curtails a young girl's fundamental rights to education, personal development, and health. Biologically, the adolescent female pelvis and reproductive tract are often not fully mature, making early pregnancy exceptionally hazardous. Young mothers face a significantly higher risk of developing obstetric fistula—an abnormal opening between the vagina and the bladder or rectum resulting from prolonged, obstructed labor without timely medical intervention. Other severe medical complications include pregnancy-induced hypertension (preeclampsia/eclampsia), severe postpartum hemorrhage, premature delivery, and low birth weight. Furthermore, babies born to adolescent mothers experience higher rates of neonatal and infant mortality. Beyond physical complications, early pregnancy and child marriage trap young girls in cycles of poverty, social isolation, and powerlessness, as they are forced into adult roles before attaining psychological maturity. Nurses working in maternal and child health settings must be vigilant in identifying pregnant adolescents, providing specialized antenatal care, screening for signs of abuse or forced union, and offering compassionate counseling and educational support.

Nutritional Taboos and Practices Affecting Pregnant Women

Nutritional taboos and restrictive dietary practices during pregnancy are prevalent in various traditional communities, negatively impacting maternal and fetal health. These cultural rules often prohibit pregnant women from consuming nutrient-dense foods such as eggs, meat, fish, snails, or certain fruits, based on unfounded beliefs that these foods will cause the baby to be born with deformities, be overly large, or develop bad character. For instance, some communities falsely believe that eating eggs will cause a child to grow up to be a thief or that eating meat will lead to difficult labor. In reality, pregnancy increases the demand for essential micronutrients and macronutrients to support maternal physiological changes and fetal growth. When pregnant women adhere to these restrictive taboos, they frequently develop iron-deficiency anemia, protein-energy malnutrition, calcium deficiency, and gestational deficiencies that impair fetal neurological and physical development. Anemic mothers face heightened risks of postpartum hemorrhage, fatigue, and poor tolerance to surgical interventions if complications arise. As nurses, identifying and addressing these nutritional taboos during antenatal visits is paramount. Nurses must conduct thorough dietary assessments, debunk cultural myths gently through health education, and encourage balanced diets rich in local, affordable, and nutritious foods to safeguard both mother and child.

Widhood Rites and Reproductive Rights Violations

Widowhood rites in many traditional Nigerian societies represent profound violations of human rights and reproductive autonomy. Following the death of a husband, grieving widows are frequently subjected to degrading, humiliating, and physically harmful rituals designed to prove their innocence regarding their spouse's death or to mourn him according to strict traditional dictates. These practices can include forced confinement, shaving of the head, wearing mourning garments for extended periods, sitting on bare floors, and being prohibited from bathing or eating properly. In some extreme cases, widows are forced to drink the water used to wash their deceased husband's corpse or are subjected to property grabbing, where in-laws dispossess them of their homes, land, and belongings, leaving them destitute. These rites inflict severe psychological distress, depression, and social isolation. Furthermore, some widowhood practices involve forced levirate marriage—where a widow is compelled to marry her late husband's brother—which severely violates her reproductive rights and bodily autonomy, increasing her vulnerability to sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. Nurses encountering widows in clinical or community settings must be observant of psychological distress, provide empathetic counseling, safeguard patients' privacy, and connect vulnerable women to social support services and legal aid organizations that protect women's property and human rights.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of Affected Patients

Clinical assessment and the identification of patients affected by harmful traditional practices require a high index of suspicion, observational skills, and exceptional communication techniques. Many victims of FGM, child marriage, or harsh widowhood rites may not actively disclose their trauma due to shame, fear of social stigma, cultural conditioning, or threats from perpetrators. Nurses must create a safe, confidential, and empathetic environment during history taking and physical examinations. During clinical assessments, healthcare providers should look for physical indicators such as scar tissue in the perineal region, signs of chronic vaginal discharge, dyspareunia, pelvic organ prolapse, or signs of obstetric fistula. When assessing pediatric or adolescent patients, nurses must be alert to indicators of forced child marriage, such as unexplained school dropout, signs of early pregnancy, or appearing accompanied by an older spouse who answers questions on the patient's behalf. Taking a comprehensive sexual and reproductive health history with trauma-informed care principles is essential. Nurses must ask direct yet sensitive questions regarding past traditional procedures, menstrual health, and psychological well-being. Early and accurate identification allows for timely medical intervention, psychological referral, and appropriate multi-disciplinary management, ultimately mitigating long-term morbidity and improving patient outcomes.

Nursing Management, Psychological Support, and Rehabilitation

Nursing management for patients affected by harmful traditional practices (HTPs) extends far beyond acute medical treatment, requiring a comprehensive, holistic, and trauma-informed approach. When managing patients with immediate complications like hemorrhage or infection from FGM or obstructed labor from child marriage, nurses must prioritize stabilizing vital signs, administering prescribed antibiotics, managing pain, and preparing patients for necessary surgical repairs (such as fistula repair or deinfibulation). Psychological support is equally critical; nurses must offer empathetic counseling, validate the patient's emotional pain, and establish trust without expressing judgment toward cultural practices. For survivors experiencing severe anxiety, depression, or post-traumatic stress disorder, formal referral to psychiatric nurses, psychologists, or social workers is mandatory. Rehabilitation involves physical therapy for pelvic floor muscles, education on sexual and reproductive health, and empowering patients with vocational skills to achieve economic independence, which helps break the cycle of vulnerability. Nurses also act as educators and patient advocates, helping survivors navigate healthcare systems and regain control over their bodies and lives. Creating a supportive, multidisciplinary care team ensures that both the physical wounds and invisible psychological scars of HTP survivors are addressed with dignity and professional excellence.

Legal Frameworks, Advocacy, and Prevention Strategies in Nigeria

Addressing harmful traditional practices in Nigeria requires robust legal frameworks, active advocacy, and multi-faceted prevention strategies. At the national level, legislation such as the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act of 2015 provides a crucial legal weapon against HTPs by criminalizing offenses such as female genital mutilation, forceful ejection from home, harmful widowhood practices, and battery. Additionally, the Child Rights Act, enacted across various states, prohibits child marriage and sets the legal age of marriage at 18. Despite these laws, enforcement remains challenging due to deep-seated cultural tolerance, lack of awareness, and weak implementation at the grassroots level. Nurses play a frontline role in advocacy and prevention by actively engaging communities through educational outreach programs, partnering with local and traditional leaders, and raising awareness about the negative health impacts of HTPs. Prevention strategies also involve empowering women and girls through education and economic opportunities, as well as training healthcare personnel to recognize, report, and manage cases ethically within legal guidelines. By combining strict adherence to legal frameworks with compassionate community engagement, nursing professionals become powerful catalysts for social change, working toward the total eradication of harmful traditional practices across Nigeria.
