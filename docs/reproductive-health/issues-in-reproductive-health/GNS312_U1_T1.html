Overview of Harmful Traditional Practices

Introduction and Socio-Cultural Drivers of Harmful Traditional Practices

Harmful traditional practices (HTPs) are deeply rooted behaviors and customs that violate the fundamental human rights, health, and dignity of individuals, particularly women and children. In many local communities, these practices are not viewed as harmful; instead, they are passed down through generations as sacred cultural obligations, markers of identity, or community protection mechanisms.

  • Patriarchy: Social systems where men hold primary power, dictating female sexuality, movement, and reproductive choices.
  • Poverty and Economic Dependence: Lack of financial autonomy forces families to rely on traditional safety nets or marry off young girls for economic survival.
  • Fear of Social Stigmatization: Communities often ostracize individuals or families who refuse to conform to established cultural norms.
  • Ignorance and Misinformation: Myths surrounding health, fertility, and spiritual purity perpetuate the cycle of harmful customs.

Nurses must understand that challenging these practices requires cultural humility. Condemning a patient's culture outright builds barriers to care, whereas respectful dialogue and community engagement open doors to lasting behavioral change.

Female Genital Mutilation: Classifications and Physical Consequences

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. It is typically performed by traditional practitioners using unsterilized blades, knives, or broken glass without anesthesia.

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV (Unclassified): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

The immediate complications include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, shock, urine retention, and acute infections. Long-term consequences manifest as chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, dyspareunia, keloid scar formation, and severe psychological trauma. Obstetric complications are also notably high, including prolonged labor, perineal tearing, and increased risk of newborn resuscitation.

Child Marriage and Early Adolescent Pregnancy Risks

Child marriage is any formal marriage or informal union where one or both parties are under eighteen years of age. It strips young girls of their childhood, halts their education, and forces them into premature sexual activity and childbearing before their bodies are biologically mature.

The physiological consequences of early adolescent pregnancy are severe. The pelvis of a young girl is often underdeveloped, leading to obstructed labor and a high incidence of obstetric fistulas—abnormal openings between the vagina and the bladder or rectum that cause continuous, uncontrollable leakage of urine and feces. Furthermore, adolescent mothers face significantly higher rates of eclampsia, systemic maternal infections, postpartum hemorrhage, and maternal mortality. Infants born to young mothers experience higher rates of prematurity, low birth weight, and neonatal death.

Nutritional Taboos and Practices Affecting Pregnant Women

Cultural beliefs often dictate what pregnant and lactating women can or cannot eat. These food taboos are usually justified by myths, such as preventing the baby from growing too large for delivery, preventing skin marks, or warding off evil spirits.

Common restrictions often target high-protein foods like eggs, meat, snails, and certain fish, as well as nutrient-dense fruits. Ironically, these are the exact nutrients required for maternal blood expansion, fetal growth, and tissue repair. Consequently, pregnant women subjected to these dietary restrictions frequently present with severe iron-deficiency anemia, gestational edema, generalized weakness, and deliver low-birth-weight infants. Nurses must identify these hidden dietary practices during antenatal booking interviews and provide gentle, culturally sensitive nutrition education.

Widowhood Rites and Reproductive Rights Violations

Widowhood rites in various patriarchal societies subject grieving women to severe physical, emotional, and psychological degradation under the guise of proving their innocence regarding their husband's death or mourning him properly.

  • Forced Cleansing: Widows are often forced to drink water used to wash their deceased husband's corpse or submit to ritual sexual cleansing with a stranger.
  • Physical Isolation: Women are confined to dark, unventilated rooms for weeks, sitting on bare floors without proper hygiene.
  • Deprivation: Widows are barred from bathing, changing clothes, eating warm meals, or touching community members.
  • Dispossession: In-laws frequently seize the widow's property, land, and children, plunging her into extreme poverty.

These practices violate basic human and reproductive rights. The extreme stress, trauma, and unhygienic conditions put these vulnerable women at high risk for major depressive disorder, severe infections, and destitution.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of Affected Patients

Identifying patients suffering from the consequences of harmful traditional practices requires a high index of suspicion, as victims often conceal the true origins of their injuries out of fear or loyalty to family members.

Nurses should observe for non-verbal cues such as reluctance to undergo physical examinations, flinching, extreme anxiety, or evasive answers during history-taking. During physical assessments, midwives and nurses must conduct gentle, trauma-informed pelvic and general examinations while looking for specific scars, missing anatomical structures, or chronic fistulas. It is vital to establish a confidential, non-judgmental environment. Asking open-ended questions about cultural practices and reproductive health history allows patients to share their experiences safely without feeling interrogated.

Nursing Management, Psychological Support, and Rehabilitation

Caring for survivors of harmful traditional practices extends far beyond treating physical wounds. Comprehensive nursing management combines clinical interventions with compassionate psychosocial rehabilitation.

  • Physical Care: Wound care for mutilation sites, surgical repair preparation for fistulas, pain management, and nutritional support for anemic patients.
  • Psychological Support: Active listening, counseling, and addressing anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).
  • Social Reintegration: Connecting patients with social workers, legal aid organizations, and women's advocacy groups to help them regain financial independence and safety.
  • Empowerment and Education: Providing health literacy regarding reproductive anatomy and autonomy so patients can make informed choices about their bodies.

Nurses act as advocates, educators, and healers, guiding vulnerable patients from trauma toward recovery and self-reliance.

Legal Frameworks, Advocacy, and Prevention in Nigeria

Eradicating harmful traditional practices requires robust legislative frameworks combined with community-level advocacy and education. In Nigeria, legal instruments such as the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act provide federal backing to criminalize practices like female genital mutilation, harmful widowhood rites, and forced child marriage.

However, laws alone are insufficient without grassroots enforcement and community engagement. Nurses play a crucial role in public health advocacy by partnering with traditional and religious leaders who hold sway over local customs. By translating clinical facts into relatable community discussions, nurses help dispel myths and promote health-seeking behaviors. Prevention efforts succeed when communities shift from viewing customs as absolute laws to recognizing them as choices that can be modified to protect the health of women and children.
