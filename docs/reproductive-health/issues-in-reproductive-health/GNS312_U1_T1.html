Overview of Harmful Traditional Practices

Introduction and Core Concepts of Harmful Traditional Practices

Harmful traditional practices refer to cultural, social, or religious behaviors and customs that violate the fundamental human rights, health, and bodily integrity of individuals, particularly women and girls. These practices are deeply rooted in patriarchal structures, systemic gender inequality, and long-standing historical customs transmitted across generations. While proponents often frame them as cultural preservation, international human rights bodies classify them as forms of violence and discrimination. In healthcare settings across sub-Saharan Africa, recognizing these behaviors requires looking beyond the immediate physical presentation to understand the underlying cultural pressures forcing individuals to comply. Nursing professionals encounter the physical and psychological fallout of these customs regularly, making a firm grasp of their societal roots essential for effective, non-judgmental care delivery.

Socio-Cultural Drivers and Community Pressures

  • Patriarchy: Social systems where men hold primary power and dominate roles in political leadership, moral authority, and property control.
  • Social Conformity: Fear of ostracization, community backlash, and being labeled an outcast drives families to continue dangerous rituals.
  • Economic Dependency: Lack of financial autonomy forces women to rely on male relatives, limiting their ability to refuse harmful customs.
  • Myth and Folklore: Unfounded beliefs linking specific practices to fertility, purity, or spiritual protection sustain compliance across generations.

Female Genital Mutilation: Classification and Physical Complications

Female genital mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Classification ranges from type one, involving partial or total removal of the clitoris, up to type four, which includes all other harmful procedures such as piercing, scraping, and infibulation. Immediate clinical complications include excruciating pain, severe hemorrhage, acute urinary retention, and shock. Long-term sequelae involve chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, keloid formation, dyspareunia, and severe obstetric complications during childbirth, such as prolonged labor and increased fetal distress.

Child Marriage and Early Adolescent Pregnancy Risks

Child marriage is a formal marriage or informal union entered into before an individual reaches eighteen years of age. It abruptly terminates a young girl's education, strips away her autonomy, and exposes her to early sexual initiation before her reproductive tract has fully matured. The primary obstetric hazard associated with early adolescent pregnancy is obstructed labor, which frequently results from cephalopelvic disproportion because the pelvis is narrow and underdeveloped. Furthermore, young mothers face extraordinarily high rates of vesicovaginal fistula, postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, and elevated neonatal mortality rates due to low birth weight and prematurity.

Nutritional Taboos and Dietary Restrictions During Pregnancy

In various rural communities, cultural taboos forbid pregnant women from consuming nutrient-dense foods such as eggs, meat, fish, and certain fruits. The rationale behind these restrictions often relies on superstitious fears, such as believing that eating meat will cause the unborn child to develop animal-like traits or that consuming eggs leads to infant greediness. Clinically, these dietary limitations precipitate severe micro- and macronutrient deficiencies, leading directly to iron-deficiency anemia, gestational protein-energy malnutrition, and poor intrauterine fetal growth. Nurses must identify these practices during antenatal booking interviews to provide targeted nutritional counseling without alienating the patient or offending local elders.

Widowhood Rites and Violations of Reproductive Autonomy

Widowhood practices in some cultures subject grieving women to degrading, unhygienic, and psychologically damaging rituals. These can include forced ingestion of water used to wash the deceased husband's corpse, prolonged isolation, shaving of body hair, and dispossession of property. From a reproductive health standpoint, certain communities mandate ritual cleansing through forced sexual intercourse with a relative of the deceased husband, exposing the widow to sexually transmitted infections and violating her bodily autonomy entirely. Nurses caring for recently bereaved women must remain vigilant for signs of acute depressive crisis, trauma, and systemic infections linked to these ceremonies.

Clinical Assessment and Identification Strategies

Identifying victims of harmful traditional practices requires a high index of suspicion, a trauma-informed approach, and exceptional communication skills. Patients may present with vague somatic complaints, chronic pelvic pain, uncharacteristic scarring, or psychological withdrawal. Clinicians should conduct private interviews away from accompanying family members or community elders who might exert coercive control over the patient. Asking open-ended questions about traditional customs, birth history, and physical changes builds trust and encourages disclosure. Documenting findings objectively using descriptive terminology without expressing moral outrage is essential for both legal records and continuity of multidisciplinary care.

Nursing Management, Psychological Support, and Rehabilitation

Comprehensive nursing management for survivors of harmful traditional practices integrates acute physical stabilization with long-term psychological rehabilitation. Physical care involves wound management, infection control, surgical referrals for reconstructive procedures like fistula repair, and nutritional repletion. Psychological support requires active listening, trauma counseling, and referral to professional mental health specialists for post-traumatic stress disorder. Nurses also act as educators and coordinators, collaborating with social workers, legal aid organizations, and community leaders to ensure the patient is protected from repeat exposure upon discharge.

Legal Frameworks and Prevention Strategies in Nigeria

Addressing harmful traditional practices requires robust legislative instruments backed by grassroots community engagement. In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act provides a vital legal framework criminalizing various forms of harmful practices, including female genital mutilation and harmful widowhood rites. However, legislation alone is insufficient due to deep-seated cultural normalization. Effective prevention strategies rely on community dialogue involving local chiefs, religious leaders, and youth groups to reframe traditions constructively. Educational outreach targeting young girls and boys helps dismantle patriarchal norms at an early age, paving the way for sustainable societal transformation.
