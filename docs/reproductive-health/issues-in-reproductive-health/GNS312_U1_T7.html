FGM Elimination Strategies and Policies in Nigeria

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation in Nigeria

Female Genital Mutilation, often called FGM, involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia for non-medical reasons. In Nigeria, this practice is deeply rooted in cultural norms, myths about chastity, and traditional beliefs regarding purity and marriageability. The practice is classified into four distinct types, ranging from the removal of the clitoral hood to severe stitching of the vaginal opening, known as infibulation. Nigerian girls and women who undergo FGM face immediate risks such as severe pain, heavy bleeding, shock, and infections. Over time, survivors often struggle with chronic pain, painful urination, scarring, and severe complications during childbirth. Recognizing FGM as a violation of human rights is the first step toward building effective strategies for its elimination across the country.

National Legislation and the VIO Act

At the federal level, the strongest legal tool against this practice is the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act, passed in 2015. While the VAPP Act criminalizes FGM nationwide, carrying penalties of imprisonment and fines, its enforcement depends heavily on domestication by individual states. Many states took years to adopt the federal law into their own legal systems, creating a fragmented landscape where a practice is illegal in one state but operates in a legal grey area just across the border. Federal agencies like the National Agency for the Prohibition of Trafficking in Persons (NAPTIP) play a key coordination role, yet federal mandates alone cannot stop deep-seated traditions without strong local enforcement.

State-Level Policies and Anti-FGM Laws

Because health and criminal laws fall under both federal and state jurisdictions, state-level legislation remains vital. Several states across the South-West and South-East, where FGM prevalence has historically been high, have enacted specific laws banning the practice and establishing specialized task forces. These state laws empower local law enforcement and community vigilantes to arrest practitioners, often referred to locally as traditional cutters. However, inconsistent implementation remains a major hurdle. States with active anti-FGM committees tend to see greater drops in prevalence, while states lacking dedicated funding often struggle to move laws from paper into active community protection.

Community-Based Approaches and Grassroots Mobilization

Policies enacted in federal and state capitals rarely succeed without the active participation of grassroots communities. Community-led initiatives, often supported by non-governmental organizations and international partners, focus on shifting social norms through open dialogues and public declarations. When an entire village or clan publicly abandons FGM, it removes the fear of social exclusion or marriage stigma for families who choose not to cut their daughters.

  • Town Hall Meetings: Engaging community members in transparent discussions about health risks.
  • Youth Advocacy Groups: Empowering young people to reject FGM as a prerequisite for marriage.
  • Alternative Rite of Passage: Creating ceremonial coming-of-age events that celebrate adulthood without physical cutting.

Role of Healthcare Professionals in FGM Prevention

A disturbing trend in the persistence of FGM is medicalization—the practice where doctors, nurses, midwives, or community health workers perform the procedure, falsely believing it is safer than traditional methods. Medicalization violates medical ethics, human rights, and professional nursing standards. Nurses and midwives occupy a frontline position to stop this trend. During antenatal visits, immunizations, and community outreaches, nurses educate mothers and families about the true physiological dangers of FGM. Furthermore, nursing and midwifery councils in Nigeria strictly penalize any health worker found engaging in or supporting the mutilation of female genitalia.

Engaging Traditional and Religious Leaders

Traditional rulers and religious clerics hold immense moral authority in Nigerian society. Policies drafted in legislative chambers often fail if local custodians of culture oppose them. Conversely, when an influential emir, oba, pastor, or imam speaks out against FGM during sermons or council meetings, community attitudes shift rapidly. Many religious leaders now use sacred texts to clarify that FGM is a cultural tradition rather than a religious command. Collaborating with these leaders bridges the gap between top-down government policies and everyday community practices.

Challenges and Barriers to Policy Implementation

Despite the existence of laws and awareness campaigns, several formidable barriers hinder the complete eradication of FGM in Nigeria. Secrecy is a major obstacle; because the practice is illegal in many areas, families have driven it underground, performing it in remote villages or at night to evade law enforcement. Additionally, deep-seated patriarchal structures, inadequate funding for state anti-FGM agencies, and corruption further weaken policy enforcement. Misinformation also persists, with some community members believing that uncircumcised women are promiscuous or unclean.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and the Path Forward

Achieving an FGM-free Nigeria requires robust monitoring and evaluation frameworks to track progress, identify high-risk zones, and measure the impact of interventions. Data collection through demographic and health surveys helps policymakers see where prevalence is dropping and where targeted campaigns are still urgently needed. Moving forward, a multi-sectoral approach—combining strict legal enforcement, continuous healthcare worker training, community empowerment, and sustained funding—offers the most reliable pathway to protecting Nigerian women and girls from this harmful practice.
