FGM Elimination Strategies and Policies in Nigeria

Introduction to Female Genital Mutilation in Nigeria

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) remains a significant public health and human rights issue in Nigeria, deeply rooted in cultural norms, tradition, and misconceptions regarding female purity and chastity. In the Nigerian context, FGM involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. It is predominantly practiced during infancy, childhood, or adolescence, though adult women may also undergo the procedure prior to marriage or childbirth in certain ethnic groups. As nursing students and future healthcare professionals in Nigeria, understanding the epidemiology and cultural drivers of FGM is crucial for effective patient assessment, advocacy, and community education. The practice leads to severe immediate and long-term physical, psychological, and obstetrical complications, including chronic pain, severe bleeding, recurrent urinary tract infections, keloid formation, psychological trauma, and obstructed labor leading to obstetric fistula. Recognizing the multifaceted nature of FGM helps nurses approach affected women and girls with cultural sensitivity, empathy, and evidence-based clinical care, ensuring that healthcare settings serve as safe zones for intervention, support, and prevention.

Legislative Frameworks and the VIO Act

Legislative frameworks are vital instruments in the national fight to eradicate Female Genital Mutilation in Nigeria. A landmark achievement in this legal battle was the enactment of the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) Act, commonly known as the VAPP Act, in 2015 at the federal level. The VAPP Act explicitly prohibits all forms of gender-based violence, including FGM, making it a criminal offense punishable by law with terms of imprisonment and financial fines. This federal legislation provides a strong legal backbone for prosecutors and human rights advocates seeking justice for victims and deterrence for perpetrators, including traditional birth attendants and medical personnel who perform the procedure. However, because Nigeria operates under a federal system, the VAPP Act initially applied only to the Federal Capital Territory (FCT) Abuja, requiring individual state legislatures to domesticate the law for it to take full effect across the federation. Nursing professionals must be aware of these legal provisions to effectively counsel patients, recognize legal protections available for young girls at risk, and understand their mandatory reporting duties when encountering suspected cases of abuse or impending mutilation within clinical or community practice.

State-Level Policies and Anti-FGM Laws

While federal legislation sets an overarching standard, state-level policies and specific anti-FGM laws are crucial for localized enforcement in Nigeria. Many states across the federation have taken the initiative to domesticate the VAPP Act or pass dedicated state laws criminalizing Female Genital Mutilation. These state-level laws account for localized sociocultural contexts, defining specific penalties, empowering state ministries of women's affairs and justice, and establishing pathways for community policing and reporting. Furthermore, some states have integrated anti-FGM mandates into primary healthcare policies, empowering local health workers to monitor vulnerable populations. However, the progress of state-level domestication is uneven; while several southern and north-central states have robust anti-FGM laws in place, some northern states lag behind. For nursing students, understanding state-specific legal frameworks is essential because nursing practice is regulated regionally, and healthcare providers must navigate local legal landscapes when managing cases of gender-based violence. Advocacy campaigns led by nurses often focus on lobbying state lawmakers to close legislative gaps and ensure that anti-FGM laws are actively enforced rather than remaining symbolic declarations on paper.

National Action Plans and Inter-Agency Collaborations

National action plans and inter-agency collaborations provide the coordinated infrastructure needed to drive sustainable FGM eradication in Nigeria. Spearheaded by federal bodies such as the Federal Ministry of Women Affairs and the Federal Ministry of Health, in partnership with international development partners like UNFPA and UNICEF, national strategies outline comprehensive multi-sectoral approaches. These frameworks integrate health, education, law enforcement, and social welfare sectors to ensure a unified response. Inter-agency collaborations facilitate resource mobilization, standardized data collection, capacity building for healthcare providers, and the implementation of large-scale public awareness campaigns. For instance, the National policy and plan of action for the elimination of FGM in Nigeria serves as a guiding document that aligns government commitments with grassroots interventions. Nursing educators and students must recognize that eradicating FGM cannot be achieved by the healthcare sector alone; it requires robust synergy between security agencies enforcing the law, ministries of education revising school curricula, and civil society organizations conducting community outreach. Through coordinated multi-agency efforts, referral pathways for survivors are strengthened, ensuring comprehensive medical, psychological, and legal support.

Community-Based Approaches and Grassroots Mobilization

Community-based approaches and grassroots mobilization are arguably the most effective strategies for shifting cultural norms and achieving sustainable FGM abandonment in Nigeria. Because FGM is deeply embedded in community identity and social acceptance, top-down legal prohibitions alone are often insufficient to change deeply held beliefs. Grassroots mobilization involves working directly with community members, youth groups, women's associations, and local gatekeepers to foster open dialogue about the harms of FGM. Interventions such as community-led total abandonment (CLTA) empower communities to collectively analyze the practice, weigh its health consequences against its perceived benefits, and publicly declare abandonment through ceremonial milestones. Nurses working in primary health care centers are ideally positioned to facilitate these grassroots conversations, utilizing participatory education methods that respect local dignity while firmly advocating for health and human rights. By empowering local champions and youth advocates, community-based approaches dismantle the fear of social ostracization that often compels families to continue the practice, ensuring that behavioral change rises organically from within the community.

Role of Healthcare Professionals in FGM Prevention

Healthcare professionals, particularly nurses and midwives, occupy a frontline position in the prevention, management, and eradication of Female Genital Mutilation in Nigeria. Nurses provide vital clinical care to survivors, treating immediate complications such as hemorrhage and shock, as well as managing long-term sequelae like chronic infections, dyspareunia, and psychological distress. In addition to clinical treatment, nurses have an ethical and professional duty to educate patients, families, and communities during antenatal visits, immunizations, and health outreach programs. A critical challenge within the healthcare sector is the medicalization of FGM—where healthcare workers themselves perform the procedure under the misconception that it is safer if done in a clinical setting. Nursing councils and professional associations in Nigeria strongly condemn medicalized FGM as a grave violation of medical ethics and professional oaths. Nursing students must be thoroughly trained to recognize, counsel against, and report any attempts at medicalization. By maintaining zero tolerance for medicalized FGM and serving as compassionate educators, nurses help dismantle the false belief that clinical settings can legitimize this harmful practice.

Engaging Traditional and Religious Leaders

Engaging traditional and religious leaders is a cornerstone strategy for successful FGM elimination in Nigeria, given the immense moral and cultural authority these figures hold over their constituents. In many Nigerian communities, traditional rulers, village chiefs, and religious clerics (both Islamic and Christian) are viewed as custodians of morality and tradition. When these leaders are sensitized and convinced of the adverse health impacts of FGM, they can use their platforms to issue decrees, preach against the practice during sermons, and influence community opinion far more effectively than external actors or government officials alone. Many progressive religious and traditional leaders in Nigeria have publicly declared that FGM has no divine or legitimate traditional backing, actively dismantling religious misconceptions that link the practice to scripture. Nursing students and public health advocates must collaborate with these key influencers, inviting them to health workshops and community dialogues. By transforming traditional and religious leaders into anti-FGM champions, public health advocates can accelerate behavioral change and overcome deep-seated resistance in conservative communities.

Challenges and Barriers to Policy Implementation in Nigeria

Despite the establishment of federal and state laws, action plans, and advocacy campaigns, several significant barriers hinder effective FGM policy implementation in Nigeria. Prominent among these challenges is weak enforcement of existing laws; law enforcement agencies often lack adequate training, resources, or sensitivity to handle gender-based violence cases, leading to underreporting and rare prosecutions of perpetrators. Additionally, deeply entrenched cultural secrecy surrounding FGM makes it difficult for authorities to detect clandestine procedures, as families often perform the cut in secret or during infancy to avoid scrutiny. Resistance from conservative traditionalists who view anti-FGM laws as an infringement on cultural heritage further complicates enforcement. Furthermore, inadequate funding for public health campaigns, poor inter-agency coordination, and a lack of accessible healthcare infrastructure in rural areas impede sustained outreach. For nursing students, understanding these systemic barriers is vital for realistic public health planning, emphasizing that legislation alone is insufficient without robust enforcement, ongoing community education, and economic empowerment for vulnerable rural families.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and the Path Forward

As Nigeria looks toward the future in its quest to eliminate Female Genital Mutilation, robust monitoring, evaluation, and continuous data collection are essential for measuring progress and refining interventions. Effective monitoring involves tracking the prevalence rates of FGM across different geopolitical zones, assessing the rate of state-level policy domestication, and evaluating the reach and impact of community-level sensitization programs. Reliable data enables policymakers, international partners, and healthcare organizations to allocate resources efficiently, identify high-risk areas, and adapt strategies to evolving trends, such as the alarming rise in the medicalization of FGM. Nursing researchers and clinicians play a pivotal role in gathering epidemiological data, conducting qualitative studies on community attitudes, and auditing clinical encounters involving FGM complications. The path forward requires sustained political will, consistent funding, rigorous enforcement of the VAPP Act across all states, and the empowerment of young people and healthcare professionals as advocates for change. By maintaining a data-driven, multi-sectoral, and compassionate approach, Nigeria can steadily move closer to achieving total abandonment of FGM and safeguarding the health and dignity of women and girls.
