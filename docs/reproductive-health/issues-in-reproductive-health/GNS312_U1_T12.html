Reasons for Early and Forced Marriage

Introduction to Early and Forced Marriage

Early and forced marriage represents a critical global public health and human rights issue affecting millions of children and adolescents worldwide, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Defined internationally as any marriage where at least one party is under the age of 18, it is often entered into without the free and full consent of the individuals involved. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding this phenomenon is vital because it directly intersects with maternal health, pediatric care, and community wellbeing. Child brides face profound physiological vulnerabilities due to biological immaturity, leading to high rates of obstetric fistulas, severe hemorrhage, and elevated maternal and infant mortality rates. Furthermore, early marriage abruptly halts a young person's personal development, trapping them in cycles of dependency and powerlessness. Nurses frequently encounter the devastating sequelae of early marriage in emergency rooms, antenatal clinics, and community health outposts. Recognizing the foundational definitions and scope of this issue allows healthcare professionals to identify at-risk populations early and provide comprehensive, empathetic care. Addressing early and forced marriage requires a multifaceted approach that goes beyond clinical treatment to encompass socioeconomic, educational, and legislative reforms. By viewing this practice through a public health and human rights lens, nursing professionals can better advocate for vulnerable populations and contribute meaningfully to the eradication of harmful traditional practices across Nigerian communities.

Poverty and Economic Hardship

Poverty and economic hardship serve as powerful drivers behind the persistence of early and forced marriage in many vulnerable communities. In resource-poor settings, families struggling with severe financial distress often view a young daughter as an economic burden. Marrying off a female child can be perceived as a survival strategy to reduce household expenses, decrease the number of mouths to feed, and secure immediate financial relief through the payment of a bride price or dowry. In some instances, families accumulate debts and utilize marriage arrangements as a form of transaction to settle financial obligations or secure loans. Furthermore, impoverished households often prioritize the education and economic advancement of male children over female children, assuming that girls will eventually move into another household, making investment in their education seem economically wasteful to struggling parents. This economic vulnerability strips young girls of their autonomy, forcing them into adult roles before they are psychologically or physically prepared. For healthcare providers, recognizing poverty as a root cause helps contextualize the desperation families may feel, shifting the nursing approach from purely punitive judgments to compassionate, community-based empowerment and poverty-alleviation initiatives. Addressing the economic dimensions through vocational training, financial literacy, and micro-credit programs for women can significantly reduce the incidence of economically motivated child marriages.

Cultural Traditions and Societal Norms

Cultural traditions and societal norms play a massive role in sustaining the practice of early and forced marriage across various regions. Deeply entrenched customs often dictate that marriage is a communal alliance rather than an individual choice, where elders and extended family members hold the ultimate authority over a young person's life trajectory. In many traditional societies, rites of passage mark the transition from childhood directly into adulthood, immediately followed by betrothal and marriage, leaving no room for adolescence or personal exploration. These unwritten rules are passed down through generations, creating a powerful social expectation that compliance is synonymous with respect, honor, and good moral character. Families who attempt to break these traditional cycles often face severe ostracism, social stigma, and alienation from their communities. Cultural expectations may also dictate strict codes of female modesty and behavior, making early marriage a mechanism to ensure social conformity. For nursing students and community health practitioners, navigating these deeply rooted cultural landscapes requires immense cultural competence, sensitivity, and patience. Effective advocacy cannot rely solely on condemning traditions; instead, it requires engaging with respected community and religious leaders to foster dialogue, reinterpret harmful customs, and promote alternative rites of passage that safeguard the health and rights of young people.

Gender Inequality and Patriarchal Systems

Gender inequality and patriarchal systems form the ideological backbone of early and forced marriage, disproportionately targeting young girls and reinforcing their subordinate status in society. Patriarchy establishes a social hierarchy where men hold primary power and dominate roles in political leadership, moral authority, social privilege, and control of property. Within this framework, females are often valued primarily for their reproductive capacity, domestic labor, and sexual compliance rather than their intellectual or professional potential. Early marriage becomes a tool to assert and maintain male control over female bodies, autonomy, and decision-making from an early age. When a young girl is married off to an older, established man, the power dynamic is inherently skewed, silencing her voice and denying her equal rights within the household. This systemic inequality restricts girls from participating in economic, educational, and political spheres, perpetuating a generational cycle of subjugation. Nurses must recognize gender inequality as a determinant of health, as power imbalances directly correlate with domestic violence, limited healthcare access, and psychological distress among women and girls. Promoting gender equality in healthcare education and community outreach empowers women to make informed choices about their bodies, reproductive health, and life goals, serving as a direct countermeasure to forced marital practices.

Protection of Family Honor and Social Status

The protection of family honor and social status is another potent driver of early and forced marriage, particularly in societies where female sexuality is viewed as the embodiment of family reputation. In such communities, any perception of premarital sexual activity, romantic involvement, or rebellious behavior by a young girl is considered a catastrophic stain on the family honor. To preemptively eliminate this perceived risk and preserve social standing, families may force a daughter into marriage at a very young age, believing that a husband will exert control and maintain her chastity. Similarly, in contexts of conflict or social displacement, families may marry off young girls rapidly to protect them from perceived sexual violence or to secure alliances with influential families, hoping to safeguard their social standing in an unstable environment. This practice stems from a culture of fear and victim-blaming, where the burden of upholding community morality is unfairly placed on the shoulders of young girls. For nurses, understanding the concept of family honor is critical when assessing patients who present with trauma, anxiety, or injuries resulting from forced marriages or related domestic violence. Healthcare professionals must provide a safe, non-judgmental environment, offering confidential support and appropriate referrals while navigating sensitive cultural imperatives that prioritize reputation over human rights.

Insecurity and Humanitarian Crises

Insecurity and humanitarian crises, including armed conflict, displacement, natural disasters, and extreme poverty, drastically amplify the incidence of early and forced marriage. When societal structures collapse, law and order disintegrate, and formal protection systems fail, families often resort to desperate coping mechanisms to ensure the survival of their children. In refugee camps and conflict-affected zones, parents frequently marry off young daughters out of a profound fear of sexual violence, abduction, or exploitation by armed combatants and strangers. Furthermore, the total loss of livelihoods and economic stability during crises leaves households unable to provide basic necessities, making the bride price or reduced household mouths an attractive survival calculation. Humanitarian settings also severely disrupt schools and community support systems, leaving adolescent girls idle, out of school, and deeply vulnerable to predatory influences. Nurses deployed in emergency humanitarian responses, refugee camps, and disaster-relief operations must be vigilant in identifying signs of forced child marriage among displaced populations. Providing trauma-informed care, establishing safe spaces for women and adolescents, and collaborating with protection agencies are vital nursing interventions during crises to safeguard vulnerable youth from exploitation.

Lack of Access to Education and Legal Protection

A profound lack of access to quality education and effective legal protection mechanisms creates an enabling environment for early and forced marriage to persist without consequence. Education is widely recognized as one of the most powerful protective factors against child marriage; when girls remain in school, they are equipped with critical thinking skills, financial literacy, and personal empowerment that delay marriage and childbearing. However, structural barriers such as school fees, long distances, lack of sanitation facilities, and cultural biases often force girls out of school prematurely. Concurrently, weak legal frameworks, inadequate enforcement of child rights laws, and corruption within judicial systems allow perpetrators of forced marriages to operate with complete impunity. Even when laws setting the minimum age of marriage at 18 exist on paper, poor dissemination, lack of community awareness, and the parallel operation of customary or religious laws often override statutory protections. Nurses must understand these systemic gaps because educational attainment and legal literacy directly correlate with health outcomes. Healthcare providers can act as vital educators within clinics and schools, promoting the importance of girl-child education and connecting victims of forced marriage with legal aid services, social workers, and protective legal resources.

Implications for Nursing Practice and Community Advocacy

For nursing students and practicing healthcare professionals in Nigeria, addressing early and forced marriage requires stepping beyond traditional clinical roles into active community advocacy and compassionate care. Nurses are often the first healthcare providers to interact with child brides presenting with severe reproductive complications, psychological trauma, malnutrition, or injuries from domestic violence. Providing trauma-informed care means creating a safe, confidential environment where young victims feel heard, respected, and supported rather than judged. Furthermore, nurses have a professional and ethical obligation to recognize mandatory reporting guidelines, protect vulnerable minors, and collaborate with multidisciplinary teams including social workers, law enforcement, and legal aid organizations. Community advocacy involves engaging local leaders, organizing educational workshops on women's health rights, and challenging harmful cultural practices through dialogue and health promotion. By integrating education on the dangers of early marriage into routine antenatal, immunization, and pediatric nursing encounters, healthcare providers can raise awareness at the grassroots level. Ultimately, empowered nursing practice actively confronts the structural determinants of health, championing legislative enforcement and community empowerment to eradicate early and forced marriage and secure a healthier future for Nigerian youth.
