Reasons for Early and Forced Marriage

Introduction and Definitions

Early marriage refers to any union where at least one party is under the age of eighteen. Forced marriage occurs without the free and full consent of one or both individuals, often involving coercion, threats, or manipulation. While these terms describe distinct human rights violations, they frequently overlap in clinical and community settings across sub-Saharan Africa. Recognizing the root drivers of these unions allows healthcare workers to identify vulnerable adolescents and provide appropriate psychosocial support.

Poverty and Economic Hardship

Financial desperation acts as a major engine driving families to marry off underage daughters. In resource-limited settings, parents may view a young female child as an economic burden or an extra mouth they can no longer feed. Marrying a daughter off can reduce household size, yield a bride price or dowry, and forge financial alliances with wealthier households. Unfortunately, this temporary economic relief often traps young girls in cycles of lifelong poverty and compromised health outcomes.

Cultural Traditions and Societal Norms

Deeply ingrained cultural expectations often dictate that a female's primary adult role is marriage and childbearing. Communities may practice generational customs where early betrothal preserves ethnic heritage or strengthens kinship ties. Defying these traditional pathways can result in severe social ostracization for the entire family unit. Nurses operating in these environments must navigate sensitive cultural dynamics while advocating for adolescent health and human rights.

Gender Inequality and Patriarchal Systems

Patriarchal structures concentrate power and decision-making authority in the hands of senior males, leaving women and girls with little autonomy over their own bodies. When females are viewed as subordinate assets rather than independent agents, their educational and career aspirations are routinely sidelined. This systemic imbalance facilitates child marriage by denying girls a voice in choosing their life partners or timing their reproductive milestones.

Protection of Family Honor and Social Status

In numerous societies, a family's reputation is closely tied to female chastity and sexual purity. Premarital sex or pregnancy outside of wedlock can bring profound shame upon an entire kinship network. To prevent perceived moral transgression or scandal, families may hurriedly marry off daughters at the first sign of puberty or adolescent interest in romance. This protective instinct prioritizes communal reputation over the physical and psychological well-being of the child.

Insecurity, Conflict, and Humanitarian Crises

- **Displacement**: Families fleeing conflict zones face severe destitution, pushing them toward child marriage as a survival mechanism. - **Protection Fears**: Parents often believe marrying off adolescent daughters in refugee camps safeguards them from sexual violence or abduction by armed groups. - **Breakdown of Services**: Institutional collapse during crises destroys legal and educational protections that normally deter early marriage. - **Orphaned Minors**: Conflict leaves many children orphaned, placing them under the care of extended relatives who may marry them off to reduce care burdens.

Education Gaps and Legal Deficits

Lack of schooling and weak legal frameworks create an environment where early marriage thrives. When girls are pulled out of school early, their future economic prospects evaporate, leaving marriage as their only viable option. Furthermore, ambiguous statutory laws, conflicting customary legal systems, and poor enforcement allow perpetrators to act without fear of prosecution. Closing these educational and legal gaps is essential for sustainable prevention.

Implications for Nursing Practice

Nurses encounter the physical and psychological fallout of early marriage daily through obstetric fistula, adolescent sepsis, high maternal mortality, and severe depression. Clinical encounters offer critical opportunities to screen for coercion and provide trauma-informed care. Beyond the bedside, nurse advocates must engage community leaders, educate families on reproductive health risks, and collaborate with social services to protect vulnerable minors from harm.
