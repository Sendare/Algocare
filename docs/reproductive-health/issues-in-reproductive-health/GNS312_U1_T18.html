Strategies for Elimination of Harmful Practices

Introduction to Harmful Traditional Practices in Reproductive Health

Harmful traditional practices (HTPs) in reproductive health represent deeply ingrained cultural behaviors that violate the fundamental human rights, bodily integrity, and health of women and girls. Within many communities across Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa, these practices persist due to historical precedents and patriarchal social structures. Common examples include female genital mutilation (FGM), child, early, and forced marriage (CEFM), virginity testing, and various forms of widowhood rites. As nursing students and future healthcare providers, understanding these practices is vital because they are major contributors to maternal morbidity and mortality, chronic physical pain, psychological trauma, and severe obstetric complications such as obstructed labor and obstetric fistula. HTPs are often shrouded in myths surrounding chastity, fertility, and cultural identity, making them difficult to eradicate without a comprehensive, compassionate, and culturally sensitive approach. Nurses are frequently the first point of contact for women suffering from the physical and mental sequelae of these practices. Consequently, recognizing the clinical indicators and social context of HTPs equips healthcare professionals to provide holistic care, advocate effectively for victims, and implement preventative measures within clinical and community settings. Eliminating HTPs requires a multi-sectoral approach that respects community members while firmly upholding the ethical principles of beneficence, non-maleficence, and patient autonomy.

Socio-Cultural Drivers and Public Health Impacts

The persistence of harmful traditional practices is fueled by complex socio-cultural drivers that vary across different ethnic groups and regions. Primary drivers include deeply entrenched gender inequality, the patriarchal desire to control female sexuality, poverty, lack of formal education, and the fear of social ostracization or stigma for families that do not conform. Public health impacts resulting from these drivers are profound and far-reaching. Practices like female genital mutilation lead to immediate complications such as severe hemorrhage, shock, and acute infections, as well as long-term consequences including chronic pelvic pain, painful sexual intercourse, psychological distress, and severe obstetric complications during childbirth. Child marriage frequently leads to early adolescent pregnancies before the pelvis is fully developed, significantly increasing the incidence of obstetric fistula, maternal mortality, and infant morbidity. As healthcare advocates, nurses must understand these root causes to design effective interventions that address the underlying societal beliefs rather than merely treating the physical symptoms. Addressing socio-cultural drivers requires dismantling myths, fostering open community dialogue, and shifting the cultural narrative toward the promotion of human rights, gender equity, and the preservation of reproductive health for all women and girls.

International and National Legal Frameworks

The elimination of harmful traditional practices is supported by a robust framework of international human rights conventions and national legislative acts. Globally, frameworks such as the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), the Maputo Protocol, and the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)—specifically Goal 5 on gender equality—provide strong mandates for governments to protect women and girls from violence and harmful practices. At the national level in Nigeria, legislation such as the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act and various state-level child rights laws and anti-FGM laws criminalize these abuses and establish legal penalties for perpetrators. However, the mere existence of these laws is insufficient; enforcement is frequently weak due to corruption, cultural complicity, fear of community backlash, and a general lack of awareness among the populace and law enforcement agencies. Nursing students must be familiar with these legal frameworks to recognize their duty of care, understand mandatory reporting obligations where applicable, and guide victims toward legal and protective support services. Bridging the gap between legislative policy and community implementation is essential for creating an environment where the rights of women and girls are legally protected and actively enforced.

Community-Based Interventions and Grassroots Advocacy

Community-based interventions and grassroots advocacy are among the most effective mechanisms for achieving sustainable behavioral change regarding harmful traditional practices. Because HTPs are deeply embedded in local culture, top-down legal mandates alone often fail unless communities themselves are actively engaged in the process of introspection and transformation. Grassroots initiatives empower local stakeholders—including youth groups, women's associations, and community health workers—to lead public conversations, education campaigns, and community pledges against practices like female genital mutilation and child marriage. Approaches such as community-led dialogues encourage open discussions about the health risks and human rights violations associated with these customs without causing defensiveness or cultural alienation. Nurses play a crucial role in these grassroots efforts by partnering with local leaders, participating in community outreach programs, and providing accurate, accessible health education. By fostering local ownership of the problem and the solution, grassroots advocacy helps dismantle social norms from within, creating a supportive environment where families can abandon harmful practices voluntarily and proudly without fear of social isolation or loss of community standing.

The Role of Healthcare Professionals in Prevention and Support

Healthcare professionals, particularly nurses and midwives, occupy a frontline position in the prevention of harmful traditional practices and the provision of compassionate support to survivors. Clinical roles encompass early identification of girls and women at risk, recognition of physical and psychological trauma during routine clinical assessments, and the delivery of non-judgmental, trauma-informed care. Nurses must be equipped to manage the immediate complications of HTPs, such as hemorrhage and infection, as well as long-term sequelae including chronic pain, obstetric fistula repairs, and psychological counseling for depression or post-traumatic stress disorder. Furthermore, nurses serve as educators during antenatal, postnatal, and pediatric visits, gently debunking cultural myths surrounding reproductive health. Beyond direct clinical care, nurses have an ethical obligation to advocate for policy changes, report abuse in accordance with national laws and professional codes of conduct, and collaborate with multidisciplinary teams—including social workers, legal aid providers, and psychologists. By maintaining a safe, empathetic, and confidential environment, healthcare professionals empower survivors to heal and act as trusted educators who can influence community attitudes toward health and wellness.

Empowering Women and Girls through Education

Empowering women and girls through formal and informal education is one of the most powerful long-term strategies for eliminating harmful traditional practices. Education expands economic opportunities, enhances critical thinking, improves health literacy, and delays the age of marriage and childbearing. When girls stay in school longer, they gain a better understanding of their human rights and reproductive anatomy, making them more resilient against societal pressures to conform to harmful customs such as child marriage or female genital mutilation. Educational programs should target not only adolescent girls but also boys and young men, fostering mutual respect and transforming patriarchal attitudes toward gender roles from an early age. Nurses and educators can collaborate to integrate reproductive health and human rights modules into school curricula and community youth programs. By equipping the younger generation with knowledge and self-efficacy, societies can break the intergenerational cycle of HTPs. An educated girl is better equipped to make informed choices about her body, negotiate safer sexual health practices, and demand quality healthcare, ultimately transforming public health outcomes across the community.

Engaging Traditional and Religious Leaders as Change Agents

Engaging traditional and religious leaders as change agents is a pivotal strategy in combating harmful traditional practices, as these figures hold immense authority and moral influence within Nigerian communities. Often, HTPs are defended on religious or cultural grounds, even when sacred texts do not actually mandate them. When respected traditional rulers, village elders, and religious clerics are educated on the severe health consequences and human rights violations associated with practices like female genital mutilation and child marriage, they can publicly denounce these customs from a position of trusted authority. This theological and cultural reframing reassures community members that abandoning HTPs does not mean betraying their heritage or faith. Nurses and public health advocates must actively build partnerships with these leaders, providing them with evidence-based data and inviting them to spearhead community declarations, public forums, and alternative rites of passage that celebrate coming-of-age without bodily harm. By transforming traditional and religious custodians into vocal advocates for maternal and child health, public health programmers can achieve rapid shifts in community norms and foster widespread compliance with protective laws.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and Sustainable Elimination Strategies

Monitoring, evaluation, and sustainable elimination strategies are essential to ensure that interventions against harmful traditional practices are effective, accountable, and enduring. Without rigorous data collection and continuous evaluation, programs risk wasting resources on strategies that produce no measurable impact on the prevalence of practices like female genital mutilation and child marriage. Monitoring involves tracking key performance indicators—such as the number of community dialogues held, cases reported, legal convictions secured, and changes in the prevalence rates of HTPs over time. Evaluation allows public health programmers and nursing leaders to assess what works, identify implementation barriers, and adapt interventions accordingly. Sustainability requires embedding these anti-HTP initiatives into national health policies, local government budgets, and primary healthcare delivery systems rather than relying solely on short-term project grants. Furthermore, intersectoral collaboration involving health, education, legal, and social welfare sectors ensures a comprehensive safety net for victims. For nursing students, understanding monitoring and evaluation highlights the importance of evidence-based practice and continuous quality improvement in public health nursing, ensuring that every effort contributes to a future free from harmful traditional practices.
