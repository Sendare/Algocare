Forced Marriage

Definition and Overview of Forced Marriage

A forced marriage is a union where one or both parties enter into the marriage without their free and full consent, often under duress, coercion, threats, or physical violence. Unlike an arranged marriage, where families introduce prospective partners but individuals retain the absolute right to refuse, a forced marriage strips away personal autonomy entirely. It is a critical public health and human rights issue that affects vulnerable populations globally, including young girls and women in various communities across Nigeria. Recognizing the distinction between cultural tradition and forced coercion is vital for healthcare professionals.

Socio-Cultural Drivers and Risk Factors

Several deep-seated socio-cultural factors drive the persistence of forced marriages. Poverty is a major catalyst, as some families view marrying off a young daughter as a way to reduce household financial burdens or secure bride price wealth. Patriarchal structures, harmful traditional practices, and religious misinterpretations also play significant roles. Additionally, during periods of conflict or economic instability, families may force marriages as a misguided protective measure for female relatives.

Physical and Mental Health Consequences

The health impacts of forced marriage are severe and long-lasting, affecting both physical and psychological well-being. Victims frequently experience early and forced sexual activity, leading to high rates of adolescent pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections, and obstetric fistulas due to physical immaturity. Psychologically, survivors suffer from high rates of depression, severe anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and suicidal ideation resulting from ongoing entrapment and abuse.

Identification and Clinical Assessment of Vulnerable Individuals

  • Behavioral cues: Noticeable withdrawal, fearfulness, reluctance to speak when a family member is present, or sudden unexplained absence from school.
  • Physical signs: Unexplained injuries, signs of sexual abuse, or untreated chronic health complaints.
  • Assessment strategy: Conducting private, one-on-one interviews away from accompanying relatives or supposed spouses to create a safe space for disclosure.

Legal Frameworks and Human Rights Violations in Nigeria

Forced marriage stands as a direct violation of international human rights and domestic laws. In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act explicitly prohibits forced marriage, rendering it a criminal offense. Despite such legislation, enforcement remains inconsistent across different states due to overlapping customary and religious legal systems. Nurses must understand these legal boundaries to effectively advocate for patients and know when to involve law enforcement or social protection agencies.

Nursing Interventions, Care, and Support Strategies

Nurses play a frontline role in caring for survivors of forced marriage by providing trauma-informed, non-judgmental care. Establishing trust is paramount, as victims often fear disclosure will bring further harm. Interventions include treating physical injuries, managing reproductive health needs, offering psychological first aid, and ensuring strict confidentiality. Nurses must also connect survivors with safe housing, legal aid, and social welfare services without putting them in greater danger.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration and Advocacy

Addressing forced marriage requires more than clinical treatment; it demands a coordinated multidisciplinary approach. Nurses must collaborate with social workers, legal aid organizations, law enforcement, and community leaders to build a protective safety net for the survivor. Advocacy involves educating communities about the harms of forced marriage and empowering young girls through health literacy. Through teamwork and active advocacy, healthcare providers help break the cycle of abuse and promote lasting social change.
