Forced Marriage

Definition and Overview of Forced Marriage

Forced marriage is a grave human rights violation and a form of gender-based violence where one or both parties are married without their free and full consent. Unlike an arranged marriage, where families assist in selecting a partner but the individuals retain the right to decline, a forced marriage involves coercion, threats, deception, physical violence, or psychological pressure that strips the individual of their autonomy. In many settings, this practice disproportionately affects young women and adolescent girls, though men and boys can also be victims. Victims are often trapped in unions established for economic gain, family honor, debt settlement, or social alignment. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding forced marriage goes beyond clinical care; it requires recognizing a profound social pathology that disrupts health, development, and basic human rights. Nurses must be able to distinguish between consensual cultural arrangements and coercive practices. Because victims rarely present saying directly that they are forced, recognizing the broader context of power dynamics, lack of personal choice, and overt control is the first step toward effective nursing intervention. Healthcare environments may be the only safe spaces where victims encounter independent professionals, making awareness of this definition critical for early detection and support.

Socio-Cultural Drivers and Risk Factors

The persistence of forced marriage is fueled by a complex interplay of socio-cultural drivers and risk factors. Poverty is a major economic catalyst, where families facing severe financial hardship may marry off young daughters to reduce household expenses or to secure a bride price and financial relief. Patriarchal structures and gender inequality also heavily contribute, as women and girls are often viewed as subordinate, commodities, or custodians of family honor rather than autonomous individuals. Cultural and religious misinterpretations, deep-seated traditional norms, and peer or community pressure further reinforce the practice, creating an environment where dissent carries severe social penalties. Risk factors that heighten an individual's vulnerability include being a female adolescent, belonging to marginalized or rural communities, lacking access to formal education, and having limited economic independence. Additionally, migration status, refugee circumstances, and the death of primary breadwinners can exponentially increase vulnerability, as families look to marriage as a coping or protective mechanism during crises. Recognizing these specific socioeconomic drivers and individual risk factors helps nursing students anticipate which populations are most at risk and approach patient histories with informed cultural sensitivity and a high index of suspicion.

Physical and Mental Health Consequences

Forced marriage inflicts profound, long-lasting physical and mental health consequences on victims. Physically, victims frequently experience early and forced sexual initiation, leading to high rates of adolescent pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and severe obstetric complications such as obstetric fistula. Sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, are common due to an inability to negotiate safe sex or condom use. Furthermore, victims are at an extremely high risk of chronic physical trauma resulting from domestic abuse, intimate partner violence, and physical restraint or torture by family members. The psychological toll is equally devastating, characterized by high incidences of depression, severe anxiety disorders, complex post-traumatic stress disorder, self-harm, and suicidal ideation. Constant fear, isolation, and the complete loss of autonomy dismantle a victim's self-esteem and mental resilience. For nurses assessing patients presenting with vague somatic complaints, chronic pain, unexplained injuries, or sudden behavioral changes, considering the sequelae of forced marriage is vital. Comprehensive nursing care requires acknowledging that physical healing cannot occur without addressing the deep-seated psychological trauma associated with these coerced and abusive living arrangements.

Identification and Clinical Assessment of Vulnerable Individuals

Identifying vulnerable individuals requires keen clinical observation, as victims rarely disclose their situation immediately due to fear of retaliation, shame, or lack of trust. Clinical assessment must take place in a private, confidential setting, ideally with a professional medical interpreter rather than a family member or accompanying partner, who may be the abettor. Nurses should look for clinical red flags such as reluctance to speak for oneself, an overbearing accompanying person who insists on answering all questions, unexplained physical injuries, frequent missed appointments, and sudden withdrawal from school or social activities. Direct, gentle, and non-judgmental screening questions should be utilized, such as asking if the patient feels safe at home, whether they were pressured into their marriage, or if they can make decisions about their own life. Assessment should also evaluate the immediate safety risks, access to identification documents, and support networks. By maintaining a high index of suspicion and utilizing trauma-informed interviewing techniques, nurses can uncover hidden cases of forced marriage and establish a vital lifeline for individuals trapped in abusive, coercive environments.

Legal Frameworks and Human Rights Violations in Nigeria

Addressing forced marriage requires a strong understanding of legal frameworks and human rights instruments that protect individuals from coercion. Internationally, forced marriage is recognized as a violation of human rights, breaching conventions such as the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), and the Maputo Protocol. In Nigeria, the legal landscape has evolved significantly; the passage of the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act of 2015 marked a milestone by explicitly criminalizing forced marriage at the federal level, imposing penalties for anyone who forces, tricks, or abets a forced marriage. Additionally, the Child Rights Act protects minors by setting the minimum age of marriage at 18, though implementation and enforcement vary across different states within the Nigerian federation. Nurses must understand these legal boundaries not to act as law enforcement, but to recognize when a crime has been committed, understand mandatory reporting obligations where applicable, and guide victims toward appropriate legal aid, protection services, and human rights organizations equipped to handle such violations.

Nursing Interventions, Care, and Support Strategies

Nursing care for victims of forced marriage must be grounded in trauma-informed principles that prioritize safety, empowerment, trust, and confidentiality. The immediate nursing priority is to ensure physical stabilization of any acute injuries, treat reproductive health complications, and provide a secure, private environment. Nurses must practice active, non-judgmental listening, validating the patient's emotional pain without pressuring them into actions they are not ready to take, which could escalate their danger. Care planning involves conducting a thorough risk assessment and developing a safety plan in collaboration with the patient, which may involve identifying safe shelters, emergency contact numbers, and trusted community supports. Documentation in the medical record must be meticulous, objective, and legally sound, recording direct quotes regarding coercion and detailed descriptions of injuries without compromising the patient's physical security if records are accessed by abusers. Furthermore, nurses facilitate holistic healing by connecting patients with mental health counseling, social services, and legal advocacy groups, ensuring that the survivor feels supported in regaining control over their body and future.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration and Advocacy

Combating forced marriage and supporting survivors effectively requires strong multidisciplinary collaboration and active professional advocacy. No single healthcare provider or discipline can handle the complex medical, legal, psychological, and social needs of a survivor on their own. Nurses must work hand-in-hand with social workers, physicians, mental health specialists, law enforcement agencies, legal aid organizations, and community or religious leaders who oppose harmful practices. Interprofessional case conferences ensure seamless coordination of care, safe shelter placement, and legal protection. Beyond the clinical setting, nursing students and professionals have a responsibility to act as advocates. This includes participating in community education campaigns to raise awareness about the harms of forced marriage, challenging patriarchal norms that enable gender-based violence, and advocating for the strict enforcement of laws like the VAPP Act. By bridging clinical practice with community outreach and advocacy, nurses contribute to dismantling the systemic structures that allow forced marriage to persist, ultimately protecting vulnerable populations across Nigeria.
