Forced Marriage

Understanding Forced Marriage and Its Scope

A forced marriage occurs when one or both parties enter into a union without their free and full consent, often under duress, coercion, physical threat, or psychological manipulation. Unlike an arranged marriage, where families assist in introducing partners but the individuals retain the ultimate right to choose and refuse, a forced marriage strips away personal autonomy entirely.

In many global regions, including parts of Nigeria, this practice intersects deeply with patriarchal structures, poverty, and harmful traditional practices. Victims frequently include young girls and adolescents, though adult men and boys can also be subjected to marital coercion. Recognizing this phenomenon requires looking beyond traditional wedding celebrations to uncover underlying elements of control, isolation, and lack of consent.

Socio-Cultural Drivers and Risk Factors

Multiple intersecting factors perpetuate the cycle of forced marriage. Economic vulnerability often drives families to marry off daughters early to reduce household financial burdens or secure bride wealth. Cultural adherence to preserving family honor, maintaining social status, or cementing tribal alliances also exerts immense pressure on individuals to comply.

  • Gender Inequality: Pervasive beliefs that undervalue female education and autonomy make women and girls prime targets for marital subjugation.
  • Poverty and Destitution: Using marriage as a survival mechanism during severe economic hardship.
  • Insecurity and Conflict: Displacement settings where families view marriage as a protective shield against sexual violence, ironically exposing victims to domestic abuse.
  • Religious Misinterpretation: Distortion of sacred texts to justify the subjugation of minors or non-consenting adults.

Physical and Mental Health Consequences

The health repercussions of forced marriage are profound, spanning acute physical trauma and chronic psychological distress. Victims frequently experience early, unprotected sexual initiation, resulting in high rates of adolescent pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections, and severe obstetric complications such as obstetric fistula.

Mental health suffers immensely due to chronic entrapment, isolation, and domestic abuse. Depression, severe anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and self-harming behaviors are prevalent among survivors. In severe cases, the psychological weight leads to completed suicide. Healthcare settings often serve as the first point of contact where these clinical manifestations become apparent to astute nursing professionals.

Clinical Identification and Assessment

Detecting a victim of forced marriage requires sharp clinical vigilance, as patients rarely present stating their marital situation outright. Instead, they visit clinics for vague physical symptoms, injuries, or routine antenatal care while accompanied by an overly dominant spouse or family member.

Indicator TypeCommon Signs in Clinical Practice
BehavioralHesitancy to speak, extreme submissiveness, looking to accompanying family members before answering
PhysicalUnexplained bruising, signs of repeated domestic trauma, untreated sexually transmitted infections
SocialUnexplained withdrawal from school, abrupt loss of social contacts, missing scheduled follow-up visits

Conducting a private interview away from accompanying relatives is the gold standard for uncovering hidden coercion.

Legal Frameworks and Human Rights in Nigeria

Forced marriage is a severe violation of international human rights, infringing upon the right to free consent, personal liberty, and bodily integrity. In Nigeria, the legal landscape has evolved to provide stronger protections, though enforcement varies across states.

The Child Rights Act (2003) sets the minimum age of marriage at 18 years, explicitly criminalizing child marriage, which is inherently forced due to a minor's inability to give legal consent. Furthermore, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act (2015) explicitly prohibits spousal battery, harmful traditional practices, and forced marriages in the Federal Capital Territory, with many state legislatures adopting localized versions. Nurses must understand these legal boundaries to ensure safe reporting and documentation.

Nursing Interventions and Care Strategies

Providing care for a survivor of forced marriage demands a trauma-informed nursing approach built on empathy, confidentiality, and safety. The immediate priority is establishing physical safety and treating acute injuries. Nurses must document all findings objectively, quoting the patient directly regarding statements of abuse or coercion.

Creating a personalized safety plan is critical, especially if the patient intends to return to an unsafe environment temporarily. Confidential record-keeping prevents perpetrators from accessing sensitive notes. Nurses also facilitate referrals to psychological counseling, social welfare agencies, and legal aid organizations while avoiding any judgmental language or victim-blaming attitudes.

Multidisciplinary Collaboration and Advocacy

No single healthcare professional can tackle the complex repercussions of forced marriage in isolation. Effective management requires seamless collaboration among nurses, physicians, social workers, legal aid providers, law enforcement, and non-governmental organizations (NGOs).

Nurses act as pivotal advocates within this network, bridging the gap between clinical care and community protection services. By participating in community sensitization programs, healthcare workers help dismantle harmful cultural norms and educate families on the legal and health consequences of forced unions. Empowering vulnerable populations through active advocacy safeguards human rights and promotes holistic health equity.
