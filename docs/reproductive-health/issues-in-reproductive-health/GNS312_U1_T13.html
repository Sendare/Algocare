Consequences of Early and Forced Marriage

Understanding Early and Forced Marriage

Early and forced marriage refers to any formal marriage or informal union where one or both parties are under the age of 18, entered into without their free and full consent. While often rooted in traditional customs, economic hardship, or gender inequality, it functions as a major violation of human rights. From a nursing and public health perspective, this practice is not just a social concern but a critical clinical emergency. Young girls forced into unions are thrust into adult roles, sexual relationships, and childbearing before their bodies and minds are fully matured. Nurses encounter the downstream effects of this practice across every healthcare setting, from emergency departments treating trauma to antenatal clinics managing high-risk pregnancies.

Physical and Obstetric Complications

The female pelvis and reproductive tract continue to grow for several years after menarche. When a young girl undergoes pregnancy and childbirth before her body is structurally ready, the risks multiply exponentially.

  • Obstetric Fistula: Prolonged, obstructed labor creates pressure necrosis between the vagina and bladder or rectum, leading to continuous, humiliating leakage of urine or feces.
  • Pelvic Floor Damage: Immature pelvic muscles suffer severe tears and laxity, predisposing the young mother to uterine prolapse later in life.
  • Severe Hemorrhage: Uterine atony and severe bleeding frequently occur because the immature myometrium fails to contract effectively after delivery.
  • Maternal Mortality: Globally, complications from pregnancy and childbirth are a leading cause of death in girls aged 15 to 19.

Mental Health and Psychological Trauma

The psychological toll on a child bride is profound and enduring. Stripped of their childhood and adolescent development phases, these girls experience intense isolation, powerlessness, and fear. Depression, anxiety, and complex post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are common clinical presentations. Many young brides face intimate partner violence and sexual coercion within their marriages, feeling unable to negotiate safe sexual practices or refuse unwanted advances due to their young age and dependent status. This chronic stress environment disrupts normal neurological development and emotional regulation, leaving scars that persist well into adulthood.

Educational Disruption and Social Isolation

Marriage and domestic responsibilities almost invariably force young girls to drop out of school. This abrupt termination of formal education robs them of foundational literacy, critical thinking skills, and vocational training. Without education, their ability to secure independent income or understand basic health information is severely compromised. Furthermore, early marriage often severs ties with peers, teachers, and community networks. Isolated in marital homes, these young women lose the social support systems crucial for emotional resilience and coping during crises.

Neonatal and Pediatric Risks

The health risks of early marriage extend directly to the children born to these young mothers. Babies born to adolescent mothers face significantly higher rates of premature birth, low birth weight, and birth asphyxia. Immature biological systems in the mother cannot always provide optimal intrauterine nutrition and development. In addition, young mothers often lack the knowledge, resources, and decision-making power needed for proper infant feeding, hygiene, and timely immunization, leading to higher rates of childhood malnutrition and infectious disease.

Nursing Assessment, Care, and Support

Nurses play a frontline role in identifying and supporting victims of early and forced marriage. Assessment requires high clinical suspicion and a trauma-informed approach, especially when treating young girls presenting with frequent injuries, unexplained pelvic pain, depression, or obstetric complications. Nurses must conduct sensitive, private interviews away from husbands or family members to create a safe space for disclosure. Care strategies include providing holistic physical treatment, psychological counseling, linking patients with social welfare services, and offering confidential health education on contraception, nutrition, and personal rights.
