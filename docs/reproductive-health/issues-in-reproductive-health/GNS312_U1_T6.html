Health Consequences of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation and Its Classifications

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia, or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. It is typically performed on young girls between infancy and adolescence, often driven by cultural, religious, or social beliefs about purity and modesty. The procedure has no health benefits and causes severe harm.

To guide clinical care and documentation, the World Health Organization classifies FGM into four distinct types:

  1. Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  2. Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  3. Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a sealing seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  4. Type IV (Other): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Nurses must understand these categories because the specific type of mutilation dictates the physiological challenges the patient will face throughout her lifetime.

Immediate Physical and Surgical Complications

The acute phase of FGM carries severe trauma because the procedure is often performed in unhygienic conditions by traditional practitioners using crude instruments like razor blades, knives, or broken glass without anesthesia.

Immediate complications demand urgent medical intervention and can quickly become life-threatening. Common acute risks include:

  • Severe Pain: Intense, acute nociceptive pain that can trigger neurogenic shock.
  • Hemorrhage: Uncontrolled bleeding from the rich vascular supply of the clitoris and labia, leading to hypovolemic shock.
  • Infection: Introduction of bacteria from unsterile tools, leading to tetanus, sepsis, pelvic cellulitis, and abscess formation.
  • Urine Retention: Swelling, pain, and spasm around the urethra preventing micturition.
  • Injury to Adjacent Organs: Accidental lacerations of the urethra, vagina, perineum, or rectum.

A common misconception is that acute complications only occur if the procedure is done deeply; however, even superficial cuts can cause fatal tetanus or massive hemorrhage in young children.

Long-Term Urological and Gynecological Sequelae

Survivors of FGM live with chronic, lifelong physical consequences that affect their daily comfort, urinary function, and reproductive health. Scar tissue formation, nerve entrapment, and chronic inflammation are major drivers of these long-term issues.

Women with Type III infibulation often experience prolonged micturition, taking several minutes to empty their bladders through a tiny pinhole opening. This stasis breeds bacteria, resulting in recurrent urinary tract infections that can eventually ascend and damage the kidneys.

Obstetric Complications and Risks During Childbirth

Childbirth for a woman with FGM, particularly Type III, carries significant hazards for both mother and baby. Dense scar tissue lacks elasticity and fails to stretch normally during the second stage of labor.

Obstructed labor is a frequent outcome, increasing the risk of perineal tears, obstetric fistula, and uterine rupture. Babies face severe hypoxia because the rigid scar tissue delays delivery, leading to low Apgar scores or stillbirth. To manage this safely, birth attendants often perform a surgical release known as defibulation—cutting the scar tissue to allow passage of the fetus—followed by perineal repair after delivery.

Psychological, Emotional, and Sexual Health Impacts

The trauma of FGM extends far beyond physical scars, deeply affecting psychological well-being and sexual function. The experience is often perceived by the child as an act of betrayal by trusted caregivers, leading to long-lasting emotional distress.

Psychologically, survivors may suffer from anxiety, depression, chronic post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and persistent feelings of incompleteness or low self-esteem. Sexually, the removal of highly sensitive erectile tissue and the presence of painful scar tissue can cause dyspareunia (painful intercourse), lack of sexual desire, and difficulty achieving orgasm. These intimate struggles frequently create severe tension in marital relationships.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Nurses encounter FGM survivors in various settings, including emergency rooms, antenatal clinics, and general outpatient departments. Identifying FGM requires a high index of suspicion, clinical sensitivity, and excellent communication skills.

When conducting a physical or history assessment, nurses must approach the patient with cultural humility and absolute confidentiality. Avoid displaying shock or judgment during pelvic examinations. Look for unexplained pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, or difficulties during routine gynecological exams like Pap smears. Asking gentle, open-ended questions about menstrual health and previous surgeries helps build trust and encourages the patient to share her history without feeling stigmatized.

Holistic Management and Patient Care Strategies

Caring for FGM survivors requires a multidisciplinary approach that addresses both physical healing and emotional recovery. Treatment plans must be individualized based on the patient's specific symptoms and life stage.

  • Medical and Surgical Care: Managing chronic infections with targeted antibiotics, performing surgical unsealing when necessary for childbirth or severe obstruction, and offering pain management for neuromas.
  • Psychological Support: Providing trauma-informed counseling or referring the patient to mental health specialists to address anxiety and depression.
  • Patient Education: Teaching proper perineal hygiene and explaining anatomical changes to help patients understand their own bodies.

Nurses coordinate these services, acting as a bridge between the patient, gynecologists, mental health counselors, and social workers.

Prevention, Advocacy, and the Role of the Nurse in Nigeria

In Nigeria, FGM remains a significant public health and human rights concern, despite federal legislation such as the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act, which bans the practice. Cultural persistence, tradition, and fear of social exclusion continue to drive the practice in certain regions.

Nurses play a pivotal role on the frontlines of prevention and advocacy. Nurses can:

  • Educate community leaders, mothers, and families about the severe health risks of FGM during antenatal and immunization visits.
  • Challenge cultural myths that link the practice to morality or chastity.
  • Advocate for policy enforcement and support community-based awareness campaigns.

By empowering women and girls through education, nurses help break the generational cycle of FGM.
