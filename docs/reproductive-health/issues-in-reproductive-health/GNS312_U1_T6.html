Health Consequences of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation and its Classification

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Rooted in deep cultural, religious, and social traditions across parts of Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, the practice is internationally recognized as a violation of human rights and bodily autonomy.

The World Health Organization classifies FGM into four distinct types to standardize documentation and clinical research:

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and apposing the labia minora and/or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV (Unclassified): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Immediate Physical and Surgical Complications

The execution of FGM, often performed in non-sterile community settings using crude instruments like razor blades, knives, or broken glass without anesthesia, triggers immediate physical trauma. Severe pain is universally experienced, accompanied by acute hemorrhage resulting from injury to the dorsal clitoral artery or labial branches.

Local infection represents another critical early hazard due to unhygienic cutting tools and application of traditional healing substances containing animal dung, herbs, or ashes to stop bleeding. Ascending infections can lead to pelvic inflammatory disease, tetanus, and septicemia. Furthermore, accidental damage to adjacent anatomical structures such as the urethra, bladder, or rectum occurs when procedures are rushed or performed by untrained practitioners in poor lighting.

Long-Term Urological and Gynecological Sequelae

Women who survive the immediate trauma of FGM frequently endure chronic, life-long physical morbidity. Scar tissue formation, keloids, and neuroma development at the site of the severed clitoris cause persistent neuropathic pain and dyspareunia. Urological complications stem from mechanical obstruction and scarring around the urethral meatus, leading to recurrent urinary tract infections, chronic dysuria, urinary retention, and sometimes calculus formation in trapped urine.

Gynecological issues are equally pervasive. Hematocolpos occurs when menstrual blood is trapped behind a scarred, narrowed vaginal opening, particularly in infibulated women. Chronic vaginal discharge, bacterial vaginosis, and pelvic pain are standard complaints resulting from altered vaginal flora and trapped secretions.

Obstetric Complications and Risks During Childbirth

Childbirth for a survivor of FGM, especially Type III, carries exponentially higher risks for both mother and neonate. The rigid scar tissue and narrowed vaginal introitus lack normal elasticity, obstructing the normal descent of the fetal head. This mechanical barrier leads to prolonged and obstructed labor, increasing the incidence of uterine rupture, obstetric fistulae (vesicovaginal or rectovaginal), and severe perineal lacerations.

Fetal distress and birth asphyxia are common because the second stage of labor is significantly prolonged. Additionally, healthcare providers often need to perform a surgical incision called a defibulation or an extensive episiotomy during delivery to allow the baby to pass, which requires meticulous repair afterward.

Psychological, Emotional, and Sexual Health Impacts

Beyond physical scars, FGM inflicts profound psychological and emotional wounds. Survivors frequently report feelings of betrayal, anxiety, depression, and chronic post-traumatic stress disorder originating from the violent violation experienced during childhood. The somatic memory of the trauma can disrupt a woman's sense of bodily integrity and trust in caregivers.

Sexual health is severely compromised. The removal of highly sensitive erectile tissue, coupled with painful scarring, often eliminates sexual pleasure and leads to severe dyspareunia, anorgasmia, and psychological aversion to sexual intercourse. These dysfunctions frequently strain marital relationships and contribute to social stigma within communities where female sexuality is strictly tied to reproductive output.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Clinical assessment requires exceptional sensitivity, privacy, and cultural humility. Many survivors feel profound shame and may conceal their status unless questioned with empathy. Nurses must conduct thorough physical assessments during routine gynecological, prenatal, or admission screenings, paying close attention to anatomical alterations of the vulva.

When taking a health history, nurses should utilize trauma-informed interviewing techniques. Direct, non-judgmental questioning regarding urinary symptoms, painful menstruation, or sexual difficulties can uncover hidden complications. Documenting the specific type of FGM accurately in the medical record is essential for planning appropriate intrapartum and gynecological care without subjecting the patient to repeated, unnecessary examinations.

Holistic Management and Patient Care Strategies

Managing the health consequences of FGM requires a multidisciplinary team including nurses, midwives, gynecologists, mental health specialists, and urologists. Medical management focuses on treating active infections, relieving chronic pain with appropriate analgesics, and performing surgical interventions such as de-infibulation or scar revision when indicated for delivery or relief of hematocolpos.

Psychological support is equally vital. Referral to counseling services helps patients process trauma and manage sexual health concerns. In Nigerian clinical settings, community-based sensitization and involving supportive family members or partners in counseling can drastically improve rehabilitation outcomes and reduce domestic friction.

Prevention, Advocacy, and the Role of the Nurse in Nigeria

Eradicating FGM demands active community engagement and legal enforcement. In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act explicitly bans FGM, imposing penalties on practitioners and enablers. However, laws alone are insufficient without grassroots education.

Nurses occupy a frontline advocacy position in Nigerian healthcare. Through community health outreaches, antenatal classes, and school health programs, nurses educate families, traditional birth attendants, and community leaders about the severe medical hazards of the practice. By empowering communities with factual health information, nurses help dismantle cultural myths and protect future generations from the lifelong consequences of FGM.
