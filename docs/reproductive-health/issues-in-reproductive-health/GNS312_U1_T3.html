FGM Types

Introduction to Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Internationally recognized as a violation of the human rights, health, and integrity of women and girls, FGM has deep-seated cultural, religious, and social roots in various parts of the world, particularly within sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East, and parts of Asia. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding FGM is critical because Nigeria has one of the highest absolute numbers of FGM cases globally. As future healthcare professionals, nurses encounter survivors in diverse clinical settings such as obstetrics, gynecology, pediatrics, and community health. FGM is typically performed on young girls ranging from infancy to adolescence, often under unhygienic conditions by traditional practitioners without anesthesia. This practice is driven by myths surrounding chastity, modesty, hygiene, and marriageability, but it carries zero medical benefits. Instead, it results in severe physical, psychological, and obstetric complications that last a lifetime. Nurses must approach this sensitive topic with cultural competence, empathy, and a strong foundation in human rights and clinical management. Recognizing the different manifestations and health impacts of FGM equips nurses to provide holistic, non-judgmental care, advocate for affected women and girls, and actively participate in community-level educational campaigns aimed at abandoning the practice across vulnerable Nigerian communities.

Overview of WHO Classification System for FGM

To standardize research, clinical care, and legal policies globally, the World Health Organization (WHO) classifies Female Genital Mutilation into four major types based on the severity and anatomical structures altered during the procedure. This classification system serves as a vital clinical tool for healthcare providers, including nurses and midwives, enabling accurate documentation, targeted treatment plans, and effective communication among multidisciplinary healthcare teams. Type I involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce. Type II involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. Type III, known as infibulation, is the most severe form and involves narrowing of the vaginal orifice with the creation of a covering seal. Type IV encompasses all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization. Understanding this categorization allows nurses to anticipate specific medical and psychological challenges associated with each type. For instance, an obstetric patient with Type III FGM will require specialized intrapartum and postpartum management, including potential defibulation. By mastering the WHO classification, nursing students build a solid diagnostic and therapeutic framework necessary for delivering specialized, compassionate, and evidence-based care to women and girls affected by any form of genital mutilation.

Type I: Clitoridectomy

Type I Female Genital Mutilation, commonly referred to as a clitoridectomy, involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce, which is the clitoral hood. Within this category, the WHO further divides it into subtypes: Type Ia involves the removal of the clitoral hood or prepuce only, while Type Ib involves the removal of both the clitoris and the prepuce. Although often perceived by practicing communities as a lesser form of mutilation compared to infibulation, Type I is a deeply traumatic surgical assault that destroys highly sensitive erectile and nerve-rich tissue. The clitoris contains thousands of sensory nerve endings crucial for sexual arousal and pleasure. Its partial or complete amputation causes immediate physical trauma, intense pain, and significant hemorrhage. In the long term, survivors of Type I FGM frequently suffer from chronic pain, neuromas (painful nerve bundles formed at the scar site), scarring, and profound psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, and sexual dysfunction. For nurses, assessing a patient with Type I FGM requires sensitivity during physical examinations, as scar tissue and residual pain can make pelvic exams distressing. Nurses must provide a safe environment, validate the patient's emotional experiences, and offer supportive counseling or referral to psychological services when necessary.

Type II: Excision

Type II Female Genital Mutilation, known as excision, is characterized by the partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora. The WHO categorizes Type II into three distinct sub-types: Type IIa involves removal of the labia minora only; Type IIb involves removal of the clitoris and labia minora; and Type IIc involves removal of the clitoris, labia minora, and labia majora. This procedure results in extensive anatomical loss of external female genitalia, leading to more profound physical disfigurement and trauma than Type I. Because a larger surface area of vascular and sensitive tissue is excised, the immediate risks of severe hemorrhage, shock, and acute bacterial infection are markedly higher. Local wound healing often leads to extensive fibrosis, keloid formation, and the bridging of scar tissue across the vulva, which can partially obstruct the vaginal opening or urethral meatus. Consequently, women and girls with Type II FGM commonly report dysuria (painful urination), recurrent urinary tract infections, dyspareunia (painful intercourse), and psychological trauma related to body image and sexual health. Nurses caring for these patients must conduct thorough genitourinary assessments, manage wound complications gently, provide pain relief, and offer compassionate, trauma-informed psychological support to address both the physical scars and the emotional burden carried by the survivor.

Type III: Infibulation

Type III Female Genital Mutilation, internationally known as infibulation or pharaonic circumcision, is the most severe and debilitating form of FGM. It involves the narrowing of the vaginal orifice with the creation of a covering seal formed by cutting and repositioning the labia minora and/or the labia majora, sometimes with stitching, with or without removal of the clitoris. The WHO categorizes this into Type IIIa (removal and apposition of the labia minora) and Type IIIb (removal and apposition of the labia majora). To create this seal, the raw edges of the labia are often stitched together using thorns, thread, or silk, and the girl's legs are tightly bound together for weeks to promote healing and fusion. Only a tiny opening is left behind to allow for the passage of urine and menstrual blood. This creates profound and chronic health complications, including urinary retention, calculus formation, severe dysmenorrhea, chronic pelvic infections, and extreme pain during sexual intercourse. Obstetric complications are life-threatening, as the fibrous scar tissue prevents normal vaginal delivery, often necessitating surgical opening known as defibulation during labor. Nursing care for infibulated women requires immense sensitivity, specialized anatomical knowledge, and careful coordination during obstetric and gynecological procedures. Nurses must advocate for the patient, ensure pain is managed effectively, and resist any cultural pressure to re-infibulate the patient after childbirth.

Type IV: Other Harmful Procedures

Type IV Female Genital Mutilation comprises all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia done for non-medical purposes. This category includes a wide range of practices such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization of the clitoris and/or surrounding genital structures. Although these procedures may sometimes be perceived as less destructive than infibulation or excision, they still carry significant physical, psychological, and infectious risks. For example, ritualistic scraping or incising of the vaginal tissue can introduce pathogens leading to severe pelvic infections, tetanus, and sepsis. Piercing and pricking with unsterilized instruments pose high risks for blood-borne viral infections, including Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV. Cauterization involves burning the genital tissue, which leads to severe tissue necrosis, intense chronic pain, and extensive scar formation that can impede future reproductive functions. Nurses must be vigilant during clinical assessments, as Type IV practices can be subtle and easily overlooked if a comprehensive physical examination is not performed. When caring for patients with a history of Type IV FGM, nurses should provide thorough wound care, monitor for signs of infection, offer tetanus prophylaxis if indicated, and provide empathetic counseling while reinforcing health education against all forms of genital modification.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Consequences Across Types

Female Genital Mutilation across all its types inflicts devastating immediate and long-term health consequences on women and girls. Immediate complications occur during or shortly after the procedure and include severe hemorrhage, excruciating acute pain, traumatic shock, urine retention resulting from injury or swelling, acute local and systemic infections including tetanus, and sometimes death due to excessive blood loss or infection. Long-term consequences can persist throughout the lifespan of the survivor. Chronically, women experience persistent vulvar and pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, formation of painful neuromas, cysts, abscesses, and severe scar tissue complications like keloids. Sexual consequences include reduced or absent sexual desire, dyspareunia, lack of orgasm, and psychological trauma such as flashbacks, anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Obstetric and gynecological complications are particularly prominent among women with Types II and III, manifesting as prolonged and obstructed labor, obstetric fistulae, perineal tears, postpartum hemorrhage, and higher rates of perinatal and maternal mortality and morbidity. Nursing students must recognize that these complications extend far beyond the physical body, deeply affecting mental health, sexual well-being, and social integration. Comprehensive care requires interprofessional collaboration involving nurses, midwives, mental health specialists, and surgeons to address the multifaceted needs of affected patients.

Nursing Assessment and Care Considerations

Providing nursing care for patients with a history of Female Genital Mutilation requires a specialized, trauma-informed, and culturally sensitive approach. Nursing assessment must begin with establishing a safe, confidential, and trusting therapeutic relationship. Because patients may feel deep shame, anxiety, or fear of judgment, nurses must communicate with empathy and ensure privacy before conducting any physical examinations. A detailed genitourinary history should be obtained gently, noting the type of FGM, urinary symptoms, sexual concerns, and obstetric history. Physical examinations require explicit informed consent, gentleness, and the presence of a chaperone when appropriate. Nurses must manage localized complications such as wound infections, urinary retention, and chronic pain through evidence-based interventions. In obstetric settings, nurses must advocate for appropriate intrapartum management, including timely defibulation for infibulated women, and ensure that healthcare providers avoid the harmful and illegal practice of re-infibulation after delivery. Psychosocial care is equally vital; nurses should screen for depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder, offering empathetic listening and referrals to mental health professionals or support groups. Finally, nurses have an ethical and professional duty to act as advocates and educators in Nigerian communities, promoting the abandonment of FGM and protecting vulnerable young girls from enduring this harmful practice.
