FGM Types

Introduction and Global Classification of FGM

Female Genital Mutilation encompasses all procedures that involve partial or total removal of the external female genitalia, or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. It is recognized internationally as a violation of the human rights of girls and women. The World Health Organization classifies these procedures into four distinct types based on the extent of tissue removal and alteration. Understanding these categories helps nurses provide accurate documentation, recognize specific anatomical damage, and plan appropriate physical and psychological care for affected women.

Type I: Clitoridectomy

Type I FGM involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce, which is the fold of skin surrounding the clitoris. When only the prepuce is removed, it is classified as Type Ia, while the removal of both the clitoris and prepuce is classified as Type Ib. Though less extensive than other forms, this procedure still causes severe immediate pain, bleeding, and long-term psychological trauma. Women with Type I procedures may experience chronic dyspareunia and reduced sexual sensitivity.

Type II: Excision

Type II FGM goes beyond the clitoris by including the partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora. Similar to Type I, it has sub-categories depending on whether the labia minora alone are excised along with the clitoris, or if the labia majora are also partially involved in some overlapping descriptions. Sub-type IIa specifies the removal of the labia minora only, while IIb involves the clitoris and labia minora, and IIc includes the labia majora. The resulting scar tissue can obstruct vaginal examinations and complicate childbirth.

Type III: Infibulation

Type III, also known as infibulation, is the most severe form of FGM. It involves narrowing the vaginal orifice with the creation of a covering seal. This seal is formed by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or labia majora, sometimes through stitching, with or without removal of the clitoris. A small opening is left for urine and menstrual blood to escape. Women with Type III face severe urological problems, painful menstruation, and extreme difficulties during pelvic exams and childbirth.

Type IV: Other Harmful Procedures

Type IV covers all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes. Examples include pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterizing the genital area. Unlike the first three types, these variations may not always involve major tissue removal, but they still carry substantial risks of infection, hemorrhage, and psychological harm. Nurses must screen for these subtlemethods during patient histories, as patients may not recognize them as forms of FGM.

Health Consequences Across All Types

  • Immediate Complications: Severe pain, excessive bleeding, shock, urine retention, and acute infections.
  • Long-Term Complications: Chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, scar tissue obstruction, and painful intercourse.
  • Obstetric Complications: Prolonged labor, perineal tears, increased risk of caesarean section, and higher rates of infant resuscitation.
  • Psychological Impact: Post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, depression, and chronic fear regarding sexual health.

Nursing Assessment and Care Considerations

Providing care for women affected by FGM requires sensitivity, cultural awareness, and clinical competence. Nurses must conduct thorough health histories while maintaining absolute privacy and avoiding judgmental language. Physical assessments should be performed gently, explaining every step before touching the patient. For women with infibulation who are pregnant, planning for defibulation may be necessary before delivery. Mental health support and compassionate communication help build trust and address long-term trauma.
