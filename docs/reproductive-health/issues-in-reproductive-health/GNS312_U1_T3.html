FGM Types

Introduction and Overview of FGM Classification

Female Genital Mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. The World Health Organization categorizes these practices into four distinct types based on the anatomical structures altered. This classification helps healthcare workers communicate clearly, assess injuries accurately, and plan targeted interventions. Cultural roots run deep in practicing communities across parts of Africa and the Middle East, requiring nurses to approach affected women with cultural sensitivity balanced against firm human rights advocacy.

Type I: Clitoridectomy

Type I FGM involves the partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce. When only the clitoral hood or prepuce is removed, it is classified as Type Ia. Total removal of the clitoris along with the prepuce falls under Type Ib. Patients presenting with this type may experience severe localized pain, hemorrhage, and psychological trauma. During physical examinations, nurses must inspect the area gently, noting scar tissue, neuromas, or signs of chronic infection.

Type II: Excision

Type II FGM goes beyond the clitoris to include the partial or total removal of the labia minora. Subdivisions help specify the extent of the tissue loss:

  • Type IIa: Removal of the labia minora only.
  • Type IIb: Partial or total removal of the clitoris and labia minora.
  • Type IIc: Partial or total removal of the clitoris, labia minora, and labia majora.

Scarring from excision often leads to adhesions, chronic discomfort, and painful intercourse later in life. Nurses should look for fused tissue or restricted vaginal introitus during assessments.

Type III: Infibulation

Type III FGM, or infibulation, is the most severe form. It involves narrowing the vaginal orifice through the creation of a covering seal. This seal is formed by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or labia majora, sometimes through stitching, with or without removal of the clitoris. A small opening is left for urine and menstrual flow to escape. Women with infibulation require careful defibulation before childbirth or examination, and they face high risks of urinary retention, chronic infections, and difficult labor.

Type IV: Other Harmful Procedures

Type IV encompasses all other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes. This category includes pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization of the genital area. While these modifications might appear less anatomically destructive than infibulation, they still carry substantial risks of infection, bleeding, and psychological harm. Nurses must document any unclassified ritualistic alterations under this heading.

Immediate and Long-Term Health Consequences

The physiological and psychological fallout of FGM spans a lifetime. Immediate complications include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, shock, urine retention, and acute infections. Long-term burdens feature chronic pelvic pain, painful menstruation, keloid scar formation, recurrent urinary tract infections, and obstetric complications like obstructed labor and perineal tearing. Psychological sequelae involve anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder.

Nursing Assessment and Care Considerations

Providing care for women with a history of FGM demands exceptional clinical tact and empathy. During history-taking, nurses should use gentle, non-judgmental language, avoiding any tone of condemnation that might cause the patient to withdraw. Physical examinations require informed consent, clear explanations of every step, and a trauma-informed approach. Care plans must address pain management, psychological support, and referral for specialized surgical or obstetric care when necessary.
