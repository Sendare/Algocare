Elimination of Early and Forced Marriage

Defining Early and Forced Marriage

Early marriage, frequently termed child marriage, involves any formal marriage or informal union where at least one party is under eighteen years of age. Forced marriage occurs without the valid, free, and informed consent of one or both individuals, often orchestrated by family members or community elders. While these two concepts remain distinct, they heavily overlap within various cultural landscapes, stripping young people of their autonomy during formative developmental years. The practice disproportionately affects young girls, abruptly halting their education, social development, and personal agency.

Socio-Cultural and Economic Drivers

Multiple systemic factors perpetuate the cycle of early unions across vulnerable communities. Extreme poverty frequently pushes families to marry off young daughters to reduce household financial burdens or to secure bride wealth. Deeply entrenched patriarchal norms prioritize male authority and view female sexuality as property or family honor to be guarded. Additionally, religious and traditional misinterpretations often legitimize the practice, framing adolescent marriage as a protective measure against premarital pregnancy or societal disgrace.

Physical and Psychological Health Consequences

Adolescent bodies are biologically unprepared for sexual activity, pregnancy, and childbirth. Obstetric complications such as obstructed labor and severe hemorrhage remain prominent causes of morbidity among young mothers. Furthermore, prolonged tissue compression during obstructed labor frequently results in obstetric fistula, leaving survivors with chronic incontinence and severe social stigmatization. Psychologically, these young brides face high rates of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and social isolation due to abrupt separation from support networks.

Legal Frameworks and Child Rights in Nigeria

Legislative protections provide the formal foundation for eradicating early and forced marriage, though enforcement varies.

  • Child Rights Act (2003): Sets the legal age of marriage at eighteen years, prohibiting the betrothal of children. However, implementation depends on state-level domestication, creating regional legal disparities.
  • Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act: Criminalizes harmful traditional practices, providing pathways for prosecuting perpetrators.
  • Nigerian Constitution: Contains fundamental rights clauses, though overlapping customary and sharia legal systems sometimes complicate child protection mandates.

Identification and Clinical Assessment by Healthcare Providers

Nurses and midwives occupy a frontline position for identifying girls at risk or those already entrapped in forced unions. Routine clinical assessments during antenatal, immunization, or general outpatient visits offer critical windows for observation. Healthcare personnel must remain vigilant for subtle warning signs, including unexplained physical injuries, chronic pelvic pain, fearfulness in the presence of an older spouse, and sudden school dropout. Establishing a private, confidential space during consultations is vital so adolescents can disclose abuse without fear of retaliation from family members.

Community Advocacy and Preventive Interventions

Preventing early marriage requires direct engagement with community and religious leaders who hold significant influence over social norms. Educational outreach programs targeting parents emphasize the lifelong economic and health benefits of keeping girls in school. Peer education networks empower adolescents with knowledge regarding their sexual and reproductive health rights. When healthcare professionals collaborate with local civil society organizations, they create a unified front that challenges harmful traditions and promotes community-led declarations against child marriage.

Rehabilitation and Holistic Care for Survivors

Supporting survivors demands a multi-sectoral approach that addresses physical, psychological, and social needs. Safe spaces and temporary shelter facilities offer immediate refuge for girls fleeing abusive unions. Clinical management includes treating reproductive injuries, providing trauma-informed counseling, and screening for sexually transmitted infections. Reintegration programs must also focus on vocational training, financial literacy, and educational catch-up classes to ensure long-term independence and prevent destitution.
