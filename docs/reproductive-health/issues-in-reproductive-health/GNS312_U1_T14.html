Elimination of Early and Forced Marriage

Understanding Early and Forced Marriage

Early and forced marriage refers to any union where one or both parties are under the age of 18, and where consent is compromised by pressure, tradition, or economic necessity. While often used interchangeably, early marriage emphasizes the age factor, whereas forced marriage highlights the lack of genuine consent. In many settings, these practices disproportionately affect young girls, abruptly ending their childhood and development. Nurses encountering young mothers in antenatal or pediatric clinics must recognize that youth alone is a major red flag for coercion, as these girls rarely have a voice in choosing their spouses.

Socio-Cultural and Economic Drivers

  • Poverty: Families facing extreme hardship often view marrying off a young daughter as a way to reduce household financial burdens.
  • Gender Inequality: Deep-seated cultural beliefs that value girls primarily for marriage and domestic roles rather than education.
  • Traditional Norms: Generational customs that prioritize lineage preservation or religious misinterpretations over child welfare.
  • Insecurity: Conflict and displacement settings where parents marry off daughters early out of fear for their physical safety or sexual violence.

Physical and Psychological Health Consequences

The biological immaturity of a young girl's body makes early pregnancy uniquely dangerous. Obstetric fistula, severe hemorrhage, and obstructed labor are devastating physical outcomes of premature childbearing. Beyond physical trauma, victims face profound psychological distress. Anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder are common among young brides who are socially isolated and trapped in non-consensual sexual relationships. A common clinical pitfall is treating a young girl's physical symptoms without screening for domestic coercion or abuse.

Legal Frameworks and Human Rights in Nigeria

Nigeria operates under a plural legal system consisting of statutory, customary, and Islamic laws, which creates complexities in protecting minors. Nationally, the Child Rights Act sets the minimum age of marriage at 18 years, yet not all states have domesticated it. Human rights instruments, including the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women, clearly state that child marriage is a violation of basic human rights, denying children their right to education, health, and a life free from violence.

Role of Nurses and Midwives in Identification and Prevention

Healthcare professionals occupy a frontline position in combating early marriage. Nurses and midwives must maintain high clinical suspicion when treating young girls presenting for antenatal care, family planning, or treatment for sexually transmitted infections. Prevention involves direct patient education during routine consultations, empowering young girls with knowledge about their reproductive rights, and identifying subtle behavioral cues of abuse or coercion during health assessments.

Community-Based Interventions and Advocacy

Sustainable elimination of early and forced marriage requires grassroots engagement. Healthcare workers collaborate with local leaders, religious figures, and youth groups to shift cultural attitudes. Advocacy programs focus on keeping girls in school, raising awareness about health hazards, and establishing community watch committees to report impending child marriages before they occur. Partnering with respected community elders helps bridge the gap between modern human rights principles and local traditions.

Support, Rehabilitation, and Care for Survivors

Survivors of early and forced marriage require comprehensive, multi-sectoral rehabilitation. Healthcare teams must provide trauma-informed care, addressing both the physical injuries of early childbearing and the deep psychological scars of abuse. Social support systems, safe shelter provisions, and educational reintegration programs are essential for helping survivors rebuild their lives. Nurses coordinate these services, ensuring that clinical treatment is seamlessly linked with social welfare and legal aid.
