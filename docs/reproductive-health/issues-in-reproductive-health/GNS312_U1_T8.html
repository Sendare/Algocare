Management of Female Genital Mutilation Complications

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation and Clinical Realities

Female Genital Mutilation, often abbreviated as FGM, encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. It is classified into four main types by the World Health Organization, ranging from type I (clitoridectomy) to type IV (all other harmful procedures such as piercing, scraping, or cauterization). In clinical practice across Nigeria, nurses frequently encounter women living with the long-term physical and psychological consequences of these procedures. Understanding the specific type of FGM a patient has experienced is vital because complications and required treatments vary greatly, particularly during childbirth and sexual intercourse.

Immediate and Long-Term Complications

The physical fallout of FGM starts the moment the procedure is performed and can persist throughout a woman's entire life. Immediate complications typically include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, shock, urine retention, and acute bacterial infections due to unsterile instruments. As the wounds heal, scar tissue develops, leading to chronic physical problems.

  • Keloids and Neuromas: Hypertrophic scars and trapped nerve endings cause persistent, localized pain.
  • Genital Tract Obstructions: Scarring can obstruct menstrual flow, leading to hematometra.
  • Chronic Infections: Trapped secretions and altered anatomy promote recurrent urinary tract infections and bacterial vaginosis.
  • Sexual Dysfunction: Dyspareunia and complete loss of sexual pleasure are extremely common.

Initial Clinical Assessment and Sensitive History Taking

Care for women affected by FGM requires an exceptionally high level of clinical tact, empathy, and cultural sensitivity. Many survivors experience deep shame, fear, or anxiety when discussing their anatomy with healthcare providers. Nurses must establish a trusting environment before attempting any physical examination. History-taking should explore urinary symptoms, sexual difficulties, menstrual patterns, and reproductive goals without judgment. During the physical assessment, the nurse must gently explain every step, gain informed consent, and document findings objectively using standard anatomical terms rather than colloquial or stigmatizing language.

Surgical Management: Defibulation and Reconstruction

Surgical intervention is frequently required for women with Type III FGM, also known as infibulation, where the labia minora or majora are stitched together. Defibulation is the surgical procedure to open the fused scar tissue, restoring normal anatomy for menstruation, urination, sexual intercourse, and childbirth. This procedure can be performed under local or regional anesthesia. In some settings, specialized centers also offer clitoral reconstruction surgery, though evidence regarding sensory recovery varies. Nurses play a crucial role in pre-operative counseling, reassuring the patient about what the surgery can achieve, and providing meticulous post-operative wound care to prevent new scar adhesions.

Psychological and Psychosexual Support

The emotional and psychological scars of FGM often run much deeper than the physical ones. Survivors frequently struggle with depression, chronic anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and severe distress regarding their sexual identity and marital relationships. Psychotherapeutic support must be integrated into routine nursing care. Counselors and mental health nurses help patients process trauma, rebuild self-esteem, and navigate intimate relationships. Couples counseling is often beneficial to educate partners about the physical realities of FGM, dispel myths, and reduce marital friction caused by dyspareunia or misconceptions about virginity and fidelity.

Obstetric and Gynecological Care

Pregnancy and childbirth present major hazards for women with a history of FGM. Infibulated scars are rigid and do not stretch easily during labor, leading to prolonged second-stage labor, severe perineal tearing, fetal distress, and obstetric fistula. Midwives and nurses must identify FGM early during antenatal booking visits. Antepartum planning should include scheduling a planned defibulation if it was not done earlier, or performing an intrapartum incision when the baby's head crowns. Routine episiotomies must be carefully managed to avoid compounding existing scar damage. Postpartum monitoring is equally critical to watch for postpartum hemorrhage and wound breakdown.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Advocacy

Nurses act as frontline caregivers, educators, and advocates for women suffering from the consequences of FGM. Daily care involves pain management, infection control, wound assessments, and empathetic listening. Beyond the bedside, nurses advocate for holistic healthcare policies that train all medical personnel in culturally safe management. Advocacy also means protecting vulnerable young girls who may be at immediate risk within their families or communities. By creating safe clinic spaces, nurses empower women to reclaim control over their bodies, health, and reproductive futures.

Prevention, Legal Frameworks, and Community Education in Nigeria

Eradicating FGM requires a multi-sectoral approach combining public health education, community engagement, and strict legal enforcement. In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act explicitly bans FGM nationwide, alongside various state-level laws. However, laws alone cannot change deeply entrenched cultural beliefs. Nurses and community health workers play a pivotal role in grassroots sensitization programs. By collaborating with local leaders, religious figures, and youth groups, healthcare professionals can dispel myths linking FGM to chastity or hygiene, thereby protecting future generations of girls from harm.
