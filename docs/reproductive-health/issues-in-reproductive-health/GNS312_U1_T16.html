Gishiri Cut

Definition and Overview of Gishiri Cut

Gishiri cut is a traditional surgical practice performed predominantly in northern Nigeria and some other West African regions. The term **'gishiri'** translates from Hausa as 'salt'. Traditional birth attendants or local healers perform this procedure using unsterilized blades, knives, or razor blades to cut the anterior vaginal wall, posterior vaginal wall, or perineum. The intervention is rooted in deeply held cultural beliefs regarding female reproductive anatomy and pathology. For student nurses encountering this practice in clinical settings, understanding that the procedure is born from traditional etiology rather than malice is essential for non-judgmental patient interactions. Despite public health campaigns, the practice persists in rural pockets due to deeply entrenched social norms.

Cultural Drivers and Societal Context

Cultural practices surrounding women's health in parts of Nigeria are heavily influenced by patriarchal structures and traditional explanatory models of disease. When women experience prolonged obstructed labor, secondary infertility, dyspareunia, or neonatal illnesses, traditional communities often attribute these symptoms to localized anatomical obstructions like a 'salted' or hardened vagina. Traditional practitioners utilize the gishiri cut as a universal remedy for these unrelated medical conditions. Addressing this practice requires culturally sensitive community dialogues rather than outright condemnation, which often drives patients away from orthodox healthcare facilities.

Anatomical Structures Affected

The female pelvic anatomy is profoundly compromised during a gishiri cut. Depending on the perceived ailment, the practitioner cuts into specific mucosal and muscular layers of the vagina and surrounding structures. - **Anterior vaginal wall**: Often incised when treating urinary symptoms, frequently resulting in vesicovaginal fistulae. - **Posterior vaginal wall and perineum**: Cut during attempts to treat infertility or prolonged labor, risking rectovaginal fistulae and severe sphincter damage. - **Urethra and bladder neck**: Vulnerable to direct laceration due to the blind nature of the traditional cutting technique. Because these cuts are performed without anatomical knowledge or visualization, adjacent organs are routinely injured.

Immediate and Long-Term Complications

The physical sequelae of a gishiri cut range from acute life-threatening emergencies to chronic debilitating conditions. Immediately following the procedure, patients face severe **hemorrhage** due to the high vascularity of the vaginal tissues and the use of non-surgical cutting instruments. **Infection** is another immediate hazard, frequently progressing to pelvic inflammatory disease, sepsis, or tetanus. Long-term complications profoundly alter a survivor's quality of life. **Vesicovaginal fistula (VVF)** and **rectovaginal fistula (RVF)** cause continuous, involuntary leakage of urine or faeces, leading to severe social ostracization, marital breakdown, and chronic vulvovaginal excoriation. Furthermore, extensive scar tissue formation results in chronic dyspareunia, secondary infertility, and severe dystocia in subsequent pregnancies.

Clinical Assessment and Nursing Diagnosis

Nurses play a frontline role in identifying survivors of harmful traditional practices. Assessment requires a high index of suspicion, particularly when managing women from endemic regions presenting with unexplained urinary or faecal incontinence, severe perineal scarring, or postpartum complications. The nursing history must be gathered with extreme gentleness and empathy to overcome patient shame and stigma. Physical examination, when performed with consent, reveals scar lines, loss of anatomical landmarks, or fistulous tracts. Standard nursing diagnoses for these patients often include *Risk for Infection*, *Chronic Pain*, *Disturbed Body Image*, and *Social Isolation*.

Medical, Surgical, and Nursing Management

Management of gishiri cut complications requires a multidisciplinary approach involving nurses, general practitioners, and specialized gynecological surgeons. Acute hemorrhage is managed through direct pressure, packing, and blood transfusion where necessary. Infectious complications require broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy and tetanus prophylaxis. For chronic structural damage, such as fistulae, **surgical repair** is the definitive treatment, often delayed for several months to allow acute inflammation and local edema to resolve. Nursing care centers around meticulous pre- and post-operative care, indwelling catheter management for urinary diversion, nutritional support, and perineal hygiene.

Prevention, Community Advocacy, and Education

Eradicating practices like the gishiri cut relies heavily on community-based advocacy and education. Nurses in primary healthcare centers serve as vital bridges between rural communities and orthodox medicine. Engaging community leaders, traditional birth attendants, and religious figures in dialogue helps reframe reproductive health education without provoking defensive cultural backlash. Outreach programs must focus on safe motherhood, antenatal care utilization, and demystifying obstetric complications. Empowering women economically and educationally further reduces reliance on harmful traditional practices.
