Gishiri Cut

Definition and Overview of Gishiri Cut

The gishiri cut is a traditional surgical practice found primarily in parts of northern Nigeria and neighboring West African regions. In Hausa, 'gishiri' translates to salt. Historically, traditional birth attendants or local healers perform this procedure using unsterilized blades, knives, or razor blades to slice the vaginal walls or the perineum. This intervention stems from deeply rooted cultural beliefs regarding women's reproductive health and childbearing. Despite national public health campaigns and clear medical evidence of harm, the practice persists in some rural communities due to lack of accessible orthodox healthcare and strong adherence to traditional norms.

Cultural Beliefs and Indications

Traditional practitioners attribute a wide variety of gynecological and obstetric symptoms to a perceived condition called 'gishiri,' which they believe involves a blockage or hardening of the vagina.

  • Prolonged labour: Believed to be caused by a narrow birth canal that requires surgical widening.
  • Infertility: Perceived as a blockage preventing conception that must be cut open.
  • Abnormal vaginal discharge: Attributed to trapped humors or localized swelling treated by incision.
  • Neonatal thrush: Sometimes traditional practitioners cut the infant's mouth or the mother's vagina believing it cures oral thrush in newborns.

Patients often turn to these practitioners because traditional explanations align with their cultural worldview and orthodox facilities may be distant or expensive.

Anatomical Structures Affected

The gishiri procedure involves blind cutting inside the vaginal vault, which endangers delicate, highly vascular pelvic structures. Depending on the direction of the blade, several critical organs suffer direct trauma:

  • Anterior vaginal wall and urethra: Cutting forward frequently leads to vesicovaginal fistulas.
  • Posterior vaginal wall and rectum: Cutting backward risks creating rectovaginal fistulas.
  • Perineum and labia: Superficial or deep lacerations that heal with extensive scarring.
  • Blood vessels: Severing local arterioles causes severe, sometimes life-threatening hemorrhage.

Immediate and Long-Term Complications

The physical and psychological fallout of a gishiri cut is severe and long-lasting. Immediate dangers include hemorrhagic shock from severed blood vessels and overwhelming pelvic sepsis due to unsterilized cutting instruments.

Long-term complications profoundly alter a woman's quality of life. The formation of abnormal openings between the bladder and vagina causes continuous, uncontrollable leakage of urine. This condition, known as a vesicovaginal fistula (VVF), leads to severe skin excoriation, chronic foul odor, and social stigmatization. Furthermore, extensive scar tissue formation inside the vagina causes apareunia or severe dyspareunia, rendering future sexual intercourse and childbirth extremely painful or impossible.

Clinical Assessment and Nursing Diagnosis

Nurses working in regions where gishiri cuts are practiced must maintain a high index of suspicion when assessing female patients with unexplained pelvic trauma, chronic discharge, or incontinence. The assessment begins with a thorough history, noting any traditional birth practices or herbal treatments used during past deliveries.

A gentle physical examination assesses the extent of scarring, vaginal stenosis, or active bleeding. Nurses inspect the perineum and vaginal walls for abnormal openings or signs of infection. Common nursing diagnoses for these patients include risk for infection, chronic pain, disturbed body image, and social isolation related to urinary incontinence.

Medical, Surgical, and Nursing Management

Management of gishiri cut complications requires a multidisciplinary approach involving nurses, general practitioners, and reconstructive surgeons. Acute bleeding requires immediate pressure, IV access, fluid resuscitation, and blood transfusion if necessary. Broad-spectrum antibiotics are initiated promptly to treat or prevent severe pelvic sepsis.

For long-term structural damage like fistulas and severe vaginal stenosis, surgical repair is necessary. Nursing care involves meticulous perineal hygiene, catheter care for patients with urinary diversions, pain management, and empathetic psychological support. Nurses also prepare patients for reconstructive surgeries and provide pre- and post-operative education.

Prevention, Community Advocacy, and Education

Eradicating harmful traditional practices like the gishiri cut requires community-level engagement rather than simple condemnation. Nurses play a crucial role as educators and advocates by partnering with local leaders, religious figures, and traditional birth attendants. Education campaigns focus on explaining the true anatomical causes of prolonged labour and the severe health risks of traditional cutting. Furthermore, expanding access to skilled antenatal care and emergency obstetric services ensures women have safe, reliable alternatives during childbirth.
