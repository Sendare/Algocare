Reasons for Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation, commonly abbreviated as FGM, encompasses all procedures that involve partial or total removal of the external female genitalia, or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Globally recognized as a violation of the human rights of women and girls, this practice has no documented health benefits. Instead, it causes severe immediate and long-term physical, psychological, and sexual harm. Millions of girls undergo the procedure every childhood or adolescence, driven by deeply entrenched community customs. Nurses encounter survivors in various clinical settings, ranging from maternity wards to general outpatient clinics, making a thorough comprehension of the underlying drivers essential for effective, non-judgmental care.

Sociocultural Beliefs and Community Traditions

Many communities continue FGM because it represents a longstanding tradition passed down through generations. People often view the practice as a crucial rite of passage that marks a girl's transition into womanhood. Rejecting the custom can lead to severe ostracism, social isolation, and community backlash for the entire family. Conformity becomes a tool for preserving cultural identity and group solidarity. When an entire village or ethnic group practices FGM for centuries, questioning its safety becomes culturally taboo. Healthcare providers must recognize that families rarely act out of malice; rather, they are yielding to immense social pressure to protect their children from being outcast.

Controlling Female Sexuality and Psychosexual Myths

A pervasive motivation behind FGM is the desire to control female sexual desire and behavior. Communities often believe that removing parts of the external genitalia will reduce a woman's libido, thereby ensuring chastity before marriage and fidelity afterward. A common misconception in these cultures is that unoperated women are naturally hypersexual and prone to promiscuity. By suppressing sexual pleasure, families attempt to safeguard family honor and prevent perceived moral corruption. Nurses must understand that this psychological rationale stems from patriarchal structures that equate female worth with sexual purity.

Religious Misconceptions and Doctrines

Although FGM predates major world religions, it is often falsely associated with religious requirements. Neither the Holy Quran nor the Christian Bible explicitly mandates female genital cutting. However, local religious leaders or misinformed elders sometimes claim that scriptures demand the practice to ensure cleanliness or modesty. Adherents follow these directives out of religious piety, unaware that the custom is cultural rather than theological. Interfaith advocacy groups and knowledgeable healthcare workers play a vital role in clarifying that true religious doctrine does not support harmful body modifications.

Marriageability and Social Status

  • Economic security: In many societies, a woman's survival depends entirely on marriage, because employment opportunities for females are scarce.
  • Marketability: Uncut women are often considered unmarriageable, bringing shame upon their households and leaving them financially vulnerable.
  • Social standing: Performing FGM is seen as an investment in a girl's future, ensuring she secures a husband and maintains community respect.
  • Family honor: A successful marriage validates the family's adherence to societal rules, boosting their overall reputation in the village.

Myths Regarding Hygiene, Aesthetics, and Fertility

Cultural rationalizations often center around bodily aesthetics and hygiene. Some communities believe that female external genitalia are inherently unclean, masculine, or offensive, and that removing them creates a smooth, feminine appearance. Another widespread myth claims that FGM enhances fertility or ensures the safe survival of newborns during childbirth. In reality, scar tissue from the procedure obstructs labor, increasing the risk of infant and maternal mortality. Challenging these aesthetic and health myths requires patient, culturally sensitive health education.

Nursing Roles in Addressing Root Causes

Nurses occupy a front-line position in combating the effects of FGM through holistic patient care and community advocacy. Clinical duties include managing chronic pain, treating obstetric complications, and offering empathetic psychological support to survivors. Beyond the bedside, nurses must educate local populations about the true physiological consequences of the practice without shaming cultural backgrounds. Collaborative community outreach helps shift public perception and empowers families to abandon the custom safely. Understanding the underlying drivers enables healthcare professionals to approach victims with compassion and effective intervention strategies.
