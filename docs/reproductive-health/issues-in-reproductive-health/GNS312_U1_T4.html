Reasons for Female Genital Mutilation

Introduction and Definition of Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-therapeutic reasons. Recognized globally as a violation of the human rights, health, and integrity of women and girls, this practice has no medical justification. The World Health Organization categorizes FGM into four distinct types, ranging from clitoridectomy to infibulation. Understanding the underlying drivers requires examining deep-seated cultural frameworks rather than viewing the procedure as an isolated act of cruelty.

Sociocultural Beliefs and Tradition

In many endemic communities across sub-Saharan Africa, FGM is perceived as a vital rite of passage from childhood to womanhood. Conformity to tradition ensures community cohesion, preserves ancestral heritage, and maintains social order. Families often comply with the practice simply to prevent social exclusion, ostracism, and ridicule. When an entire community views the ritual as a moral obligation, individual dissent carries a heavy social penalty.

Control of Female Sexuality and Psychosexual Motives

A prominent driver of FGM is the belief that altering female anatomy controls or reduces female sexual desire. Communities often hold the misconception that unoperated women are hypersexual and prone to promiscuity. By removing parts of the external genitalia, particularly the clitoris, traditional belief systems attempt to ensure chastity before marriage and fidelity within marriage. This patriarchal control reinforces the notion that a woman's body is tied to family honor.

Religious Misconceptions and Doctrines

  • Islam: FGM is frequently misattributed to Islamic teachings, although the Quran contains no mention of it, and major Islamic scholars reject it.
  • Christianity: No biblical text mandates or endorses female genital cutting, yet practitioners in some Christian communities still maintain the custom.
  • Traditional Beliefs: Indigenous animist religions often incorporate bodily modification into spiritual pacts and fertility rites.

Status, Marriageability, and Social Acceptance

In many settings, an unoperated girl is considered unmarriageable, unclean, or immature. Because marriage often serves as the primary avenue for financial security and social standing for women in rural economies, families permit the procedure to secure their daughters' futures. Men in these communities may refuse to marry unablated women due to prevailing cultural stigmas. Consequently, economic survival and social acceptance become powerful drivers for perpetuating the cycle.

Hygiene, Aesthetics, and Fertility Beliefs

Aesthetic preferences in certain communities dictate that female genitalia are inherently dirty, ugly, or prone to continuous malevolent growth if left unaltered. Cutting is thus rationalized as a hygienic purification measure. Furthermore, erroneous physiological beliefs link external female organs to male sterility or child mortality during birth, reinforcing the idea that removal ensures clean reproduction.

Nursing Interventions and Addressing Root Causes

Nurses play a pivotal role in combating FGM through community education, sensitive clinical care, and advocacy. Healthcare providers must approach affected women without judgment, recognizing the physical and psychological trauma of the procedure. Effective nursing care involves treating immediate complications like hemorrhage and infection, as well as managing chronic issues such as dyspareunia, obstetric fistulae, and psychological distress. Educational outreach must engage community leaders and men to shift cultural norms sustainably.
