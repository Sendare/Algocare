Medicalisation of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation and its Medicalisation

Female genital mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-therapeutic reasons. Historically performed by traditional practitioners using unsterilized instruments, a dangerous shift has occurred where healthcare providers now perform the procedure. This phenomenon, known as medicalisation, creates a false sense of safety. Practitioners attempt to legitimize a harmful human rights violation by utilizing clinical settings, local anesthesia, and surgical blades. Rather than reducing harm, medicalised FGM institutionalizes violence against women and girls while bypassing the vital public health duty of care.

Drivers and Sociocultural Pressures Behind the Practice

Deeply entrenched patriarchal values and social norms sustain female genital mutilation across various regions. Communities often view the procedure as a necessary rite of passage into womanhood, a measure to ensure chastity, or a requirement for marriageability. When medicalisation enters this equation, healthcare professionals are frequently swayed by familial pleas, community standing, or financial gain. Practitioners may convince themselves that performing a modified or 'nipped' version prevents severe traditional cutting, thereby participating in a harmful cultural compromise that violates professional ethics.

Physical, Psychological, and Reproductive Health Complications

Regardless of whether female genital mutilation is performed with a rusty blade by a traditional cutter or a sterile scalpel by a clinician, the physical and psychological damage remains profound.

  • Immediate Complications: Severe hemorrhage, agonizing pain, neurogenic shock, acute urine retention, and accidental injury to adjacent pelvic structures.
  • Long-term Physical Sequelae: Chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, keloid formation, painful intercourse, and the development of vulvar cysts.
  • Obstetric Complications: Prolonged labor, obstructed labor, extensive perineal tearing, postpartum hemorrhage, and heightened rates of perinatal mortality.
  • Psychological Sequelae: Post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic anxiety, depression, flashbacks, and severe fear during intimate relationships.

Ethical and Legal Frameworks Against FGM Medicalisation

Medicalised FGM represents a direct betrayal of foundational bioethical principles. Beneficence and non-maleficence require practitioners to act in the best interest of the patient and do no harm; performing an anatomically mutilating procedure directly violates this duty. Furthermore, it breaches the principle of autonomy, as procedures are typically inflicted upon minor girls who cannot give informed consent. Internationally and nationally, legal frameworks categorically prohibit medicalised FGM. In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act criminalizes all forms of FGM, including procedures performed by medical personnel, carrying severe penalties of imprisonment and fines alongside the revocation of professional licenses.

Global and National Health Authority Stances

Major international health organizations maintain a unified, uncompromising stance against the medicalisation of female genital mutilation. The World Health Organization, UNICEF, and the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics explicitly state that health workers must never perform FGM under any circumstances, including symbolic cuts or requests for reinfibulation. National regulatory bodies, such as the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria and the Medical and Dental Council of Nigeria, align with these global declarations. They mandate that any involvement in cutting constitutes professional misconduct, establishing clear disciplinary pathways to penalize complicit clinicians.

The Role of the Nurse in Prevention and Advocacy

Nurses occupy a frontline position in stopping the spread of medicalised FGM through community education, clinical vigilance, and policy advocacy. In antenatal and immunization clinics, nurses engage families in empathetic dialogues regarding the lifelong health risks of cutting. During clinical encounters, sharp observational skills help identify girls at risk, particularly those presenting from high-prevalence communities during traditional holiday periods. Beyond the bedside, nurses collaborate with local leaders, school administrators, and women's groups to dismantle myths surrounding female anatomy and champion human rights.

Nursing Care and Support for Survivors

Providing holistic care for survivors of female genital mutilation requires deep clinical sensitivity, trauma-informed approaches, and expert physical management. Nurses must establish a trusting, non-judgmental environment, acknowledging the immense courage it takes for survivors to seek help. Physical interventions range from managing chronic urinary infections and pain to specialized obstetric care during labor and delivery. Psychological support involves active listening, validating emotional trauma, and facilitating timely referrals to mental health professionals. Comprehensive recovery demands that care plans address both the visible scars and the deep psychological wounds of survivors.
