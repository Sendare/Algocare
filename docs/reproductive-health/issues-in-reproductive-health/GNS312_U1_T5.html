Medicalisation of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding FGM and the Shift Toward Medicalisation

Female Genital Mutilation, commonly known as FGM, involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia for non-medical reasons. Traditionally performed by traditional practitioners using unsterilized tools, the practice has seen a concerning shift. Medicalisation of FGM occurs when healthcare workers—such as doctors, nurses, or midwives—perform these procedures in clinics, hospitals, or private homes, mistakenly believing it makes the act safer.

Health professionals who perform FGM violate core ethical oaths to do no harm. Changing the setting from a village hut to a sterile clinic does not change the procedure into legitimate healthcare. The act remains a violation of human rights and bodily autonomy, regardless of who holds the scalpel or how clean the environment is.

Drivers Behind Medicalised FGM

Why do trained medical professionals participate in a harmful practice? The pressure comes from deep-rooted sociocultural expectations. Families often seek out nurses or doctors because they fear the social rejection, stigma, or unmarriageability their daughters might face if uncircumcised.

Another major driver is the harm reduction myth. Some misguided health workers agree to perform the procedure thinking they can minimize bleeding or infection compared to traditional cutters. Additionally, financial gain tempts some practitioners in under-resourced areas where performing the procedure offers quick cash.

Health Complications Across the Lifespan

The physical and psychological fallout of FGM lasts a lifetime, whether the cut was done by a traditional circumciser or a registered nurse.

  • Immediate complications: Severe pain, excessive bleeding, heavy swelling, and shock.
  • Long-term physical issues: Chronic infections, scarring, painful urination, and keloid formation.
  • Obstetric complications: Difficult labor, severe tearing, and increased risk of newborn death during childbirth.
  • Psychological trauma: Anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and deep fear during intimate relationships.

Global Stances and Legal Frameworks

Major international bodies—including the World Health Organization, the United Nations, and the International Council of Nurses—have issued clear joint statements condemning the medicalisation of FGM. They classify it as a violation of fundamental human rights and professional ethics.

At the national level, many countries have enacted strict legislation criminalizing FGM. Crucially, these laws specifically target healthcare professionals who perform or assist in the procedure, carrying heavy penalties such as prison time and the permanent revocation of nursing or medical licenses.

The Role of the Nurse in Prevention and Advocacy

Nurses occupy the front lines of community health, placing them in an ideal position to prevent FGM. Advocacy begins with community education, where nurses dispel myths about cleanliness and safety regarding medicalised cuts. By engaging with community and religious leaders, nurses help shift local mindsets away from the practice.

Inside the clinic, nurses must remain vigilant. Recognizing families at risk—such as young girls brought in for suspicious checkups ahead of holiday travel seasons—allows nurses to intervene early with counseling and protective referrals.

Holistic Nursing Care and Support for Survivors

Caring for FGM survivors requires sensitivity, clinical skill, and deep empathy. Many survivors carry profound emotional scars and may feel intense anxiety during routine pelvic examinations. Nurses must practice trauma-informed care: explaining every step, asking permission before touching, and offering a supportive, judgment-free environment.

Clinical care often involves managing chronic infections, treating scar tissue, and providing specialized support during pregnancy and labor. When necessary, nurses connect survivors with mental health professionals and support groups to aid their emotional healing.
