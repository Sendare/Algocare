Postnatal Care

Introduction and Definition of Postnatal Care

Postnatal care, also known as postpartum care, is the critical phase that begins immediately after the birth of the placenta and extends for the first six weeks (42 days) of life, a period scientifically known as the puerperium. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding postnatal care is vital because the postpartum period carries significant maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality risks, particularly in resource-constrained settings. During this phase, the mother's body undergoes massive anatomical and physiological adaptations to return to the pre-pregnant state, while the newborn adapts to extrauterine life. Postnatal care is designed to provide comprehensive health promotion, disease prevention, early detection, and prompt management of complications for both mother and baby. Care encompasses physical assessments, emotional support, nutritional counseling, guidance on hygiene, promotion of exclusive breastfeeding, and facilitation of family planning. The World Health Organization (WHO) and Nigerian national health guidelines emphasize that the first 24 hours, the first week, and the sixth week are critical contact points. By providing holistic and culturally sensitive postnatal care, nurses play a front-line role in achieving Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) related to reducing maternal and child mortality in Nigeria. Ultimately, a well-managed postnatal period lays a strong foundation for lifelong family health and healthy child development.

Physiological Changes and Involution of the Puerperium

Physiological changes during the puerperium involve profound involutional processes where maternal organs and systems return to their non-pregnant state. The most prominent process is uterine involution, where the uterus rapidly decreases in size, dropping from weighing about 1,000 grams immediately postpartum down to approximately 50-60 grams by six weeks. This process is driven by strong myometrial contractions, often aided by oxytocin release during breastfeeding, and is clinically monitored by assessing the height of the uterine fundus, which descends roughly one centimeter or one fingerbreadth per day. Within two weeks, the uterus sinks back into the true pelvis and becomes impalpable abdominally. Endometrial regeneration occurs alongside involution, resulting in postpartum vaginal discharge known as lochia. Lochia transitions through three stages: lochia rubra (red, lasting days 1-3), lochia serosa (pinkish-brown, lasting days 4-10), and lochia alba (yellowish-white, lasting from day 10 up to 6 weeks). Cardiovascular adaptations include the elimination of pregnancy-induced hypervolemia through postpartum diuresis and diaphoresis. Cervical, vaginal, and pelvic floor tissues also heal and regain tone, though the external os of the cervix remains permanently altered from a dimple to a transverse slit. Nurses must understand these normal physiological milestones to accurately differentiate healthy recovery from abnormal deviations such as subinvolution or secondary postpartum hemorrhage.

Immediate Postnatal Assessment and Monitoring

The immediate postnatal assessment and monitoring phase occurs in the first two hours following childbirth, which is the highest-risk window for primary postpartum hemorrhage and acute maternal instability. Nursing students must recognize that vigilance is paramount during this time. The nurse should systematically monitor vital signs—specifically blood pressure, pulse rate, and temperature—every 15 minutes for the first hour, and every 30 minutes for the second hour. Assessment of the uterus is critical: the nurse must palpate the fundus to ensure it remains firm, well-contracted, and centered, as a boggy or displaced uterus indicates uterine atony and potential hemorrhage. Alongside uterine checks, the nurse must assess the amount, color, and clots of vaginal bleeding (lochia), and inspect the perineum for signs of expanding hematoma, excessive swelling, or intact episiotomy/tear repairs. Bladder distension is a common complication that contributes to uterine atony; therefore, the nurse must encourage and monitor spontaneous voiding, checking for urinary retention. For the newborn, immediate assessment includes evaluating Apgar scores, checking vital signs, assessing thermoregulation, ensuring skin-to-skin contact, and initiating early breastfeeding. Documenting all findings meticulously and reporting any abnormal trends, such as tachycardia, falling blood pressure, or heavy bleeding, to the midwife or doctor can save a mother's life.

Routine Postnatal Care Schedules and Assessments

Routine postnatal care schedules ensure continuous clinical surveillance and support for the mother-baby dyad throughout the puerperium. According to standard guidelines, key contact points include within 24 hours, at 48-72 hours, between 7-14 days, and at 6 weeks postpartum. During each scheduled visit, the nurse performs a comprehensive head-to-toe physical assessment. For the mother, this includes checking vital signs, examining the breasts for engorgement or nipple trauma, assessing uterine involution and lochia, checking perineal healing or cesarean wound integrity, and screening for signs of urinary tract infection or thromboembolism. Emotional wellbeing is continuously evaluated to catch postpartum blues or depression early. For the newborn, assessments focus on weight changes, feeding effectiveness, jaundice screening, umbilical cord healing, and checking elimination patterns (wet diapers and stooling). Additionally, the nurse reviews immunization schedules and provides anticipatory guidance on infant care milestones. In the Nigerian context, community health extension workers and nurses utilize home visits and postnatal clinics to bridge gaps in care, ensuring that women who deliver outside formal facilities still receive these vital routine checks. Systematic documentation during these visits fosters continuity of care and improves maternal-neonatal health outcomes.

Breastfeeding Support and Lactation Management

Breastfeeding support and lactation management are cornerstone responsibilities of the maternity nurse. Exclusive breastfeeding—feeding an infant only breast milk with no other foods or liquids, not even water, for the first six months of life—provides unmatched nutritional, immunological, and psychological benefits. Nurses must actively promote early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of birth. Effective lactation management begins with teaching mothers correct positioning and attachment (latch) techniques. Good attachment is characterized by the baby's mouth covering a large portion of the lower areola, the mouth opened wide, the lower lip turned outward, and the chin touching the breast, which prevents painful cracked nipples and ensures efficient milk transfer. Nurses must educate mothers on responsive feeding, encouraging them to feed on demand day and night. Common challenges such as engorgement, sore nipples, plugged ducts, and mastitis require prompt nursing intervention, including warm compresses, gentle massage, correct latch adjustment, and encouraging frequent emptying. In Nigeria, where infectious diseases pose significant risks, exclusive breastfeeding drastically reduces infant morbidity and mortality from diarrhea and respiratory infections. Nurses also provide guidance for mothers with special medical conditions, ensuring safe infant feeding practices while empowering them with knowledge and confidence.

Identification and Management of Postnatal Complications

The identification and management of postnatal complications require sharp clinical acuity from nursing students. Complications can manifest physically and endanger the mother's life if unrecognized. Secondary postpartum hemorrhage—defined as excessive bleeding occurring between 24 hours and 12 weeks postpartum—is frequently caused by retained placental tissue or subinvolution and infection, managed through oxytocics, antibiotics, or surgical evacuation. Puerperal sepsis, an infection of the genital tract occurring at any time between rupture of membranes or labor and the 42nd day postpartum, presents with fever, lower abdominal pain, and foul-smelling lochia, requiring prompt broad-spectrum antibiotics and supportive care. Thromboembolic disorders, including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), are heightened by pregnancy-related hypercoagulability and immobility, presenting with unilateral leg swelling, pain, or sudden shortness of breath. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy can also persist or present postpartum (postpartum preeclampsia), requiring blood pressure monitoring and antihypertensive therapy. Perineal wound complications, such as hematomas, cellulitis, or wound dehiscence, demand aseptic wound care, analgesia, and sitz baths. By understanding the classic signs and pathophysiology of these conditions, nurses can facilitate rapid referrals and life-saving interventions.

Mental Health and Postpartum Emotional Well-being

Mental health and postpartum emotional well-being are crucial yet frequently neglected aspects of comprehensive postnatal care. Hormonal shifts, sleep deprivation, physical discomfort, and lifestyle adjustments place new mothers at high risk for psychological distress. Nursing students must distinguish between the three main categories of postpartum emotional responses: postpartum blues, postpartum depression, and postpartum psychosis. Postpartum blues is a very common, mild, and transient condition occurring within the first few days after birth, characterized by mood swings, tearfulness, anxiety, and fatigue, which resolves spontaneously within two weeks with reassurance and social support. Postpartum depression is a more severe, persistent mood disorder that can emerge anytime within the first year, presenting with profound sadness, feelings of worthlessness, inability to care for oneself or the baby, and loss of interest in activities, requiring professional psychological counseling and potential pharmacotherapy. Postpartum psychosis is a psychiatric medical emergency characterized by hallucinations, delusions, confusion, and thoughts of harming the infant or self, demanding immediate psychiatric hospitalization. Nurses should utilize validated screening tools, such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), provide a non-judgmental listening ear, involve family support systems, and reduce stigma to promote maternal mental health.

Postnatal Nutrition, Hygiene, and Self-Care

Postnatal nutrition, hygiene, and self-care are essential for restoring maternal energy stores and preventing infection during recovery. Nutritional requirements during the postnatal period, particularly for lactating mothers, exceed those during pregnancy because milk production demands significant caloric and nutrient expenditure. Nurses should educate mothers on consuming a balanced diet rich in proteins, calcium, iron, fruits, vegetables, and complex carbohydrates, alongside maintaining adequate hydration by drinking plenty of water. Iron and folate supplementation should be continued, especially in Nigeria where anemia is prevalent. Personal hygiene is vital for preventing puerperal infections; mothers should be instructed on proper perineal hygiene, including washing from front to back after elimination, frequent perineal pad changes, and proper handwashing. C-section wound care requires keeping the incision clean and dry while monitoring for signs of redness or discharge. Rest and sleep hygiene are equally critical; new mothers must be encouraged to rest when the baby sleeps to combat exhaustion. Pelvic floor exercises, such as Kegels, should be taught to restore muscle tone, prevent urinary incontinence, and improve pelvic circulation. Empowering mothers with self-care practices fosters autonomy and accelerates overall physical recovery.

Family Planning and Postpartum Contraception

Family planning and postpartum contraception are vital components of postnatal care that empower women to space pregnancies and protect maternal and child health. Interpregnancy intervals of at least 24 months are recommended by the WHO to reduce adverse perinatal outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal mortality. Nurses must provide comprehensive, non-judgmental counseling on available contraceptive methods during antenatal and postnatal visits. The choice of contraceptive depends on breastfeeding status, medical history, and personal preference. Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM) is an effective natural method for exclusively breastfeeding women during the first six months, provided menses has not returned. Progestin-only methods—such as progestin-only pills (minipills), depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) injections, and subdermal implants—are safe for lactating mothers and do not interfere with milk supply or quality. Combined hormonal contraceptives (containing estrogen and progestin) are generally avoided during the first 3 weeks postpartum due to the elevated risk of venous thromboembolism, and are discouraged during the first 6 weeks for exclusively breastfeeding mothers as estrogen can diminish milk production. Barrier methods, intrauterine devices (IUDs), and permanent sterilization options should also be discussed, ensuring couples make informed, autonomous choices.

Discharge Planning, Community Follow-up, and Immunization

Discharge planning, community follow-up, and immunization mark the final transition of facility-based postnatal care into long-term community health management. Effective discharge planning begins well before the mother leaves the health facility, ensuring she and her family understand danger signs for both mother (such as heavy bleeding, severe headache, fever, and calf swelling) and baby (such as poor feeding, lethargy, jaundice, and fever). Nurses must review appointment dates for the baby's immunization schedule, explaining the vaccines given at birth (Bacille Calmette-Guérin [BCG], Oral Polio Vaccine [OPV] zero dose, and Hepatitis B) and scheduling subsequent visits at 6, 10, and 14 weeks as per the Nigerian National Programme on Immunization (NPI). Community linkages, such as involving community health workers, home visits, and mother support groups, are essential for maintaining contact with women in rural or hard-to-reach Nigerian communities. This continuity helps bridge dropouts in immunization and postnatal checkups. Proper documentation of the child health card and maternal postnatal record ensures that any health worker can track the infant's growth milestones and the mother's recovery trajectory, solidifying the continuum of care.
