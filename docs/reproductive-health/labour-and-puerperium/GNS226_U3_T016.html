Postnatal Care

Introduction and Definition of Postnatal Care

The postnatal period, or puerperium, begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six weeks (42 days). During this critical phase, maternal physiological and anatomical changes revert to the non-pregnant state, and the newborn adapts to extrauterine life. Postnatal care encompasses the systematic monitoring, support, and health education provided to both mother and baby. In many healthcare settings across Nigeria, this window is also referred to as the postnatal period or the fourth stage of labor, underscoring its clinical importance.

Physiological Changes and Involution of the Puerperium

Involution is the rapid structural and functional regression of the reproductive organs back to their pre-gravid condition. The uterus undergoes severe muscular contractions to constrict blood vessels at the placental site, drastically reducing in size over the first two weeks. Vaginal discharge known as **lochia** changes progressively in color and composition from rubia to serosa, and finally alba. Meanwhile, the pelvic floor muscles recover tone, and blood volume shifts back to baseline through diuresis and diaphoresis.

Immediate Postnatal Assessment and Monitoring

The first two hours post-delivery carry the highest risk for fatal maternal hemorrhage. Routine bedside monitoring requires frequent assessment of vital signs, uterine consistency, bladder fullness, and lochia volume. A soft, boggy uterus indicates uterine atony, which demands immediate fundal massage and oxytocic administration. Furthermore, inspecting the perineum for expanding hematomas or unrepaired lacerations prevents silent blood loss.

Routine Postnatal Care Schedules and Assessments

Structured contact points ensure ongoing detection of delayed complications for both mother and infant. The recommended schedule typically includes assessments at 24 hours, 48 to 72 hours, 7 to 14 days, and 6 weeks postpartum. Each visit evaluates psychological adjustment, infant feeding effectiveness, involution, wound healing from episiotomies or caesarean sections, and return of fertility.

Breastfeeding Support and Lactation Management

Initiating breastfeeding within the first hour of life promotes bonding, stimulates oxytocin release, and provides the infant with immune-protective colostrum. Proper positioning and a wide latch prevent nipple trauma, engorgement, and mastitis. Exclusive breastfeeding is recommended for the first six months of life, meaning the infant receives only breast milk without additional water, formula, or other foods.

Identification and Management of Postnatal Complications

Postpartum morbidity can escalate rapidly if warning signs go unnoticed. Healthcare providers must educate women and families on red flag symptoms that demand urgent hospital presentation. - **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH)**: Blood loss exceeding 500 mL following vaginal birth or 1000 mL following caesarean section. - **Puerperal Sepsis**: Bacterial infection of the reproductive tract, characterized by fever, foul-smelling lochia, and lower abdominal pain. - **Thromboembolism**: Formation of blood clots in deep veins or lungs, presenting with unilateral leg swelling, warmth, or sudden dyspnea. - **Hypertensive Disorders**: Late-onset pre-eclampsia can still manifest postpartum, evidenced by severe headaches, visual disturbances, and high blood pressure.

Mental Health and Postpartum Emotional Well-being

Hormonal shifts, sleep deprivation, and lifestyle adjustments place new mothers at risk for emotional distress. While transient 'baby blues' resolve spontaneously within two weeks, persistent sadness, severe anxiety, and feelings of inadequacy may indicate **postpartum depression**. Severe cases can escalate to **postpartum psychosis**, a psychiatric emergency marked by hallucinations and delusions. Routine screening helps identify women needing specialized psychological support.

Postnatal Nutrition, Hygiene, and Self-Care

Energy demands during lactation often exceed pregnancy requirements. A balanced diet rich in protein, iron, calcium, and fluids supports recovery and milk production. Perineal hygiene involves gentle cleansing with clean water from front to back to prevent ascending reproductive tract infections. Adequate rest, although challenging with a newborn, remains essential for physical healing and psychological resilience.

Family Planning and Postpartum Contraception

Return of fertility can precede the resumption of menstruation, making postpartum family planning counselling crucial before hospital discharge. Progestin-only contraceptives, barrier methods, and intrauterine devices are safe options that do not interfere with lactation. Conversely, combined hormonal contraceptives are generally avoided during the first few weeks due to the elevated baseline risk of thromboembolism.

Discharge Planning, Community Follow-up, and Immunization

Successful transition home requires clear discharge instructions, red-flag education, and scheduled community health follow-ups. Nurses ensure that newborn immunizations, such as BCG and Oral Polio Vaccine at birth, are administered and documented. Community health extension workers play a vital role in home visits, reinforcing breastfeeding practices, checking maternal healing, and bridging the gap between hospital care and home.
