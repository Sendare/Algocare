Postnatal Care

Introduction and Definition of Postnatal Care

Postnatal care, also called the puerperium, begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and continues for six weeks, or forty-two days. This period is a critical transitional phase where the mother's body undergoes massive anatomical and physiological adjustments while she adapts to her new role. Many fatal complications, such as postpartum hemorrhage and severe infections, occur during these early weeks. Providing structured support ensures both mother and newborn receive timely interventions to promote recovery, establish successful infant feeding, and monitor general well-being.

Physiological Changes and Involution of the Puerperium

During the puerperium, the reproductive organs return to their non-pregnant state, a process known as involution.

  • Uterine involution: The uterus shrinks rapidly, dropping by about one centimeter every day. By two weeks postpartum, it sinks back into the true pelvis and can no longer be felt abdominally.
  • Lochia: Vaginal discharge following childbirth changes in color and consistency over time. It starts as lochia rubra (bright red), turns to lochia serosa (pinkish-brown), and finally becomes lochia alba (yellowish-white).
  • Cervix and vagina: The cervix regains its firm consistency and closes gradually over the first week, while the stretched vagina slowly recovers its tone.

Immediate Postnatal Assessment and Monitoring

The first two hours after birth require intense vigilance because postpartum hemorrhage can develop without warning. Nurses check vital signs every fifteen minutes initially, watching carefully for rising pulse rates and falling blood pressures as early signs of shock. Palpating the uterine fundus is mandatory to ensure it remains firm and contracted; a soft or boggy uterus signals uterine atony. Additionally, checking the bladder is crucial because a full bladder pushes the uterus aside and prevents proper contraction.

Breastfeeding Support and Lactation Management

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life stimulates milk production and encourages bonding. Colostrum, the thick yellowish milk produced first, provides vital antibodies and nutrients for the newborn. Proper attachment (latch) prevents sore, cracked nipples and ensures the baby gets enough milk. Nurses must teach mothers how to position the baby comfortably and recognize hunger cues rather than feeding strictly by the clock.

Identification and Management of Postnatal Complications

Nurses must remain alert to warning signs that indicate serious postpartum illness. Postpartum hemorrhage, endometritis, and thromboembolism top the list of dangerous complications.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding usually caused by uterine atony or retained placental fragments.
  • Endometritis: Uterine infection marked by foul-smelling lochia, fever, and lower abdominal pain.
  • Thromboembolism: Blood clots forming in the deep veins of the legs, presenting with swelling, warmth, and unilateral pain.

Mental Health and Emotional Well-being

The sudden drop in hormones after delivery leaves many women vulnerable to emotional distress. Postpartum blues is very common, causing mild tearfulness and mood swings that resolve within two weeks without medical treatment. However, if these feelings deepen into persistent sadness, hopelessness, or inability to care for the baby, it may be postpartum depression. Severe cases can escalate to postpartum psychosis, a psychiatric emergency requiring immediate intervention.

Postnatal Nutrition, Hygiene, and Self-Care

Recovery requires adequate rest, balanced nutrition, and proper hygiene. Lactating mothers need extra calories and high fluid intake to support milk production. Perineal hygiene is critical to prevent infection, especially for women with perineal tears or episiotomies; washing the area with clean water from front to back and changing sanitary pads frequently reduces bacterial growth. Early ambulation should be encouraged to improve circulation and lower the risk of blood clots.

Family Planning and Postpartum Contraception

Fertility can return before menstruation resumes, making postpartum contraception counseling essential before hospital discharge. Progestin-only methods, such as mini-pills, injectables, or implants, are safe for breastfeeding mothers because they do not interfere with milk supply. Combined estrogen-progestin contraceptives are usually delayed in early breastfeeding due to the risk of reducing milk production and increasing blood clot risks. Barrier methods like condoms provide both pregnancy prevention and protection against sexually transmitted infections.

Discharge Planning, Community Follow-up, and Immunization

Comprehensive discharge planning prepares the mother for life at home by reinforcing warning signs, infant care, and follow-up appointments. The standard schedule includes postnatal checks at three days, six weeks, and further intervals as needed. During these visits, nurses assess infant growth, check the mother's healing, and administer required immunizations like the Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) and oral polio vaccine for the baby, alongside tetanus boosters or rubella vaccines for the mother if indicated.
