Normal Puerperium

Introduction and Phases of the Puerperium

The puerperium, commonly referred to as the postpartum period, begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for approximately six weeks. During this critical window, the mother's body undergoes profound anatomical and physiological adjustments to return to its pre-pregnant state. Clinicians divide this period into three distinct phases:

  • Immediate puerperium: The first 24 hours following childbirth, a critical time requiring intensive monitoring for potential postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Early puerperium: Lasting from 24 hours up to the end of the first week postpartum, during which lactation is established and early involution begins.
  • Remote puerperium: Extends from the second week through the sixth week, marking the gradual return of all pelvic organs to their non-pregnant condition.

Uterine Involution and Physical Changes

Following birth, the uterus must shrink dramatically through a process called involution. Immediately after the placenta is expelled, the fundus can be felt roughly halfway between the symphysis pubis and the umbilicus, weighing about 1,000 grams. By one week postpartum, it weighs around 500 grams, and by six weeks, it returns to its pre-pregnancy weight of approximately 60 to 70 grams.

This rapid reduction occurs because individual muscle fibers undergo autolysis, breaking down excess protein and shrinking in size. At the same time, the placental site heals through exfoliation and the growth of new endometrium, preventing permanent scarring. Nurses monitor involution by palpating the fundal height daily; it should descend by roughly one centimeter or one fingerbreadth every 24 hours.

Lochia: Characteristics and Progression

Postpartum uterine discharge, known as lochia, consists of blood, mucus, and tissue debris. Tracking the color and amount of lochia helps nurses evaluate how well the uterus is healing. It progresses through three distinct stages:

  1. Lochia rubra: Occurs from day one to day four. It is bright red in color and consists mainly of blood, decidual tissue, and trophoblastic debris.
  2. Lochia serosa: Appears from day five to day nine. The discharge becomes pinkish-brown or serous as the bleeding slows down and white blood cells take over.
  3. Lochia alba: Starts around day ten and can last up to six weeks. It is yellowish-white and contains mostly mucus, epithelial cells, and bacteria.

A sudden return to bright red bleeding after lochia has turned serosa or alba can indicate retained placental fragments or secondary postpartum hemorrhage.

Systemic Maternal Adaptations

The cardiovascular system undergoes rapid adjustments as the extra fluid volume from pregnancy is eliminated. Diuresis (excessive urination) and diaphoresis (heavy sweating) peak within the first few days postpartum to clear out the nearly two liters of extra plasma volume. Blood pressure usually remains stable, though a transient rise can occur due to the sudden shift in circulation.

Meanwhile, the gastrointestinal tract regains its tone, but mothers frequently experience constipation caused by sluggish bowel motility, residual perineal pain, and fear of tearing stitches during defecation. The urinary bladder also has increased capacity and decreased sensitivity to pressure following birth, which puts the patient at high risk for overdistension and urinary tract infections.

Lactation and Breast Changes

Breast changes begin during pregnancy under hormonal stimulation, but milk production shifts gears after the placenta is delivered. The sudden drop in progesterone and estrogen triggers the release of prolactin, which stimulates milk synthesis. When the baby suckles, the posterior pituitary releases oxytocin, causing the let-down reflex that ejects milk from the alveoli into the ducts.

For the first two to three days, the breasts secrete colostrum, a yellowish fluid rich in antibodies and protein that protects the newborn's gut. Around the third or fourth day, mature milk comes in, and breasts may become temporarily swollen, warm, and tender—a condition known as engorgement. Frequent, effective feeding helps prevent engorgement and establishes a healthy milk supply.

Psychological Adaptation and the Baby Blues

The transition to motherhood involves deep emotional adjustments alongside physical recovery. Renowned theorist Reva Rubin described maternal adaptation through distinct phases: the taking-in phase (where the mother is passive and relies on others for care), the taking-hold phase (where she takes charge of her own care and the baby's), and the letting-go phase (where she accepts her new role and family dynamics).

Many mothers experience transient mood swings, tearfulness, and anxiety during the first week postpartum, a common condition called the baby blues. This is largely driven by rapid hormone shifts and sleep deprivation. Unlike postpartum depression, the baby blues resolve on their own within two weeks without formal psychiatric treatment, requiring mainly reassurance, rest, and family support.

Postnatal Maternal Assessment and Care

Routine nursing assessment in the postpartum unit follows a systematic framework known by the acronym BUBBLE-EE: Breasts, Uterus, Bowel, Bladder, Lochia, Episiotomy/Perineum, Emotional status, and Extremities (assessing for deep vein thrombosis).

Perineal care is vital, especially for women who had an episiotomy or a tear. Nurses instruct patients to use cold packs during the first 24 hours to reduce swelling, followed by warm sitz baths to promote healing and comfort. Hand hygiene and proper perineal hygiene—wiping from front to back—prevent ascending bacterial infections. Early ambulation is also encouraged to lower the risk of blood clots in the legs.
