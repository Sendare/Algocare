Abnormal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Abnormal Labour

Abnormal labour, clinically referred to as dystocia or difficult labour, is characterized by slow, abnormal progress of labour. Normal labour follows a predictable, progressive path through specific stages resulting in the vaginal delivery of the foetus without complications. When the process deviates from this expected timeline or pattern, it compromises both maternal and foetal well-being. In Nigerian maternity units, identifying this deviation early is critical because prolonged obstructed labour remains a leading contributor to severe obstetric morbidity, including obstetric fistulae and uterine rupture.

Clinically, dystocia manifests as cervical dilation failing to progress at expected rates or the foetus failing to descend through the pelvis despite adequate uterine contractions. Defining the exact boundary between normal and abnormal relies heavily on the active phase of the first stage of labour, where a crossing of the alert line on the partograph serves as an immediate warning sign.

The Four Ps of Labour Mechanics

Normal parturition depends on the harmonious interaction of four distinct physiological and anatomical components. Dysfunction in any single element can precipitate dystocia.

  • Power: The force generated by uterine contractions and maternal pushing efforts, driving the concept of uterine inertia if inadequate.
  • Passenger: The foetus, including its size, presenting part, lie, attitude, and position; malpresentations like brow or face presentation cause mechanical obstruction.
  • Passage: The maternal bony pelvis and soft tissues of the birth canal; a contracted pelvis or pelvic tumors can physically block descent.
  • Psyche: The emotional and psychological state of the mother; severe anxiety and fear release catecholamines that can inhibit effective uterine contractility.

Classification and Patterns of Labour Abnormalities

Labour abnormalities are categorized based on their distinct graphical presentation on the partograph and the specific phase of labour in which they occur. Recognizing these patterns helps clinicians pinpoint whether the primary issue is slow dilation or delayed descent.

  • Protraction disorders: Labour is progressing, but at a slower-than-normal rate, encompassing protracted active-phase dilation or protracted descent.
  • Arrest disorders: Complete cessation of progress, such as secondary arrest of dilation where contractions are present but cervical change stops for two hours or more.
  • Prolonged latent phase: Latent phase exceeding 20 hours in nulliparous women or 14 hours in multiparous women.
  • Failure to descend: Lack of progressive downward movement of the foetal presenting part in the second stage of labour.

Causes and Risk Factors of Dystocia

Dystocia rarely stems from a single isolated issue; rather, it typically arises from multifactorial origins tied directly to the four mechanical components of labour. Hypotonic uterine dysfunction is the most common cause of progress failure, often resulting from maternal exhaustion, heavy sedation, or overdistended uteri caused by polyhydramnios or multiple gestations. Cephalopelvic disproportion occurs when the foetal head is too large to pass through the maternal pelvis, a risk factor heavily influenced by childhood malnutrition affecting pelvic bone development.

Malpositions, such as persistent occipitoposterior positions, force the foetus to navigate a suboptimal path through the pelvis. Soft tissue obstructions like uterine fibroids, full bladders, or ovarian cysts can also impede descent. Identifying these risk factors during antenatal booking allows midwives and obstetricians to anticipate complications and plan appropriate delivery routes.

Clinical Assessment and Monitoring Using the Partograph

The partograph is a graphical representation of labour used to monitor maternal and foetal condition alongside the progress of cervical dilation. Regular plotting allows health personnel to detect deviations before they become life-threatening. The WHO partograph features an alert line and an action line set four hours to the right of the alert line. When the cervical dilation curve crosses the alert line, it warns the midwife that labour is protracted and the woman should be transferred to a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility if she is in a primary health centre.

Crossing the action line mandates immediate reassessment and active intervention. Monitoring also encompasses tracking foetal heart rate, amniotic fluid color, maternal pulse, blood pressure, and urine output, ensuring a holistic evaluation of the labouring dyad.

Nursing Management and Supportive Interventions

Nursing care during abnormal labour focuses on continuous surveillance, physiological support, and early reporting of abnormal findings. Midwives and nurses must maintain accurate partographic records, monitoring uterine contraction frequency, duration, and intensity every half-hour. Encouraging maternal mobility, optimal positioning such as lateral recumbent or upright postures, and adequate hydration helps improve uterine perfusion and cope with pain.

Emotional support is vital; explaining every procedure reduces anxiety and lowers catecholamine levels that hinder contractions. Monitoring maternal bladder status by encouraging frequent voiding prevents bladder distension, which can obstruct descent. Continuous communication with the medical team ensures timely interventions when progress stalls.

Medical and Surgical Management of Dystocia

Management strategies depend entirely on the underlying cause identified during clinical assessment. When hypotonic uterine dysfunction is diagnosed in the absence of cephalopelvic disproportion, oxytocin augmentation is initiated via an infusion pump with strict titration to achieve adequate contraction patterns without causing hyperstimulation.

If mechanical obstruction or cephalopelvic disproportion is confirmed, oxytocin is strictly contraindicated because it can cause uterine rupture; instead, prompt caesarean section is performed. When delay occurs in the second stage with a fully dilated cervix and the foetus is in a favourable low station, operative vaginal delivery using a vacuum extractor or forceps may be employed by skilled practitioners.

Complications and Maternal-Fetal Outcomes

Unresolved abnormal labour carries severe health risks for both mother and child. Prolonged pressure from the foetal head on maternal soft tissues can cause ischemic necrosis, leading to vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistulae—a devastating condition prevalent in regions with delayed access to emergency obstetric care. Uterine rupture can occur if obstructed labour is mismanaged with oxytocin or left to proceed unassisted.

Maternal exhaustion, postpartum hemorrhage secondary to uterine atony, and severe puerperal sepsis are frequent systemic complications. Foetal risks include acute hypoxia, birth asphyxia, meconium aspiration syndrome, traumatic intracranial hemorrhage from difficult instrumental deliveries, and neonatal death.
