Abnormal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Abnormal Labour

Abnormal labour, clinically referred to as dystocia or difficult childbirth, occurs when the progress of labour deviates significantly from the normal physiological curve. In normal labour, the cervix dilates progressively and the fetus descends through the birth canal in a predictable, timely manner. When this process stalls or slows down unduly, it places both mother and baby at severe risk of exhaustion, infection, and birth asphyxia.

From a practical standpoint, the definition of abnormal labour hinges on time and progress. If a primigravida (a woman having her first baby) spends an excessive number of hours in the first stage without adequate cervical change, or if pushing in the second stage exceeds standard time limits without descent, nurses must recognize this delay immediately. Early identification prevents prolonged obstructed labour, which remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and obstetric fistula in low-resource settings.

The Four Ps of Labour

Labour progresses successfully through the interplay of four primary factors. When any one of these elements fails, abnormal labour ensues:

  • Power: The force generated by uterine contractions and maternal pushing efforts. Inadequate contractions lead to poor cervical dilation.
  • Passenger: The fetus, including its size, presentation, and position. A large baby or a breech position can obstruct progress.
  • Passage: The maternal pelvis and soft tissues. A narrow or contracted bony pelvis creates mechanical obstruction.
  • Psyche: The emotional state, anxiety levels, and psychological readiness of the mother. High stress releases catecholamines that can inhibit uterine contractility.

Classification and Types of Abnormal Labour

Abnormal labour manifests in several distinct patterns that nurses must learn to differentiate during intrapartum care:

  1. Prolonged Latent Phase: Lasting longer than 20 hours in nulliparas or 14 hours in multiparas before active labour truly begins.
  2. Protracted Active Phase: Cervical dilation proceeds at a slower rate than the normal baseline of 1 centimeter per hour.
  3. Arrest of Dilatation: No progressive cervical change occurs for two hours or more during the active phase despite adequate contractions.
  4. Arrest of Descent: The fetus fails to move down further through the pelvis for a specific timeframe during the second stage of labour.

Causes and Risk Factors of Dystocia

Understanding why dystocia happens allows maternity nurses to anticipate complications in high-risk bookings. Uterine inertia—where the myometrium fails to contract effectively—often stems from overdistension due to polyhydramnios or multiple gestation. Cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD) occurs when the baby's head is simply too large to pass safely through the maternal bony pelvis, a common scenario in short-statured mothers or macrosomic infants. Malpositions like persistent occiput posterior (where the baby faces the mother's abdomen) force the widest diameter of the fetal head against the pelvis, halting descent. Additionally, extreme maternal fatigue or heavy regional anesthesia can blunt the natural urge to push, turning a normal second stage into a prolonged struggle.

Clinical Assessment and Monitoring Using the Partograph

The partograph is a standardized graphical tool used to monitor the progress of labour and maternal-fetal wellbeing. Plotting cervical dilation against time helps nurses identify deviations instantly. The graph features an alert line and an action line. When the plotted cervical dilation crosses the alert line, it serves as a warning that labour is slower than normal and the patient must be transferred to a higher level of care if seen in a primary clinic. If the dilation crosses the action line—usually positioned four hours to the right of the alert line—definite medical and surgical interventions must be initiated without delay.

Beyond cervical dilation, the partograph tracks fetal heart rate, amniotic fluid color, maternal pulse, blood pressure, and urine output. A common clinical error is failing to update the partograph regularly during busy shifts; however, accurate and timely charting is the single best defense against undetected prolonged labour.

Nursing Management and Interventions

Active nursing care during abnormal labour focuses on supportive measures, comfort, and safety. Nurses should encourage frequent positional changes, such as lateral recumbent or upright kneeling positions, which use gravity to help correct malpositions like occiput posterior. Ensuring adequate hydration through oral fluids or intravenous lines prevents maternal ketosis and exhaustion. Continuous emotional reassurance reduces panic, thereby lowering stress hormone levels. Regular monitoring of vital signs and fetal heart tones ensures that any sign of fetal distress is caught early. Nurses must also advocate for the patient, ensuring she understands every procedure before it is performed.

Medical and Surgical Management

When supportive nursing measures fail to correct dystocia, medical and surgical interventions become necessary:

  • Oxytocin Augmentation: Infused via an infusion pump to stimulate stronger and more frequent contractions when uterine inertia is present. It requires strict fetal heart monitoring because hyperstimulation can cause fetal distress.
  • Artificial Rupture of Membranes (ARM): Also known as amniotomy, this procedure releases prostaglandins and brings the fetal head directly against the cervix to speed up dilation.
  • Operative Vaginal Delivery: The use of forceps or vacuum extractors when the second stage is prolonged and the fetal head is low in the pelvis.
  • Cesarean Section: Performed when cephalopelvic disproportion, severe fetal distress, or absolute mechanical obstruction prevents safe vaginal delivery.

Complications and Maternal-Fetal Outcomes

Failing to manage abnormal labour promptly leads to severe, life-threatening complications for both mother and baby. Prolonged pressure from the fetal head on maternal soft tissues can cause ischemic necrosis, resulting in obstetric fistulae (vesicovaginal or rectovaginal). Uterine rupture is another catastrophic risk, particularly if oxytocin is used improperly in the presence of cephalopelvic disproportion. Postpartum hemorrhage frequently follows prolonged labour because the exhausted myometrium fails to contract effectively after delivery to clamp down on placental sites. For the newborn, prolonged hypoxia causes birth asphyxia, meconium aspiration syndrome, and low Apgar scores, highlighting why diligent intrapartum nursing care is paramount.
