Abnormal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Abnormal Labour

Abnormal labour, clinically referred to as dystocia or difficult labour, represents a significant deviation from the normal physiological progress of childbirth. In the context of obstetric nursing in Nigeria, recognizing abnormal labour early is vital for reducing maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality. Normal labour follows a predictable, progressive sequence through distinct stages, resulting in the vaginal delivery of the fetus and placenta without undue trauma. Conversely, abnormal labour is characterized by abnormally slow progress (prolonged labour), arrest of dilation or descent, or mechanical obstructions that prevent a safe vaginal birth. Dystocia can manifest in the latent or active phase of the first stage of labour, or during the second stage. As nursing students preparing for professional examinations, you must understand that the diagnosis of abnormal labour is established when cervical dilation fails to progress at the expected rate or when fetal descent is compromised. Delay in identifying these deviations can lead to catastrophic complications such as uterine rupture, postpartum hemorrhage, severe birth asphyxia, and obstetric fistula, which remains a major public health concern in resource-limited settings. A thorough grasp of the baseline definition allows midwives and nurses to advocate promptly for interventions, utilizing evidence-based guidelines to transition the patient from conservative monitoring to active medical or surgical management when necessary.

The Four Ps of Labour: Power, Passenger, Passage, and Psyche

The progress and outcome of labour depend heavily on the interaction of four critical elements known as the Four Ps of Labour: Power, Passenger, Passage, and Psyche. Understanding these components is essential for nursing students assessing why labour may become abnormal. 'Power' refers to uterine contractions and maternal pushing efforts; inadequate frequency, intensity, or duration of contractions leads to hypotonic uterine dysfunction and slow progress. 'Passenger' encompasses the fetus, placenta, and membranes. Fetal factors such as macrosomia, abnormal presentations (e.g., brow or face presentation), and malpositions (e.g., occipito-posterior position) can create mechanical barriers to delivery. 'Passage' relates to the maternal pelvis and soft tissues. A contracted pelvis, abnormal pelvic shapes (such as android or platypelloid), or pelvic tumors can obstruct the birth canal, making normal descent impossible. Finally, 'Psyche' reflects the emotional and psychological state of the mother. High levels of maternal anxiety, fear, stress, and lack of support trigger the release of catecholamines, which can inhibit effective uterine contractions and impede cervical dilation. When assessing a parturient woman, nurses must systematically evaluate all four Ps to pinpoint the exact root cause of abnormal labour progression.

Classification and Types of Abnormal Labour

Abnormal labour is broadly classified based on the underlying mechanical or physiological failure. The primary types include abnormalities of the forces (power disorders), abnormalities of the birth canal (passage disorders), and abnormalities of the fetus (passenger disorders). Power disorders are subclassified into hypotonic uterine dysfunction, where contractions are too weak and infrequent to dilate the cervix, and hypertonic or uncoordinated uterine dysfunction, where basal tone is elevated and contractions are erratic, ineffective, and painful. Passage disorders involve cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD), where the fetal head is too large to fit through the maternal pelvis, or soft tissue obstructions like cervical scarring or fibroids. Passenger disorders include malpositions like persistent occipitoposterior position, malpresentations such as breech, face, or transverse lie, and structural anomalies like hydrocephalus. Another critical classification categorizes abnormal labour into protraction disorders (slower than normal progress in dilation or descent) and arrest disorders (complete cessation of progress). Recognizing these distinct types allows the multidisciplinary obstetric team to tailor interventions specifically to the problem, distinguishing between dysfunctional labour that may respond to oxytocin augmentation and mechanical obstruction that strictly requires surgical delivery.

Causes and Risk Factors of Dystocia

Dystocia does not occur in a vacuum; various maternal and fetal risk factors predispose a woman to abnormal labour. Nulliparity is a major risk factor, as first-time mothers have unstrengthened pelvic floor tissues and uncoordinated uterine muscle patterns. Other maternal factors include advanced maternal age, maternal obesity, short stature (which often correlates with a contracted pelvis), and structural uterine anomalies such as a septate or bicornuate uterus. Induction of labour, especially in unfavorable bishop scores, and the overuse of epidural anesthesia can also contribute to uterine muscle relaxation and subsequent hypotonic dysfunction. Fetal risk factors include macrosomia (fetal weight over 4,000 grams), post-term pregnancy, multiple gestation (twins, triplets), and fetal anomalies like hydrocephalus or sacrococcygeal teratoma. Environmental and systemic factors, such as maternal exhaustion, dehydration, and lack of continuous intrapartum support, further compound these risks. Nurses must conduct a comprehensive antenatal and intrapartum risk assessment to identify women predisposed to dystocia, ensuring close surveillance and timely preparation for complex obstetric interventions.

Clinical Assessment and Monitoring Using the Partograph

The partograph is an essential, graphical tool used in Nigerian maternity units to monitor labour progress, maternal condition, and fetal well-being. Clinical assessment using the partograph helps detect abnormal labour early before life-threatening complications occur. Active management of labour plots cervical dilation against time, utilizing two critical lines: the alert line and the action line. The alert line starts at 4 cm of cervical dilation and signifies a rate of dilation of at least 1 cm per hour. When the plotting of cervical dilation moves to the right of or crosses the alert line, it serves as a warning that labour may be prolonged, prompting closer observation and transfer to a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility if necessary. The action line, typically drawn four hours to the right of the alert line, indicates when definitive medical or surgical interventions—such as amniotomy, oxytocin augmentation, or cesarean section—must be implemented. In addition to cervical dilation, the partograph tracks descent of the fetal head, uterine contractions per 10 minutes, fetal heart rate, maternal pulse, blood pressure, temperature, urine output, and drugs or fluids administered. Nursing students must master accurate partograph plotting, as it is the gold standard for objective decision-making during intrapartum care.

Nursing Management and Interventions

Nursing management of abnormal labour requires a combination of vigilant monitoring, supportive care, and timely intervention. Once dystocia is identified, the nurse's primary role is to ensure continuous maternal and fetal assessment. Fetal heart rate monitoring via intermittent auscultation or electronic fetal monitoring is essential to detect signs of fetal distress. Maternal vital signs—including temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and hydration status—must be closely monitored, and intravenous access should be secured to correct or prevent dehydration and electrolyte imbalance. Supporting the 'Psyche' is equally critical; nurses should provide continuous emotional support, reassuring presence, encouragement, and pain management strategies. Positioning the mother in upright, lateral, or hands-and-knees positions can encourage optimal fetal rotation and descent, particularly in malpositions. The nurse must also monitor bladder distension, encouraging frequent voiding or catheterization, as a full bladder can impede fetal head descent. Clear, accurate documentation on the partograph and prompt communication with the attending physician or obstetrician when deviations occur are core competencies for every nursing student.

Medical and Surgical Management

Medical and surgical management of abnormal labour is determined by the underlying cause identified during clinical assessment. If hypotonic uterine dysfunction is diagnosed and cephalopelvic disproportion has been conclusively ruled out, medical augmentation of labour using oxytocin infusion is often initiated. Oxytocin stimulates rhythmic uterine contractions, but it must be administered via an infusion pump with extreme caution and continuous fetal monitoring to prevent uterine hyperstimulation and fetal hypoxia. Artificial rupture of membranes (amniotomy) may also be performed to enhance uterine activity and allow direct assessment of amniotic fluid clarity. When mechanical obstructions such as CPD, transverse lie, or severe fetal distress are present, surgical intervention via emergency cesarean section is the definitive and safest management strategy. In cases where the cervix is fully dilated, the membranes are ruptured, the vertex is engaged at a low station, and the mother is exhausted or failing to push effectively, instrumental vaginal delivery using forceps or a ventouse (vacuum extractor) may be considered by skilled obstetric personnel. Nurses play a crucial collaborative role in preparing patients for theatre, obtaining informed consent, checking emergency equipment, and providing meticulous post-procedure care.

Complications and Maternal-Fetal Outcomes

Failure to recognize and manage abnormal labour promptly leads to severe maternal and fetal complications. Prolonged and obstructed labour places immense physiological stress on both mother and baby. Maternal complications include uterine rupture—a life-threatening surgical emergency characterized by sudden severe pain, cessation of contractions, and fetal distress or disappearance of fetal parts. Other risks include postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) due to uterine atony from prolonged muscle fatigue, severe maternal exhaustion, genital tract trauma, and puerperal sepsis following prolonged rupture of membranes. Long-term morbidities, particularly prevalent in developing nations, include vesicovaginal fistula (VVF) and rectovaginal fistula resulting from prolonged tissue ischemia and necrosis caused by the impacted fetal head. Fetal and neonatal complications are equally grave, encompassing birth asphyxia due to placental hypoperfusion, meconium aspiration syndrome, intracranial hemorrhage from traumatic instrumentation, neonatal sepsis, and fetal death. For nursing students, understanding these devastating outcomes reinforces the clinical imperative for vigilant intrapartum monitoring, prompt escalation of care, and skilled advocacy during childbirth.
