Safe Delivery

Introduction to Safe Delivery and the Clean Birth Kit

Safe delivery practices aim to prevent maternal and neonatal infections, which remain leading causes of mortality in many low-resource settings. The cornerstone of community-level and low-resource facility delivery is the Clean Birth Kit, an inexpensive and life-saving intervention. Midwives and nurses must ensure these kits are inspected, unexpired, and readily accessible before labour progresses.

  • Soap: Used for thorough handwashing by the birth attendant and for washing the mother's perineal area before delivery.
  • Clean surface: A plastic sheet or mat placed under the mother to prevent contamination from the floor or bed.
  • Cord tie: Clean string or tape used to ligate the umbilical cord securely after birth.
  • New blade: A sterile razor blade used to cut the umbilical cord cleanly, avoiding tetanus infection.
  • Cloth: A clean wrapper to dry and wrap the newborn immediately to prevent heat loss.

Preparation of the Delivery Environment and Equipment

A well-prepared environment reduces anxiety for the mother and allows the nurse to manage emergencies efficiently. The room should be warm, draft-free, and well-lit. Temperature regulation is especially critical for the newborn, who cannot shiver and quickly develops hypothermia in cold surroundings.

Essential equipment must be checked and arranged before the second stage begins. This includes sterile delivery trays, suction apparatus, resuscitation equipment for both mother and baby, and emergency medications such as oxytocin. Hand hygiene facilities with running water and soap or alcohol-based hand rub must be operational. Privacy screens and clean linens complete the basic setup, ensuring dignity and infection control go hand in hand.

Monitoring and Assessment During Labour

Continuous monitoring safeguards both maternal and fetal well-being throughout active labour. The partograph is the standard graphical tool used to record cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, uterine contractions, and maternal vital signs over time.

During the first stage, assess cervical dilation and effacement every four hours, or sooner if contractions become intense. Fetal heart rate must be auscultated using a fetoscope or Doppler immediately after a contraction every 30 minutes in the active phase. In the second stage, increase fetal heart rate auscultation to every 5 minutes. Maternal blood pressure, pulse, and temperature are monitored regularly to detect early signs of exhaustion, dehydration, or infection.

The Clean Delivery Process and the Essential Cleans

To prevent puerperal sepsis and neonatal infections, clinical practice relies on the concept of the "essential cleans" during birth:

  1. Clean Hands: Proper hand hygiene performed by all healthcare personnel using soap and water or antiseptic rub.
  2. Clean Perineum: Washing the mother's perineum and vulva with clean water and antiseptic solution from front to back.
  3. Clean Surface: Ensuring the delivery bed or mat is clean and free of biological contaminants.
  4. Clean Cord Cutting and Care: Using a sterile instrument to cut the cord and applying nothing (or recommended antiseptic) to the cord stump post-delivery.

Avoid routine enemas, routine perineal shaving, and routine amniotomy, as these do not improve clean birth outcomes and can increase discomfort or infection risks.

Immediate Care of the Newborn and Apgar Scoring

Immediately after birth, most newborns require simple routine care. Dry the baby thoroughly with a warm towel while simultaneously assessing breathing and crying. Remove the wet towel and place the infant skin-to-skin on the mother's chest, covering both with a dry blanket to promote bonding and maintain body temperature.

The Apgar score is assessed at 1 minute and 5 minutes after birth. It evaluates five parameters: Appearance (skin color), Pulse (heart rate), Grimace (reflex irritability), Activity (muscle tone), and Respiration (breathing effort). Each is scored from 0 to 2, with a maximum score of 10. A score below 7 at 5 minutes indicates the need for continued close monitoring or resuscitation support.

Management of the Third Stage of Labour and Prevention of PPH

The third stage spans from the birth of the baby until the expulsion of the placenta and membranes. This is the most dangerous period for the mother due to the high risk of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH). Active management of the third stage of labour (AMTSL) is standard practice to reduce blood loss.

AMTSL consists of three key steps:

  1. Administration of a uterotonic: Give 10 units of oxytocin intramuscularly within one minute of infant delivery.
  2. Controlled cord traction: Gently pull on the cord while applying counter-pressure on the suprapubic area (placental grip) when the uterus is contracted.
  3. Uterine massage: Massage the fundus immediately after placental delivery and every 15 minutes for the first two hours to ensure it remains contracted.

Infection Prevention and Control in Obstetric Practice

Infection prevention protects both mother and baby from healthcare-associated pathogens. Standard precautions must be maintained during every delivery, regardless of the mother's known infection status.

Key practices include wearing sterile gloves during vaginal examinations and delivery, wearing protective eyewear and aprons if splashing of body fluids is anticipated, and safely disposing of sharps in puncture-proof containers immediately after use. Decontaminate instruments in chlorine solution before washing with soap and water and sterilizing. Hand hygiene before and after touching the patient remains the single most effective way to break the chain of infection.

Identification and Emergency Management of Intrapartum Complications

Intrapartum complications can escalate rapidly, requiring calm, decisive action from the nursing team. Common emergencies include shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse, and postpartum hemorrhage.

  • Cord Prolapse: If the umbilical cord slips below the presenting part, relieve pressure immediately by placing the mother in a knee-chest or exaggerated Sims position and pushing the presenting part upward with a sterile gloved hand until emergency caesarean section. Do not manipulate the exposed cord.
  • Shoulder Dystocia: When the baby's shoulder gets stuck behind the pubic bone, apply McRoberts maneuver (flexing maternal thighs sharply toward her abdomen) and suprapubic pressure. Never pull forcefully on the baby's head.

Postnatal Maternal Care and Documentation

The immediate postnatal period—the first two hours after birth—requires vigilant monitoring because complications can still arise even after the placenta is delivered. Monitor maternal vital signs, uterine firmness, and vaginal bleeding every 15 minutes for the first hour, and every 30 minutes for the second hour.

Accurate documentation is the final legal and clinical responsibility of the nurse. Record the exact time of delivery, the condition of the mother and baby, Apgar scores, estimated blood loss, medications administered, and any complications encountered. Clear notes ensure continuity of care across nursing shifts and protect both the patient and the healthcare provider.
