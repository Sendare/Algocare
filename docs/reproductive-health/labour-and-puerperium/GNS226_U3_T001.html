Labour

Introduction and Definition of Labour

Labour is the physiological process by which the products of conception—fetus, placenta, and membranes—are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. To be clinically classified as normal or term labour, the onset must occur spontaneously between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation with a single fetus presenting in the vertex position. The entire process culminates without artificial augmentation or major surgical intervention, resulting in the birth of a healthy newborn and the safe delivery of the placenta. From a practical nursing standpoint, recognizing the true onset of labour prevents premature interventions or unnecessary hospital admissions. True labour is characterized by progressive, regular uterine contractions that increase in frequency, intensity, and duration, accompanied by the effacement and dilation of the cervix. Additionally, the passage of the 'show'—a mucus plug mixed with blood from the ruptured cervical capillaries—serves as a classic physical indicator that the cervix is actively changing.

Causes and Physiology of the Onset of Labour

The exact trigger that initiates human labour remains a complex interplay of endocrine, paracrine, and mechanical signals rather than a single isolated event. As pregnancy reaches term, a shift in the hormonal balance occurs, notably characterized by a withdrawal of progesterone's relaxant effect on the myometrium and an increase in estrogen sensitivity. Estrogen stimulates the synthesis of myometrial receptors, particularly gap junctions and oxytocin receptors, making the uterine muscle far more responsive to contractile stimuli. Simultaneously, the fetal-placental unit and maternal tissues release local signaling molecules: - **Prostaglandins**: Synthesized within the decidua and fetal membranes, these compounds promote cervical ripening and stimulate powerful myometrial contractions. - **Oxytocin**: Released in a pulsatile fashion from the posterior pituitary gland, this hormone works synergistically with local prostaglandins to drive forceful uterine contractions. - **Corticotropin-Releasing Hormone (CRH)**: Levels rise exponentially toward the end of gestation, driving placental cortisol production and subsequently increasing estrogen synthesis. - **Mechanical Distension**: Overstretching of the uterine muscle fibers due to fetal growth activates stretch-sensitive ion channels, triggering local excitation.

Normal Anatomy and Mechanisms of Normal Labour

The successful passage of the fetus through the maternal pelvis relies on a coordinated sequence of movements known as the cardinal movements of labour. These mechanical adjustments allow the irregularly shaped fetal head to navigate the varying diameters of the pelvis. The bony pelvis, classified typically as gynecoid, offers the most favorable dimensions for a vaginal delivery. As uterine contractions push the fetus downward, the following cardinal movements occur sequentially: 1. **Engagement**: The widest diameter of the fetal presenting part passes down into the pelvic inlet. 2. **Descent**: Continuous downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal, driven by uterine contractions and maternal pushing. 3. **Flexion**: The fetal chin tucks firmly against the chest, presenting the smallest suboccipitobregmatic diameter to the pelvis. 4. **Internal Rotation**: The fetal head rotates, usually bringing the occiput to the anterior position underneath the maternal pubic symphysis. 5. **Extension**: As the head reaches the perineum, it pivots upward and emerges from the vagina through extension. 6. **Restitution and External Rotation**: The head aligns back with the shoulders, and the shoulders subsequently rotate internally to fit the pelvic outlet. 7. **Expulsion**: The anterior shoulder delivers under the pubic arch, followed quickly by the posterior shoulder and the rest of the body.

The Four Stages of Labour and Clinical Management

| Stage | Clinical Definition | Primary Nursing Actions | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **First Stage** | From the onset of true labour contractions until full cervical dilation (10 cm). | Monitor contraction pattern, assess vital signs, perform sterile vaginal examinations judiciously, and offer emotional support. | | **Second Stage** | From full cervical dilation until the complete birth of the baby. | Guide maternal pushing efforts, monitor fetal heart rate continuously or intermittently, and prepare sterile delivery equipment. | | **Third Stage** | From the birth of the infant until the complete delivery of the placenta and membranes. | Administer uterotonic drugs as ordered, monitor for postpartum hemorrhage, and inspect the placenta for completeness. | | **Fourth Stage** | The immediate postpartum period lasting about one to two hours following placental delivery. | Monitor lochial flow, check uterine tone frequently, assess vital signs, and encourage early maternal-infant bonding. | Clinical management across these stages requires vigilant observation. The first stage is divided into a latent phase of slow cervical change and an active phase characterized by rapid dilation. During the third stage, active management of the third stage (AMTSL)—which includes administering an oxytocic agent, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage—significantly reduces the incidence of primary postpartum hemorrhage, a major cause of maternal mortality in busy clinical settings across Nigeria.

Maternal and Fetal Assessment During Labour

Continuous surveillance during labour ensures early detection of fetal distress and maternal exhaustion. The partograph serves as a vital graphical tool used globally in maternity units to record progress of labour, maternal condition, and fetal well-being on a single sheet of paper. Plotting cervical dilation against time helps clinicians instantly spot deviation from normal progress, signaling when to intervene. Fetal monitoring focuses on assessing heart rate patterns relative to uterine contractions. Normal baseline fetal heart rate ranges between 110 and 160 beats per minute. Accelerations indicate a healthy, responsive nervous system, whereas decelerations—such as late decelerations caused by uteroplacental insufficiency—require immediate repositioning, oxygen administration, and evaluation by the medical team. Maternal pulse, blood pressure, temperature, and urine output are tracked concurrently to identify signs of dehydration, infection, or preeclampsia.

Pain Management and Supportive Care in Labour

Labour pain is unique, complex, and intensely personal. It involves visceral pain from cervical dilation and uterine ischemia during the first stage, transitioning to sharp somatic pain as the fetus stretches the perineum during the second stage. Effective supportive care combines non-pharmacological comfort measures with pharmacological interventions tailored to patient preference, hospital resources, and safety. Non-pharmacological approaches empower coping and reduce anxiety: - Continuous emotional support and a reassuring presence from nursing staff. - Ambulation, birth balls, and frequent position changes to assist fetal descent. - Hydrotherapy, warm showers, and gentle massage of the lower back. - Breathing techniques and focused relaxation. When non-pharmacological methods are insufficient, pharmacological options are introduced. These range from systemic opioids like pethidine to regional anesthesia such as epidural blocks, which provide excellent pain relief while allowing the mother to remain alert and participatory.

Complications of Labour and Emergency Nursing Interventions

Despite careful monitoring, labour can occasionally deviate into obstetric emergencies that threaten the life and health of both mother and baby. Recognizing these warning signs promptly allows nurses to initiate life-saving measures and alert the multidisciplinary medical team without delay. Key complications include: - **Dystocia (Prolonged Labour)**: Caused by power issues (ineffective contractions), passenger issues (malpresentation or macrosomia), or passage issues (pelvic contraction). Management involves oxytocin augmentation or preparing for a cesarean section. - **Cord Prolapse**: The umbilical cord slips down ahead of the presenting part, compromising fetal oxygenation through vessel compression. The immediate nursing action is to insert a sterile gloved hand into the vagina to elevate the presenting part off the cord while placing the patient in a knee-chest position. - **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH)**: Excessive bleeding following delivery, most commonly caused by uterine atony. Immediate interventions include bimanual uterine massage, empty bladder catheterization, and administering uterotonic drugs. - **Uterine Rupture**: A catastrophic tearing of the uterine wall, frequently associated with previous uterine scars or obstructed labour, presenting with sudden sharp pain and loss of fetal station.
