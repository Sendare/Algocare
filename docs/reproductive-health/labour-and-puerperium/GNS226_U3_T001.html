Labour

Introduction and Definition of Labour

Labour is the physiological process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. For clinical purposes, labour is defined as regular, painful uterine contractions that increase in frequency and intensity, accompanied by progressive effacement and dilation of the cervix.

True labour must be carefully differentiated from false labour, known medically as Braxton Hicks contractions. Braxton Hicks contractions are irregular, do not increase in intensity, and fail to produce cervical changes. When a pregnant woman arrives at the clinic or labour ward, the nurse must perform a thorough assessment to determine whether true labour has truly begun, preventing unnecessary hospital admissions while ensuring timely care for active cases.

Physiology and Causes of Labour Onset

The exact trigger that starts human labour remains a complex interaction of hormonal, mechanical, and biochemical signals. As the pregnancy reaches term, several key changes occur within the maternal-fetal unit:

  • Progesterone Withdrawal: Progesterone relaxes the uterine muscles throughout pregnancy. A decline in its relative effect allows the myometrium to become sensitive to oxytocin.
  • Oxytocin Release: The posterior pituitary gland secretes oxytocin, which stimulates powerful contractions of the uterine muscle.
  • Prostaglandin Synthesis: Fetal membranes and the decidua produce prostaglandins, which soften the cervix and enhance myometrial contractility.
  • Fetal Cortisol Surge: The mature fetal adrenal gland releases cortisol, which shifts the hormonal balance in the placenta toward estrogen dominance.

Mechanisms of Normal Labour

For a baby to pass successfully through the birth canal, the fetus must perform specific positional adjustments known as the cardinal movements of labour. These movements occur concurrently as the fetus descends under the force of uterine contractions.

  1. Engagement: The widest part of the fetal head enters the maternal pelvic inlet.
  2. Descent: The downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal.
  3. Flexion: The fetal chin tucks tightly against the chest to present the smallest head diameter.
  4. Internal Rotation: The fetal head rotates, usually bringing the occiput to the front beneath the pubic symphysis.
  5. Extension: The head emerges from the vagina as the occiput pivots upward.
  6. Restitution and External Rotation: The head turns back to align naturally with the shoulders.
  7. Expulsion: The anterior and posterior shoulders are born, followed quickly by the rest of the body.

The Four Stages of Labour and Clinical Management

Labour is divided into four distinct clinical stages, each requiring specific nursing interventions and monitoring protocols.

StageClinical DescriptionKey Nursing Care
First StageOnset of true labour to full cervical dilation (10 cm)Monitor contractions, track cervical changes, encourage hydration
Second StageFull cervical dilation to the birth of the babyAssist with pushing, monitor fetal heart rate, prepare delivery tools
Third StageBirth of the baby to the delivery of the placentaAdminister uterotonics, inspect placenta, watch for bleeding
Fourth StageFirst one to two hours following placental deliveryMonitor vital signs, assess uterine tone, check lochia flow

A common misconception is that the third stage is completely risk-free once the baby cries; however, postpartum hemorrhage most commonly originates during this stage due to uterine atony.

Maternal and Fetal Assessment During Labour

Continuous and intermittent assessment ensures the safety of both mother and baby. Nurses evaluate uterine contractions by palpating the fundus, noting frequency, duration, and intensity.

Fetal well-being is primarily monitored using auscultation with a fetoscope or Doppler, or through continuous electronic fetal monitoring (EFM). The baseline fetal heart rate normally ranges between 110 and 160 beats per minute. Nurses pay close attention to decelerations—dips in the fetal heart rate—which can indicate fetal distress or umbilical cord compression. Maternal vital signs, including blood pressure, pulse, and temperature, are checked regularly to detect infection, dehydration, or pregnancy-induced hypertension.

Pain Management and Supportive Care in Labour

Labour pain is intense and multifaceted, influenced by physical, emotional, and cultural factors. Effective nursing care combines non-pharmacological comfort measures with pharmacological options when requested and appropriate.

Non-pharmacological techniques include massage, hydrotherapy, breathing exercises, position changes, and continuous emotional presence. These methods help reduce anxiety and enhance the mother's ability to cope.

Pharmacological management ranges from systemic analgesics, such as opioids, to regional anesthesia like epidural blocks. The nurse plays a vital role in educating the mother about her choices, monitoring side effects like maternal hypotension or respiratory depression, and supporting her decisions without judgment.

Complications and Emergency Nursing Interventions

While most births proceed normally, unexpected emergencies can arise at any moment. Nurses must remain vigilant to recognize signs of danger early.

  • Cord Prolapse: The umbilical cord slips ahead of the presenting part, cutting off fetal oxygen supply. The nurse must immediately insert a sterile gloved hand to elevate the presenting part off the cord and position the mother in a knee-chest position.
  • Shoulder Dystocia: The baby's shoulder becomes stuck behind the pubic bone after the head is born. The nurse must quickly apply suprapubic pressure and assist with the McRoberts maneuver.
  • Uterine Rupture: A tear in the uterine wall, often associated with previous uterine scars, causing sudden severe pain and fetal distress.

Prompt recognition and rapid team communication are vital to saving maternal and infant lives.
