Labour

Introduction and Definition of Labour

Labour is defined as the physiological process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. It typically occurs at term, which is between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation, resulting in the birth of a viable infant. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding this definition forms the baseline for distinguishing normal physiological births from complicated or preterm events that require urgent medical intervention. Labour is clinically characterized by regular, painful uterine contractions that increase in frequency, duration, and intensity, accompanied by progressive effacement and dilation of the cervix, and downward descent of the presenting part of the fetus. True labour must be carefully differentiated from false labour (Braxton Hicks contractions), which are irregular, do not cause cervical changes, and often subside with rest or hydration. Recognizing the onset of true labour prevents premature admissions and allows nurses to prepare the mother psychologically and physically for the journey ahead. Furthermore, holistic nursing care during this phase involves assessing maternal vital signs, evaluating emotional readiness, establishing a supportive nurse-patient relationship, and providing accurate documentation in the partograph. A thorough understanding of these foundational principles equips the student nurse to provide safe, evidence-based maternity care within various healthcare settings across Nigeria, from primary health care clinics to tertiary teaching hospitals.

Causes and Physiology of the Onset of Labour

The exact trigger for the onset of human labour remains multifactorial, involving a complex interplay of endocrine, paracrine, and mechanical factors. As pregnancy reaches term, there is a shift in the hormonal balance within the maternal-fetal unit. Progesterone, which maintains uterine quiescence and relaxes smooth muscle throughout pregnancy, begins to decline in its effective action, while estrogen levels rise, increasing the sensitivity of the myometrium to uterotonic agents. This hormonal shift upregulates oxytocin receptors in the myometrium, preparing the uterus for coordinated, forceful contractions. Additionally, fetal cortisol production increases towards the end of gestation, stimulating the placenta to produce prostaglandins, which play a vital role in cervical ripening and softening by breaking down collagen fibers. Oxytocin, synthesized in the hypothalamus and released by the posterior pituitary gland, acts directly on myometrial cells to stimulate powerful contractions via a positive feedback loop known as Ferguson's reflex; as the fetal presenting part stretches the lower uterine segment and cervix, neuroendocrine signals trigger further release of oxytocin. Mechanical stretching of the uterine muscle fibers by the growing fetus also activates intracellular signaling pathways that promote contractility. Understanding these physiological mechanisms is crucial for nursing students, as it explains why certain interventions, such as the administration of oxytocin or prostaglandins, are utilized to induce or augment labour in clinical practice.

Normal Anatomy and Mechanisms of Normal Labour

The normal anatomy of the female pelvis and the mechanisms of labour dictate how a fetus navigates the birth canal. The bony pelvis, particularly the gynecoid pelvis, provides the ideal passage for fetal descent. To successfully traverse this passage, the fetus undergoes a series of cardinal movements: engagement, descent, flexion, internal rotation, extension, restitution, and external rotation, followed by expulsion. Engagement occurs when the widest diameter of the fetal presenting part passes through the pelvic inlet. Descent is the continuous downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal, driven by uterine contractions and maternal pushing. Flexion allows the smallest diameter of the fetal head to present to the maternal pelvis, aided by resistance from the pelvic floor. As the head reaches the pelvic floor, internal rotation usually turns the occiput anteriorly towards the symphysis pubis, aligning the longest diameter of the fetal head with the longest diameter of the pelvic outlet. Extension occurs as the head is born under the pubic arch, followed by restitution, where the head twists back to align with the shoulders. External rotation then aligns the shoulders anteroposteriorly for delivery. Student nurses must understand these cardinal movements to accurately monitor labour progress during vaginal examinations and to anticipate normal deliveries or recognize malpositions early.

The Four Stages of Labour and Clinical Management

Labour is clinically divided into four distinct stages, each requiring specific nursing management. The first stage spans from the onset of true labour contractions to full cervical dilation (10 cm) and is subdivided into a latent phase and an active phase. During the first stage, nurses monitor contractions, assess cervical dilation using sterile vaginal examinations, encourage hydration, and promote optimal maternal positioning. The second stage begins at full cervical dilation and ends with the complete delivery of the fetus. Nursing care here involves coaching the mother during pushing, monitoring fetal heart tones closely after every contraction, and preparing for neonatal resuscitation and immediate skin-to-skin contact. The third stage starts immediately after the delivery of the fetus and concludes with the expulsion of the placenta and membranes. Active management of the third stage (AMTSL)—which includes the administration of a uterotonic drug like oxytocin, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage—is crucial to prevent postpartum hemorrhage, a leading cause of maternal mortality in Nigeria. The fourth stage encompasses the first one to two hours immediately following placental delivery, a critical window for stabilizing the mother. Nursing responsibilities in this stage include monitoring vital signs, assessing uterine tone to prevent atony, evaluating lochial discharge, and supporting early breastfeeding initiation.

Maternal and Fetal Assessment During Labour

Continuous and systematic assessment of both the mother and the fetus is the cornerstone of safe intrapartum care. Maternal assessment includes regular monitoring of vital signs (blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiration) to detect early signs of infection, hemorrhage, or pre-eclampsia. Nurses must also assess uterine contraction frequency, duration, and strength, alongside monitoring maternal hydration, urinary output, and psychological well-being. Fetal well-being is primarily evaluated through intermittent auscultation using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler ultrasound, or via continuous electronic fetal monitoring (cardiotocography) when indicated. Fetal heart rate baseline, variability, accelerations, and decelerations provide vital clues regarding fetal oxygenation and acid-base status. In many resource-constrained settings across Nigeria, the WHO partograph is an essential, inexpensive tool used to record all observations of labour progress, maternal condition, and fetal condition on a single sheet of graph paper. The partograph features an alert line and an action line; crossing the action line signals potential prolonged labour or fetal distress, prompting timely referral to a higher level of care. Student nurses must master plotting parameters on the partograph accurately, as it facilitates rapid decision-making, multidisciplinary communication, and reduces maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality.

Pain Management and Supportive Care in Labour

Labour pain is a unique physiological and psychological experience influenced by cultural background, previous experiences, and emotional support. Effective pain management and supportive care enhance the birthing experience and promote maternal coping mechanisms. Non-pharmacological comfort measures play a vital role and can be safely administered by student nurses. These include continuous emotional presence (doula support or supportive nursing care), encouragement, hydrotherapy (warm showers or baths), massage, counter-pressure, breathing techniques, and encouraging upright or mobile maternal positions to facilitate fetal descent. Pharmacological pain relief options may also be utilized depending on resource availability and institutional protocols. These range from systemic analgesics (such as opioids like pethidine or tramadol, administered with caution to avoid neonatal respiratory depression) to regional anesthesia techniques like epidural or spinal blocks, which provide excellent pain relief during the first and second stages. Nursing responsibilities during pharmacological pain management include monitoring maternal blood pressure, observing for adverse drug reactions, assessing respiratory rate, and ensuring continuous fetal monitoring. Providing compassionate, non-judgmental, and individualized supportive care empowers the parturient woman, reduces anxiety, and fosters a positive childbirth experience within the Nigerian healthcare system.

Complications of Labour and Emergency Nursing Interventions

Despite careful monitoring, complications can arise unexpectedly during labour, demanding prompt identification and decisive emergency nursing interventions. Common complications include postpartum hemorrhage, shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse, uterine rupture, and fetal distress. Shoulder dystocia occurs when the fetal anterior shoulder impacts against the maternal pubic symphysis after head delivery; nurses must immediately assist by applying McRoberts maneuver (hyperflexion of maternal legs) and suprapubic pressure while calling for obstetric help. Umbilical cord prolapse is an obstetric emergency where the cord slips below the presenting part, compromising fetal oxygenation; the nurse must immediately insert gloved fingers into the vagina to elevate the presenting part off the cord, place the mother in a knee-chest or exaggerated Sims position, and prepare for immediate emergency cesarean section. Fetal distress, indicated by abnormal fetal heart rate patterns such as severe bradycardia or late decelerations, requires intrauterine resuscitation measures including maternal repositioning to the left lateral position, oxygen administration, stopping oxytocin infusions, and rapid delivery preparation. Postpartum hemorrhage, frequently caused by uterine atony, requires immediate fundal massage, administration of uterotonics, and establishment of intravenous access for fluid resuscitation. Student nurses must remain calm, act within their scope of practice, communicate effectively with the multidisciplinary team, and execute emergency protocols swiftly to save maternal and neonatal lives.
