Neonatal Tetanus

Introduction and Definition of Neonatal Tetanus

Neonatal tetanus, locally called 'kuku' or 'efere' in some Nigerian communities, is a severe, often fatal systemic infection affecting newborns. It is caused by the neurotoxin produced by the bacterium *Clostridium tetani*. The disease typically strikes infants within their first month of life, usually between the third and fourteenth day after birth, after they had previously been sucking and crying normally. While rare in high-income countries due to routine maternal immunization, it remains a significant public health concern in parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, directly linked to inadequate healthcare access and traditional birth practices.

Aetiology and Pathophysiology

  • The Organism: *Clostridium tetani* is an anaerobic, spore-forming, Gram-positive bacillus whose spores survive indefinitely in soil, dust, and animal feces.
  • Spore Entry: Spores enter the infant's body primarily through the unhealed umbilical stump, particularly when cut with unsterile instruments.
  • Toxin Production: Under low-oxygen conditions in the dead tissue of the stump, spores germinate into active bacteria that release tetanospasmin.
  • Nerve Travel: The toxin travels via retrograde axonal transport to the central nervous system, blocking inhibitory neurotransmitters like GABA and glycine.
  • Muscle Spasms: The loss of inhibition leads to unchecked, simultaneous contraction of opposing muscle groups, causing severe spasms and rigidity.

Modes of Transmission and Risk Factors in Nigeria

Transmission in Nigeria is heavily tied to traditional home deliveries and unsafe umbilical cord care. When an umbilical cord is severed using unsterilized blades, knives, broken glass, or scissors, tetanus spores gain direct entry. Subsequent application of harmful substances to the stump—such as animal dung, ashes, mud, or unpurified herbal mixtures—further increases infection risk. Additionally, infants born to unimmunized mothers lack passive immunity via transplacental antibodies, leaving them completely vulnerable during their first fragile weeks of life.

Clinical Manifestations and Staging

The clinical picture of neonatal tetanus is distinct and distressing. The initial sign is almost always the baby's inability to suck or feed, followed by extreme irritability and restlessness. Soon, generalized muscle stiffness sets in, accompanied by characteristic facial spasms known as trismus (lockjaw) and risus sardonicus (a grimacing, sardonic smile). As the disease progresses, painful, generalized muscle spasms are triggered by minimal stimuli like touch, bright light, or sudden noises. Spasms involving the respiratory muscles can lead to cyanosis, hypoxia, and apnea.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis

Diagnosing neonatal tetanus relies primarily on careful clinical evaluation rather than laboratory tests. The nurse performs a thorough head-to-toe assessment, paying close attention to the umbilical stump for signs of sepsis, redness, or traditional dressings. Assessment also involves checking the baby's ability to open the mouth and evaluating muscle tone. A key clinical test used by clinicians is the spatula test, where touching the posterior pharyngeal wall with a tongue depressor normally causes the baby to bite down or spasm rather than gag. Nurses must document the frequency, duration, and triggers of all muscle spasms.

Medical and Nursing Management

  • Quiet Environment: Place the infant in a dark, quiet incubator or corner to minimize sensory triggers that provoke spasms.
  • Neutral Thermal Environment: Maintain stable body temperature without overheating.
  • Medication Administration: Administer human tetanus immune globulin (HTIG) to neutralize unbound toxin, and antibiotics like metronidazole to eliminate the bacteria.
  • Airway Support: Keep suction equipment and oxygen readily available to manage respiratory distress during sudden spasms.
  • Nutrition: Provide alternative feeding methods such as nasogastric tube feeding to prevent aspiration and maintain hydration without stimulating jaw spasms.

Potential Complications

Neonatal tetanus carries a high mortality rate, and survivors often face significant acute complications. Asphyxia and hypoxia frequently occur due to prolonged laryngospasm or severe rigidity of the chest wall muscles. Aspiration pneumonia is another major hazard resulting from difficulties with swallowing and tube feeding. Prolonged muscle contractions and immobility can cause muscle contractures and skin breakdown. Furthermore, prolonged hypoxic episodes during severe spasms may lead to permanent neurological deficits and developmental delays in surviving infants.

Prevention Strategies and Public Health Nursing

Prevention remains the cornerstone of eliminating neonatal tetanus. Public health nurses play a vital role in administering tetanus toxoid (TT) vaccines to pregnant women during antenatal care visits, ensuring maternal and neonatal protection. Educating traditional birth attendants (TBAs) on hygienic delivery practices—specifically the 'clean chain' of clean hands, clean surface, clean cord cut, and clean cord care—is essential in rural Nigerian communities. Community outreach programs also encourage facility-based deliveries where sterile surgical instruments and skilled attendants are readily available.
