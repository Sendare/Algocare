Partograph

Introduction and Definition of the Partograph

The partograph is a pictorial, graphical overview of labour recorded on a single sheet of paper. Developed by WHO and modified for global use, it provides a continuous clinical record of the progress of labour and the condition of both mother and fetus. In busy Nigerian maternity units—ranging from primary health care centres to tertiary teaching hospitals—this tool serves as an early warning system. It transforms complex physiological observations into a visual trend, allowing midwives and student nurses to spot deviations from normal labour patterns before they escalate into life-threatening obstetric emergencies like obstructed labour or uterine rupture.

Components and Structure of the Partograph

A standard WHO partograph is divided into distinct sections that capture every vital metric of the birth process. Understanding these sections prevents charting errors during high-stress shifts. The layout typically includes:

  • Patient Information: Administrative details such as gravida, para, hospital number, and time of rupture of membranes.
  • Fetal Condition: Tracks fetal heart rate, amniotic fluid color, and degree of skull bone molding.
  • Progress of Labour: Features the cervical dilatation graph, descent of the fetal head, and uterine contractions frequency.
  • Medication and Fluids: Records oxytocin administration, drugs given, and intravenous fluids.
  • Maternal Condition: Monitors maternal pulse, blood pressure, temperature, and urine output (protein and volume).

Fetal Condition Assessment

Monitoring the unborn child during labour requires rigorous documentation of specific fetal indicators. The fetal heart rate is typically auscultated every 30 minutes using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler device, recording the baseline rate over one minute immediately after a contraction.

Amniotic fluid status is evaluated and coded upon rupture of membranes: C for clear liquor, M for meconium-stained liquor (indicating potential fetal hypoxia), B for blood-stained, and A for absent liquor.

Skull bone molding reflects how the fetal cranium adapts to the maternal pelvis, graded from 0 (separated bones) to +3 (overlapping bones locked together, a classic sign of obstructed labour).

Progress of Labour Evaluation

Evaluating the active phase of labour centers on cervical dilatation and the descent of the fetal head. Cervical dilatation is assessed via vaginal examination typically performed every four hours—or sooner if clinically indicated—and plotted with an 'X' on the graph.

The descent of the fetal head is measured in relation to the ischial spines (abdominal fifths method or station) and marked with an open circle 'O'. Active labour is expected to progress at a rate of at least 1 cm per hour once the cervix reaches 4 cm dilatation. Any deviation from this expected trajectory highlights delayed progress.

Maternal Condition and Medication Monitoring

Maternal wellbeing is just as critical as fetal surveillance during childbirth. Vital signs follow a strict timeline: blood pressure is recorded every four hours, while pulse rate is measured every 30 minutes to detect early signs of exhaustion, hemorrhage, or infection. Temperature is checked every two to four hours.

Urine analysis is documented for protein and acetone (ketones) to screen for pre-eclampsia and maternal starvation or dehydration, respectively. Furthermore, all interventions—such as oxytocin infusion rates (drops per minute), intravenous fluid types, and administered analgesics—must be meticulously charted in their respective grid rows.

Understanding Alert and Action Lines

The graphical heart of the partograph features two diagonal reference lines starting at 4 cm cervical dilatation: the alert line and the action line.

  • Alert Line: Drawn from 4 cm to 10 cm at a rate of 1 cm per hour. When the cervical dilatation curve crosses to the right of this line, it warns that labour is proceeding slower than normal, requiring transfer from a primary clinic to a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility.
  • Action Line: Parallel to and four hours to the right of the alert line. If the plotting crosses the action line, it mandates immediate medical intervention, intensive evaluation, and definitive management decisions such as augmentation or cesarean section.

Nursing Responsibilities and Decision Making

Midwives and student nurses hold the frontline responsibility for accurate partograph maintenance. Diligent plotting requires precision, punctuality, and clinical integrity—falsifying or retroactively guessing vital signs compromises patient safety. Beyond documentation, the nurse must synthesize the data: recognizing when a fetal heart rate drops below 110 beats per minute, noting maternal pyrexia, or identifying crossing of the alert line. Effective communication with the attending obstetrician ensures that timely referrals and life-saving interventions occur without delay.
