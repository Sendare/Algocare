Partograph

Introduction and Definition of the Partograph

The partograph is a pictorial, graphical overview of labour used to monitor both maternal and fetal well-being during childbirth. Developed originally by Friedman and later modified by the World Health Organization (WHO), it serves as an early warning system. By plotting cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, and uterine contractions against time on a single sheet of paper, midwives and nurses can instantly see whether labour is progressing normally or running into complications.

In many busy clinical settings across West Africa, this simple chart prevents prolonged and obstructed labour, which remains a leading cause of maternal death and morbidity. It changes labour management from a mental guesswork exercise into an objective, data-driven nursing intervention.

Components and Structure of the Partograph

The standard WHO partograph is divided into three distinct sections that must be completed systematically:

  1. Fetal Condition: Tracks the fetal heart rate, the state of the amniotic membranes and liquor (amniotic fluid), and the degree of fetal skull molding.
  2. Progress of Labour: Contains the cervical dilation graph, descent of the fetal head, and uterine contractions frequency and duration.
  3. Maternal Condition: Records maternal pulse, blood pressure, temperature, urine output (protein and acetone), and drugs or intravenous fluids administered.

All these entries are plotted against real elapsed time. Maintaining this structure ensures that no vital sign is forgotten during busy shift changes.

Fetal Condition Assessment on the Partograph

  • Fetal Heart Rate (FHR): Must be auscultated every 30 minutes using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler, and plotted as a clear dot on the grid.
  • Amniotic Fluid (Membranes and Liquor): Indicated by specific letters: I for membranes intact, C for membranes ruptured with clear fluid, M for meconium-stained fluid, B for blood-stained fluid, and A for absent fluid.
  • Molding: Reflects how the fetal cranial bones overlap as they navigate the pelvis. Recorded as + (bones touching), ++ (bones overlapping but easily separable), or +++ (bones severely overlapping and non-separable).

Meconium-stained liquor or severe molding (+++) are red flags signaling potential fetal distress that demand immediate medical review.

Progress of Labour Evaluation

Tracking labour progress relies heavily on serial vaginal examinations performed every four hours once active labour is established.

  • Cervical Dilation: Measured in centimeters from 0 to 10 and marked on the graph with an 'X'.
  • Descent of the Head: Measured using abdominal palpation in relation to the ischial spines (often using fifths palpable above the pelvic brim), marked with a circle ('O').
  • Contractions: Counted over a 10-minute window, noting their frequency and duration in seconds. Squares are usually shaded to represent contraction length.

Rapid changes or stagnant dilation patterns on this section immediately guide decisions regarding augmentation or referral.

Maternal Condition and Medication Monitoring

While watching the baby is vital, maternal well-being dictates whether labour can safely continue. Nurses must record:

  • Blood Pressure: Measured and recorded every 4 hours, or more frequently if hypertension is suspected.
  • Pulse Rate: Checked every 30 minutes because an escalating pulse can be the earliest sign of maternal dehydration, exhaustion, or hemorrhage.
  • Temperature: Monitored every 2 to 4 hours to catch early signs of intrauterine infection.
  • Urine Analysis: Checked for protein and acetone every time the mother voids.
  • Drugs and Fluids: Every oxytocin drop, analgesic, or intravenous fluid bag must be documented accurately at the exact time administered.

Understanding Alert and Action Lines

The graphical heart of the partograph is the grid featuring two critical diagonal lines starting at 4 cm of cervical dilation:

  • The Alert Line: Starts at 4 cm dilation and moves rightward at a rate of 1 cm per hour. When the plot of cervical dilation crosses to the right of this line, it warns that labour is slower than normal. The patient should be transferred to a comprehensive emergency obstetric care facility if she is in a primary health clinic.
  • The Action Line: Parallel to the alert line and placed 4 hours to the right of it. When cervical dilation crosses the action line, immediate medical intervention and critical re-evaluation (such as considering a caesarean section or correcting hypotonic inertia) are mandatory.

Ignoring these lines leads to catastrophic obstructed labour and uterine rupture.

Nursing Responsibilities and Decision Making

Effective use of the partograph requires clinical competence, attentiveness, and prompt action. Nurses must never simply file the chart away as paperwork; it is a dynamic clinical tool. Responsibilities include accurate plotting, recognizing abnormal trends before catastrophe strikes, communicating findings clearly with medical officers, and offering continuous emotional and physical support to the laboring woman.

When a deviation occurs, the nurse initiates timely referrals or prepares the patient for emergency interventions. Professional accountability means staying vigilant, ensuring every entry is timestamped, and advocating fiercely for the mother and baby when the chart signals trouble.
