Ophthalmia Neonatorum

Introduction and Definition of Ophthalmia Neonatorum

Ophthalmia neonatorum is defined as a form of acute conjunctivitis occurring in an infant during the first month of life. Characterized by marked conjunctival hyperemia, chemosis, and a varying degree of purulent ocular discharge, this condition demands immediate clinical attention. In resource-limited settings across Nigeria, infectious conjunctivitis in neonates remains a significant public health concern due to potential progression to corneal ulceration and permanent visual impairment if intervention is delayed.

Etiology and Common Causative Organisms

The causative agents of ophthalmia neonatorum are broadly divided into infectious and chemical categories. Infectious causes can be bacterial, viral, or chlamydial.

  • Neisseria gonorrhoeae: A Gram-negative diplococcus responsible for hyperacute, severe purulent conjunctivitis with rapid corneal destruction.
  • Chlamydia trachomatis: The most common infectious cause, typically presenting with a subacute onset and mucopurulent discharge.
  • Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pneumoniae: Bacterial pathogens that cause varying degrees of purulent inflammation.
  • Herpes simplex virus (HSV): Can cause vesicular eyelid lesions alongside conjunctivitis, posing systemic dissemination risks.
  • Silver nitrate: A chemical agent historically used for prophylaxis that can cause transient, non-infectious chemical conjunctivitis.

Pathophysiology and Modes of Transmission

Transmission of infectious ophthalmia neonatorum occurs almost exclusively vertically during the intrapartum period. As the fetus passes through an infected birth canal, maternal cervical and vaginal secretions containing pathogens directly inoculate the newborn's conjunctival sac. Postnatal transmission via contaminated hands, towels, or unwashed birth attendants is also possible, though less common.

Once introduced, the organism adheres to the conjunctival epithelium, triggering a robust inflammatory response characterized by neutrophil recruitment, vascular congestion, and copious exudate production. Neisseria gonorrhoeae possesses unique virulence factors, including pili and lipopolysaccharide endotoxins, allowing it to penetrate the uncompromised corneal epithelium directly, which accounts for its devastating destructive potential.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Thorough assessment requires gentle handling of the neonate, as excessive pressure on edematous eyelids can risk globe rupture if the cornea is thinned. The nurse must inspect the periorbital area, eyelids, and conjunctiva, noting the character, color, and volume of any discharge.

Diagnostic confirmation is critical for targeted therapy. The nurse assists with or performs conjunctival swab collection for:

  • Gram staining: Rapidly identifies Gram-negative diplococci indicative of gonococcal infection.
  • Culture and sensitivity: Identifies specific bacterial pathogens and guides antibiotic selection.
  • Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) or Giemsa staining: Preferred for detecting Chlamydia trachomatis inclusions.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management requires a coordinated approach combining systemic and topical antimicrobial therapies based on laboratory identifications.

For confirmed gonococcal infection, systemic administration of ceftriaxone is standard to prevent systemic complications such as arthritis or meningitis, accompanied by frequent saline eye irrigations to clear purulent debris. Chlamydial conjunctivitis is managed using systemic erythromycin or similar oral macrolides, as topical drops alone are frequently insufficient to eradicate nasopharyngeal carriage.

Nursing care centers on strict infection control. Hand hygiene before and after eye care is paramount. Nurses must wear gloves, dispose of contaminated cotton swabs properly, and isolate washbasins to prevent nosocomial spread within the postnatal ward. Eye cleaning should proceed from the inner canthus outward using separate sterile swabs for each wipe.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

Delay in diagnosis or inappropriate treatment can lead to severe structural ocular damage. Corneal ulceration is a primary hazard, which can progress rapidly to corneal perforation, iris prolapse, and endophthalmitis. Permanent corneal scarring may result in residual visual impairment or amblyopia. In cases of chlamydial infection, concurrent inclusion conjunctivitis can be associated with neonatal chlamydial pneumonia. The overall prognosis is excellent when prophylactic measures are universally applied and established infections receive prompt, targeted antimicrobial therapy.

Prevention and Prophylactic Measures in Newborn Care

Prevention remains the cornerstone of reducing the incidence of ophthalmia neonatorum. Credé's method, involving the instillation of prophylactic eye agents shortly after birth, is a standard component of essential newborn care protocols globally and across Nigerian health facilities. Commonly utilized prophylactic agents include 1% tetracycline ointment or 0.5% erythromycin ophthalmic ointment. Povidone-iodine 2.5% aqueous solution is also utilized in various settings due to its broad antimicrobial efficacy.

Antenatal screening and treatment of maternal sexually transmitted infections during pregnancy significantly reduce the risk of vertical transmission. Nurse midwives play an essential role in health education, emphasizing the importance of booking early for antenatal care and ensuring every newborn receives immediate eye prophylaxis as part of standard delivery room protocols.
