Ophthalmia Neonatorum

Introduction and Definition

Ophthalmia neonatorum is defined as any purulent conjunctivitis occurring in a newborn within the first twenty-eight days of life. This condition develops when infectious agents contaminate the infant's eyes during passage through the birth canal. Early identification is crucial because untreated eye infections in neonates can progress rapidly, leading to severe corneal damage and permanent visual impairment. While some mild irritation can happen due to chemical prophylaxis given right after birth, true ophthalmia neonatorum involves significant inflammation and discharge that requires prompt medical attention.

Causative Organisms and Etiology

The microorganisms responsible for ophthalmia neonatorum are typically acquired from the maternal reproductive tract during childbirth. The two most prominent and dangerous bacterial agents are Neisseria gonorrhoeae and Chlamydia trachomatis.

  • Neisseria gonorrhoeae: Causes hyperacute gonococcal conjunctivitis, which presents with abundant pus and rapid tissue destruction.
  • Chlamydia trachomatis: The leading cause of inclusion conjunctivitis of the newborn, typically showing a slightly slower onset.
  • Other Pathogens: Can include bacteria like Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and herpes simplex virus.

Clinical Manifestations and Onset

Symptoms of ophthalmia neonatorum vary depending on the specific causative organism, particularly regarding the time of onset after birth.

OrganismTypical Time of OnsetMain Clinical Features
Chemical Prophylaxis6 to 12 hoursMild redness and tearing, resolves quickly
Neisseria gonorrhoeae2 to 5 daysSevere eyelid swelling, thick purulent discharge
Chlamydia trachomatis5 to 14 daysMild to moderate swelling, watery or mucoid discharge

Gonococcal infections demand urgent care because the heavy pus accumulation can compromise the cornea within hours.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis

Thorough assessment involves inspecting the newborn's eyes for redness, edema, chemosis, and the exact character of any discharge. Nurses must handle the infant's eyelids with extreme care to avoid accidental corneal trauma during inspection. Diagnostic confirmation relies on collecting conjunctival scrapings or discharge swabs for Gram staining, culture, and polymerase chain reaction testing to pinpoint the exact pathogen.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management requires targeted antimicrobial therapy based on laboratory findings, along with frequent eye irrigation using sterile normal saline to remove accumulated secretions. For gonococcal infections, systemic antibiotic therapy like ceftriaxone is essential. Chlamydial infections require systemic oral erythromycin, as topical drops alone are usually insufficient to clear the nasopharyngeal carriage. Nurses must wear gloves, perform strict hand hygiene, and dispose of contaminated cotton swabs properly to prevent spreading the infection to other infants or healthcare workers.

Prevention and Prophylactic Measures

Preventing ophthalmia neonatorum begins with treating maternal infections during prenatal care. Additionally, routine prophylactic eye treatment is administered to all newborns shortly after delivery. Erythromycin 0.5% ophthalmic ointment is the standard agent used in many regions to prevent gonococcal conjunctivitis. The nurse must instill the ointment carefully into the lower conjunctival sac of each eye without touching the tube tip to the eye structures, ensuring the entire surface is covered.
