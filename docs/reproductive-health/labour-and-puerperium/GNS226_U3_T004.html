Physiology of Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Labour is the physiological process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. For labour to be classified as normal or eutocia, several specific conditions must be met. It must begin spontaneously without artificial induction, occur at term between 37 and 42 completed weeks of pregnancy, present with a single fetus in the cephalic or head-first position, and proceed without artificial assistance or major complications. The entire process culminates in the birth of a healthy newborn and the safe delivery of the placenta, while maintaining the physical and emotional well-being of the mother.

The Four Ps of Labour: Power, Passage, Passenger, and Psyche

The progress of childbirth depends on four interacting factors known as the Ps of labour. When these factors work in harmony, delivery proceeds smoothly.

  • Power: Refers to uterine contractions and maternal pushing efforts that force the fetus downward.
  • Passage: Refers to the maternal pelvis and soft tissues of the birth canal through which the baby must travel.
  • Passenger: Refers to the fetus, along with the placenta and amniotic membranes, and how the baby's size and position affect transit.
  • Psyche: Refers to the emotional state, past experiences, and cultural background of the mother, which directly influence her pain perception and coping abilities.

Hormonal Triggers in the Initiation of Labour

The exact trigger that starts human labour remains a complex interaction of maternal and fetal signals, rather than a single sudden event. As pregnancy reaches term, a delicate hormonal balance shifts. Progesterone, which relaxes uterine muscles and maintains pregnancy, begins to lose its dominant effect. Simultaneously, estrogen levels rise, increasing the sensitivity of the uterine muscle to stimuli.

The fetus also plays a direct role by releasing cortisol from its adrenal glands, which stimulates the placenta to produce more prostaglandins. These prostaglandins work alongside oxytocin, a hormone released by the posterior pituitary gland, to stimulate powerful, coordinated uterine contractions and soften the cervix.

The Four Stages of Normal Labour

Labour is divided into four distinct clinical stages, each characterized by specific physiological milestones.

  • First Stage: Begins with the onset of true labour contractions and ends with full cervical dilation at 10 centimeters. It is subdivided into latent and active phases.
  • Second Stage: Begins at full cervical dilation and ends with the actual birth of the infant.
  • Third Stage: Begins immediately after the baby is born and concludes with the delivery of the placenta and fetal membranes.
  • Fourth Stage: Lasts for about one to two hours following placental delivery, during which time maternal stabilization and initial physiological recovery occur.

Mechanism of Normal Labour: Cardinal Movements

As the passenger travels through the passage, the fetal head undergoes a series of precise positional changes called the cardinal movements of labour. These adjustments allow the oddly shaped fetal head to navigate the varying diameters of the maternal pelvis.

Maternal Physiological Adaptations During Labour

Labour places tremendous physical demands on the mother's body, triggering widespread systemic changes. The cardiovascular system experiences a significant increase in cardiac output with every contraction, as blood is squeezed out of the uterus and back into the maternal circulation. Blood pressure rises during contractions due to pain and muscular effort.

Respiratory rate increases to meet the higher oxygen demands of active muscle work. The gastrointestinal system slows down dramatically, meaning digestion is delayed and gastric emptying is reduced. Consequently, nausea and vomiting are common. Renal changes occur as the bladder is compressed by the descending fetus, predisposing the woman to urinary retention if she does not void regularly.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring of Normal Labour

Continuous, systematic monitoring ensures the safety of both mother and baby throughout the labour process. Nurses perform regular abdominal palpations using Leopold's maneuvers to determine fetal position and presentation. Contractions are monitored for frequency, duration, and intensity by placing a hand on the fundus or using electronic monitoring equipment.

Vaginal examinations are performed selectively to assess cervical dilation, effacement, and fetal station, while minimizing the risk of infection. Fetal heart rate is tracked meticulously to detect early signs of distress. Beyond physical parameters, the nurse monitors maternal vital signs, hydration status, emotional coping, and pain levels, providing continuous encouragement and comfort measures.
