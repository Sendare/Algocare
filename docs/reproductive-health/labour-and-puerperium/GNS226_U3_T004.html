Physiology of Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Labour is the physiological process by which the products of conception are expelled from the uterus to the outside world. For labour to be classified as normal or eutocia, specific criteria must be met. It must occur at term, typically between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation, with a single fetus presenting in the cephalic position. The onset must be spontaneous without artificial induction or augmentation, and the process must conclude safely for both mother and newborn without requiring extensive surgical intervention. When managing patients in Nigerian tertiary and secondary health facilities, confirming gestational age accurately through early ultrasound or reliable menstrual history remains a vital step before labeling any labour as low-risk.

The Four Ps of Labour

The progress and successful outcome of childbirth depend on the harmonious interaction of four core factors, universally known as the Ps:

  • Power: The physiological force generated by uterine contractions and maternal pushing efforts, which effaces and dilates the cervix and expels the fetus.
  • Passage: The maternal bony pelvis and the soft tissues of the birth canal, which must possess adequate dimensions and compliance to allow fetal descent.
  • Passenger: The fetus, placenta, and membranes, influenced by fetal size, presentation, position, and skull moulding.
  • Psyche: The emotional and psychological state of the mother, where extreme anxiety or fear can release catecholamines and inhibit effective uterine contractility.

Hormonal Triggers in the Initiation of Labour

The exact cascade that sparks human labour involves a delicate biochemical shift between progesterone and estrogen. Throughout pregnancy, progesterone acts as a uterine relaxant, maintaining quiescence. As term approaches, a functional progesterone withdrawal occurs, while estrogen levels rise, increasing myometrial sensitivity. The fetal hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis also plays a major role by releasing cortisol, which stimulates placental estrogen synthesis. Concurrently, local intrauterine production of prostaglandins and oxytocin triggers coordinated myometrial contractions and cervical ripening.

The Four Stages of Labour

Labour is clinically divided into distinct phases to help monitor progression accurately:

  1. First Stage: Begins with the onset of true uterine contractions and ends with complete cervical dilatation at ten centimeters. It is subdivided into latent and active phases.
  2. Second Stage: Commences with complete cervical dilatation and concludes with the complete birth of the baby.
  3. Third Stage: Spans from the delivery of the infant until the complete expulsion of the placenta and fetal membranes.
  4. Fourth Stage: The immediate postpartum recovery period, usually lasting up to two hours after placental delivery, where hemodynamic stabilization is closely monitored.

Mechanism of Normal Labour

As the fetus navigates the maternal pelvis, it undergoes a series of precise positional movements known as the cardinal movements of labour. These movements allow the irregular shape of the fetal head to adapt to the varying diameters of the birth canal.

  • Engagement: The widest diameter of the fetal presenting part passes below the pelvic inlet.
  • Descent: Downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal.
  • Flexion: The fetal chin tucks tightly against the chest, presenting a smaller suboccipitobregmatic diameter.
  • Internal Rotation: The head rotates, typically bringing the occiput to an anterior position beneath the pubic symphysis.
  • Extension: The head passes under the pubic arch through extension, allowing the face and chin to be born.
  • Restitution and External Rotation: The head aligns back with the shoulders, and the shoulders rotate internally.
  • Expulsion: The anterior and posterior shoulders are delivered, followed quickly by the rest of the body.

Maternal Physiological Adaptations During Labour

Childbirth places considerable physical demands on maternal organ systems. Cardiovascular output surges with every contraction as blood is squeezed out of the uterine muscle back into the systemic circulation, raising maternal blood pressure and pulse. The respiratory rate increases to meet higher oxygen demands, sometimes leading to hyperventilation if pain management is inadequate. Gastrointestinal motility slows down significantly, which increases aspiration risks if oral foods or heavy liquids are consumed during active labour. Renal function adjusts as pressure from the presenting part can affect bladder emptying, making periodic assessment of urinary retention a clinical priority.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Systematic nursing observation ensures that deviations from normal labour are identified early. Monitoring involves regular evaluation of maternal vital signs, assessment of contraction frequency, intensity, and duration, and tracking fetal well-being using intermittent auscultation or electronic fetal monitoring. The partograph serves as a vital graphical tool in Nigerian labor wards to record cervical dilatation, descent, and fetal heart rate against time, helping clinicians spot prolonged labour patterns before complications arise. Emotional reassurance and guidance on pain coping techniques remain foundational pillars of intrapartum nursing care.
