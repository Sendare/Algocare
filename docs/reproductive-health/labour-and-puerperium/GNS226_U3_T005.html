Mechanism of Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Normal labour, also known as parturition, is defined as the physiological process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. For labour to be classified as 'normal' or 'eutocia', it must meet specific criteria established in obstetric practice. These criteria include a spontaneous onset of uterine contractions, a vertex presentation of the fetus, term gestation (typically between 37 and 42 completed weeks), and a duration that is neither excessively prolonged nor precipitate, without complications for either the mother or the newborn. Understanding this definition is fundamental for nursing students studying maternal and child health at Algocare, as it sets the baseline for identifying abnormal variations, known as dystocia. Nurses play a critical role during this phase by providing continuous psychological support, monitoring maternal vital signs, assessing uterine contraction patterns, and reassuring the mother as she transitions through the various stages of childbirth. Recognizing normal progress helps in preventing unnecessary medical interventions and promotes positive maternal-fetal outcomes within the healthcare setting.

Prerequisites and Cardinal Features of Labour

The progress of normal labour depends on four critical prerequisites, often referred to in midwifery and nursing education as the 'four Ps': Power (uterine contractions), Passage (the maternal pelvis and soft tissues), Passenger (the fetus and placenta), and Psyche (the psychological state of the mother). Power refers to the rhythmic, involuntary contractions of the myometrium that cause effacement and dilation of the cervix. Passage involves the maternal bony pelvis and the lower genital tract resistance. Passenger encompasses fetal size, lie, presentation, and position. Psyche relates to the emotional readiness, anxiety levels, and coping mechanisms of the parturient woman. Alongside these prerequisites, the cardinal features of labour describe the precise mechanical movements the fetus makes to navigate the maternal pelvis. These movements ensure that the smallest diameter of the fetal head aligns with the corresponding diameters of the birth canal. As nursing students preparing for professional examinations, mastering both the prerequisites and the cardinal movements allows you to accurately chart partographs, anticipate obstetric complications, and deliver safe, evidence-based intrapartum care in various healthcare environments.

Engagement and Descent

Engagement is the first major cardinal movement in the mechanism of normal labour. It occurs when the biparietal diameter—the widest transverse diameter of the fetal head—passes down through the pelvic inlet to reach or pass below the level of the ischial spines. In nulliparous women, engagement often occurs a few weeks before the onset of labour, while in multiparous women, it may not happen until labour is well underway. Following engagement, descent is the continuous downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal. Descent is brought about by multiple forces, including intrauterine pressure from contractions, the amniotic pressure fluid wedge, abdominal muscle contractions during the second stage, and the straightening of the fetal body. For successful birth, descent must be continuous, though its speed may vary. Midwives and nurses assess descent during vaginal examinations by measuring the station of the presenting part in relation to the ischial spines. Documenting engagement and monitoring steady descent helps clinicians identify potential cephalopelvic disproportion early, ensuring timely interventions and safeguarding both maternal and fetal well-being.

Flexion of the Fetal Head

As the descending fetal head meets resistance from the cervix, pelvic walls, or pelvic floor, flexion occurs as the next cardinal movement. Flexion is an involuntary mechanism where the fetal chin is brought into direct contact with the fetal thorax. This mechanical movement is crucial because it substitutes a smaller diameter for a larger one. Instead of presenting the longer occipitofrontal diameter (measuring approximately 11.5 cm), the fully flexed head presents the much smaller suboccipito-bregmatic diameter (measuring approximately 9.5 cm) to the birth canal. This efficient adaptation significantly reduces the surface area required for the head to pass through the pelvis. For nursing students, understanding flexion highlights why an adequately flexed head progresses smoothly through labour, whereas deflexed attitudes can lead to prolonged labour and mechanical dystocia. During internal examinations, a nurse or midwife will feel the occiput becoming the leading and most dependent part, with fontanelles providing important landmarks for assessing fetal head position and the degree of flexion achieved.

Internal Rotation

Internal rotation is the cardinal movement whereby the fetal head turns within the pelvis, usually changing its orientation from an oblique or transverse position to an anteroposterior position. In the vast majority of vertex presentations, the occiput rotates anteriorly towards the maternal symphysis pubis, though occasionally it may rotate posteriorly towards the hollow of the sacrum. This rotation is caused by the shape of the pelvic floor muscles, specifically the levator ani, which act like a gutter or a sloping slide directing the leading part of the fetus forward. As the pelvic floor slopes downward and forward, the lowest part of the head—which is the occiput—hits this resistance first and naturally rotates anteriorly to find the path of least resistance where the pelvic outlet is widest in its anteroposterior diameter. For nursing and midwifery personnel, monitoring internal rotation is essential. A failure to rotate correctly can result in persistent occipitoposterior positions, leading to prolonged second stage labour and an increased requirement for operative vaginal delivery or cesarean section.

Extension of the Fetal Head

Once internal rotation is complete and the fetal head reaches the perineum, the next movement is extension. Extension allows the head to escape from the vulval outlet. As the occiput pivots around the inferior margin of the symphysis pubis, the base of the occiput acts as a hypomochlion (a fulcrum). The forces of uterine contractions and maternal abdominal pushing drive the head downward, but because the vaginal outlet opens forward and upward, the head is forced to sweep forward and upward over the perineum. Consequently, the vertex, brow, face, and chin successively bulge and emerge over the perineal margin. The nurse attending the birth must support the perineum during this delicate phase to prevent severe perineal lacerations and control the speed of crowning and head delivery. Proper communication with the mother, instructing her when to push and when to pant, helps facilitate a controlled extension and protects the maternal soft tissues from undue trauma.

Restitution and External Rotation

Immediately after the head is delivered, it undergoes restitution. Restitution is the passive untwisting of the fetal neck that occurs because the head rotates back to align naturally with the shoulders, which remain in an oblique position inside the pelvis. For example, if the head rotated to the left anterior position during internal rotation, it will now twist back toward the left to line up with the shoulders. Following restitution, external rotation occurs. This movement is driven by the internal rotation of the shoulders as they enter the pelvis and encounter the same sloping pelvic floor muscles. The shoulders rotate into the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvis, and this rotation is externally visible because the fetal head turns further to face the mother's inner thigh (either right or left depending on the original position). Observing restitution and external rotation is crucial for nursing and midwifery students because it confirms that the shoulders are properly aligned for safe delivery and reassures the birth attendant that no nuchal cord or shoulder dystocia complications are immediately obstructing progress.

Expulsion of the Fetus

Expulsion is the final stage of the mechanism of normal labour, during which the rest of the fetal body is delivered. Once external rotation is complete, the anterior shoulder appears under the symphysis pubis and is delivered first by a lateral flexion of the body upward toward the mother's abdomen. Following the release of the anterior shoulder, the posterior shoulder is swept forward and delivered over the perineum. Once the shoulders are free, the rest of the body—the trunk, the breech, and the lower limbs—follows rapidly and with relative ease because they are narrower than the head and shoulders. Nurses and midwives must be prepared to receive the baby, maintain a neutral thermal environment, assess the infant's cry and tone, and place the newborn skin-to-skin on the mother's chest if the baby is vigorous. Documenting the exact time of complete fetal expulsion marks the official end of the second stage of labour and initiates the third stage, which involves the delivery of the placenta and membranes. Vigilant observation during expulsion helps prevent immediate postpartum complications such as excessive bleeding or shoulder dystocia emergencies.
