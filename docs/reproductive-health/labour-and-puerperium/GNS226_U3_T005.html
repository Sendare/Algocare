Mechanism of Normal Labour

Introduction and Prerequisites of Normal Labour

Labour is the physiological process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. For labour to be considered normal or spontaneous, specific clinical criteria must be met at the onset. The pregnancy must reach term, typically between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation. The fetus must present by the vertex, meaning the head enters the pelvis first. Labour must begin spontaneously without medical or surgical induction, and it should proceed without undue complications for either mother or baby.

Several core factors drive this process, commonly known as the powers of labour. Uterine contractions provide the primary force, creating intermittent pressure that dilates the cervix and pushes the fetus downward. Secondary forces come from maternal pushing efforts during the second stage, aided by abdominal muscle contractions. The passenger refers to the fetus, particularly its size, presentation, and position. Finally, the passage represents the maternal bony pelvis and soft tissues that the baby must navigate.

Engagement and Descent

As labour progresses, the fetus must pass through the birth canal by executing a series of precise mechanical movements called the cardinal movements of labour. While these movements are described step-by-step for clarity, many happen simultaneously.

  • Engagement: This occurs when the widest part of the presenting fetal part—usually the biparietal diameter of the head—passes completely through the pelvic inlet down to the level of the ischial spines, reaching station zero.
  • Descent: This is the continuous downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal. Descent is essential for all other cardinal movements to take place and is driven primarily by uterine contractions and maternal bearing-down efforts.

Flexion of the Fetal Head

Once the descending fetal head meets resistance from the walls of the pelvis, the cervix, or the pelvic floor, flexion occurs. The baby's chin is forced downward to touch the chest.

This movement changes the presenting diameter of the fetal head from the wider occipitofrontal diameter to the much smaller suboccipito-bregmatic diameter. Presenting a smaller diameter allows the head to pass through the pelvis with the least amount of resistance. If flexion fails, the baby's head enters the pelvis at a wider angle, which can significantly prolong labour or cause an arrest of descent.

Internal Rotation

As the flexed head continues to descend past the ischial spines, it reaches the sloping, hammock-like muscles of the pelvic floor, specifically the levator ani. These muscles guide the fetal head to turn so that the occiput moves anteriorly toward the mother's pubic symphysis.

This crucial turn is known as internal rotation. It aligns the longest diameter of the fetal head (the anteroposterior diameter) with the longest diameter of the pelvic outlet. Without this rotation, the baby's head would become stuck transversely against the ischial spines, preventing further progress down the birth canal.

Extension of the Fetal Head

Once internal rotation is complete and the head reaches the perineum, extension takes place. The base of the occiput comes to rest directly beneath the pubic symphysis, acting as a physical pivot point or fulcrum.

Driven by uterine contractions from above and the upward-sloping resistance of the perineal floor from below, the fetal head pivots around the pubic bone. The chin, face, and brow emerge sequentially over the perineum, sweeping upward across the vaginal opening. Midwives support the perineum carefully during this phase to control the speed of delivery and prevent severe perineal tearing.

Restitution and External Rotation

Immediately after the head is born, it is free from the confines of the pelvis. However, the baby's shoulders remain oriented inside the pelvis at an oblique angle. Because of this, the head naturally twists back to align itself correctly with the shoulders in a movement called restitution.

Following restitution, external rotation occurs. The shoulders rotate internally inside the pelvis to fit the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic outlet. This internal rotation of the shoulders forces the baby's head to turn externally another 45 degrees, pointing toward the mother's left or right thigh, depending on its original position.

Expulsion of the Fetus

The final stage of the mechanism of labour is the expulsion of the rest of the body. Once external rotation is complete, the anterior shoulder appears under the pubic symphysis.

The attendant clinician gently guides the baby's body upward to allow the posterior shoulder to sweep over the perineum. Following the delivery of the shoulders, the rest of the trunk and the lower limbs follow easily and rapidly because they are much narrower than the head and shoulders. The birth of the baby is now complete, marking the successful end of the second stage of labour.
