Mechanism of Normal Labour

Introduction and Prerequisites of Normal Labour

Labour is the physiological process by which the products of conception are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. For labour to be classified as normal or eutocia, specific baseline conditions must be met. The pregnancy must be a singleton, achieving a term gestation between 37 and 42 completed weeks. The fetus must present by the vertex, and the entire process must culminate spontaneously without artificial intervention or undue complications.

Four fundamental factors dictate the progress and success of labour, commonly referred to in clinical obstetrics as the four Ps:

  • Passage: The maternal bony pelvis and soft tissues through which the fetus must navigate.
  • Passenger: The fetus, including its size, lie, presentation, and attitude.
  • Power: The force generated by uterine contractions and maternal abdominal wall straining.
  • Psyche: The psychological state, emotional readiness, and pain coping mechanisms of the mother.

Cardinal Movements of the Fetus

As the fetus travels down the birth canal, it undergoes a series of precise, overlapping positional adjustments to adapt its smallest diameter to the varying dimensions of the maternal pelvis. These maneuvers are known as the cardinal movements of labour. While described as distinct sequential steps for study purposes, many of these movements occur simultaneously in clinical reality.

The standard sequence includes engagement, descent, flexion, internal rotation, extension, restitution, external rotation, and finally, expulsion. Each movement is a direct mechanical response of the fetus to the shape of the pelvic cavity and the downward pressure exerted by uterine contractions.

Engagement and Descent

Engagement occurs when the widest transverse diameter of the fetal presenting part—usually the biparietal diameter in a vertex presentation—passes successfully through the maternal pelvic inlet to reach or pass below the level of the ischial spines. In many primigravid women in Nigeria, engagement happens around 36 to 38 weeks of pregnancy, weeks before true labour pains begin. In multiparous women, however, engagement frequently delays until active labour has already started.

Descent is the continuous downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal. It is driven by four primary forces: pressure from amniotic fluid, direct pressure of the uterine fundus on the breech during contractions, involuntary contraction of the maternal abdominal muscles, and the straightening of the fetal body. Descent must persist throughout all other cardinal movements for delivery to advance.

Flexion and Internal Rotation

As the descending fetal head meets resistance from the cervix, pelvic walls, or pelvic floor muscles, it undergoes flexion. This passive movement forces the fetal chin to press firmly against its chest. Flexion changes the presenting diameter from the larger occipitofrontal to the much smaller suboccipito-bregmatic diameter, measuring roughly 9.5 centimeters, which eases passage through the pelvis.

Following adequate flexion and further descent, the fetal head encounters the sloping, gutter-shaped levator ani muscles of the pelvic floor. This muscular sling forces the head to undergo internal rotation. The occiput rotates anteriorly by 45 degrees toward the pubic symphysis, moving from an oblique or transverse position into the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic outlet. This crucial turn aligns the long axis of the head with the long axis of the pelvic outlet.

Extension of the Fetal Head

Once internal rotation is complete and the head reaches the perineal floor, delivery occurs via extension. The base of the occiput clings closely to the inferior margin of the pubic symphysis, acting as a pivoting fulcrum.

As uterine contractions push the fetus downward and forward, the head is forced to sweep upward and out of the vagina. The perineum stretches tightly over the face, allowing the bridge of the nose, eyes, mouth, and finally the chin to emerge successively over the perineal margin. Midwives support the perineum carefully during this phase to prevent severe perineal tears.

Restitution, External Rotation, and Expulsion

Immediately after the head is born, it is freed from the constraints of the pelvis and undergoes restitution. The head untwists, passively rotating back 45 degrees to align itself naturally with the shoulders, which remain in the oblique pelvic diameter.

Following restitution, external rotation occurs. The shoulders rotate internally within the pelvis to fit into the anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic outlet. This causes the fetal head to rotate another 45 degrees externally, bringing the occiput back toward the side it originally faced.

With the shoulders now aligned anteroposteriorly, expulsion takes place rapidly. The anterior shoulder slips out beneath the pubic symphysis, followed by the upward lateral flexion of the body to deliver the posterior shoulder. The rest of the trunk and lower limbs slide out effortlessly, completing the birth.
