Care of the Newborn

Introduction and Immediate Assessment of the Newborn

The transition of a neonate from intrauterine to extrauterine life is one of the most critical phases in human development. Immediate assessment upon birth focuses on establishing effective respiration, supporting a patent airway, and evaluating the overall well-being of the newborn. As soon as the infant is delivered, the nurse or midwife must perform a rapid initial evaluation while simultaneously drying and stimulating the baby. This quick appraisal addresses three vital questions: Is the gestation term? Is the amniotic fluid clear of meconium? Is the infant breathing or crying, and do they have good muscle tone? If the answer to these is yes, the newborn remains with the mother for skin-to-skin contact, which promotes bonding, stabilizes temperature, and initiates early breastfeeding. If the answer is no, the infant requires immediate resuscitation steps, which follow the Neonatal Resuscitation Program (NRP) guidelines: opening the airway, providing positive pressure ventilation, and monitoring heart rate. In Nigerian clinical settings, maintaining a clean delivery environment and ensuring the immediate presence of a trained birth attendant are paramount to preventing birth asphyxia, a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality. Nurses must act swiftly, documenting time of birth, initial responses, and immediate interventions accurately. Continuous observation for pallor, cyanosis, and respiratory distress ensures that any early signs of compromise are identified and managed without delay, laying a solid foundation for the infant's subsequent health journey.

Apgar Scoring and Physiological Adaptation at Birth

Physiological adaptation at birth requires major functional changes across multiple organ systems, most notably the respiratory and cardiovascular systems. In utero, the lungs are fluid-filled and gas exchange occurs via the placenta. With the first breath, pulmonary vascular resistance drops dramatically, pulmonary blood flow increases, and the fetal shunts—the ductus arteriosus, ductus venosus, and foramen ovale—begin to functional close. To evaluate how well the neonate is adapting to extrauterine life, the Apgar score is assessed at 1 minute and 5 minutes after birth, and sometimes at 10 minutes if the score remains low. The Apgar acronym stands for Appearance (color), Pulse (heart rate), Grimace (reflex irritability), Activity (muscle tone), and Respiration (respiratory effort). Each parameter is assigned a score of 0, 1, or 2, yielding a total possible score of 10. A score of 7 to 10 indicates good condition requiring only routine care; a score of 4 to 6 signifies moderate depression requiring stimulation or oxygen; and a score of 0 to 3 indicates severe distress demanding immediate resuscitation. In nursing practice, the Apgar score serves as a rapid tool to gauge the efficacy of resuscitation efforts, though it is never used to decide whether to initiate resuscitation, which must begin before the 1-minute score is even calculated. Understanding these physiological shifts helps nurses anticipate potential complications like persistent pulmonary hypertension or transitional respiratory distress.

Routine Newborn Care and Prophylactic Measures

Routine newborn care encompasses standardized prophylactic interventions designed to protect the neonate from infection, hemorrhagic disease, and ophthalmic complications immediately following birth. Once the infant is stable and transitional evaluations are complete, eye prophylaxis is administered. This usually involves instilling tetracycline eye ointment (1%) or erythromycin into the conjunctival sac within the first hour of life to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis acquired during passage through the birth canal. Additionally, a prophylactic intramuscular injection of Vitamin K (phytonadione) is given to prevent vitamin K deficiency bleeding (VKDB), a potentially fatal hemorrhagic disorder to which newborns are vulnerable due to sterile guts that cannot yet synthesize vitamin K. In Nigeria, where infectious morbidity is a significant public health concern, strict adherence to these prophylactic measures is mandatory in all healthcare facilities. Cord care is another crucial routine component; the umbilical cord stump must be kept clean and dry, avoiding traditional harmful substances such as cow dung, herbs, or saliva, which frequently introduce tetanus spores and lead to neonatal tetanus. Nurses must educate mothers on hygienic cord care using chlorhexidine gel or methylated spirit as per national guidelines. Anthropometric measurements—including weight, length, and head circumference—are also recorded during this routine care phase to establish baseline data for ongoing pediatric growth monitoring.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Heat Loss

Thermoregulation is a critical physiological priority for the newborn, as infants possess a high ratio of body surface area to mass, thin subcutaneous fat layers, and a limited ability to shiver. Newborns generate heat primarily through non-shivering thermogenesis, which involves the metabolism of brown adipose tissue (brown fat). However, this metabolic process consumes significant oxygen and glucose, putting cold-stressed infants at high risk for hypoglycemia, hypoxia, and metabolic acidosis. Heat loss occurs through four primary mechanisms: conduction (direct contact with cold surfaces), convection (drafts of cool air), evaporation (moisture on the skin converting to vapor), and radiation (transfer of heat to cooler solid objects not in direct contact). To prevent hypothermia, nurses must dry the newborn immediately at birth with warm towels, place them in skin-to-skin contact with the mother under a warm blanket, or place them under a radiant warmer if resuscitation is required. Caps should be placed on the infant's head to reduce radiant and convective heat loss, as the head represents a large surface area. In resource-constrained settings in Nigeria, where power outages can disrupt incubator functions, kangaroo mother care (KMC) serves as an effective, low-cost intervention for maintaining stable body temperature in both preterm and low-birth-weight infants. Monitoring axillary temperature regularly is an essential nursing responsibility to detect early deviations from the normal range of 36.5°C to 37.5°C.

Newborn Feeding: Breastfeeding Initiation and Support

Early initiation of breastfeeding is one of the most impactful interventions for neonatal survival, nutrition, and maternal-infant bonding. World Health Organization guidelines recommend putting the newborn to the breast within the first hour of life. Colostrum, the thick, yellowish first milk, is exceptionally rich in immunoglobulins (especially secretory IgA), proteins, and vitamins, acting as the infant's first immunization against gastrointestinal and respiratory infections. Successful breastfeeding relies heavily on proper positioning and latch-on. The infant's mouth should encompass not just the nipple, but a large portion of the areola, with the lower lip turned outward and the chin touching the breast. Nurses play a pivotal role in assessing latch technique, monitoring for signs of effective milk transfer such as audible swallowing and adequate wet diapers, and providing emotional encouragement to the mother. In the Nigerian context, exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life is actively promoted by national health policies, meaning no water, other liquids, or foods should be given unless medically indicated. Nurses must educate mothers against common cultural practices that discourage colostrum feeding or encourage early water supplementation. Addressing maternal challenges such as engorgement, inverted nipples, or sore nipples promptly prevents premature cessation of breastfeeding and safeguards infant health.

Systematic Physical Examination of the Neonate

A systematic physical examination of the neonate is performed within the first 24 hours of life to detect congenital anomalies, birth injuries, and overall physical maturity. The nurse or clinician should conduct the examination in a warm, well-lit environment, observing universal precautions. The head is inspected for shape, symmetry, molding, and the status of fontanelles—the anterior fontanelle should be soft and flat, while the posterior fontanelle is smaller and triangular. EYES are checked for red reflex to rule out congenital cataracts, and ears are inspected for placement and tags. The chest is auscultated for heart sounds and clear breath sounds, noting any murmurs or asymmetry. The abdomen should be soft, rounded, and non-distended, with two arteries and one vein visible in the umbilical cord stump. Genitalia are evaluated for patency, descent of testes in males, and labial swelling in females. The spine is palpated along its entire length to rule out spina bifida occulta or dimples. Extremities are assessed for range of motion, symmetry, and congenital hip dysplasia using maneuvers such as the Ortolani and Barlow tests. Neurological assessment includes evaluating primitive reflexes such as the Moro, rooting, suck, and grasp reflexes. Documenting these findings accurately ensures that deviations are flagged for specialist referral.

Common Neonatal Variations and Minor Disorders

During the transitional and early neonatal periods, infants frequently exhibit various benign physiological variations and minor disorders that can cause anxiety for parents if not properly explained. Physiologic jaundice is a common phenomenon appearing after the first 24 to 48 hours of life, caused by the breakdown of fetal red blood cells and immature liver conjugation of bilirubin; it typically resolves on its own without intervention. Erythema toxicum neonatorum—a benign, blotchy, maculopapular rash with small pustules—frequently appears on the trunk and limbs within the first few days and resolves spontaneously without treatment. Milia, tiny white pinpoint papules on the bridge of the nose and chin caused by blocked sebaceous glands, disappear as skin pores open. Mongolian spots, which are benign bluish-gray pigmented areas commonly seen over the lumbosacral region in infants of African, Asian, and Mediterranean descent, fade gradually over the first few years of life and should not be mistaken for bruises. Acrocyanosis, a bluish discoloration of the hands and feet, is a normal response to immature peripheral circulation in response to cooler ambient temperatures. Nurses play a crucial counseling role by reassuring mothers that these conditions are self-limiting and do not indicate pathology, thereby reducing parental panic and unnecessary medical interventions.

Identification of Danger Signs and Neonatal Complications

Prompt identification of danger signs and neonatal complications is essential for reducing neonatal mortality, particularly in Nigerian healthcare settings where delayed presentation can be fatal. Nurses must educate mothers and caregivers to recognize red-flag symptoms that require immediate medical evaluation. Key danger signs include severe jaundice (especially if it spreads to the palms and soles within the first 24 hours or persists beyond two weeks), difficulty breathing (evidenced by chest indrawing, grunting, or tachypnea with a respiratory rate above 60 breaths per minute), lethargy or inability to feed, abnormal body temperature (either fever above 37.5°C or hypothermia below 36.5°C), and seizures or abnormal twitching. Additionally, signs of localized or systemic infection, such as pus draining from the umbilical stump, redness spreading around the umbilicus, or skin pustules, warrant urgent intervention. Neonatal sepsis, birth asphyxia, and severe hyperbilirubinemia are among the leading causes of newborn admission and mortality in Nigeria. Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion, perform serial clinical evaluations, and facilitate swift referrals to specialized neonatal care units when any danger sign is detected.

Discharge Planning and Maternal Education in the Nigerian Context

Discharge planning and maternal education represent the final, vital phase of institutional newborn care, bridging the gap between hospital-based support and home management in the Nigerian context. Discharge planning should begin upon admission, ensuring that parents are thoroughly prepared for the transition home. Nurses must provide comprehensive education on essential topics, including exclusive breastfeeding techniques, recognizing neonatal danger signs, maintaining thermal care, practicing safe sleep positioning (supine position to reduce SIDS risk), and proper hygiene. In resource-limited settings, mothers must be specifically counseled on local danger signs, the avoidance of harmful cultural practices regarding cord care or feeding, and the importance of scheduled postnatal follow-up visits and immunizations according to the Nigerian National Programme on Immunization (NPI) schedule (such as BCG and OPV at birth). Nurses should assess the home environment's readiness, confirm that the mother feels confident handling the baby, and provide clear contact information for the healthcare facility. Effective discharge education empowers mothers to act as frontline health advocates for their infants, significantly improving neonatal survival rates and promoting long-term child health and development.
