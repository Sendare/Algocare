Care of the Newborn

Immediate Assessment and Apgar Scoring

When a baby is delivered, the immediate priority is establishing an open airway and assessing how well the transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life is going. Nurses use the Apgar score at exactly one minute and five minutes after birth to evaluate five distinct physiological signs: heart rate, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability, and color. Each parameter receives a score of 0, 1, or 2.

  • Heart rate: Absent is 0, below 100 beats per minute is 1, and above 100 beats per minute is 2.
  • Respiratory effort: Absent is 0, slow or irregular breathing is 1, and crying vigorously is 2.
  • Muscle tone: Limp is 0, some flexion of extremities is 1, and active movement is 2.
  • Reflex irritability: No response is 0, grimace is 1, and a cough, sneeze, or vigorous cry is 2.
  • Color: Blue or pale all over is 0, pink body with blue extremities (acrocyanosis) is 1, and completely pink is 2.

A total score of 7 to 10 indicates good transition, 4 to 6 suggests moderate depression requiring resuscitation support, and 0 to 3 indicates severe distress needing immediate life-saving interventions.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Heat Loss

Newborns are highly vulnerable to rapid cooling due to a high surface area-to-mass ratio, thin skin, and limited stores of brown adipose tissue. Unlike adults, full-term infants cannot shiver to generate warmth; instead, they rely on non-shivering thermogenesis by metabolizing brown fat, which rapidly depletes their glucose and oxygen reserves. Nurses must prevent cold stress through four primary mechanisms of heat loss:

  • Evaporation: Loss of heat when amniotic fluid on the skin turns into vapor. Immediate drying with warm towels prevents this.
  • Conduction: Direct transfer of heat to cooler objects, such as a cold weighing scale or examining table. Using pre-warmed blankets mitigates this risk.
  • Convection: Loss of heat to surrounding cool air currents. Keeping the delivery room draft-free protects the infant.
  • Radiation: Transfer of heat to cooler surfaces nearby that are not in direct contact, such as cold outer walls or windows.

Uncorrected cold stress leads directly to hypoglycemia, respiratory distress, and metabolic acidosis.

Routine Prophylactic Measures and Newborn Care

Standardized care immediately following birth includes essential prophylaxis to protect the neonate from infection and hemorrhage. Vitamin K (phytonadione) is administered intramuscularly as a single dose to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn, since neonatal gut sterility prevents adequate clotting factor synthesis during the first week of life.

Ophthalmic prophylaxis, typically using erythromycin 0.5% ointment, is instilled into the lower conjunctival sac of both eyes within an hour of birth. This statutory intervention prevents ophthalmia neonatorum, a severe eye infection caused by maternal gonorrhea or chlamydia exposure during vaginal delivery.

Bath is delayed until the infant's core temperature stabilizes, usually after several hours. The cheesy, protective substance known as vernix caseosa should be left intact or gently rubbed into the skin as it possesses natural antimicrobial and moisturizing properties.

Systematic Physical Examination of the Neonate

A comprehensive physical assessment establishes a baseline and identifies congenital anomalies. Nurses perform this exam head-to-toe in a warm, well-lit environment.

During head inspection, palpate the fontanels: the anterior fontanel is diamond-shaped and closes around 12 to 18 months, while the posterior fontanel is triangular and closes by 2 months. A caput succedaneum is localized soft tissue swelling that crosses suture lines due to pressure, whereas a cephalhematoma is bleeding between the skull bone and periosteum that does NOT cross suture lines.

Assess the chest for symmetry and listen to breath sounds, noting that periodic breathing with short pauses is normal in neonates. Inspect the abdomen for a two-artery, one-vein umbilical cord. Check the spine for tufts of hair or dimples, which can indicate spina bifida occulta. Finally, elicit primitive reflexes including Moro, rooting, sucking, and palmar grasp.

Breastfeeding Initiation and Support

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life optimizes maternal-infant bonding and stimulates milk production through prolactin and oxytocin release. Colostrum, the thick yellowish pre-milk fluid, is rich in maternal antibodies, protein, and vitamins, offering crucial passive immunity.

Proper latch is essential to prevent maternal nipple trauma and ensure effective milk transfer. The infant's mouth should cover a large portion of the areola, not just the nipple, with lips flanged outward and the chin pressed firmly against the breast. Nurses should teach mothers feeding cues, such as rooting, hand-to-mouth movements, and gentle stretching, rather than waiting for late-stage frantic crying.

Exclusive breastfeeding is recommended for the first six months of life. Nurses assess milk transfer adequacy by monitoring wet diapers: at least six heavy wet diapers daily by day six of life, alongside steady weight gain after initial physiological postpartum weight loss.

Identification of Danger Signs and Neonatal Complications

Nurses educate parents and monitor hospitalized infants closely for early signs of serious neonatal illness. Because newborns have immature immune systems, infections can escalate rapidly into sepsis. Key danger signs that demand immediate medical evaluation include:

  • Temperature instability: Persistent fever above 37.5°C or hypothermia below 36.5°C.
  • Respiratory distress: Nasal flaring, sternal retractions, grunting, and tachypnea greater than 60 breaths per minute.
  • Jaundice in the first 24 hours: Pathological yellowing of the skin or sclera appearing on day one requires immediate bilirubin testing.
  • Feeding intolerance: Refusal of feeds, projectile vomiting, or abdominal distension.
  • Lethargy: Extreme difficulty waking up or a weak, high-pitched cry.

Recognizing these red flags early prevents irreversible neurological damage or neonatal mortality.

Discharge Planning and Maternal Education in the Nigerian Context

Discharge planning bridges hospital care and home management, focusing heavily on maternal education tailored to local realities. Nurses must ensure mothers understand cord care, emphasizing that the umbilical stump should be kept clean and dry, avoiding harmful traditional applications such as saliva, ash, or native concoctions that introduce tetanus spores.

Education must cover safe sleep positioning: always placing the infant supine on a firm mattress to reduce the risk of sudden infant death syndrome. Furthermore, nurses discuss immunization schedules, reinforcing the importance of BCG, oral polio, and hepatitis B vaccines administered at birth and during routine clinic visits.

In the Nigerian context, discharge instructions should also address warning signs of malaria and dehydration from diarrhea, practical home hygiene, and empowering mothers to return promptly to the nearest health facility when danger signs arise.
