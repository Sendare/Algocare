Care of the Newborn

Immediate Assessment and Resuscitation at Birth

The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life demands rapid physiological adaptation. Immediately following delivery, the infant is placed under a radiant warmer to prevent heat loss while the airway is cleared if excessive secretions are present. Initial evaluation focuses on three core parameters: respiratory effort, heart rate, and color. If an infant is term, breathing well, and has good muscle tone, routine care proceeds uninterrupted on the mother's chest. However, gasping, apnea, or a heart rate below 100 beats per minute triggers immediate intervention according to neonatal resuscitation guidelines, beginning with stimulation and airway positioning, followed by positive pressure ventilation if spontaneous breathing fails to establish.

Apgar Scoring and Physiological Adaptation

The Apgar score provides a standardized numerical evaluation of the newborn's physical condition at 1 and 5 minutes after birth. - **Appearance (Color)**: Evaluates central cyanosis versus generalized pinkness. - **Pulse (Heart Rate)**: Assessed via auscultation or palpation of the umbilical cord base. - **Grimace (Reflex Irritability)**: Measured by response to suctioning or mild tactile stimuli. - **Activity (Muscle Tone)**: Evaluates degree of flexion and resistance to extension. - **Respiration (Respiratory Effort)**: Assessed by the strength and regularity of crying and breathing. Scores range from 0 to 2 for each category, yielding a total out of 10. A score of 7 to 10 indicates good transition, 4 to 6 signals moderate depression requiring intervention, and 0 to 3 demands immediate, aggressive resuscitation.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Heat Loss

Neonates are highly susceptible to rapid body cooling due to a high surface area-to-mass ratio, thin skin, and minimal subcutaneous fat. Newborns cannot shiver to generate heat; instead, they rely on the metabolism of brown adipose tissue, which rapidly consumes oxygen and glucose, leading to hypoglycemia and metabolic acidosis if cold stress occurs. | Mechanism of Heat Loss | Clinical Description | |---|---| | Evaporation | Heat loss through the conversion of amniotic fluid on the skin into vapor | | Conduction | Transfer of heat directly to a cooler surface the infant touches | | Convection | Loss of heat to surrounding cooler air currents | | Radiation | Transfer of heat to cooler objects nearby that are not in direct contact | Preventative measures include immediate drying at birth, placing the infant on skin-to-skin contact with the mother under a warm blanket, and performing examinations in draft-free environments.

Routine Newborn Prophylactic Measures

Standard prophylactic interventions are administered shortly after birth to protect the neonate against preventable infections and hemorrhagic conditions. Ophthalmic ointment, typically erythromycin or tetracycline, is instilled into both conjunctival sacs within an hour of birth to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum caused by maternal Neisseria gonorrhoeae or Chlamydia trachomatis. Additionally, a prophylactic intramuscular injection of Vitamin K is given to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn, as infants possess sterile guts lacking the bacterial flora necessary to synthesize their own clotting factors.

Newborn Feeding: Breastfeeding Initiation and Support

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life provides the infant with colostrum, a thick, yellowish fluid rich in maternal antibodies, immunoglobulins, and white blood cells that confer passive immunity. Proper latching requires the infant to take a large portion of the areola into the mouth, not just the nipple, preventing maternal nipple trauma and ensuring effective milk transfer. Exclusive breastfeeding on demand for the first six months of life optimizes growth, neurodevelopment, and gastrointestinal health without requiring supplementary water or formula.

Systematic Physical Examination of the Neonate

A comprehensive physical examination should be performed within the first twenty-four hours of life. The sequence should be structured to minimize distress, beginning with inspection and auscultation of the heart and lungs while the infant is quiet. The head is inspected for molding, caput succedaneum, or cephalhematoma, and fontanelles are palpated to check for tension or size. The abdomen is palpated for organomegaly or masses, and the umbilical cord is checked for the presence of two arteries and one vein. Musculoskeletal checks include evaluating for developmental dysplasia of the hip using the Ortolani and Barlow maneuvers.

Common Neonatal Variations and Minor Disorders

Parents often experience anxiety regarding normal physiological variations that appear during the first week of life. Erythema toxicum is a benign, self-limiting newborn rash characterized by blotchy red spots with central white or yellow papules that resolve spontaneously without treatment. Physiological jaundice frequently appears after 24 hours of life due to immature hepatic conjugation of bilirubin and elevated red blood cell turnover. Other common findings include milia on the nose, Epstein pearls on the hard palate, and transient acrocyanosis of the hands and feet.

Identification of Danger Signs and Neonatal Complications

Early recognition of clinical deterioration is vital for reducing neonatal morbidity and mortality. Healthcare providers and family caregivers must monitor for specific red flags that indicate serious underlying illness, such as severe sepsis, birth asphyxia, or congenital anomalies. Any inability to feed, persistent vomiting, lethargy, temperature instability (fever or hypothermia), grunting, nasal flaring, chest indrawing, or convulsions must be treated as a medical emergency requiring immediate referral and stabilization.

Discharge Planning and Maternal Education in the Nigerian Context

Discharge preparation begins upon admission and focuses on equipping mothers with practical skills for home care. In the Nigerian context, education must address local realities, emphasizing hygienic cord care using dry cord care or recommended antiseptics while discouraging harmful traditional practices like applying saliva, charcoal, or herbal concoctions to the umbilicus. Mothers are educated on recognizing danger signs, maintaining thermal warmth, ensuring exclusive breastfeeding, and adhering to the national immunization schedule, including the Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) and oral polio vaccines administered at birth.
