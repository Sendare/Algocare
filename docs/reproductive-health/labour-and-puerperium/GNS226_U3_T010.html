Newborn Resuscitation

Introduction to Newborn Resuscitation and Physiology

Newborn resuscitation is a critical skill for nursing students and healthcare professionals practicing in Nigeria, where prompt and effective intervention can significantly reduce neonatal mortality rates. Transitioning from intrauterine to extrauterine life requires a complex sequence of physiological changes. In utero, the fetus depends entirely on the placenta for gas exchange, and the lungs are filled with fluid with high pulmonary vascular resistance. At birth, the sudden clamping of the umbilical cord and the first breath alter this hemodynamic profile dramatically. Air replaces fluid in the alveoli, pulmonary arterioles relax, pulmonary blood flow increases, and the ductus arteriosus and foramen ovale begin to close functionally. Failure to establish effective breathing leads to secondary apnea, severe bradycardia, and hypoxia. Understanding this transition helps nurses anticipate which newborns may fail to make the transition independently, especially in resource-limited settings where rapid recognition of respiratory depression is vital. When a newborn does not cry or breathe spontaneously, prompt intervention is required to clear the airway, stimulate breathing, and provide respiratory support to prevent permanent anoxic injury or death. As student nurses, mastering the neonatal resuscitation algorithm ensures that you can act methodically during high-stress obstetrical emergencies.

Initial Assessment and Risk Identification at Birth

Initial assessment at birth begins immediately upon delivery by asking three rapid screening questions: Is the gestation term? Is the amniotic fluid clear of meconium? Is the newborn crying or breathing? Is there good muscle tone? If the answer to all these questions is yes, the infant can remain with the mother for routine care. However, if any answer is no, the infant requires immediate separation for initial steps of resuscitation. Risk identification is paramount in Nigerian maternity wards, where factors such as maternal hypertension, gestational diabetes, prolonged rupture of membranes, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, prematurity, and multiple gestations significantly increase the likelihood of neonatal compromise. Midwives and nurses must collaborate closely with obstetric teams to review antenatal histories before delivery, ensuring that personnel skilled in neonatal resuscitation are present at high-risk births. Preparation involves checking equipment, ensuring radiant warmers are switched on, and having suction apparatus and bag-valve-mask devices ready. By quickly identifying risk factors and conducting a rapid evaluation of the newborn's physiological status within the first few seconds of life, nursing professionals can avert severe birth asphyxia and establish a foundation for timely intervention.

Routine Care and Stabilization Steps

For newborns who pass the initial screening questions, routine care and stabilization are provided to maintain normal body temperature and support successful transition. The golden minutes following birth require maintaining a neutral thermal environment because hypothermia in neonates is strongly associated with increased morbidity and mortality, particularly in low-resource settings. Routine care includes drying the infant thoroughly with warm towels, removing wet linen, placing the baby skin-to-skin on the mother's chest, and covering them with a dry blanket. Delayed cord clamping, typically for at least one to three minutes in vigorous term and preterm infants, improves iron stores and reduces intraventricular hemorrhage. While providing routine care, the nurse must continue to monitor the newborn's color, breathing, and activity. Eye prophylaxis using tetracycline eye ointment and administration of Vitamin K intramuscularly to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn are standard components of immediate post-birth care. If the infant shows signs of respiratory distress, cyanosis, or poor tone during routine care, the nurse must rapidly transition from routine care to active resuscitation steps. Continuous vigilance ensures that minor cooling or transitional delays are caught and corrected before they escalate into major clinical emergencies.

Clearing the Airway and Tactile Stimulation

If a newborn requires intervention, the initial steps under the Neonatal Resuscitation Program (NRP) guidelines include providing warmth, positioning the head in a 'sniffing' position to open the airway, clearing secretions if necessary, and drying and stimulating the baby. Positioning is critical; over-extending or flexing the neck can obstruct the trachea. If secretions are visible and obstructing the airway, or if the infant is breathing with difficulty, suctioning is performed using a bulb syringe or suction catheter, remembering to suction the mouth before the nose ('M before N') to prevent aspiration. Gentle tactile stimulation, such as rubbing the back or flicking the soles of the feet, can be applied to stimulate breathing in infants with secondary apnea or delayed respiratory effort. However, nurses must avoid vigorous or harmful forms of stimulation, such as slapping or holding the baby upside down, which are outdated and dangerous practices. If tactile stimulation fails to prompt spontaneous and effective breathing within a few seconds, the nurse must recognize that the infant is not responding and immediately prepare to initiate positive pressure ventilation. Documenting the duration of stimulation and the infant's response is essential for ongoing clinical handover.

Positive Pressure Ventilation (PPV) and Monitoring

Positive Pressure Ventilation (PPV) is the single most important and effective step in neonatal resuscitation for infants who fail to breathe after initial stimulation. PPV is indicated if the newborn is apneic, gasping, or has a heart rate below 100 beats per minute after the initial steps. The nurse must apply a properly sized face mask over the nose and mouth, ensuring an airtight seal without pressing down on the eyes or compressing the trachea. Ventilation rate should be maintained at 40 to 60 breaths per minute using a rhythmic cadence: 'Breathe, two, three, breathe, two, three.' Initial pressure settings must be adequate to inflate the lungs, usually monitored by chest movement and rising heart rate. Pulse oximetry is ideally placed on the right hand (pre-ductal) to monitor oxygen saturation against target ranges for age in minutes, and an ECG lead may be attached for continuous heart rate tracking. If the heart rate does not increase or chest movement is absent, corrective steps (MR. SOPA: Mask adjustment, Reposition airway, Suction mouth/nose, Open mouth, Pressure increase, Alternative airway) must be rapidly executed. Effective PPV usually results in a rapid improvement in heart rate and color within the first 30 seconds of application.

Chest Compressions in Neonatal Resuscitation

Chest compressions are indicated in neonatal resuscitation when the heart rate remains persistently below 60 beats per minute despite adequate ventilation with 100% oxygen through an endotracheal tube or well-fitting mask for at least 30 seconds. In neonates, cardiac arrest is almost always asphyxial in origin, meaning that establishing adequate lung ventilation is far more critical than delivering chest compressions alone. Compressions are performed using the 'two-thumb, encircling-hands technique,' which is preferred over the two-finger method because it generates higher peak systolic and coronary perfusion pressures. The thumbs are placed on the lower third of the sternum, just below the intermamillary line, while the hands encircle the chest to support the spine. The depth of compression should be approximately one-third of the anteroposterior diameter of the chest. Compressions and ventilations must be coordinated in a 3:1 ratio, equating to 90 compressions and 30 breaths per minute, maintaining the rhythm: 'One-and-two-and-three-and-breath-and.' This coordination requires excellent teamwork between nurses and other clinical staff. Chest compressions are continued until the heart rate recovers to 60 beats per minute or higher, at which point compressions are stopped while PPV is maintained until the heart rate exceeds 100 beats per minute.

Emergency Medications and Vascular Access

Despite effective ventilation and chest compressions, emergency medications may occasionally be required if the newborn's heart rate remains persistently below 60 beats per minute. Epinephrine is the primary medication indicated to stimulate cardiac activity and increase peripheral vasoconstriction, thereby improving coronary and cerebral blood flow. It is administered intravenously (preferred via an umbilical venous catheter) or endotracheally while IV access is being established, though the IV dose differs significantly from the endotracheal dose. Nurses must calculate dosages precisely based on the neonate's birth weight to avoid severe toxicity or hypertensive crises. Normal saline boluses may also be administered if there is acute blood loss or signs of hypovolemia (such as poor perfusion, pale skin, or weak pulses) that do not respond to resuscitation efforts. Establishing vascular access quickly is a critical nursing competency; the umbilical vein catheter (UVC) is the most reliable emergency route in the delivery room. Nurses assisting in this procedure must maintain strict aseptic technique, prepare the umbilical cord stump properly, and secure the catheter at the correct depth to prevent complications like infection, air embolism, or hepatic laceration. Sodium bicarbonate is rarely recommended unless prolonged resuscitation has failed and severe metabolic acidosis is documented.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Ongoing Evaluation

Post-resuscitation care is a vital phase where nurses monitor stabilized newborns to prevent secondary complications such as hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE), hypoglycemia, hypothermia, and persistent pulmonary hypertension. Once spontaneous circulation and effective breathing are restored, the infant must be transferred to a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) or a special care baby unit (SCBU) for ongoing evaluation. Continuous monitoring of vital signs—including heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, temperature, and oxygen saturation—is essential. Blood glucose levels must be checked frequently because depleted glycogen stores put resuscitated neonates at high risk for hypoglycemia. Neurological monitoring is crucial; signs of HIE, such as altered consciousness, hypotonia, abnormal posturing, or seizures, must be identified early, as therapeutic hypothermia (cooling protocols) may be indicated within the first six hours of life in qualifying infants. Furthermore, nurses must provide compassionate support and clear communication to the parents, explaining the infant's condition and addressing their emotional needs during this vulnerable period. Detailed documentation of the resuscitation events, Apgar scores at 1, 5, and 10 minutes, medications given, and the newborn's response completes the clinical record.
