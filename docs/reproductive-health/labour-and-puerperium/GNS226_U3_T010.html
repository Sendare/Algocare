Newborn Resuscitation

Transition from Fetal to Neonatal Life

At birth, the newborn transitions from a fluid-filled intrauterine environment to an air-breathing extrauterine existence. In the womb, the placenta handles gas exchange, and the lungs are filled with fluid. When the umbilical cord is clamped and the baby takes their first breath, pulmonary vascular resistance drops drastically. This allows blood to flow into the lungs for oxygenation. Failure of this transition leads to primary or secondary apnea, requiring immediate nursing intervention to establish effective ventilation and prevent hypoxic-ischemic injury.

Initial Assessment and Risk Factors

Every birth requires a rapid evaluation to determine if resuscitation is necessary. The delivery room nurse must ask four key pre-birth questions: What is the gestational age? Is the amniotic fluid clear? Are there additional risk factors, such as multiple gestations? Is spontaneous breathing or crying present, and does the baby have good muscle tone? Infants born preterm, through meconium-stained fluid, or with poor tone require immediate separation for specialized stabilization.

Routine Care and Stabilization Steps

For healthy term infants who are crying and have good muscle tone, routine care is provided while staying with the mother.

  • Provide warmth: Dry the infant thoroughly with warm towels and place them skin-to-skin.
  • Clear the airway: Position the head in a neutral sniffing position. Suction the mouth and then the nose only if secretions obstruct breathing.
  • Dry and stimulate: Rub the back or extremities gently to stimulate breathing.
  • Evaluate: Continuously assess respirations, heart rate, and color.

Positive Pressure Ventilation

If a newborn is apneic, gasping, or has a heart rate below 100 beats per minute after initial steps, positive pressure ventilation (PPV) is the single most important and effective step in neonatal resuscitation. The nurse must apply a properly sized mask to create a seal over the nose and mouth without pressing on the eyes. Ventilations are delivered at a rate of 40 to 60 breaths per minute using the rhythm 'Breath, two, three, Breath, two, three.' Effective PPV is indicated by a rising heart rate and bilateral chest movement.

Chest Compressions and Coordination

Chest compressions are indicated when the heart rate remains persistently below 60 beats per minute despite at least 30 seconds of effective positive pressure ventilation with chest movement. The preferred technique is the two-thumb encircling hands method, pressing on the lower third of the sternum to a depth of one-third of the anteroposterior diameter of the chest. Compressions and ventilations must be coordinated in a 3:1 ratio—three compressions followed by one breath—yielding a total of 90 compressions and 30 breaths per minute.

Emergency Medications and Vascular Access

Medications are rarely needed in neonatal resuscitation because establishing adequate ventilation usually corrects bradycardia. However, if the heart rate remains below 60 beats per minute despite effective ventilation and chest compressions, epinephrine is indicated. The preferred route of administration is an emergency umbilical venous catheter (UVC) or an intraosseous needle. Epinephrine stimulates heart rate and contractility, while volume expanders like normal saline are given if there is evidence of acute blood loss or shock.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Ongoing Evaluation

Once spontaneous circulation and breathing are restored, the infant enters the post-resuscitation phase. These newborns are at high risk for clinical deterioration, hypoglycemia, hypothermia, and persistent pulmonary hypertension. The nurse must monitor vital signs closely, maintain target oxygen saturations, manage temperature actively using radiant warmers, and provide frequent neurological assessments. Family support and clear documentation of all interventions and time intervals are critical nursing responsibilities during this stabilization window.
