Newborn Resuscitation

Transition from Intrauterine to Extrauterine Life

At birth, the newborn experiences a profound physiological shift. Fluid-filled alveoli must rapidly clear to allow air entry, which triggers pulmonary vasodilation and a dramatic drop in pulmonary vascular resistance. Concurrently, the clamping of the umbilical cord closes the low-resistance placental circuit, raising systemic vascular pressure. Functional closure of the foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus follows shortly after, establishing independent cardiopulmonary circulation. Failure to achieve this transition leads to persistent fetal circulation and secondary apnea, requiring immediate external intervention to reverse hypoxia and bradycardia.

Initial Assessment and Risk Identification

Preparation begins before delivery by reviewing maternal and obstetric risk factors. Rapid evaluation at the moment of birth rests on three quick clinical questions:

  • Gestational age: Is the baby term or preterm?
  • Amniotic fluid: Is the liquor clear of meconium?
  • Breathing or crying: Is the infant establishing effective respiratory effort?

If the answers are favorable, the baby stays with the mother for routine care. If any answer raises concern, immediate separation for targeted resuscitation steps is warranted.

Routine Care and Stabilization Steps

For babies who are breathing well, crying, and born at term, standard management centers on keeping the infant warm and supporting natural transition. Delayed cord clamping for at least one minute is recommended for vigorous infants to improve iron stores. Skin-to-skin contact with the mother prevents hypothermia through radiant heat transfer. Gentle drying of the skin removes amniotic fluid, which halts evaporative heat loss. Continuous observation of breathing and color ensures early detection of unexpected deterioration.

Airway Clearance and Tactile Stimulation

When an infant presents with inadequate breathing or limp muscle tone upon initial assessment, prompt action is required under the warmer. Position the head in a neutral sniffing position to align the pharynx, trachea, and larynx. Clear secretions from the mouth and then the nose using a bulb syringe or suction catheter only if heavy secretions obstruct breathing. Apply gentle tactile stimulation, such as rubbing the back or flicking the soles of the feet, to prompt spontaneous breathing. Avoid aggressive methods like slapping, which cause physical trauma.

Positive Pressure Ventilation and Monitoring

If the baby remains apneic, gasping, or exhibits a heart rate below 100 beats per minute after brief stimulation, initiate positive pressure ventilation (PPV) within the golden minute. Connect a self-inflating bag, flow-inflating bag, or T-piece resuscitator to a well-fitting face mask that covers the nose and mouth without pressing on the eyes. Ventilate at a rate of 40 to 60 breaths per minute. Assess chest rise to confirm effective lung expansion. Pulse oximetry placed on the right wrist or hand provides crucial feedback on pre-ductal oxygen saturation trends.

Chest Compressions in Neonatal Resuscitation

Chest compressions become necessary when the heart rate remains persistently below 60 beats per minute despite 30 seconds of effective, ventilated PPV with chest movement. Use the two-thumb encircling hands technique, placing thumbs on the lower third of the sternum just below the intermamillary line, while fingers support the spine. The compression-to-ventilation ratio is 3:1, consisting of three compressions and one breath every two seconds. This sequence delivers 90 compressions and 30 breaths per minute to circulate oxygenated blood.

Emergency Medications and Vascular Access

Drug therapy is rarely required in newborn resuscitation because poor heart rates usually stem from inadequate ventilation rather than primary cardiac failure. If the heart rate remains below 60 beats per minute despite effective ventilation and coordinated chest compressions, administer adrenaline (epinephrine). The preferred route is intravenous through an umbilical venous catheter (UVC), though an endotracheal dose can serve as a temporary bridge while vascular access is established. Normal saline boluses are indicated if acute blood loss is suspected.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Monitoring

Infants who require extensive resuscitation are at high risk for multi-organ dysfunction, hypoglycemia, and secondary deterioration. Transfer the stabilized newborn to a neonatal intensive care unit or special care baby unit for close monitoring of vital signs, blood glucose, and acid-base balance. Therapeutic hypothermia protocols should be evaluated immediately for term infants presenting with moderate to severe hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Family support and clear communication regarding the infant's condition are vital components of the post-resuscitation phase.
