Immediate Care of the Newborn

Immediate Assessment and the Apgar Score

The transition from the fluid-filled womb to the extrauterine environment requires rapid physiological adaptation. The Apgar score is a quick, practical method to evaluate the physical condition of a newborn immediately after birth. Performed at exactly one minute and five minutes of life, this tool guides immediate resuscitation decisions if the infant struggles to adapt. In cases where the five-minute score remains below seven, additional assessments are typically performed every five minutes for up to twenty minutes.

  • Appearance (Color): Evaluates central and peripheral skin perfusion, ranging from blue or pale to completely pink.
  • Pulse (Heart Rate): Assessed using a stethoscope or by feeling the pulse at the base of the umbilical cord; a rate above 100 beats per minute is normal.
  • Grimace (Reflex Irritability): Tested by observing the infant's reaction to suctioning the nares or a gentle slap on the sole of the foot.
  • Activity (Muscle Tone): Measures the degree of flexion and spontaneous movement in the extremities.
  • Respiration (Respiratory Effort): Assesses the presence, strength, and regularity of breathing and crying.

Students often make the mistake of using the Apgar score to dictate the *exact* steps of resuscitation. Resuscitation must begin immediately based on heart rate and breathing status, often before the one-minute Apgar score is even calculated.

Airway Clearance and Respiratory Support

Establishing a patent airway is the absolute first priority in immediate newborn care. Most infants clear their respiratory passages spontaneously, but gentle suctioning using a bulb syringe or a sterile catheter may be necessary if secretions obstruct breathing. Suctioning should always begin with the mouth before the nose to prevent the infant from gasping and aspirating pooled mucus or amniotic fluid.

A newborn who fails to initiate effective breathing within the first minute requires positive pressure ventilation. Nurses must monitor for signs of respiratory distress, such as nasal flaring, sternal retractions, and grunting. Persistent central cyanosis or a heart rate below 100 beats per minute despite airway clearance indicates an urgent need for advanced resuscitation measures.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Hypothermia

Newborns are exceptionally vulnerable to rapid heat loss due to a high ratio of surface area to body mass, thin skin, and limited subcutaneous fat. Preventing hypothermia is critical because cold stress triggers metabolic acidosis, hypoglycemia, and respiratory distress. Heat is lost through four primary mechanisms: evaporation, conduction, convection, and radiation.

  • Evaporation: Heat loss occurs when amniotic fluid on the skin turns into vapor; drying the infant immediately at birth prevents this.
  • Conduction: Heat transfers away from the baby when placed on a cold surface, such as an unheated scale or table.
  • Convection: Cool air currents moving across the skin draw heat away from the body.
  • Radiation: Heat radiates from the infant to nearby colder objects, walls, or windows without direct contact.

To combat these forces, healthcare providers dry the infant thoroughly, place them under a radiant warmer, use pre-warmed blankets, and promote immediate skin-to-skin contact with the mother.

Infectious Disease and Ophthalmia Neonatorum Prevention

During passage through the birth canal, a newborn may be exposed to maternal pathogens such as *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* and *Chlamydia trachomatis*. Without prophylactic intervention, these organisms can cause severe conjunctivitis known as ophthalmia neonatorum, which can lead to corneal ulceration and permanent blindness.

Standard prophylactic care involves instilling an antimicrobial agent into both conjunctival sacs within the first hour of life. Erythromycin 0.5% ophthalmic ointment is most commonly used. When applying the ointment, the nurse gently pulls down the lower lid, creates a single thin ribbon from the inner canthus to the outer canthus, and avoids touching the tube to the eye to prevent contamination. Excess ointment can be wiped away gently after one minute.

Vitamin K and Hemorrhagic Disease Prevention

Newborn infants have a sterile gut and lack the intestinal flora necessary to synthesize vitamin K, a crucial cofactor required to produce clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. Without supplementation, newborns face a significant risk of bleeding complications known as hemorrhagic disease of the newborn.

To prevent spontaneous bleeding in the brain, gastrointestinal tract, or circumcision sites, a single intramuscular dose of vitamin K is administered shortly after birth. The preferred injection site is the vastus lateralis muscle in the anterolateral thigh. Nurses must use a 25-gauge, 5/8-inch needle and stabilize the leg firmly to prevent injury during administration.

Anthropometric Measurements and Identification

Accurate baseline measurements are essential for assessing a newborn's growth trajectory and gestational age. Weight, length, head circumference, and chest circumference are measured and recorded in the immediate postpartum period. Weight is taken on a pre-warmed, zeroed scale without clothing or diapers. Head circumference is measured at the widest part, just above the eyebrows and around the occipital prominence at the back of the head.

Simultaneously, secure identification is established to prevent infant abduction or mix-ups. Matching tamper-proof bands carrying identical identification numbers are placed securely around the infant's wrist and ankle, and a corresponding band is placed on the mother. Footprints and maternal fingerprinting are often completed before the infant leaves the delivery suite.

Initiation of Breastfeeding and Skin-to-Skin Contact

Placing the healthy newborn directly onto the mother's bare chest immediately after birth—known as kangaroo care or skin-to-skin contact—provides profound physiological and psychological benefits. Skin-to-skin contact stabilizes the infant's heart rate, blood pressure, and body temperature while reducing stress hormones and crying.

During the first hour of life, healthy newborns experience an alert phase known as the period of reactivity, during which instinctual feeding behaviors peak. Infants naturally exhibit rooting reflexes, hand-to-mouth movements, and crawling motions toward the breast. Encouraging early breastfeeding initiation during this window promotes successful lactation, fosters maternal-infant bonding, and utilizes colostrum's high concentration of protective antibodies.

Ongoing Monitoring and Stabilization

Following the completion of immediate care procedures, the newborn enters a phase of transition that requires regular observation. Vital signs—including heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature—are monitored every 30 minutes until stable for two hours. Nurses watch closely for signs of hypoglycemia, such as jitteriness, poor tone, weak cry, or temperature instability, particularly in infants of diabetic mothers or those born small for gestational age.

The umbilical cord is checked to ensure the clamp is secure and that no bleeding occurs from the umbilical vessels. Once the infant's temperature stabilizes and feeding is established, the newborn is prepared for transfer to the postpartum mother-baby unit or nursery, wrapped securely in warm blankets with proper identification intact.
