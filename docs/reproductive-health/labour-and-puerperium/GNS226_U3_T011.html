Immediate Care of the Newborn

Introduction and Objectives of Immediate Newborn Care

The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life is the most dramatic physiological adjustment a human being will ever make. Within seconds of birth, the neonate must independently execute gas exchange, drastically alter cardiovascular circulation, and initiate autonomous temperature regulation. Immediate newborn care encompasses the systematic interventions delivered in the delivery suite or operating theater to support this monumental shift. The primary objective is to safeguard survival, prevent perinatal morbidity, and lay the foundation for optimal developmental health. Every midwife and nurse attending a delivery must anticipate potential complications, ensure equipment is fully functional, and apply standard precautions universally. By streamlining these early moments, health professionals minimize the physiological stress of birth and detect congenital anomalies before secondary complications arise.

Immediate Assessment and Apgar Scoring

The Apgar score provides a standardized numerical evaluation of the newborn's clinical status at 1 and 5 minutes after birth. Developed by Dr. Virginia Apgar, it evaluates five distinct physiological parameters, each assigned a score of 0, 1, or 2:

  • Appearance (Skin Color): Completely blue or pale (0), body pink with blue extremities/acrocyanosis (1), completely pink (2).
  • Pulse (Heart Rate): Absent (0), below 100 beats per minute (1), 100 beats per minute or greater (2).
  • Grimace (Reflex Irritability): No response to stimulation (0), grimace to suction or gentle slap on the soles (1), cry or active withdrawal (2).
  • Activity (Muscle Tone): Flaccid (0), some flexion of extremities (1), well-flexed and active movement (2).
  • Respiration (Respiratory Effort): Absent/apneic (0), slow, irregular, or hypoventilation (1), good, crying vigorously (2).

A score of 7 to 10 indicates good transition status, 4 to 6 signifies moderate depression requiring stimulation and oxygen support, and 0 to 3 denotes severe distress demanding immediate resuscitation. If the 5-minute score remains below 7, scoring continues every 5 minutes up to 20 minutes.

Establishing and Maintaining a Patent Airway

Clearing the upper airway takes precedence immediately upon delivery of the head, before the delivery of the shoulders and trunk, if meconium is present or secretions obstruct breathing. Routine deep suctioning of vigorous newborns is no longer recommended, as it can cause laryngeal spasm, vagal bradycardia, and mucosal trauma. Instead, gentle wiping of the mouth and nose with a sterile gauze is often sufficient. If mechanical suction is required due to heavy mucus, meconium, or amniotic fluid, use a bulb syringe or low-pressure wall suction, clearing the mouth first before the nose to prevent aspiration. Always monitor heart rate during airway manipulation, because excessive pharyngeal stimulation triggers severe vagal responses.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Hypothermia

Newborns possess a high surface-area-to-mass ratio, thin skin with minimal subcutaneous fat, and an inability to shiver effectively, making them exceptionally vulnerable to rapid heat loss. Cold stress triggers a cascade of non-shivering thermogenesis via brown adipose tissue metabolism, consuming large amounts of oxygen and glucose, which rapidly leads to severe hypoglycemia and metabolic acidosis.

Mechanism of Heat LossClinical Prevention Strategy
EvaporationThoroughly dry the infant immediately at birth with pre-warmed towels and discard wet linen.
ConductionPlace the baby on pre-warmed radiant warmers, scales, and wrap in warm blankets.
ConvectionKeep the delivery room free from drafts, closed doors, and away from air conditioner vents.
RadiationPosition cribs and examination tables away from cold exterior windows and exterior walls.

Maintaining a neutral thermal environment between 36.5°C and 37.5°C axillary is a vital marker of quality nursing care.

Prevention of Infection and Ophthalmia Neonatorum

Neonates enter the world with immature immune systems, leaving them highly susceptible to bacterial colonization. Ophthalmia neonatorum is a severe form of conjunctivitis acquired during passage through a birth canal infected with *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* or *Chlamydia trachomatis*. Left untreated, it can cause corneal ulceration and irreversible blindness. Prophylactic antimicrobial eye ointment—traditionally 1% tetracycline or 0.5% erythromycin—is instilled into the lower conjunctival sac of each eye within the first hour after birth. Nurses must clean the eyelids gently from the inner canthus outward before instillation. Do not flush the ointment immediately, as adequate contact time is necessary for effective prophylaxis. Universal aseptic technique during delivery and meticulous cord care further protect the infant from generalized sepsis and omphalitis.

Vitamin K Administration and Hemorrhagic Disease of the Newborn

Newborns are born with a sterile intestinal tract devoid of the bacterial flora required to synthesize vitamin K, a vital cofactor for clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. Consequently, infants experience a natural dip in clotting ability between days two and seven of life, putting them at risk for Hemorrhagic Disease of the Newborn (HDN), which can manifest as catastrophic intracranial bleeding or gastrointestinal hemorrhage. To prevent this, a single intramuscular dose of 0.5 to 1.0 mg of phytonadione (vitamin K) is administered into the vastus lateralis muscle using a 25-gauge, 5/8-inch needle during the initial newborn care routine. Nurses must secure the leg firmly, cleanse the site with an antiseptic swab, and apply gentle pressure afterward while providing comfort measures.

Identification and Anthropometric Measurements

Accurate documentation of physical size establishes baseline data for tracking growth and nutritional health. Anthropometric measurements include weight, length, head circumference, and chest circumference. The infant is weighed naked on a calibrated scale, ensuring safety by keeping a hand hovering just above the baby. Head circumference is measured around the most prominent part of the occiput and just above the supraorbital ridges. Length is measured from the crown of the head to the heel of the extended leg. Concurrently, foolproof identification protocols must be completed before leaving the delivery room. Matching tamper-proof identification bands bearing the mother's hospital number and baby's data are secured to the infant's wrist and ankle, and footprints are recorded to prevent mix-ups.

Initiation of Breastfeeding and Skin-to-Skin Contact

Immediate and continuous skin-to-skin contact (kangaroo mother care) involves placing the naked infant prone directly on the mother's bare chest, covered with a warm dry blanket. This practice stabilizes body temperature, regulates heart rate and blood glucose, and reduces stress hormones. It also fosters early maternal-infant attachment and activates the infant's innate feeding instincts. Within the first hour of life—often termed the golden hour—the newborn experiences a period of quiet alertness ideal for latching. During this time, the infant exhibits feeding cues such as rooting, sucking motions, and hand-to-mouth movements. Nurses facilitate this by assisting the mother with positioning, ensuring a wide, asymmetric latch to prevent nipple trauma and promote effective colostrum transfer.

Ongoing Monitoring and Preparation for Transfer to Mother

Before transferring the newborn to the postnatal ward or rooming-in with the mother, a final systematic head-to-toe clinical check is performed to rule out major structural anomalies, birth injuries, or signs of distress. Nurses assess vital signs—including respiratory rate, heart rate, and temperature—documenting stability. The umbilical cord clamp is checked to ensure no bleeding is present. The nurse confirms that all prophylactic interventions, eye ointment, vitamin K, and identification bands are correctly documented and completed. Throughout this period, maternal education begins, teaching parents how to recognize danger signs such as lethargy, poor feeding, respiratory difficulty, and jaundice, ensuring a safe transition to home or postpartum care.
