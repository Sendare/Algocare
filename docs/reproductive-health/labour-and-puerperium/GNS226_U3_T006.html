Management of Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Normal labour, also known as eutocia, refers to the physiological process by which a viable fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina. To be classified as normal, the pregnancy must be at term, typically between 37 and 42 completed weeks, with a single fetus presenting by the vertex. The onset of labour is marked by regular, painful uterine contractions that progressively increase in frequency and intensity, resulting in demonstrable cervical effacement and dilation.

Key features of normal labour include spontaneous onset and progression without artificial induction or excessive medical intervention. The presentation is occipito-anterior, and the entire process occurs without complications to either the mother or the newborn. Understanding this baseline is crucial for midwives and nurses because recognizing normal physiological progress prevents unnecessary interventions and helps identify deviations early.

Stages of Labour and Physiology

Labour is divided into four distinct clinical stages, each characterized by specific physiological events:

  1. First Stage: Begins with the onset of true labour pains and ends with complete cervical dilation (10 cm). It is subdivided into the latent phase (slow cervical change up to 5 cm) and the active phase (rapid cervical dilation from 5 cm to 10 cm).
  2. Second Stage: Begins with complete cervical dilation and ends with the birth of the baby. During this stage, maternal pushing efforts combine with uterine contractions to move the fetus through the birth canal.
  3. Third Stage: Begins immediately after the baby is born and ends with the expulsion of the placenta and membranes.
  4. Fourth Stage: The immediate postpartum period, lasting roughly one to two hours after placental delivery, where maternal stabilization and monitoring for haemorrhage take place.

Admission Assessment and Initial Evaluation

When a pregnant woman arrives at the maternity unit, the nurse must conduct a rapid yet thorough evaluation to establish baseline status and determine labour progress. This initial assessment guides immediate care decisions and establishes a therapeutic rapport.

  • History Taking: Review antenatal cards, estimated due date, parity, gravidity, rupture of membranes, and contraction patterns.
  • Vital Signs: Measure maternal blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiration to rule out infection or pre-eclampsia.
  • Abdominal Examination: Perform Leopold's maneuvers to assess fetal lie, presentation, position, and engagement, alongside fetal heart rate auscultation.
  • Vaginal Examination: Evaluate cervical dilation, effacement, station, membrane status, and pelvic adequacy, provided there is no bleeding from a low-lying placenta.

First Stage of Labour: Monitoring and Care

Care during the active phase of the first stage focuses on continuous maternal support, infection control, and vigilant monitoring of both mother and fetus to detect fetal distress or labour dystocia early.

Fetal Heart RateEvery 30 minutes in active phase
Maternal PulseEvery 30 minutes
Blood PressureEvery 4 hours (or more if elevated)
TemperatureEvery 4 hours
ContractionsEvery 30 minutes (frequency and duration)
Vaginal ExaminationEvery 4 hours for cervical progress

Encourage the mother to empty her bladder every two hours to prevent delayed fetal descent. Provide continuous emotional encouragement, oral hydration with clear fluids if permitted, and freedom of movement as long as membranes remain intact.

Second Stage of Labour: Conduct of Delivery

The second stage demands intense focus from the nurse and birth attendant. As the cervix reaches full dilation, the maternal urge to push becomes overwhelming. The nurse must support effective pushing, protect the perineum, and prepare for immediate newborn care.

Positioning plays a major role; upright or lateral positions often enhance pelvic diameters compared to a flat dorsal position. Guide the mother to push with uterine contractions rather than continuous breath-holding. As the baby's head crowns, apply controlled counter-pressure to the fetal head to prevent rapid expulsion and reduce severe perineal tearing. Once the head is delivered, check for a nuchal cord, allow restitution, deliver the anterior shoulder, and gently lift for the posterior shoulder.

Third Stage of Labour and AMTSL

Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) remains a leading cause of maternal mortality. Active Management of the Third Stage of Labour (AMTSL) is a standard intervention proven to significantly reduce blood loss and the incidence of PPH.

AMTSL consists of three essential steps:

  1. Uterotonic Administration: Administer 10 international units of oxytocin intramuscularly within one minute of infant birth. Oxytocin is the drug of choice because it causes rapid uterine contraction with minimal side effects.
  2. Controlled Cord Traction (CCT): Apply gentle traction on the umbilical cord while performing suprapubic counter-pressure to support placental separation.
  3. Uterine Massage: Gently massage the uterine fundus immediately after the placenta is delivered to ensure it remains contracted.

Inspect the expelled placenta carefully to ensure completeness, checking for missing cotyledons or membranes that could cause retained tissue and secondary bleeding.

Fourth Stage of Labour and Immediate Postpartum Care

The fourth stage of labour is the first two hours following the delivery of the placenta. This critical transition period carries the highest risk for postpartum haemorrhage, making vigilant observation essential.

Monitor maternal vital signs, uterine tone, and lochial flow every 15 minutes for the first hour, and every 30 minutes during the second hour. A boggy, relaxed uterus indicates uterine atony, requiring immediate fundal massage and assessment of bladder distension. Facilitate early skin-to-skin contact between mother and newborn, which promotes thermoregulation, bonding, and early initiation of breastfeeding. Encourage the newborn to latch, as oxytocin released during suckling helps keep the maternal uterus firmly contracted.

Documentation and Partograph Utilization

The partograph is a graphical tool used to monitor labour progress and maternal-fetal well-being. Its proper use prevents prolonged and obstructed labour by providing an early warning system for abnormal progress.

The partograph records cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, contraction frequency, descent of the head, and maternal vitals on a standardized grid. Two crucial reference lines guide clinical decision-making:

  • Alert Line: Starts at 4 cm of cervical dilation and represents the rate of progress of the slowest 10% of primigravidae. If the plotting crosses to the right of the alert line, the nurse must transfer the patient to a facility with comprehensive emergency obstetric care.
  • Action Line: Parallel and four hours to the right of the alert line. Crossing this line demands immediate medical intervention and evaluation for possible augmentation or caesarean section.
