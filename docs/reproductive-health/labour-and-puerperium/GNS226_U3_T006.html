Management of Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Normal labour, often termed eutocia, refers to the physiological process by which a viable fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina. To be clinically classified as normal, the process must begin spontaneously, present with a cephalic presentation, occur at term between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation, and proceed without artificial augmentation or mechanical interference where possible. Furthermore, it must conclude with the mother and newborn in good condition. In Nigerian obstetric practice, recognizing true labour from false labour (braxton hicks contractions) prevents premature hospital admissions and unnecessary anxiety for the parturient woman. True labour is characterized by regular uterine contractions that progressively increase in frequency, intensity, and duration, accompanied by progressive effacement and dilation of the cervix, as well as the passage of the bloody show.

Stages of Labour and Physiology

Labour is divided into four distinct clinical stages, each governed by specific physiological mechanisms involving uterine smooth muscle contractility, hormonal signaling, and mechanical forces. - **First Stage**: Begins with the onset of true labour contractions and ends with complete cervical dilation at 10 centimeters. It is subdivided into the latent phase (slow cervical change up to 4 cm) and the active phase (rapid cervical dilation from 5 cm to 10 cm). - **Second Stage**: Commences with full cervical dilation and terminates with the complete birth of the baby, driven by involuntary uterine contractions combined with voluntary maternal pushing efforts (bearing down). - **Third Stage**: Spans from the delivery of the neonate to the complete expulsion of the placenta and fetal membranes, relying on uterine retraction to seal open maternal blood vessels. - **Fourth Stage**: Covers the immediate postpartum period, typically the first two hours following placental delivery, where physiological stabilization and monitoring for hemorrhage occur.

Admission Assessment and Initial Evaluation

A comprehensive admission assessment establishes baseline parameters for both mother and fetus, ensuring any deviations from normal physiological ranges are identified early. Upon a pregnant woman's arrival at the labour ward, the midwife performs a systematic evaluation: 1. **History Taking**: Reviewing antenatal records, parity, gestational age, time of membrane rupture, and contraction patterns. 2. **Physical Examination**: Measuring maternal vital signs (blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiration) and assessing general hydration status. 3. **Abdominal Examination**: Utilizing Leopold's maneuvers to determine fetal lie, presentation, position, and descent, alongside measuring fundal height. 4. **Fetal Heart Rate Auscultation**: Listening via a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler ultrasound immediately after a contraction to establish a baseline. 5. **Vaginal Examination**: Assessing cervical dilation, effacement, station, membrane status, and pelvic architecture, performed with strict aseptic technique.

First Stage of Labour: Monitoring and Care

Continuous, supportive care during the first stage promotes physiological progress and provides reassurance to the woman. Monitoring involves tracking maternal well-being, fetal condition, and labour progression. Maternal blood pressure is checked every four hours, while temperature and pulse are monitored more frequently if membranes have ruptured. Fetal heart rate is assessed every thirty minutes during the active phase. Encouraging mobility, adequate hydration, and frequent bladder emptying facilitates normal progression. A full bladder displaces the uterus, impedes effective contractions, and obstructs fetal descent. Pain management strategies range from non-pharmacological methods like massage, positioning, and continuous psychological support to pharmacological options where available.

Second Stage of Labour: Conduct of Delivery

The second stage requires vigilant observation as the fetus navigates the birth canal. Maternal positioning should be upright or lateral rather than supine, which reduces aortocaval compression and enhances pelvic dimensions. Midwives coach the mother to push with natural urge during contractions rather than practicing prolonged directed breath-holding valsalva maneuvers. As the fetal head crowns, controlled delivery of the head is managed to prevent perineal lacerations. Once the head is delivered, checking for a nuchal cord precedes the delivery of the shoulders—anterior shoulder first, followed by the posterior. Immediate skin-to-skin contact is initiated upon birth for stable neonates, facilitating thermoregulation and bonding.

Third Stage of Labour: Active Management (AMTSL)

Active Management of the Third Stage of Labour (AMTSL) is a standard evidence-based intervention designed to prevent postpartum haemorrhage, which remains a leading cause of maternal mortality in Nigeria. AMTSL comprises three core components administered immediately after the birth of the baby: - **Uterotonic Administration**: An intramuscular injection of oxytocin (10 IU) within one minute of birth stimulates strong, sustained uterine contractions. - **Controlled Cord Traction (CCT)**: Applying gentle traction on the umbilical cord while simultaneously counter-applying supra-pubic pressure (Kupferberg maneuver) safely delivers the placenta. - **Uterine Massage**: Massaging the uterine fundus through the maternal abdominal wall immediately after placental delivery to maintain contraction tone. Examiners frequently test the precise order and timing of these steps; notably, the uterotonics must be given before attempting cord traction to avoid uterine inversion.

Fourth Stage of Labour and Immediate Postpartum Care

The fourth stage of labour spans the first two hours following the delivery of the placenta, a critical transition window where the highest risk of primary postpartum haemorrhage occurs. Midwives must maintain rigorous monitoring protocols: assessing maternal blood pressure, pulse rate, temperature, lochial flow, and uterine tone every fifteen minutes for the first hour, and every thirty minutes for the second hour. Palpating the uterus confirms it remains firm and contracted (often described as a 'hard ball'). A boggy, relaxed uterus indicates uterine atony, requiring immediate fundal massage and secondary uterotonic administration. Additionally, perineal inspection is performed to check for repair integrity if an episiotomy or tear was sutured, and early breastfeeding initiation is actively supported.

Documentation and Partograph Utilization

The partograph is a pictorial overview of labour that serves as an early warning system for identifying prolonged labour and obstructed labour. It graphically plots cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, descent of the head, contraction frequency, and maternal vital signs against elapsed time. - **Alert Line**: Represents the rate of slowest normal labour dilation (1 cm per hour from 4 cm onwards). If the plot crosses to the right of this line, the woman requires close observation and evaluation. - **Action Line**: Placed four hours to the right of the alert line. Crossing this line indicates that active management or transfer to a comprehensive facility is mandatory. Accurate, contemporaneous documentation on the partograph and clinical notes ensures continuity of care, legal protection, and timely clinical intervention.
