Referral to Health Centre

Introduction to Postnatal Referral Systems

The postnatal period, or puerperium, is a critical time when complications can arise suddenly for both mother and newborn. A referral system acts as a bridge, connecting primary health clinics with higher-level facilities equipped to handle complex emergencies. Effective referral networks save lives by ensuring that patients reach the right level of care without unnecessary delays. In community-based Nigerian healthcare, nurses and midwives serve as the primary gatekeepers who must recognize when a condition exceeds the capability of their local health centre.

Indications for Referral During the Puerperium

Not every minor postnatal discomfort requires a hospital transfer, but certain clinical findings demand immediate escalation. Midwives must continuously evaluate mothers during the postnatal visits for abnormal signs.

  • Secondary Postpartum Haemorrhage: Excessive vaginal bleeding occurring after the first 24 hours post-delivery, often due to retained products of conception or infection.
  • Postnatal Sepsis: Persistent fever, lower abdominal pain, and foul-smelling lochia indicating uterine infection.
  • Severe Hypertensive Disorders: New-onset high blood pressure or postpartum pre-eclampsia, which can lead to seizures or stroke.
  • Thromboembolic Events: Unilateral calf pain, swelling, and redness suggesting deep vein thrombosis.

Maternal Warning Signs Requiring Urgent Transfer

Certain emergency signs can cause rapid maternal collapse and death if transfer is delayed. Midwives must look out for the triad of severe headache, blurred vision, and epigastric pain, which strongly suggest impending eclampsia. Uncontrolled postpartum haemorrhage leading to pale conjunctivae, dizziness, and low blood pressure requires immediate volume replacement and surgical intervention at a comprehensive facility. Any mother expressing severe depressive symptoms with thoughts of harming herself or her baby must also be referred promptly for psychiatric support.

Neonatal Red Flags and Indications for Escalation

Newborns deteriorate with alarming speed, making early recognition of danger signs vital for survival. Midwives and nurses must educate mothers on these red flags and act swiftly when they observe them in the clinic.

  • Inability to Feed: A baby who suddenly stops sucking or cannot swallow milk.
  • Convulsions: Abnormal body twitching, eye-rolling, or repetitive mouth movements.
  • Hypothermia or Fever: Axillary temperature below 36.5°C or above 37.5°C.
  • Severe Jaundice: Yellow discoloration of the skin and eyes extending to the palms and soles within the first few days of life.
  • Omphalitis: Redness around the umbilical stump spreading to the abdominal wall with pus discharge.

Pre-Stabilization Before Transfer

Before dispatching a mother or baby to a higher-level facility, the primary health centre must initiate emergency life-saving measures. Stabilization is not meant to treat the underlying condition fully, but to keep the patient alive during transit. For a bleeding mother, this means securing large-bore intravenous access and running normal saline while massaging the uterus. For a convulsing baby or mother, airway management and administration of emergency anticonvulsants like magnesium sulphate or diazepam are necessary. Never send an unstable patient away without basic resuscitation measures in place.

Communication and Documentation in Referrals

A well-written referral note serves as the clinical voice of the primary health worker when handing over a patient. The document must clearly state the patient's identity, the time of assessment, vital signs, the suspected diagnosis, and the pre-referral treatment given. Verbal communication is equally important; calling the receiving hospital ahead of time allows the emergency team to prepare a bed, blood products, or an incubator. Poor documentation often leads to duplication of tests, dangerous treatment delays, and fragmentation of care.

Overcoming Barriers to Referral in Nigeria

Despite clear protocols, many maternal and neonatal referrals fail due to systemic and cultural bottlenecks. Financial constraints, often called out-of-pocket payment delays, frequently prevent families from approving an immediate transfer. Transportation shortages, poor road networks, and lack of functional ambulances further worsen geographical delays, especially in rural Nigerian communities. Additionally, traditional beliefs and preference for traditional birth attendants can cause dangerous delays in reaching formal healthcare. Midwives must engage in community sensitization and work with local leaders to encourage prompt acceptance of hospital referrals.
