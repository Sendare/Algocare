Neonatal Jaundice

Introduction and Definition of Neonatal Jaundice

Neonatal jaundice refers to the yellowish discoloration of the skin and the sclera in a newborn infant. This yellow tint is caused by the accumulation of bilirubin, a yellow pigment that is a byproduct of the normal breakdown of red blood cells. While it is an extremely common condition during the first week of life, healthcare providers must carefully evaluate every jaundiced baby. In many infants, the condition is harmless, but failing to monitor levels properly can lead to severe complications. Spotting this yellow color early in the newborn nursery or during home visits is a core responsibility for nurses caring for mothers and babies.

Bilirubin Production and Metabolism in Newborns

Understanding how the body handles bilirubin helps explain why newborns are so prone to yellow skin.

  1. Production: Newborns have a higher concentration of red blood cells than adults, and these cells also have a shorter lifespan. When these cells break down, they release large amounts of unconjugated bilirubin.
  2. Transport: Unconjugated bilirubin does not dissolve well in water. It binds to albumin in the blood and travels to the liver for processing.
  3. Conjugation: Inside the liver, an enzyme called glucuronyl transferase converts the bilirubin into a water-soluble form known as conjugated bilirubin.
  4. Excretion: The conjugated bilirubin passes into the gut through the bile. Bacteria and enzymes in the intestines help eliminate it through stool, though some can be reabsorbed into the bloodstream if gut motility is slow.

Physiological versus Pathological Jaundice

Differentiating between normal newborn transition and disease processes is a vital clinical skill.

  • Physiological Jaundice: This occurs in most healthy newborns. It typically appears after the first 24 hours of life, peaks around the third to fifth day, and fades by the end of the second week. It happens because the newborn liver is immature and red blood cell turnover is high.
  • Pathological Jaundice: This condition appears within the first 24 hours of life, rises very rapidly, or stays elevated for a long time. Causes often include blood type incompatibilities, severe infections, or genetic conditions like G6PD deficiency. Pathological jaundice requires prompt medical investigation and intervention to prevent brain damage.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

In Nigeria and many sub-Saharan African settings, neonatal jaundice presents unique clinical challenges due to specific local risk factors and healthcare barriers.

  • G6PD Deficiency: Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency is an inherited enzyme defect that is very common in the region. Affected babies exposed to certain triggers, such as naphthalene balls (camphor) stored in baby clothes, experience rapid red blood cell breakdown.
  • ABO and Rhesus Incompatibility: Blood group mismatches between mother and baby trigger immune-mediated hemolysis.
  • Neonatal Sepsis: Bacterial infections acquired in the community or hospital impair liver function and worsen bilirubin accumulation.
  • Delayed Presentation: Many families first seek care late, often trying traditional herbs or exposure to sunlight outdoors before visiting a hospital clinic, which delays effective treatment.

Clinical Assessment and Physical Examination

Clinical assessment begins with careful observation of the infant under natural, well-lit conditions. Nurses should gently blanch the baby's skin over a bony prominence, such as the nose or sternum, to check the underlying color of the skin.

It is easy to misjudge the severity of jaundice by looking at the skin alone, especially across different skin pigmentations. Therefore, visual inspection must be paired with a review of feeding history, stool color, and urine output. Pale stools and dark urine suggest a dangerous condition called biliary atresia rather than normal newborn jaundice. Nurses also assess the infant's muscle tone, reflexes, and level of alertness to catch early warning signs of brain irritation.

Diagnostic Investigations and Monitoring

Accurate measurement of bilirubin is essential to guide treatment decisions. Relying on visual inspection alone is unreliable because the eye cannot accurately guess exact blood levels.

  • Serum Bilirubin (SBR): A blood test that measures total and direct bilirubin levels. It remains the gold standard for clinical decision-making.
  • Transcutaneous Bilirubinometer (TcB): A handheld device placed on the skin that gives a quick estimate of bilirubin levels without a needle prick. It is useful for screening, though high readings usually require confirmation with a blood test.
  • Blood Group and Coombs Test: Determines ABO and Rhesus compatibility between mother and infant.
  • G6PD Screening and Full Blood Count: Identifies underlying hemolysis, anemia, or infection.

Medical Management and Treatment Modalities

When bilirubin levels rise above safe thresholds, medical interventions are initiated to lower levels and protect the brain.

  • Phototherapy: The most common treatment. The baby is placed under special blue-green lights. Light energy transforms unconjugated bilirubin in the skin into water-soluble isomers that the liver and kidneys can excrete easily.
  • Exchange Transfusion: Used in severe cases where bilirubin levels are dangerously high or phototherapy fails. The baby's blood is gradually removed in small amounts and replaced with donor blood.
  • Hydration and Feeding: Ensuring adequate breast milk or formula intake promotes bowel movements, which helps clear bilirubin from the gut.

Complications: Acute Bilirubin Encephalopathy and Kernicterus

If severe jaundice goes untreated, bilirubin crosses the blood-brain barrier and deposits in brain tissues, particularly the basal ganglia. This causes devastating neurological damage.

  • Acute Bilirubin Encephalopathy (ABE): The early, acute manifestation of bilirubin toxicity. Symptoms include extreme lethargy, poor sucking, high-pitched crying, and muscle tone changes, such as backward arching of the body known as opisthotonos. ABE can be reversible if treated aggressively.
  • Kernicterus: The chronic, permanent form of brain damage. Survivors suffer from lifelong disabilities, including choreoathetoid cerebral palsy, hearing loss, developmental delay, and upward gaze abnormalities. Nurses play a critical role in recognizing early signs of ABE so that urgent interventions can prevent permanent kernicterus.

Nursing Care Plan and Parental Education

Comprehensive nursing care bridges hospital treatment and home safety. During phototherapy, nurses ensure the baby's eyes are securely covered with opaque patches to protect the retina from light damage, monitor body temperature closely, and reposition the infant frequently to expose all skin surfaces to the light. Fluid intake must be monitored, as phototherapy increases insensible water loss.

Discharge education is equally vital. Nurses must teach parents how to recognize the return of yellow skin, explain the importance of exclusive and frequent feeding, and warn families against harmful home remedies like using camphor balls or exposing babies to unfiltered sunlight, which can cause severe sunburn and dehydration.
