Essential Obstetric Care

Introduction to Essential Obstetric Care

Essential Obstetric Care (EOC) refers to life-saving interventions delivered to pregnant women and newborns to manage complications that can cause maternal and neonatal death. Most maternal deaths happen around childbirth or shortly after, often due to sudden, unpredictable complications. EOC is divided into two main levels based on the facility's capacity: Basic Emergency Obstetric Care and Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric Care. Understanding these levels helps nurse-midwives prioritize care, recognize worsening clinical signs early, and initiate prompt interventions or referrals before a minor issue becomes a fatal crisis.

Components of Basic Emergency Obstetric Care (BEmOC)

Basic Emergency Obstetric Care (BEmOC) services can be provided in lower-level health facilities, such as primary health centers and basic health clinics, staffed by skilled birth attendants and nurse-midwives. A facility must provide all seven signal functions to be classified as a true BEmOC center:

  1. Parenteral antibiotics: Administering injectable antibiotics to treat maternal infections like chorioamnionitis or sepsis.
  2. Parenteral oxytocics: Using injectable uterotonic drugs to prevent or treat postpartum hemorrhage.
  3. Parenteral anticonvulsants: Giving magnesium sulfate or diazepam to control seizures in severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia.
  4. Manual removal of placenta: Removing a retained placenta manually to stop excessive bleeding.
  5. Removal of retained products: Performing manual vacuum aspiration (MVA) for incomplete miscarriages.
  6. Assisted vaginal delivery: Using vacuum extractors or forceps to assist a difficult second stage of labour.
  7. Neonatal resuscitation: Resuscitating a newborn who fails to breathe at birth using a bag and mask.

Components of Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric Care (EmOC)

Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric Care (CEmOC) includes all seven basic signal functions of BEmOC, plus two additional advanced clinical interventions that require a fully equipped hospital, a surgical theatre, and a medical doctor or specialist:

  • Blood transfusion: Screening, storing, and infusing whole blood or blood products for women experiencing severe hemorrhage or profound anemia.
  • Surgical obstetric delivery: Performing emergency Caesarean sections and other complex laparotomies for obstructed labour or uterine rupture.

Clinically, healthcare providers often make the mistake of waiting until a patient is in profound shock before arranging a transfer to a CEmOC facility. Recognizing surgical indications early ensures that mothers reach a comprehensive facility before irreversible organ damage occurs.

Identification and Management of Obstetric Complications

The major direct causes of maternal mortality are hemorrhage, hypertensive disorders, sepsis, and obstructed labour. Nurse-midwives must recognize the subtle early warning signs of these conditions. Postpartum hemorrhage is marked by excessive vaginal bleeding and a boggy uterus. Severe pre-eclampsia presents with rising blood pressure, severe headache, visual disturbances, and brisk deep tendon reflexes. Obstructed labour shows lack of cervical progress, strong contractions without descent, and caput succedaneum on the fetal head. Timely intervention, such as starting intravenous lines, summoning medical help, and preparing for emergency evacuation, saves lives.

The Role of the Nurse-Midwife in Maternal Resuscitation

When a pregnant woman collapses or enters obstetric shock, the nurse-midwife must lead immediate resuscitation using a systematic ABCDE approach (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure).

Partograph Utilization and Monitoring During Labour

The partograph is a pictorial overview of labour used to monitor maternal and fetal well-being and the progress of cervical dilation. It helps nurse-midwives detect abnormal labour early and decide when to refer a patient. The active phase of labour begins when the cervix is dilated 4 centimeters. The partograph features an alert line and an action line, spaced four hours apart. If the cervical dilation plot crosses the alert line, the patient must be closely monitored and transferred to a facility with comprehensive care if progress is slow. If the plot crosses the action line, immediate medical intervention or surgical delivery is mandatory.

Infection Prevention and Universal Precautions in Labour Wards

Childbirth involves exposure to blood, amniotic fluid, and other body fluids, putting both mothers and healthcare workers at risk of infections like HIV, Hepatitis B, and puerperal sepsis. Universal precautions must be practiced for every patient regardless of their known infection status. Nurse-midwives must perform proper hand hygiene before and after every client contact, wear sterile gloves during vaginal examinations and deliveries, and use personal protective equipment like aprons and eye protection when splashing is possible. Proper decontamination, cleaning, and autoclaving of instruments prevent hospital-acquired infections.

Referral Systems and Emergency Transport in Nigeria

A functioning referral system connects primary health clinics with secondary and tertiary hospitals, ensuring that women with severe complications receive higher-level care. In Nigeria, delays in reaching emergency care—often called the second delay—contribute heavily to maternal deaths due to poor roads, lack of vehicles, and financial barriers. Nurse-midwives play a key coordinating role by stabilizing the patient, writing a clear referral note detailing findings and treatments given, communicating in advance with the receiving hospital, and organizing safe transportation with an attendant when possible.
