Puerperal Sepsis

Introduction and Definition of Puerperal Sepsis

Puerperal sepsis remains a leading direct cause of maternal mortality, particularly in low-resource settings across Sub-Saharan Africa. Formally defined as an infection of the genital tract occurring anytime between the rupture of membranes or labor and the 42nd day postpartum, it manifests with clinical signs such as pelvic pain, fever, abnormal vaginal discharge, and delayed uterine involution. In Nigerian tertiary hospitals, unhygienic traditional practices, prolonged rupture of membranes, and unsterile vaginal examinations during prolonged labor significantly contribute to the incidence of this preventable postpartum emergency.

Pathophysiology and Common Causative Organisms

Following delivery, the placental site leaves a large, raw, vascular surface inside the uterus that serves as an ideal culture medium for bacteria. Pathogens ascend from the lower genital tract or are introduced via contaminated instruments. - **Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Strep)**: Highly virulent, causes rapid-onset, severe invasive infection with profound systemic toxicity. - **Escherichia coli and Enterobacteriaceae**: Normal gut flora that frequently contaminate the perineum and ascend into the uterine cavity. - **Staphylococcus aureus**: Often implicated in wound infections from perineal tears or caesarean section incisions. - **Anaerobes (Bacteroides species)**: Thrive in necrotic tissue, particularly following prolonged rupture of membranes or obstructed labor.

Risk Factors and Predisposing Conditions

Several obstetric and socio-economic variables heighten a woman's vulnerability to developing an ascending reproductive tract infection. - **Prolonged Rupture of Membranes**: Breaks the physical barrier protecting the intrauterine cavity. - **Multiple Vaginal Examinations**: Each digital check during labor increases the likelihood of introducing external bacteria. - **Caesarean Section**: Major abdominal surgery with a direct incision into a contaminated or colonized organ. - **Anemia and Malnutrition**: Compromise the systemic immune response and cellular repair mechanisms. - **Obstructed or Prolonged Labor**: Causes extensive tissue edema, ischemia, and necrosis.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment Findings

Early recognition of symptoms prevents progression to septic shock and multi-organ failure. The hallmark sign is a postpartum pyrexia—a temperature of 38 degrees Celsius or higher on any two of the first ten days following delivery, exclusive of the first 24 hours. Patients frequently report lower abdominal or pelvic pain, localized tenderness upon uterine palpation, and subinvolution. The lochia may transition from normal lochia rubra to foul-smelling, purulent, or profuse discharge. As systemic involvement worsens, tachycardia, chills, diaphoresis, and altered mental status emerge.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Investigations

Prompt laboratory and imaging investigations confirm the source and severity of infection, guiding targeted therapy. A complete blood count typically demonstrates marked leukocytosis with a shift to the left, indicating active infection. High vaginal swabs and endocervical cultures identify the specific causative pathogen and its antibiotic sensitivities, though broad-spectrum therapy must start before results return. Blood cultures are vital if systemic inflammatory response syndrome is suspected. Pelvic ultrasound scans help rule out retained products of conception or pelvic abscesses.

Medical and Antimicrobial Management

Rapid administration of parenteral broad-spectrum antibiotics forms the cornerstone of medical treatment. Initial regimens must cover gram-positive, gram-negative, and anaerobic organisms, typically combining agents like ampicillin, gentamicin, and metronidazole. Therapy should be reassessed and adjusted based on culture and sensitivity reports. If retained products of conception are confirmed or suspected, surgical evacuation via gentle curettage or manual vacuum aspiration is performed under antibiotic cover to remove the infected nidus.

Nursing Care and Supportive Management

Comprehensive nursing care stabilizes vital functions and promotes recovery. Monitor vital signs every four hours, paying close attention to temperature spikes, falling blood pressure, and rising pulse rates indicative of shock. Maintain accurate fluid balance charts with indwelling urinary catheterization to monitor renal perfusion and urine output. Administer prescribed intravenous fluids and antibiotics punctually. Encourage early ambulation when stable to prevent thromboembolic events, and provide emotional support to the mother, who may be separated from her newborn due to illness.

Potential Complications and Prevention Strategies

Uncontrolled puerperal sepsis can escalate to severe, life-threatening complications including peritonitis, pelvic abscess, septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and chronic pelvic inflammatory disease resulting in secondary infertility. Prevention focuses on quality antenatal care, screening and treating lower genital tract infections during pregnancy, maintaining strict aseptic technique during labor and delivery, minimizing unnecessary vaginal examinations, practicing active management of the third stage of labor, and administering prophylactic antibiotics for caesarean sections.
