Puerperal Sepsis

Introduction and Definition of Puerperal Sepsis

Puerperal sepsis, historically known as childbed fever, remains a significant global health concern and a major direct cause of maternal morbidity and mortality, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria. It is formally defined as an infection of the genital tract occurring at any time between the rupture of membranes or the onset of labor and the 42nd day postpartum, characterized by the presence of two or more specific clinical signs. These hallmark indicators include pelvic pain, fever (a temperature of 38.0°C or higher on any two of the first ten days postpartum, exclusive of the first 24 hours), abnormal vaginal discharge such as foul-smelling lochia, and a delay in the rate of reduction of the size of the uterus, known as subinvolution. For nursing students preparing for professional examinations, understanding this definition is vital because early identification directly impacts maternal survival rates. The infection typically begins at the placental site and can rapidly spread throughout the reproductive tract and beyond if not promptly recognized and treated. As future frontline healthcare providers in Nigeria, midwives and nurses must maintain high clinical suspicion during the puerperium, routinely assessing postpartum mothers for subtle changes in temperature, pulse, and uterine tenderness during routine hospital checks and community postpartum visits.

Pathophysiology and Common Causative Organisms

The pathophysiology of puerperal sepsis typically originates at the raw, vascular placental implantation site, which provides an ideal, warm, and nutrient-rich medium for bacterial proliferation. Following delivery, the normal physiological barriers of the acidic vaginal pH and intact mucous membranes are disrupted, facilitating the ascension of microorganisms from the lower genital tract into the uterine cavity, a condition known as endometritis. From this localized uterine lining infection, pathogens can spread via the lymphatic system, the bloodstream through thrombophlebitis, or directly through the fallopian tubes into the peritoneal cavity, potentially culminating in generalized peritonitis and septic shock. The spectrum of causative organisms is diverse, comprising both endogenous flora of the lower genital tract and exogenous pathogens introduced during labor and delivery. The most common bacterial culprits include aerobic and anaerobic streptococci (particularly Group A and Group B Streptococcus), Gram-negative bacilli such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella species, and Proteus species, as well as anaerobic bacteria like Bacteroides fragilis. Additionally, sexually transmitted pathogens like Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae can contribute. Recognizing these causative agents is crucial for selecting appropriate empirical antibiotic therapies in clinical practice, as polymicrobial infections are frequently encountered in postpartum sepsis cases.

Risk Factors and Predisposing Conditions

Identifying risk factors and predisposing conditions for puerperal sepsis is a core competency for obstetric nurses, as prevention and early intervention rely heavily on risk stratification. Factors that compromise maternal immune defenses, increase exposure to pathogens, or cause tissue trauma significantly elevate the likelihood of infection. Obstetric risk factors include prolonged rupture of membranes (typically exceeding 18 to 24 hours before delivery), multiple digital vaginal examinations during labor (especially after membrane rupture), cesarean delivery (which carries a substantially higher risk than vaginal birth), prolonged and obstructed labor, and manual removal of the placenta or retained placental products. Maternal systemic conditions also play a critical role; pre-existing anemia, malnutrition, obesity, diabetes mellitus, and immunosuppressive states impair the body's ability to combat invading microorganisms. Socioeconomic and environmental factors frequently encountered in Nigerian healthcare settings, such as late booking for antenatal care, home deliveries attended by unskilled birth attendants under unhygienic conditions, and postpartum anemia from hemorrhage, further compound these risks. By identifying these predisposing factors early during pregnancy and labor, nurses can advocate for prophylactic measures, such as timely antibiotic administration for prolonged membrane rupture, thereby mitigating the incidence of severe sepsis.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment Findings

The clinical presentation of puerperal sepsis varies depending on the severity of the infection and the specific anatomical structures involved, ranging from localized endometritis to fulminant septic shock. The nurse must conduct systematic assessments to detect early and progressive signs of infection. The earliest and most consistent manifestation is a pyrexia (temperature $\ge$ 38.0°C), frequently accompanied by tachycardia that is disproportionately high relative to the temperature elevation. Patients commonly report lower abdominal or pelvic pain and uterine tenderness upon palpation, along with subinvolution, where the uterus remains large, boggy, and soft instead of contracting firmly. Vaginal discharge is typically abnormal; lochia may be profuse, dark, purulent, and foul-smelling, although a lack of foul odor does not rule out infection, especially with Group A Streptococcus. As the infection ascends or spreads systemically, clinical findings may progress to chills, rigors, general malaise, headache, and severe anorexia. In advanced cases or septic shock, nurses must be vigilant for warning signs such as hypotension, tachypnea, oliguria or anuria, altered mental status (confusion, lethargy), and cool, clammy extremities. Early recognition of these signs during routine postpartum ward rounds enables prompt escalation of care and prevents multi-organ dysfunction.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Investigations

Diagnostic evaluation of suspected puerperal sepsis requires a combination of prompt clinical assessment and targeted laboratory and imaging investigations to identify the source and causative organism. The nurse plays a critical role in collecting specimens and preparing the patient for diagnostic procedures. Initial baseline laboratory tests include a complete blood count (CBC), which typically reveals a marked leukocytosis with a left shift (elevated immature neutrophils), though leukopenia can sometimes indicate overwhelming sepsis. Inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are significantly elevated and assist in monitoring treatment response. Crucially, microbiological investigations must be performed prior to initiating broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy; these include high vaginal swabs (HVS) or endocervical swabs for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity (MCS), and paired blood cultures drawn from two different sites to detect bacteremia. Urinalysis and urine culture should also be ordered to rule out or identify concurrent urinary tract infections. Imaging studies, such as pelvic ultrasonography, are valuable tools to identify retained products of conception (RPOC) or pelvic abscesses, while computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may be utilized to diagnose deep pelvic vein thrombophlebitis when conservative antibiotic therapy fails.

Medical and Antimicrobial Management

Medical and antimicrobial management of puerperal sepsis is a medical emergency aimed at eradicating infection, stabilizing vital signs, and preventing systemic complications. Treatment should be initiated immediately after collecting appropriate diagnostic cultures without waiting for laboratory results. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy is the cornerstone of management, designed to cover a wide range of Gram-positive, Gram-negative, and anaerobic organisms. A classic and widely utilized empirical regimen combines a broad-spectrum penicillin or cephalosporin (such as ampicillin or ceftriaxone) with an aminoglycoside (such as gentamicin) and an anaerobic agent (such as metronidazole). Nurses must administer these intravenous medications strictly according to scheduled times, monitor for adverse drug reactions, and adjust dosages based on renal function test results. In addition to antimicrobials, fluid resuscitation with crystalloids (such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate) is essential to maintain tissue perfusion and correct hypovolemia. If pelvic ultrasonography confirms the presence of retained products of conception, surgical evacuation of the uterus via vacuum aspiration or gentle curettage must be performed under antibiotic cover to remove the primary focus of infection. In severe cases unresponsive to medical therapy, surgical interventions such as hysterectomy may become life-saving measures to remove necrotic, infected uterine tissue.

Nursing Care and Supportive Management

Comprehensive nursing care and supportive management are vital components in the recovery of a patient with puerperal sepsis, requiring meticulous monitoring, physical comfort measures, and psychological support. The nurse must institute continuous or frequent vital sign monitoring—including temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation—to detect early signs of clinical deterioration or improvement. Accurate fluid balance charting is mandatory; indwelling urinary catheterization should be maintained to measure hourly urine output, ensuring adequate renal perfusion and guiding fluid resuscitation needs. Pain management is another critical responsibility, utilizing prescribed analgesics and non-pharmacological comfort measures to alleviate lower abdominal discomfort and uterine cramping. Nurses must also promote optimal hygiene, performing regular perineal care using antiseptic solutions, encouraging frequent pad changes, and teaching proper handwashing techniques to prevent cross-contamination. Nutritional support is essential for tissue repair and immune recovery; nurses should encourage a high-protein, vitamin-rich diet and adequate hydration once oral intake is tolerated. Furthermore, because prolonged illness and separation from the newborn can cause severe emotional distress and anxiety, nurses must provide empathetic psychological support, facilitate maternal-infant bonding when safe, and involve family members in supportive care.

Potential Complications and Prevention Strategies

Awareness of potential complications and the implementation of robust prevention strategies are essential for reducing the burden of puerperal sepsis in maternal healthcare. If left untreated or inadequately managed, puerperal sepsis can lead to severe, life-threatening complications including septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), acute kidney injury, pelvic abscesses, chronic pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), and secondary infertility. Thrombotic events such as septic pelvic thrombophlebitis can also occur, posing risks of pulmonary embolism. Prevention strategies must be applied across the continuum of care, spanning antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum periods. Antenatal care efforts include screening and treating anemia, urinary tract infections, and lower genital tract infections like bacterial vaginosis or group B streptococcus colonization. During labor and delivery, strict adherence to aseptic techniques, minimizing unnecessary vaginal examinations, practicing active management of the third stage of labor to reduce hemorrhage and retained tissue, and implementing evidence-based guidelines on antibiotic prophylaxis for cesarean deliveries are paramount. Postpartum prevention relies on maintaining high standards of hospital hygiene, educating mothers on perineal hygiene and warning signs of infection, and ensuring skilled attendance at all births, particularly within community settings across Nigeria.
