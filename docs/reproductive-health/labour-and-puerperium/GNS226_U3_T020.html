Congenital Anomalies

Introduction and Definition of Congenital Anomalies

A congenital anomaly, often referred to as a birth defect, is a structural or functional abnormality that develops before birth. These conditions are present at birth and can significantly impact a child's physical health, mental development, or overall survival. Some anomalies are immediately visible on the outside of the body, while others involve internal organs like the heart, kidneys, or brain and may not be discovered until later in life. In clinical practice, recognizing these conditions early allows healthcare providers to plan appropriate interventions and give families realistic guidance.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

The exact cause of many congenital anomalies remains unknown, but several environmental and genetic factors increase the risk. In Nigeria and similar resource-limited settings, specific challenges play a major role in their occurrence:

  • Maternal Nutritional Deficiencies: Inadequate intake of vital nutrients, particularly folic acid before and during early pregnancy, significantly raises the risk of neural tube defects like spina bifida.
  • Infections During Pregnancy: Infections such as malaria, rubella, and syphilis contracted by the pregnant mother can severely disrupt normal fetal organ development.
  • Herbal and Unregulated Drug Use: Self-medication and the use of unprescribed herbal concoctions during the first trimester can expose the developing fetus to teratogenic substances.
  • Consanguinity: Marriages between close blood relatives increase the chance of children inheriting recessive genetic disorders.

Classification: Structural versus Functional Anomalies

Medical professionals divide congenital anomalies into two broad categories based on how they affect the body.

Structural anomalies involve physical malformations or missing body parts. Examples include cleft lip, clubfoot, spina bifida, and major heart valve defects. These are usually detected through physical examination or imaging scans.

Functional anomalies involve metabolic, neurological, or sensory problems that affect how the body works. Examples include sickle cell disease, congenital hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis, and various metabolic disorders. A baby with a functional anomaly may look completely normal on the outside, but their internal chemical processes or nervous systems do not function correctly.

Common Structural Anomalies in Newborns

Midwives and nurses frequently encounter specific structural defects during routine newborn assessments. Cleft lip and palate involve incomplete fusion of facial structures, making feeding difficult and increasing the risk of aspiration. Neural tube defects, such as anencephaly and spina bifida, occur when the neural tube fails to close completely during the first month of pregnancy. Musculoskeletal anomalies like congenital talipes equinovarus, commonly known as clubfoot, cause the foot to twist out of shape or position. Gastrointestinal defects, including imperforate anus or abdominal wall defects like omphalocele, require urgent surgical evaluation soon after birth.

Antenatal and Neonatal Assessment of Congenital Defects

Detecting anomalies early improves outcomes for both mother and child. Antenatal assessment relies heavily on routine ultrasound scans during pregnancy, which can visualize fetal anatomy, amniotic fluid volume, and growth patterns. Maternal blood tests, such as alpha-fetoprotein screenings, also help identify increased risks for specific neural tube or chromosomal problems.

Neonatal assessment begins immediately at delivery with the Apgar score and a thorough head-to-toe physical examination. Midwives and nurses inspect the spine for dimples or tufts of hair, check the symmetry of limbs, verify the patency of the anus, and listen closely to heart sounds. Spotting subtle signs early prevents delayed diagnoses that could endanger the newborn.

Immediate Midwifery and Nursing Management at Birth

When a baby is born with a visible congenital anomaly, the immediate nursing priorities are maintaining a patent airway, supporting breathing, and regulating body temperature. For infants with open neural tube defects, the exposed lesion must be covered with a sterile, moist, non-adherent saline dressing to prevent infection and moisture loss, and the baby should be nursed in a prone or side-lying position to protect the sac. For facial clefts, specialized feeding techniques using adaptive bottles prevent choking. Above all, nurses must manage their own reactions and maintain a calm, reassuring demeanor to support anxious parents.

Surgical Interventions and Multidisciplinary Care

Many structural anomalies require surgical correction to improve function and quality of life. Surgeries range from emergency operations for bowel obstructions to staged procedures for complex heart defects or cleft lips and palates. Because congenital anomalies affect multiple body systems, care requires a collaborative team. Paediatricians, surgeons, nurses, physiotherapists, dietitians, and social workers must coordinate their efforts. Nurses act as a central communication link between the family and the rest of the healthcare team, ensuring that pre-operative preparations and post-operative recovery plans are followed smoothly.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

The long-term outlook for a child with a congenital anomaly depends heavily on the specific organ system involved, the severity of the defect, and how quickly medical treatment is received. Common complications include chronic infections, developmental delays, feeding difficulties, and lifelong physical disabilities. In resource-constrained settings, delayed presentation and limited access to pediatric surgical specialists can worsen outcomes. However, with early diagnosis, timely surgical repair, and consistent follow-up care, many children survive and grow up to live productive, fulfilling lives.

Psychological Support and Parental Counseling

Discovering that a newborn has a congenital anomaly often triggers profound emotional distress, including shock, grief, guilt, and anxiety in parents. Cultural beliefs and societal stigma surrounding birth defects in some communities can add to this burden, occasionally leading to maternal blame or rejection. Nurses play a crucial role by offering empathetic, non-judgmental listening and clear, honest information. Connecting parents with support groups or counselors helps them process their feelings, adapt to their new reality, and bond effectively with their baby.

Prevention Strategies and Public Health Education

Many congenital anomalies can be prevented through targeted public health measures and community education. Key prevention strategies include promoting periconceptional folic acid supplementation for all women of childbearing age, encouraging early and regular antenatal care, and ensuring access to vaccinations against teratogenic infections like rubella. Public health campaigns also focus on discouraging self-medication with herbal concoctions during pregnancy and raising awareness about the risks of consanguineous marriages. Nurses serve as frontline educators, delivering these vital messages during antenatal visits, immunisation clinics, and community outreach programs.
