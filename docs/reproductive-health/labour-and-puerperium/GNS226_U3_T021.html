Postnatal Follow-up

Introduction and Schedule of Routine Postnatal Visits

The postnatal period begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six weeks, a critical phase often referred to as the puerperium. During this timeframe, maternal physiological systems return to their pre-pregnant state while the newborn adapts to extrauterine life. Despite the high risk of maternal and neonatal morbidity during these early weeks, follow-up attendance in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria remains suboptimal due to geographical barriers, financial constraints, and cultural practices of seclusion. A structured schedule of routine visits ensures timely identification of complications and provides continuous support for infant feeding and maternal recovery. - **First 24 hours**: Immediate postpartum observation in the delivery facility for hemorrhage, vital sign stability, and initiation of breastfeeding. - **Days 2 to 3**: Early postnatal contact assessing for uterine contraction, lochia, breastfeeding latch, and neonatal jaundice. - **Day 7 to 14**: Second contact focusing on perineal wound healing, psychological adjustment, and exclusion of early sepsis. - **Week 6**: Final routine comprehensive evaluation confirming complete physiological involution, reviewing contraception, and updating immunization schedules.

Physiological Recovery and Maternal Assessment

Evaluating maternal recovery requires a systematic head-to-toe examination. The most critical physical marker is **uterine involution**, monitored by palpating the fundus to ensure it descends by approximately one centimeter per day, becoming a pelvic organ by the tenth day. Clinicians must observe **lochia** progression from rubra (bright red) to serosa (pinkish brown) and finally alba (yellow-white), noting any foul odor or heavy clots that suggest subinvolution or retained products of conception. Perineal tears or episiotomy sites demand daily inspection for hematoma formation, excessive edema, and signs of infection. Bladder function must be verified, as postpartum urinary retention and trauma from prolonged labor increase the risk of urinary tract infections. Vital signs should normalize rapidly, though mild transient bradycardia is common. Persistent tachycardia or low-grade fever frequently serves as the earliest warning sign of impending postpartum sepsis.

Newborn Evaluation and Neonatal Care

The postnatal visit offers an essential window to evaluate newborn adaptation and growth. Assessment begins with confirming established feeding patterns, ensuring the infant latches correctly and voids clear urine regularly, which serves as a reliable proxy for adequate hydration. The umbilical cord stump must be checked for signs of omphalitis, characterized by periumbilical redness, foul discharge, or induration. Jaundice appearing within the first twenty-four hours indicates pathological processes like hemolytic disease, whereas physiological jaundice typically peaks around the third to fifth day. Weight checks are mandatory; an initial weight loss of up to ten percent is normal, but failure to regain birth weight by two weeks warrants lactation support and evaluation for failure to thrive. Finally, verification of initial immunizations, such as BCG and Oral Polio Vaccine zero, completes the neonatal check.

Identification and Management of Postnatal Complications

Postnatal complications can escalate rapidly and claim maternal lives if overlooked. Postpartum hemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal mortality, requiring vigilance even after discharge if late bleeding occurs. Postpartum preeclampsia can manifest up to six weeks post-delivery, presenting with new-onset hypertension, severe headaches, and visual disturbances. Infection control is equally paramount. Endometritis presents with uterine tenderness, lower abdominal pain, and foul-smelling lochia, while mastitis manifests as localized breast erythema, pain, and high fever, often progressing from plugged milk ducts or nipple fissures. Nurses must actively screen for these pathology markers at every clinical contact and educate families on warning signs that demand immediate health facility re-admission.

Mental Health Screening and Postpartum Depression

Psychological adjustment following childbirth varies widely, yet emotional health is frequently neglected in busy clinical environments. Transient **baby blues** affect many women within the first week, characterized by tearfulness, mood swings, and anxiety, resolving spontaneously without medical therapy. When these symptoms persist, deepen, or interfere with daily functioning, they transition into **postpartum depression**. Screening tools, such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, should be routinely integrated into the six-week visit. Left unrecognized, severe cases can escalate into **postpartum psychosis**, a psychiatric emergency marked by hallucinations, delusions, and risk of harm to the infant. Compassionate listening, destigmatizing emotional struggles, and involving supportive family members form the cornerstone of early nursing intervention.

Family Planning and Contraceptive Counseling

Resumption of sexual activity can occur before the traditional six-week postpartum check, making proactive contraceptive counseling an essential component of early postnatal care. Lactational Amenorrhea Method offers effective temporary protection exclusively for exclusively breastfeeding mothers whose menses have not returned and whose infants are under six months old. For ongoing spacing, a range of options must be discussed objectively based on maternal medical history and future reproductive desires: - **Progestin-only methods**: Pills, injectables, or implants that do not interfere with milk supply and can be initiated immediately postpartum. - **Combined hormonal contraceptives**: Estrogen-containing pills or rings that are typically delayed for at least three weeks postpartum due to venous thromboembolism risks. - **Intrauterine devices**: Can be inserted immediately post-placental delivery or deferred until four to six weeks postpartum to reduce expulsion rates.

Nutrition, Self-Care, and Health Education

Recovery from childbirth places high metabolic demands on the maternal body, particularly for women who are breastfeeding. Nutritional counseling must emphasize increased caloric intake with high-quality proteins, iron-rich foods to counter postpartum anemia, and adequate hydration. Micronutrient supplementation, especially iron and folate, should continue for at least six to twelve weeks post-delivery. Beyond diet, education must cover pelvic floor muscle exercises to prevent long-term urinary incontinence, safe resumption of physical activity, and adequate rest strategies amid fragmented infant sleep schedules. Addressing cultural myths surrounding postpartum food restrictions or infant care practices with empathy ensures that health guidance is culturally resonant and practically adopted.
