Postnatal Follow-up

Introduction to Postnatal Care and Follow-up

The postnatal period, or puerperium, begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six weeks. This phase is a critical transition for both mother and newborn, marked by rapid physical, psychological, and social adjustments. Despite the physiological completion of childbirth, maternal mortality and morbidity remain high during these early weeks, making structured follow-up essential.

Postnatal care aims to monitor recovery from birth, support lactation, facilitate newborn adaptation, and detect complications early. In many low-resource settings, traditional practices often isolate new mothers or discourage early reporting of danger signs. Effective follow-up bridges the gap between hospital discharge and community life, ensuring that mothers receive continuous guidance, emotional reassurance, and timely clinical intervention.

Physiological Changes and Puerperal Recovery

Following delivery, the maternal body undergoes profound involution. The uterus must contract firmly to close open blood vessels at the placental site, a process aided by oxytocin release during breastfeeding. This reduction in uterine size is accompanied by changes in vaginal discharge, known as lochia, which transitions from bright red (rubra) to pinkish-brown (serosa), and finally to yellowish-white (alba).

Simultaneously, the cardiovascular system readjusts as excess pregnancy blood volume is eliminated through diuresis and diaphoresis. Hormonal shifts trigger a drop in estrogen and progesterone, facilitating lactogenesis. Bladder tone may be temporarily reduced due to pelvic floor trauma or regional anesthesia, increasing the risk of urinary retention and infection.

Schedule of Routine Postnatal Visits

A well-defined schedule ensures that both mother and baby receive timely evaluations without placing an undue burden on healthcare infrastructure.

  • First Contact: Within 24 hours of birth, whether in the facility or during a home visit.
  • Second Visit: Between days 4 and 7 following delivery to assess breastfeeding and check for jaundice.
  • Third Visit: At six weeks postpartum for a comprehensive physical, psychological, and family planning review.

Additional visits are scheduled immediately if complications arise. Community health workers play a vital role between these formal clinical appointments by conducting home visits and reinforcing warning signs.

Comprehensive Maternal Assessment

A systematic clinical assessment of the mother evaluates multiple body systems to ensure normal recovery. Vital signs are measured first to rule out hidden infection or hemorrhage. Abdominal palpation confirms that the uterus is firm and decreasing in size appropriately.

Perineal inspection is essential for women who experienced tears or an episiotomy; nurses look for excessive swelling, hematoma formation, or signs of wound dehiscence and infection. Breast examination checks for nipple integrity, engorgement, and blocked ducts. Nurses must also assess urinary and bowel function, confirming that the client has voided adequately and passed gas or stool.

Newborn Evaluation and Neonatal Care

Neonatal assessment focuses on transitional adaptation to extrauterine life. The newborn is evaluated for active breathing, adequate color, and normal heart rate. Cord care is reviewed, emphasizing dry cord care to prevent infection. Temperature regulation is vital, as newborns easily develop hypothermia; skin-to-skin contact is encouraged to stabilize infant temperature and blood sugar.

Feeding assessment is integrated into every interaction. Exclusive breastfeeding on demand is promoted, checking for proper latch and swallowing sounds. The nurse also screens the infant for physiological jaundice, checking the skin and sclera, and verifies that newborn immunizations and metabolic screenings are up to date.

Identification and Management of Postnatal Complications

Nurses must educate mothers and families on key danger signs that require immediate medical attention.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage: Heavy vaginal bleeding that soaks more than one pad per hour or contains large clots.
  • Puerperal Sepsis: Persistent fever, chills, and foul-smelling lochia indicating uterine infection.
  • Pre-eclampsia: Severe headaches, blurred vision, and high blood pressure appearing after childbirth.
  • Thromboembolism: Unilateral calf pain, swelling, and redness in the lower extremities.

Prompt reporting and rapid clinical escalation save lives, as many postpartum complications can deteriorate swiftly if ignored.

Mental Health Screening and Postpartum Depression

Emotional vulnerability is common following childbirth due to hormonal drops, sleep deprivation, and lifestyle adjustments. While mild mood swings, known as the "baby blues," resolve spontaneously within two weeks, persistent depressive symptoms require professional support.

Nurses should screen mothers using validated tools like the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale during routine visits. Symptoms of postpartum depression include overwhelming sadness, loss of interest in activities, inability to bond with the infant, and intrusive thoughts of harm. Creating a safe, non-judgmental space encourages mothers to express emotional struggles without fear of stigma.

Family Planning and Contraceptive Counseling

Postnatal family planning is crucial for maternal health, allowing the body adequate time to recover before subsequent pregnancies. Ovulation can resume before the return of menstruation, meaning women can become pregnant even while exclusively breastfeeding if lactational amenorrhea criteria are not strictly met.

Counseling should begin antenatally and be reinforced during postnatal checks. Options include progestin-only pills, injectables, implants, intrauterine devices, and barrier methods. The choice of contraceptive must respect the mother's reproductive goals, health history, and breastfeeding status, ensuring safe and effective birth spacing.

Nutrition, Self-Care, and Health Education

Recovery requires adequate nourishment and rest. Lactating mothers have increased caloric and fluid demands to support milk production and tissue healing. A diet rich in protein, iron, calcium, and fresh produce helps prevent postpartum anemia and fatigue.

Self-care education encompasses pelvic floor exercises to strengthen muscles weakened by delivery, adequate rest by sleeping when the baby sleeps, and maintaining personal hygiene. Nurses should actively involve partners and family members in health education to build a supportive home environment that prioritizes maternal well-being.
