Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Normal labour, also known as parturition, is the physiological process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. To be classified as normal, or spontaneous vertex labour, several criteria must be met:

  • Onset: Spontaneous in nature, without artificial induction or stimulation.
  • Gestational Age: Occurs at term, typically defined as between 37 and 42 completed weeks of pregnancy.
  • Presentation: The fetus presents by the vertex (cephalic presentation).
  • Route: Vaginal delivery without major surgical intervention such as a caesarean section.
  • Duration: Completed within an acceptable timeframe without prolonged phases.
  • Outcome: Results in the birth of a live, healthy infant with no undue complications for the mother or baby.

Understanding this baseline definition helps student nurses distinguish physiological progression from pathological deviations that require medical intervention in busy Nigerian maternity wards.

Prerequisites and Signs of the Onset of Labour

Before true labour begins, a woman may experience false labour or prodromal signs that indicate her body is preparing. Braxton Hicks contractions are irregular, painless uterine tightening that do not cause cervical dilation.

True labour onset is signaled by specific physiological changes:

  1. True Contractions: Regular uterine contractions that progressively increase in frequency, duration, and intensity, eventually causing progressive cervical effacement and dilation.
  2. Show: The expulsion of the mucus plug mixed with a small amount of blood from the endocervical glands as the cervix begins to soften and open.
  3. Rupture of Membranes: Spontaneous rupture of the amniotic sac, releasing amniotic fluid, which may present as a sudden gush or a continuous trickle.

Exam pitfall: Do not confuse Braxton Hicks contractions with true labour; true contractions intensify with walking, whereas false contractions often diminish with rest.

The Four Stages of Labour

Labour progresses through four distinct anatomical and physiological stages, each with specific clinical management goals.

StageDescriptionKey Milestone
First StageOnset of true contractions to full cervical dilation (10 cm)Complete cervical dilation
Second StageFull dilation to the birth of the infantDelivery of the baby
Third StageDelivery of the infant to the expulsion of the placentaDelivery of the placenta
Fourth StageFirst one to four hours following placental deliveryMaternal stabilization

The first stage is further divided into a latent phase (gradual dilation up to 4 cm) and an active phase (rapid dilation from 5 cm to 10 cm). The fourth stage is a critical monitoring period for postpartum hemorrhage.

Physiological Mechanisms of Labour

As the fetus navigates the maternal pelvis, it performs a series of passive movements known as the cardinal movements of labour. These adjustments allow the irregularly shaped fetal head to pass through the varying diameters of the pelvis.

The cardinal movements occur in a sequential, overlapping pattern:

  1. Engagement: The widest diameter of the fetal presenting part passes down into the pelvic inlet.
  2. Descent: The downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal.
  3. Flexion: The fetal chin is brought into close contact with the fetal chest, reducing the head diameter.
  4. Internal Rotation: The head rotates, typically so the occiput moves anteriorly toward the maternal symphysis pubis.
  5. Extension: The head emerges from the vagina as the occiput passes beneath the pubic symphysis.
  6. Restitution and External Rotation: The head aligns itself back with the shoulders, and the shoulders rotate internally.
  7. Expulsion: The anterior shoulder delivers, followed quickly by the posterior shoulder and the rest of the body.

Initial Assessment and Admission of the Woman in Labour

When a pregnant woman arrives at the labor ward, a prompt, systematic assessment establishes baseline maternal and fetal well-being. Greet the woman with empathy, acknowledging her anxiety and pain.

Review her antenatal card for risk factors, estimated gestational age, blood group, genotype, and screening results for HIV and hepatitis. Perform an abdominal palpation using Leopold's maneuvers to determine fetal lie, presentation, position, and engagement. Assess uterine contractions for frequency, duration, and strength. Auscultate the fetal heart rate using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler ultrasound. Finally, conduct a vaginal examination, only after assessing for bleeding, to evaluate cervical dilation, effacement, membrane status, and station of the presenting part.

Nursing Management and Monitoring During Labour

Continuous, meticulous monitoring safeguards both mother and fetus during the active phase of labour. The primary tool for this task is the partograph, a graphical record of the progress of labour and maternal-fetal condition.

Key parameters recorded on the partograph include:

  • Fetal Heart Rate: Monitored and charted every 30 minutes.
  • Cervical Dilation: Assessed and plotted every 4 hours during the active phase.
  • Contractions: Frequency and duration checked hourly.
  • Maternal Vital Signs: Blood pressure monitored every 4 hours; pulse and temperature recorded more frequently if membranes rupture.
  • Urine Output: Assessed for volume, protein, and acetone.

The partograph features an alert line and an action line. If cervical dilation crosses to the right of the alert line, caution is required; if it crosses the action line, active medical or surgical intervention must be initiated.

Pain Relief and Comfort Measures

Labour pain is intense and multifaceted, influenced by physical, emotional, and cultural factors. Providing effective relief preserves maternal coping mechanisms and prevents exhaustion.

Non-pharmacological comfort measures should be offered first. Continuous emotional support and reassurance from a birth companion or nurse significantly reduce anxiety. Other non-pharmacological techniques include lower back massage, warm baths or showers, controlled breathing exercises, position changes, and hydrotherapy.

When non-pharmacological methods prove insufficient, pharmacological options may be administered. Pethidine or tramadol provides systemic analgesia, though care must be taken regarding neonatal respiratory depression if delivery occurs too soon after administration. Regional anesthesia, such as an epidural, offers effective pain relief while keeping the mother awake, though availability varies across healthcare facilities in Nigeria.

Conducting a Normal Delivery and Immediate Care of the Newborn

As the woman enters the second stage, she experiences an involuntary urge to push. Position her comfortably, ideally in a semi-recumbent or lateral position. As the fetal head crowns, protect the perineum with a sterile towel and control head extension to prevent severe perineal tearing.

Once the head delivers, check gently around the neck for the umbilical cord. Allow restitution to occur, then deliver the anterior shoulder followed by the posterior shoulder with gentle downward and upward traction.

Immediate care of the newborn focuses on establishing respirations and preventing hypothermia. Place the baby skin-to-skin on the mother's chest, dry the infant thoroughly, clear the airway if secretions obstruct breathing, and assess the Apgar score at 1 and 5 minutes post-birth. Administer prophylactic eye drops and vitamin K to complete immediate care.
