Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Normal labour, also known as parturition, is defined as the physiological process by which a viable fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. For a labour to be classified as normal or eutocia, it must meet specific criteria established by the World Health Organization and standard obstetric guidelines. These criteria include spontaneous onset at term, typically between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation, with a single fetus presenting by the vertex. The process must progress naturally without artificial induction, augmentation, or undue prolongation, and it should culminate in a vaginal delivery without complications for either the mother or the newborn. Understanding this definition is fundamental for nursing students in Nigeria, as it establishes the baseline for distinguishing physiological childbirth from pathological conditions like dystocia or preterm labour, thereby guiding timely and appropriate clinical interventions in maternity units across diverse healthcare settings. Nurses must be well-versed in recognizing normal patterns of uterine activity and cervical change to provide competent care during this critical transition.

Prerequisites and Signs of the Onset of Labour

Before labour officially begins, several physiological prerequisites must be met to ensure the body is ready for childbirth. These include an adequate pelvic architecture, a term fetus in a favorable longitudinal lie and vertex presentation, and sufficient uterine muscle preparedness. The actual onset of labour is signaled by distinct clinical indicators that every nursing student must recognize. The primary sign is the presence of true labour contractions, which are regular, progressive in frequency, intensity, and duration, and accompanied by progressive effacement and dilation of the cervix. Unlike false labour (Braxton Hicks contractions), true contractions do not subside with rest or hydration and are typically felt starting in the lower back and radiating to the abdomen. Other premonitory or warning signs that may precede true labour by days or hours include 'lightening' (engengagement of the fetal head into the pelvic brim, relieving diaphragmatic pressure), 'show' (expulsion of the plug of mucus tinged with blood from the cervical canal), and occasionally spontaneous rupture of the membranes (forewaters breaking). Recognizing these signs allows midwives and nurses to accurately determine whether a woman is in true labour and needs hospital admission.

The Four Stages of Labour

Labour is clinically divided into four distinct stages, each characterized by specific physiological events and nursing care priorities. The first stage spans from the onset of true labour contractions to full cervical dilation (10 cm). It is subdivided into the latent phase (gradual dilation up to about 4 cm) and the active phase (rapid dilation from 4 cm to 10 cm). The second stage begins at full cervical dilation and concludes with the complete birth of the baby. During this stage, maternal pushing efforts synergize with uterine contractions to expel the fetus through the birth canal. The third stage starts immediately after the birth of the infant and ends with the delivery of the placenta and fetal membranes. This stage carries a significant risk of postpartum hemorrhage, requiring vigilant observation. Finally, the fourth stage encompasses the first one to two hours immediately following placental delivery. This is a critical recovery period where the mother's vital signs stabilize, uterine retraction is monitored to prevent hemorrhage, and the golden hour of maternal-infant bonding and early initiation of breastfeeding takes place. Mastery of these four stages enables nursing students to anticipate complications and provide targeted, stage-specific intrapartum care.

Physiological Mechanisms of Labour

The physiological mechanisms of labour, often referred to as the cardinal movements, describe how the fetus adapts its position and attitude to successfully navigate the maternal bony pelvis during the second stage. These movements occur concurrently rather than in absolute isolation, though they are taught sequentially. First is engagement, where the widest diameter of the fetal presenting part (usually the biparietal diameter) passes through the pelvic inlet. As contractions continue, descent occurs, driven by uterine pressure, abdominal muscles, and amniotic fluid pressure. Flexion follows, where the fetal chin is brought into direct contact with the fetal thorax, presenting the smallest suboccipitobregmatic diameter to the pelvis. Internal rotation then occurs, usually turning the occiput anteriorly towards the symphysis pubis so the head aligns with the widest anteroposterior diameter of the pelvic outlet. Following birth of the head by extension, restitution takes place, where the head untwists to align with the shoulders. Internal rotation of the shoulders causes external rotation of the head, and finally, lateral flexion allows the anterior and posterior shoulders to deliver under the pubic arch. A clear understanding of these mechanisms is essential for nursing and midwifery students to identify malpositions and assist safely during vaginal deliveries.

Initial Assessment and Admission of the Woman in Labour

The initial assessment and admission of a woman presenting in labour is a critical gateway procedure in obstetric nursing. When a pregnant woman arrives at the health facility, the nurse must warmly receive her and establish a therapeutic, reassuring rapport to alleviate anxiety. The admission process begins with a comprehensive history taking, including parity, gestational age, onset and frequency of contractions, state of the membranes (whether liquor is leaking and its color), and presence of vaginal bleeding. Baseline vital signs—blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiration—must be recorded immediately to screen for pre-eclampsia, infection, or hemorrhage. A systematic physical examination follows, including abdominal palpation using Leopold's manoeuvres to determine fetal lie, presentation, position, and engagement, alongside auscultation of the fetal heart rate to ensure fetal wellbeing. An external or internal vaginal examination is performed (with informed consent and asepsis) to assess cervical effacement, dilation, station, and membrane status, provided there is no bright red bleeding suggestive of placenta previa. Urinalysis is checked for protein and glucose. Finally, the nurse documents all findings accurately on the partograph, which serves as the foundational graphical record for monitoring the progress of labour and maternal-fetal condition.

Nursing Management and Monitoring During Labour

Continuous, diligent nursing management and monitoring are vital to ensure a safe outcome for both mother and baby throughout labour. The nurse utilizes the partograph to record vital parameters at prescribed intervals. Maternal pulse and blood pressure are monitored every 4 hours (or more frequently if abnormal), temperature every 4 hours, and urine output and constituents are checked regularly. Fetal heart rate (FHR) auscultation using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler ultrasound is performed every 30 minutes in the active first stage and every 5 minutes during the second stage, particularly immediately after a contraction, to detect early signs of fetal distress like bradycardia or late decelerations. Uterine contractions are assessed for frequency, duration, and strength every 30 minutes. Vaginal examinations are typically repeated every 4 hours to track cervical dilation and descent of the presenting part, minimizing frequency to reduce infection risk. The nurse also encourages optimal positioning, mobility, bladder emptying every 2 hours, and adequate hydration. Effective communication and continuous emotional support help reduce maternal stress, which positively influences uterine function. Any deviation from normal parameters must be promptly communicated to the attending physician or senior midwife.

Pain Relief and Comfort Measures

Pain during labour is intense, complex, and influenced by physiological, psychological, and cultural factors. Providing adequate pain relief and comfort measures is a core responsibility of the nurse, promoting a more positive childbirth experience. Non-pharmacological comfort measures should be offered first and continued throughout labour; these include continuous emotional support (doula or continuous nurse presence), massage, back rubs, hydrotherapy (warm showers or baths if membranes are intact), breathing and relaxation techniques, position changes, and the use of birth balls. These methods empower the woman, reduce anxiety, and can modulate pain perception without adverse neonatal effects. When non-pharmacological methods are insufficient, pharmacological options may be administered based on availability and medical orders. Systemic analgesics such as opioids (e.g., pethidine or tramadol) can be given during the first stage, though nurses must monitor for maternal respiratory depression and neonatal sedation. Regional anesthesia, such as epidural or spinal analgesia, provides excellent pain relief but requires specialized monitoring of maternal blood pressure and bladder function. The nurse must balance effective pain control with the safety of both mother and fetus.

Conducting a Normal Delivery and Immediate Care of the Newborn

Conducting a normal delivery requires precision, aseptic technique, and calm confidence. As the second stage concludes, the nurse prepares the delivery tray, personal protective equipment, and resuscitation equipment. The mother is positioned comfortably (e.g., in lithotomy or lateral position). As the fetal head crowns and distends the perineum, controlled delivery of the head is facilitated by applying gentle downward pressure to prevent rapid expulsion and perineal tearing. Once the head is delivered, the nurse checks around the neck for a nuchal cord; if present, it is gently slipped over the head or clamped and cut if tight. The shoulders are delivered sequentially by gentle downward traction for the anterior shoulder and upward traction for the posterior shoulder. Immediate care of the newborn then begins: the baby is received in a sterile, warm towel, placed on the mother's abdomen or chest for skin-to-skin contact, and dried thoroughly while assessing breathing and crying. The Apgar score is assessed at 1 and 5 minutes. Active management of the third stage is then initiated with prophylactic uterotonics (such as oxytocin) to facilitate placental separation and minimize postpartum hemorrhage, followed by controlled cord traction and examination of the placenta for completeness.
