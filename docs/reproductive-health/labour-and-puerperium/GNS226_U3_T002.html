Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Normal labour, often termed eutocia, is the physiological process by which a viable fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. For labour to be classified as normal, it must meet several strict clinical criteria. It must be spontaneous in onset, starting without medical or surgical induction. The presentation must be cephalic, meaning the baby's head comes first. It must occur at term, which is defined as completed weeks of pregnancy from 37 up to 42 weeks. Throughout the process, the progress must remain normal without undue prolongation. Finally, it must culminate in the birth of a healthy baby with a good Apgar score, and there must be no major complications for either the mother or the newborn.

Prerequisites and Signs of the Onset of Labour

Before true labour begins, a pregnant woman may experience premonitory signs that indicate her body is preparing for birth. These signs include lightening, where the fetal head descends into the pelvic brim, often making breathing easier for the mother but increasing pelvic pressure. Braxton Hicks contractions become more frequent and noticeable. The cervix undergoes ripening, becoming softer and partially effaced.

True labour is distinguished from false labour by specific criteria:

  • Contractions: True labour contractions become regular, increase in frequency and intensity, and are not relieved by walking or resting.
  • Cervical change: True labour causes progressive effacement and dilation of the cervix.
  • Show: The expulsion of the mucus plug mixed with blood, known as the 'show', indicates cervical dilation.
  • Membranes: Rupture of the amniotic sac may occur, releasing amniotic fluid.

Initial Assessment and Admission of the Woman in Labour

When a woman arrives at the maternity unit in suspected labour, a prompt and welcoming initial assessment establishes her baseline health status and that of her unborn baby. The nurse reviews her antenatal card to check laboratory results, estimated due date, and any risk factors identified during pregnancy.

Physical and clinical evaluations include:

  1. Vital signs: Measure blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiration.
  2. Abdominal examination: Perform Leopold's maneuvers to determine fetal lie, presentation, position, and engagement.
  3. Fetal heart rate: Auscultate using a Pinard stethoscope or a Doppler ultrasound device.
  4. Vaginal examination: Assess cervical dilation, effacement, membrane status, and fetal station, provided there is no bright red bleeding suggesting placenta praevia.

The Four Stages of Labour

Labour progresses through four distinct anatomical and clinical phases, each with specific care goals.

  • First Stage: Lasts from the onset of true labour contractions until the cervix is fully dilated at 10 centimeters. It is subdivided into the latent phase (slow dilation up to 4 cm) and the active phase (rapid dilation from 5 cm to 10 cm).
  • Second Stage: Extends from full cervical dilation to the complete birth of the baby. During this stage, maternal pushing efforts assist uterine contractions.
  • Third Stage: Begins immediately after the baby is born and lasts until the complete expulsion of the placenta and fetal membranes.
  • Fourth Stage: Covers the first one to two hours after placental delivery, a critical window where postpartum hemorrhage monitoring is vital.

Physiological Mechanisms of Labour

As the fetus travels down the birth canal, it performs a series of passive movements known as the cardinal movements of labour. These adjustments allow the irregularly shaped fetal head to navigate the varying diameters of the maternal pelvis.

  • Engagement: The widest part of the fetal head enters the pelvic inlet.
  • Descent: The downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal.
  • Flexion: The baby's chin tucks tightly against its chest to present the smallest head diameter.
  • Internal Rotation: The head rotates so the occiput moves toward the front of the pelvis.
  • Extension: The head passes under the pubic bone and extends outward into the world.
  • Restitution and External Rotation: The head aligns back with the shoulders, which then rotate internally.
  • Expulsion: The anterior shoulder is born, followed quickly by the posterior shoulder and the rest of the body.

Nursing Management and Monitoring During Labour

Continuous observation safeguards both mother and baby throughout the active phase of labour. The partograph is a graphical tool used globally to record cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, contraction frequency, and maternal vital signs over time. Nurses plot these observations meticulously to spot early signs of prolonged labour or fetal distress.

Supportive care includes encouraging regular bladder emptying every two hours to prevent obstructed descent, offering sips of clear fluids or ice chips, and promoting optimal maternal positions like left lateral or upright postures. Continuous communication reassures the mother, lowers anxiety levels, and fosters a trusting therapeutic relationship.

Pain Relief and Comfort Measures

Labour pain is complex, influenced by physical, psychological, and cultural factors. Non-pharmacological comfort measures should be offered first and continued throughout labour. These include back rubs, hydrotherapy in a warm shower or bath, continuous emotional support, and breathing exercises. Changing positions frequently helps manage discomfort and encourages fetal rotation.

When non-pharmacological methods are insufficient, pharmacological options can be provided. Analgesics such as opioids can take the edge off pain, though large doses close to birth may cause neonatal respiratory depression. Regional anesthesia, particularly epidural block, offers excellent pain relief when managed by trained anesthetists while monitoring maternal blood pressure closely for potential hypotension.

Conducting a Normal Delivery and Immediate Care of the Newborn

When the second stage reaches completion and the perineum bulges with the crowning of the fetal head, preparation for delivery begins. The nurse or midwife supports the perineum and controls the speed of head birth to prevent severe lacerations. Once the head is born, check for a nuchal cord around the neck before allowing external rotation and the birth of the shoulders.

Immediate care of the newborn focuses on the transition to extrauterine life:

  • Clear airway: Gently wipe the nose and mouth if secretions obstruct breathing.
  • Thermal care: Dry the baby thoroughly with warm towels and place skin-to-skin on the mother's chest.
  • Assessment: Evaluate heart rate, respiratory effort, and tone using the Apgar scoring system at 1 and 5 minutes after birth.
  • Identification: Apply identification bands securely to the newborn.
