Prevention of HIV Infection

Foundations of HIV Prevention

Preventing human immunodeficiency virus acquisition requires a multi-layered approach combining behavioral, biomedical, and structural strategies. Because the virus primarily spreads through specific body fluids—blood, semen, vaginal secretions, and breast milk—interventions target the specific modes of transmission: sexual contact, parenteral exposure, and vertical transmission. In Nigerian clinical practice, public health initiatives focus heavily on breaking the transmission cycle at community and facility levels. Nursing professionals stand at the forefront of these efforts, translating complex epidemiological data into actionable health education for diverse patient populations.

The ABCD Approach to Behavioral Prevention

Behavioral interventions remain a cornerstone of public health programming, often summarized through the classic ABCD framework:

  • Abstinence: Delaying sexual debut among youths or choosing not to engage in sexual intercourse.
  • Be faithful: Maintaining a mutually faithful relationship with an uninfected, single partner.
  • Condom use: Consistent and correct use of male or female latex barriers during every sexual encounter.
  • Drugs and Death: Avoiding recreational substance abuse that impairs judgment and safe decision-making, alongside prompt management of sexually transmitted infections that increase mucosal vulnerability.

Biomedical Interventions: PrEP, PEP, and Treatment as Prevention

Biomedical science has transformed prevention by utilizing antiretroviral drugs to stop viral replication before or immediately after exposure.

InterventionTimingPrimary Purpose
PrEP (Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis)Taken daily before potential exposurePrevents uninfected individuals from acquiring the virus
PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis)Initiated within 72 hours after exposureStops viral establishment following a high-risk event
TasP (Treatment as Prevention)Continuous treatment for diagnosed personsSuppresses viral load to undetectable levels, eliminating sexual transmission

Achieving an undetectable viral load through consistent antiretroviral therapy effectively halts transmission to sexual partners, a concept validated by large-scale clinical trials.

Universal Precautions and Occupational Safety

Healthcare settings present distinct risks for parenteral transmission through needle-stick injuries, sharp instrument cuts, and mucous membrane splashes. Universal precautions—treating every patient's blood and body fluids as potentially infectious—protect clinical staff and patients alike.

Key protocols include:

  1. Hand hygiene: Washing hands before and after every patient contact and immediately after removing gloves.
  2. Personal protective equipment: Donning gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection when splashes or sprays of body fluids are anticipated.
  3. Safe injection practices: Never recapping used needles using two hands, and disposing of sharps immediately into puncture-resistant safety boxes.
  4. Environmental controls: Decontaminating surfaces with appropriate disinfectant solutions following spills.

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission

Without intervention, the rate of vertical HIV transmission from an infected mother to her child during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding ranges from 15% to 45%. Comprehensive PMTCT programs drastically reduce this risk to below 5%.

Essential interventions span the entire perinatal continuum:

  • Routine voluntary counseling and testing for pregnant women during antenatal care visits.
  • Immediate initiation of lifelong antiretroviral therapy for all diagnosed pregnant and lactating mothers.
  • Administration of prophylactic antiretroviral syrups to the newborn infant for the first weeks of life.
  • Infant feeding counseling that supports safe practices, whether through exclusive replacement feeding where acceptable and feasible, or exclusive breastfeeding combined with maternal antiretroviral therapy.

Community Outreach and the Nurse's Advocacy Role

Clinical interventions alone cannot eradicate HIV transmission without addressing the social drivers of the epidemic, including stigma, discrimination, and gender inequality. Nurses serve as educators, counselors, and advocates within community settings. By facilitating peer support groups, demystifying transmission myths, and promoting confidential testing, nurses help normalize health-seeking behaviors. In resource-constrained settings, nurse-led community testing drives early diagnosis, linking vulnerable populations to care before immune deterioration occurs.
