Prevention of HIV Infection

Introduction to HIV Prevention

Preventing the transmission of the Human Immunodeficiency Virus remains a central pillar of global public health nursing. HIV attacks the immune system, specifically destroying CD4 cells, which weakens the body's ability to fight off infections and diseases. Without proper intervention, the virus leads to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome. Because there is currently no absolute cure, stopping new infections through targeted education, behavioral changes, and biomedical tools is our best defense. Nurses encounter patients across diverse community and hospital settings, making every patient interaction a crucial opportunity for risk assessment, counseling, and harm reduction.

The ABCD Strategy of HIV Prevention

The classic ABCD strategy provides a simple yet comprehensive framework for individual behavior change and risk reduction.

  • Abstinence: Choosing not to engage in sexual intercourse is the only 100% effective method to prevent sexually transmitted HIV.
  • Be faithful: Maintaining a mutually faithful, uninfected sexual relationship with a single partner eliminates the risk of acquiring the virus from outside sources.
  • Condom use: Consistent and correct use of male and female barrier methods during every sexual act significantly lowers the risk of fluid exchange.
  • Drugs and devices: Avoiding illicit intravenous drug use and sharing needles, alongside utilizing modern biomedical devices like PrEP, adds an extra layer of defense.

Biomedical Interventions: PrEP and ART as Prevention

Modern pharmacology has shifted HIV prevention from purely behavioral methods to powerful biomedical solutions. Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis involves HIV-negative individuals taking antiretroviral medications daily to prevent acquiring the virus if exposed.

Furthermore, Treatment as Prevention utilizes antiretroviral therapy for diagnosed individuals to suppress their viral load to undetectable levels. A viral load that is consistently undetectable means the virus cannot be sexually transmitted to partners, a concept widely known as Undetectable Equals Untransmittable or U=U. Nurses play an essential role in educating patients about adherence, because missing doses can cause the virus to rebound and develop drug resistance.

Universal Precautions and Hospital Safety

In healthcare environments, safety protocols protect both patients and clinical staff from bloodborne pathogen transmission. Universal precautions require treating all human blood, body fluids, and moist tissues as if they are infected with HIV, Hepatitis B, or other bloodborne germs.

  • Hand hygiene: Wash hands thoroughly before and after every patient contact and immediately after removing gloves.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: Wear gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection whenever exposure to splashes or sprays of body fluids is anticipated.
  • Sharps safety: Never recap used needles by hand, and immediately dispose of all syringes and sharp instruments in puncture-resistant biohazard containers.

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis is an emergency medical response meant to prevent HIV infection after a high-risk exposure event, such as an occupational needlestick or sexual assault. PEP consists of a combination of antiretroviral medications that must be started as soon as possible—ideally within two hours, and no later than 72 hours—following the exposure. The full course typically lasts for 28 days. Nurses managing an exposure incident must immediately wash the affected wound with soap and water, report the incident, facilitate rapid baseline testing for both the source and the exposed worker, and provide empathetic counseling regarding adherence to the full regimen.

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission

Without interventions, an HIV-positive mother can transmit the virus to her child during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding. PMTCT programs provide a comprehensive cascade of care to eliminate vertical transmission. Pregnant women receive routine HIV screening during antenatal visits. Those who test positive are immediately started on antiretroviral therapy to suppress their viral load. During labor and delivery, safe obstetric practices are followed, and the newborn receives prophylactic antiretroviral syrup shortly after birth. In settings where safe replacement feeding is not affordable or sustainable, exclusive breastfeeding while the mother remains on continuous ART is recommended to nourish the infant while minimizing transmission risks.

Community Education and Nursing Roles

Nurses act as frontline educators and advocates in breaking down the stigma and discrimination that often hinder HIV prevention efforts. Community outreach involves dispelling myths about how the virus spreads—such as believing it is transmitted through casual contact, sharing utensils, or mosquito bites. Nurses provide non-judgmental counseling, distribute condoms, promote routine voluntary testing, and link vulnerable populations to care services. By fostering an environment of trust and empathy, nurses empower individuals and communities to take charge of their sexual health and adopt safe practices.
