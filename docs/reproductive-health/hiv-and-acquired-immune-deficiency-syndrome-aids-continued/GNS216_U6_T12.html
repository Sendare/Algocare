Infant Feeding in HIV

Core Principles of Infant Feeding in HIV

Balancing infant nutrition and the prevention of vertical transmission is a primary clinical challenge. The main objective is to maximize the child's chance of HIV-free survival while ensuring adequate growth and development.

  • Vertical Transmission: Passing the virus from mother to child during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding.
  • HIV-Free Survival: An infant who remains alive and uninfected with HIV at a defined endpoint, usually 12 to 24 months.
  • Replacement Feeding: Feeding infants who receive no breast milk with a diet that nutritionally supports them until they can consume adequate family foods.

Exclusive Breastfeeding and Maternal Antiretroviral Therapy

Current global health recommendations support breastfeeding for HIV-positive mothers, provided strict conditions are met. When a mother takes her antiretroviral therapy (ART) consistently and achieves viral suppression, the risk of transmitting the virus through breast milk drops significantly.

Exclusive breastfeeding means the infant receives only breast milk and no other foods, liquids, or water, not even plain water, for the first six months of life. Giving other liquids damages the delicate lining of the baby's immature gut, creating microscopic openings that make it much easier for the HIV virus in breast milk to enter the infant's bloodstream. Continuous maternal ART adherence remains the single most effective safeguard during this period.

Replacement Feeding: Safety and Preparation

When replacement feeding is chosen, it must satisfy the AFASS criteria: Affordable, Feasible, Acceptable, Sustainable, and Safe. If these conditions are missing, artificial feeding carries a high risk of death from severe diarrhea and malnutrition.

The Dangers of Mixed Feeding

Mixed feeding occurs when an infant receives both breast milk and other foods, such as cow's milk, formula, or traditional porridges, before six months of age. This feeding method carries a much higher risk of HIV transmission than exclusive breastfeeding.

Mixed feeding inflames the infant's intestinal tract. The combination of breast milk and foreign proteins damages the mucosal barrier of the gut. This inflammation increases viral shedding and provides an open pathway for the virus to cross into the baby's circulatory system. Healthcare providers must actively discourage mixed feeding during counseling sessions.

Transition to Complementary Foods

At six months of age, breast milk alone can no longer meet the growing nutritional demands of the infant. Complementary feeding must begin, introducing safe and nutrient-dense family foods while continuing breastfeeding up to 12 months or longer depending on local guidelines and viral suppression status.

  • Introduce mashed local staples like yam, plantain, or maize pap enriched with protein sources like mashed beans, egg, or fish.
  • Maintain strict hygiene during food preparation and storage to prevent diarrheal illnesses.
  • Continue maternal ART without interruption throughout the complementary feeding phase.

Nursing Assessment and Infant Feeding Counseling

Nurses play a pivotal role in supporting HIV-positive mothers to make informed infant feeding choices. Counseling must be non-judgmental, culturally sensitive, and based on the mother's individual clinical and social circumstances.

The nurse assesses maternal viral load status, adherence to antiretroviral drugs, breast health, and household financial stability. Counseling involves helping the mother weigh the risks and benefits of breastfeeding versus replacement feeding. Clear, patient-centered communication ensures the mother feels supported rather than coerced, improving both maternal mental health and infant survival outcomes.
