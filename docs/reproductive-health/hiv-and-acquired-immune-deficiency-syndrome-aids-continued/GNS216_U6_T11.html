Prevention of Mother to Child Transmission (PMTCT)

Introduction and Pathophysiology of PMTCT

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission, commonly known as **PMTCT**, encompasses a comprehensive package of interventions aimed at halting the spread of HIV from an infected mother to her child during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding. Without targeted medical intervention, the risk of vertical transmission ranges from 15% to 45%. Viral transmission occurs when maternal blood, genital secretions, or breast milk containing free viral particles or infected lymphocytes cross the placental barrier or enter the infant's mucosal surfaces. Key physiological routes of vertical transmission include: - **In utero**: Transplacental passage of the virus, particularly during the third trimester or when acute maternal infection occurs. - **Intrapartum**: Exposure of the infant to maternal blood and cervical secretions during the birth process. - **Postnatal**: Ingestion of infected breast milk through exclusive or mixed feeding practices throughout the lactation period.

The Comprehensive 4-Pronged Strategy

Global public health frameworks organize PMTCT into four distinct prongs to ensure a holistic approach. This strategy moves beyond merely treating pregnant women to addressing primary prevention and family planning. - **Prong 1**: Primary prevention of HIV infection among women of childbearing age through education, barrier methods, and pre-exposure prophylaxis. - **Prong 2**: Prevention of unintended pregnancies among women living with HIV by offering safe, voluntary family planning and contraceptive counseling. - **Prong 3**: Prevention of HIV transmission from pregnant and breastfeeding women living with HIV to their infants by utilizing antiretroviral regimens and safe delivery practices. - **Prong 4**: Provision of appropriate treatment, care, and support to women living with HIV, their children, and their families to maintain long-term health and wellness.

Antenatal Testing, Counseling, and Option B-Plus

Routine offering of HIV testing during antenatal care visits, often framed as 'opt-out' testing, remains the primary gateway for PMTCT services. Every pregnant woman presenting to a clinic in high-prevalence settings like Nigeria should receive pre-test counseling, undergo rapid diagnostic testing, and receive immediate post-test discussions. Under current global guidelines, **Option B-Plus** serves as the standard of care. This protocol dictates that all pregnant and breastfeeding women diagnosed with HIV are immediately initiated on a lifelong, single-tablet regimen of **Tenofovir, Lamivudine, and Dolutegravir (TLD)**, regardless of their CD4 count or clinical WHO staging. This approach protects the mother's health, drastically reduces viral load, and remains protective during subsequent pregnancies and future breastfeeding periods.

Intrapartum and Labor Management

Clinical management during labor requires meticulous infection control and adherence to standardized obstetric protocols to minimize infant exposure to maternal fluids. Routine procedures that increase mucosal trauma, such as early artificial rupture of membranes or routine episiotomies, should be carefully evaluated or avoided unless strictly indicated for obstetric emergencies. Elective cesarean sections scheduled before the onset of labor and rupture of membranes can significantly reduce vertical transmission, particularly for mothers with high or unknown viral loads near term. However, for women on consistent ART with suppressed viral loads, vaginal delivery remains safe and appropriate. Throughout labor, continued adherence to maternal oral ART is vital, and invasive internal fetal monitoring devices should be avoided to prevent breaks in the fetal skin barrier.

Postnatal Infant Prophylaxis and Testing

Newborns exposed to HIV require immediate postnatal interventions to neutralize any virus transmitted during labor. Prophylactic antiretroviral syrups, typically **Nevirapine** or **Zidovudine**, are initiated within hours of birth and continued for a specified duration, usually 6 weeks, or longer if high-risk factors are present. Infant diagnosis relies on virological testing because maternal antibodies cross the placenta and cause false-positive results on standard rapid antibody tests. In resource-constrained settings, **Dried Blood Spot (DBS)** samples are collected for **DNA Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR)** testing at 6 weeks of age, or at the earliest opportunity thereafter. A final antibody test is performed later in childhood, usually around 18 months or after cessation of breastfeeding, to confirm definitive HIV serostatus.

Infant Feeding Choices and Nursing Support

Balancing the nutritional needs of the infant with the prevention of postnatal transmission presents a delicate clinical challenge. Global guidelines recommend **exclusive breastfeeding** for the first six months of life for mothers living with HIV who are on continuous, suppressed ART, followed by the introduction of appropriate complementary foods while continuing breastfeeding up to 12 months or longer. Mixed feeding—giving breast milk alongside infant formula, water, or early solid foods—carries a significantly higher risk of HIV transmission than exclusive breastfeeding because mixed feeding damages the delicate gut mucosa of the infant, facilitating viral entry. Nurses must provide non-judgmental counseling, support maternal adherence to ART, and ensure mothers understand the critical importance of avoiding mixed feeding.
