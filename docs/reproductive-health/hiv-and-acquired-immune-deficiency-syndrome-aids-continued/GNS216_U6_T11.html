Prevention of Mother to Child Transmission (PMTCT)

Introduction to PMTCT and Global Context

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission, commonly known as PMTCT, refers to comprehensive interventions provided to women living with HIV during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period. Without any medical intervention, the risk of passing the virus from a mother to her child ranges from 15% to 45%. Effective PMTCT programs can drop this transmission rate to below 5%. Globally, sub-Saharan Africa bears the highest burden of paediatric HIV infections, making these interventions a core priority in community and hospital nursing practice. As a nurse, understanding this context helps frame clinical work not just as treating an individual, but as protecting an entire generation.

Pathophysiology and Timing of Transmission

HIV can pass from a mother to her infant at three distinct biological junctures: during pregnancy (in utero), during labor and delivery (intrapartum), and through breastfeeding (postnatal).

  • In utero transmission: Occurs when the virus crosses the placental barrier, often due to maternal viremia or placental inflammation.
  • Intrapartum transmission: Happens as the baby passes through the birth canal, exposed to maternal blood and genital secretions.
  • Postnatal transmission: Occurs when the infant ingests breast milk containing the virus.

A common misconception is that transmission happens exclusively during delivery; however, without intervention, a significant portion occurs via breastfeeding when mothers are not on treatment.

The 4-Pronged Strategy of PMTCT

The World Health Organization outlines a comprehensive 4-pronged strategy to eliminate new HIV infections among children and keep their mothers alive:

  1. Primary prevention: Stopping primary HIV infection among women of childbearing age.
  2. Preventing unintended pregnancies: Providing family planning counseling and contraceptives to women living with HIV.
  3. Preventing transmission from mother to child: Using antiretroviral drugs and safe delivery practices during pregnancy and lactation.
  4. Care, treatment, and support: Providing ongoing clinical care, support, and treatment for women, children, and their families living with HIV.

Antenatal Testing, Counseling, and ART Regimens

Routine opt-out HIV counseling and testing is offered to every pregnant woman during her first antenatal care visit. Opt-out testing means the test is part of standard routine antenatal screening, and the patient is informed that the test will be done unless she specifically declines. When a woman tests positive, immediate initiation of Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) is critical, regardless of her CD4 count or clinical stage. Lifelong ART suppresses maternal viral load to undetectable levels, which is the single most effective way to prevent passing the virus to the baby.

Intrapartum and Safe Delivery Management

Intrapartum management focuses on reducing infant exposure to maternal blood and genital secretions. Women already established on ART with a suppressed viral load can have a safe vaginal delivery. However, if a mother has a high or unknown viral load near the time of delivery, a scheduled caesarean section before the onset of labor and rupture of membranes is often recommended to reduce transmission risk. Nurses must ensure continuous adherence to oral ART during labor and avoid invasive procedures such as artificial rupture of membranes or unnecessary episiotomies when possible.

Postnatal Care and Infant Prophylaxis

Infants born to mothers living with HIV require preventive care immediately after birth. This includes giving the newborn a course of antiretroviral prophylaxis (such as syrup Zidovudine or Nevirapine) typically for the first 6 weeks of life to protect against any virus acquired during delivery. Early infant diagnosis using DNA PCR testing is scheduled at 6 weeks of age or at the earliest possible contact. Nurses play a crucial role in scheduling follow-up visits, reinforcing the importance of infant prophylaxis syrup, and providing psychological support to anxious mothers.

Infant Feeding Choices in the Context of HIV

Infant feeding presents a delicate balance between providing optimal nutrition and preventing postnatal HIV transmission. Current public health guidelines recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life, accompanied by maternal ART, followed by the introduction of appropriate complementary foods up to 12 months or longer. Mixed feeding—giving breast milk alongside water, formula, or solid food early on—is strongly discouraged because it damages the infant's gut lining and increases the risk of transmission. When replacement feeding is safe, feasible, affordable, sustainable, and acceptable, formula milk can be used instead of breastfeeding.

Nursing Roles and Community Support

Nurses are the backbone of PMTCT programs, serving as educators, counselors, clinical care providers, and community advocates. Beyond the hospital walls, nurses work with community health workers to trace defaulters who miss clinic appointments, reduce deep-rooted stigma, and encourage male partner involvement. Engaging male partners improves testing rates and helps women adhere to their medication regimens. Providing a non-judgmental, confidential environment encourages mothers to stay in care, protecting both maternal health and child survival.
