Prevention of Mother to Child Transmission (PMTCT)

Introduction to PMTCT and Global Context

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) of HIV remains a cornerstone of global and national public health strategies, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa where the burden of pediatric HIV is historically highest. Mother-to-child transmission (MTCT), also known as vertical transmission, refers to the spread of HIV from an HIV-infected mother to her child during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding. Without any interventions, the risk of transmission ranges from 15% to 45%. However, with comprehensive PMTCT programs that include universal antenatal HIV screening, prompt initiation of lifelong antiretroviral therapy (ART) for the mother, safe obstetric practices, and infant prophylaxis, this risk can be dramatically reduced to below 5%. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding the PMTCT framework is vital as Nigeria accounts for a significant share of the global MTCT burden. Nurses serve at the frontline of healthcare delivery, playing pivotal roles in client education, stigma reduction, counseling, and direct clinical management. By implementing evidence-based PMTCT protocols, nurses help bridge the gap toward achieving the goal of an AIDS-free generation, ensuring that infants are born HIV-negative and mothers remain healthy to care for their families. Comprehensive knowledge of global targets, such as the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) elimination goals, empowers nursing professionals to advocate for better maternal and child health outcomes within local primary health care centers and tertiary hospitals alike.

Pathophysiology of Mother-to-Child HIV Transmission

Understanding the pathophysiology of Mother-to-Child HIV Transmission (MTCT) is essential for effective nursing care and intervention planning. HIV can be transmitted from an infected mother to her child across three distinct biological windows: intra-uterine (during pregnancy), intrapartum (during labor and delivery), and postnatal (through breastfeeding). Intra-uterine transmission occurs when the virus crosses the placental barrier, which can be facilitated by placental inflammation, maternal co-infections such as malaria or sexually transmitted infections, or high maternal viral loads. The intrapartum period represents the highest risk window for acute transmission in the absence of interventions, as the infant is exposed to maternal blood, genital secretions, and amniotic fluid through micro-transfusions and direct mucosal contact during delivery. Postnatal transmission occurs because HIV is present in breast milk, including both cell-free virus and infected cells, which can cross the infant's immature gastrointestinal mucosa. Several risk factors significantly increase the probability of MTCT, most notably a high maternal viral load (which is the single most important predictor), low maternal CD4 cell count indicating advanced immunosuppression, primary maternal HIV infection during pregnancy or lactation, and prolonged duration of breastfeeding. Conversely, suppressing the maternal viral load to undetectable levels through consistent antiretroviral therapy is the most effective way to block transmission across all three pathways, protecting the infant during gestation, birth, and the postnatal period.

The 4-Pronged Strategy of PMTCT

The global framework for the Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) is structured around a comprehensive 4-pronged strategy designed to address primary prevention, unintended pregnancies, vertical transmission, and holistic care. Prong 1 focuses on the primary prevention of HIV infection among women of childbearing age through health education, promotion of safe sex practices, provision of condoms, and treatment of sexually transmitted infections. Prong 2 targets the prevention of unintended pregnancies among women living with HIV by offering safe, voluntary family planning and contraceptive counseling, thereby reducing the sheer number of exposed infants. Prong 3 is the core clinical intervention: preventing HIV transmission from women living with HIV to their infants by providing antiretroviral prophylaxis or treatment during pregnancy, labor, and breastfeeding, alongside safe obstetric practices. Prong 4 emphasizes the provision of appropriate treatment, care, and support for women living with HIV, their children, and their families, recognizing that maternal health and child survival are inextricably linked. For nurses, mastering these four prongs ensures a holistic approach that extends beyond the maternity ward into community outreach, family planning clinics, and chronic disease management. By addressing primary prevention alongside clinical management, nurses help reduce new infections in the community while safeguarding the health of HIV-positive mothers and their exposed infants.

Antenatal Care, Counseling, and HIV Testing

Antenatal care (ANC) serves as the primary entry point for PMTCT services, making the initial booking visit a critical window for intervention. Nurses must provide routine, opt-out HIV counseling and testing (HCT) to all pregnant women during their first ANC visit, ensuring that testing is voluntary, confidential, and accompanied by pre-test and post-test counseling. Opt-out testing means that HIV testing is a standard component of the routine antenatal screening panel (alongside blood group, genotype, and packed cell volume), and the client is informed that the test will be performed unless she explicitly declines. Pre-test counseling addresses the benefits of knowing one's status, the risk of vertical transmission, and consent, while post-test counseling focuses on coping with results, partner notification, and immediate linkage to care. For women who test HIV-negative, prevention messages and repeat testing during the third trimester or at labor are vital, especially in high-prevalence settings where seroconversion can occur during pregnancy or lactation. For women who test HIV-positive, counseling must be supportive, empathetic, and free of judgment to mitigate stigma and encourage treatment adherence. Nurses play a crucial role in addressing partner testing and disclosure, which are vital for adherence support and overall family health. Effective ANC counseling establishes a therapeutic nurse-client relationship that encourages retention in care, promotes viral suppression, and lays the foundation for a safe pregnancy and delivery.

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) Regimens for Pregnant and Breastfeeding Women

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is the backbone of PMTCT, with current international and national guidelines advocating for 'Option B+' or universal lifelong ART for all pregnant and breastfeeding women living with HIV, regardless of their CD4 count or clinical staging. Under this approach, once a woman is diagnosed with HIV, ART is initiated immediately and continued for life, offering numerous benefits: it rapidly suppresses maternal viral load, preserves maternal health, prevents sexual transmission to uninfected partners, and provides continuous protection during current and future pregnancies and breastfeeding periods. The preferred first-line regimen typically consists of a Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF) combined with Lamivudine (3TC) and Dolutegravir (DTG). DTG is favored for its high genetic barrier to resistance, rapid viral load suppression, and excellent safety profile. Nursing responsibilities involve educating clients on the critical importance of 100% adherence to achieve and maintain viral suppression, screening for potential side effects, managing minor adverse reactions, and reinforcing that skipping doses can lead to drug resistance and increased risk of transmission to the baby. Nurses also monitor CD4 counts and viral load testing where available, providing continuous encouragement and emotional support to overcome adherence barriers such as pill fatigue, financial constraints, and fear of disclosure.

Intrapartum and Labor Management in PMTCT

Intrapartum and labor management require specialized nursing care to minimize the risk of HIV transmission from mother to infant during delivery. Nurses must review the client's antenatal records to confirm her HIV status, adherence to ART, and most recent viral load results. For women with a suppressed viral load or who have been on consistent ART, a vaginal delivery is safe and appropriate. However, elective caesarean section (scheduled before the onset of labor and rupture of membranes) may be indicated for women with high or unknown viral loads near term to reduce infant exposure to infectious fluids. Throughout labor, universal standard precautions must be strictly observed by all healthcare personnel to protect both staff and patient. Invasive procedures such as artificial rupture of membranes (ARM), routine use of episiotomies, and instrumental deliveries (such as forceps or vacuum extraction) should be avoided when possible to reduce fetal exposure to maternal blood and secretions. For women with high viral loads, intravenous Zidovudine (AZT) infusion may be administered during labor as an adjunct to maternal ART. Immediate newborn care includes gentle wiping of the infant to remove maternal blood and secretions, avoiding vigorous suctioning that could traumatize mucosal tissues, and initiating infant antiretroviral prophylaxis and early newborn care protocols promptly.

Postnatal Care and Infant Prophylaxis

Postnatal care and infant prophylaxis are critical components of the PMTCT continuum, designed to protect the newborn during the vulnerable postnatal and breastfeeding period. All infants born to HIV-positive mothers must receive antiretroviral prophylaxis—typically with Nevirapine (NVP) and/or Zidovudine (AZT) syrup—starting as soon as possible after birth (within 6 to 12 hours) and continuing for 6 weeks or longer depending on maternal viral load and breast-feeding status. Nurses are responsible for calculating accurate infant dosages based on birth weight, educating mothers on how to administer syrups correctly, and emphasizing compliance. In addition to ARV prophylaxis, infant care includes initiating Cotrimoxazole preventive prophylaxis (CPP) around 6 weeks of age to prevent Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia and other opportunistic infections until HIV infection is definitively excluded. Early infant diagnosis (EID) is another vital nursing responsibility; this involves collecting dried blood spot (DBS) samples for HIV DNA PCR testing at 6 weeks of age (or at the earliest opportunity thereafter). Nurses must counsel mothers on the importance of returning for EID results, continuing serial antibody testing at 18 months or after cessation of breastfeeding, and maintaining retention in the pediatric HIV care cascade to ensure timely confirmation of status and initiation of treatment if a child tests positive.

Infant Feeding Options in the Context of HIV

Infant feeding in the context of HIV requires careful counseling, balancing the nutritional and immunological benefits of breastfeeding against the risk of postnatal HIV transmission. Current World Health Organization and national guidelines recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first 6 months of life for infants born to HIV-positive mothers on ART, followed by the introduction of appropriate complementary foods and continued breastfeeding up to 12 months or longer. This recommendation is premised on the mother being virally suppressed on ART, which dramatically lowers the risk of transmission via breast milk while providing the unmatched nutritional, developmental, and immunological benefits of human milk. However, mixed feeding—giving breast milk alongside infant formula, water, or other foods in the first 6 months—is strictly discouraged because it damages the infant's immature intestinal mucosa and significantly increases the risk of HIV transmission and fatal diarrheal illnesses. If replacement feeding (formula milk) is acceptable, feasible, affordable, sustainable, and safe (AFASS criteria), it can be an option, but nurses must educate mothers on safe preparation, hygiene, and the lack of maternal antibodies. Nurses play a crucial role in supporting breastfeeding mothers by assessing latch techniques, treating mastitis or cracked nipples promptly, reinforcing maternal ART adherence to maintain viral suppression, and providing individualized counseling tailored to the mother's socio-economic context.

Nursing Roles and Community Support in PMTCT

Nurses are the linchpin of PMTCT programs, serving educators, counselors, clinicians, and community advocates. Within clinical settings, nurses provide compassionate prenatal care, administer ART, manage labor, deliver infant prophylaxis, and conduct early infant diagnosis. Beyond hospital walls, nursing roles extend deeply into the community to combat stigma, dispel myths, and bridge the gap between health facilities and local populations. Stigma remains a major barrier to PMTCT uptake, leading many women to hide their status, default on medications, or avoid delivery in health facilities. Community support groups, peer mentor mothers, and community health extension workers play a transformative role in tracing lost-to-follow-up clients, providing psychosocial support, and encouraging male partner involvement. Nurses collaborate with community leaders and local organizations to organize sensitization campaigns that normalize HIV testing and promote family-centered care. Furthermore, nurses advocate for health system strengthening—ensuring uninterrupted supplies of ARV drugs, HIV test kits, and laboratory reagents. By fostering an environment of empathy, confidentiality, and respect, nursing professionals empower women living with HIV to take charge of their health, adhere to treatment regimens, and successfully raise healthy, HIV-negative children.
