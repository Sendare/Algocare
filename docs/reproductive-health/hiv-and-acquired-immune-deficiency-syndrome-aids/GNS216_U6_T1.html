Basics of HIV and AIDS

Introduction to HIV and AIDS

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) represent one of the most significant global health challenges, particularly within sub-Saharan Africa, including Nigeria. HIV is a retrovirus that specifically targets and progressively destroys the human immune system, eroding the body's natural defense mechanisms against opportunistic infections and malignancies. As the virus replicates, it depletes CD4+ T-lymphocytes, which are critical coordinators of cellular immunity. When the immune system becomes severely compromised—indicated by a drop in CD4+ cell count below 200 cells/mm3 or the presence of specific AIDS-defining illnesses—the infection is classified as AIDS. Understanding the fundamentals of HIV and AIDS is crucial for nursing students, as nurses are at the forefront of providing compassionate, evidence-based care, reducing stigma, and implementing preventative public health strategies. In the Nigerian healthcare context, nurses frequently encounter patients at various stages of the disease continuum, making a thorough grasp of the viral pathology, transmission dynamics, and clinical management essential for effective patient outcomes and community health education.

Pathophysiology and Mechanism of Infection

The pathophysiology of HIV centers on its classification as a retrovirus, meaning it carries its genetic material in the form of RNA rather than DNA. Upon entering the human body, HIV utilizes specific surface glycoproteins (gp120 and gp41) to bind to CD4 receptors and co-receptors (such as CCR5 or CXCR4) located primarily on the surface of helper T-cells, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Once attached, the viral envelope fuses with the host cell membrane, allowing the viral capsid to enter the cytoplasm. Inside the cell, the enzyme reverse transcriptase converts the single-stranded viral RNA into double-stranded DNA. This newly formed viral DNA is then transported into the host cell nucleus, where another viral enzyme, integrase, inserts it into the host's own cellular genome, creating a provirus. The host cell's machinery is subsequently hijacked to transcribe and translate new viral components. Protease, another crucial viral enzyme, cleaves newly synthesized proteins to assemble mature viral particles, which bud off from the host cell to infect neighboring cells, eventually leading to the widespread destruction of the immune system.

Modes of Transmission

Understanding the modes of transmission of HIV is foundational for effective nursing practice, prevention counseling, and post-exposure management. HIV is transmitted primarily through specific bodily fluids, which include blood, semen, pre-seminal fluid, rectal fluids, vaginal fluids, and breast milk. The primary routes of transmission are unprotected sexual intercourse (vaginal or anal) with an infected partner, sharing contaminated needles, syringes, or other drug-injection equipment, and vertical transmission from an HIV-infected mother to her child during pregnancy, childbirth, or breastfeeding (mother-to-child transmission, or MTCT). In healthcare settings, accidental needlestick injuries or mucosal exposure to infected blood also pose a occupational risk. It is equally important for nursing students to understand how HIV is not transmitted. The virus cannot be spread through casual contact, such as hugging, shaking hands, sharing eating utensils, using public toilets, swimming pools, or through insect bites like mosquito stanzas. Health education emphasizing these facts helps mitigate social stigma and fear surrounding people living with HIV.

Stages of HIV Infection

The natural history of an untreated HIV infection is categorized into three distinct clinical stages: Acute HIV Infection, Chronic HIV Infection (also known as clinical latency), and AIDS. The acute stage occurs within 2 to 4 weeks after initial exposure, characterized by a massive viral replication spike and a sharp drop in CD4+ cell counts, often presenting with flu-like symptoms. Following the acute phase, the infection enters the chronic or asymptomatic latency stage, where viral reproduction slows down considerably, though the virus remains active. During this phase, individuals may remain entirely asymptomatic for years, although viral replication continues to gradually damage the immune system. Without antiretroviral therapy, this stage eventually culminates in AIDS, the final and most severe stage. In the AIDS stage, the immune system is severely compromised, rendering the individual highly vulnerable to opportunistic infections such as Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, tuberculosis, cryptococcal meningitis, and malignancies like Kaposi's sarcoma. Recognizing these stages allows nurses to anticipate patient needs, provide appropriate monitoring, and encourage adherence to life-saving medications.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Clinical manifestations of HIV vary widely depending on the stage of the disease, the patient's baseline immune status, and the presence of coinfections. During the acute phase, patients frequently exhibit non-specific symptoms that mimic infectious mononucleosis or influenza, including fever, fatigue, maculopapular rash, lymphadenopathy, pharyngitis, and myalgia. As the disease progresses into clinical latency, many individuals experience few or no symptoms, though persistent generalized lymphadenopathy may occur. When the disease advances to AIDS, clinical signs become much more severe and reflective of systemic immune failure. Common AIDS-defining manifestations include chronic diarrhea resulting in severe weight loss (often termed 'wasting syndrome'), persistent unexplained fevers, drenching night sweats, oral thrush (candidiasis), and neurological complications such as HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder. Furthermore, patients are at high risk for opportunistic infections; for instance, pulmonary tuberculosis is extremely prevalent in Nigeria and frequently co-occurs with HIV, presenting with chronic cough, hemoptysis, and weight loss. Nurses must maintain high clinical suspicion for these symptoms to facilitate early referral, intervention, and supportive nursing care.

Diagnosis and Laboratory Testing

Accurate and timely diagnosis of HIV is critical for initiating prompt treatment and preventing further transmission. Laboratory testing generally falls into three categories: antibody tests, combination antibody/antigen tests, and nucleic acid tests (NAT). Antibody tests check for antibodies produced by the body against HIV, though they have a 'window period'—the time between infection and when antibodies can be detected by a test—which can range from several weeks to three months. Combination or fourth-generation assays detect both HIV antibodies and the p24 antigen, a viral protein, which significantly shortens the window period and allows for earlier detection. Nucleic acid tests directly target viral RNA in the blood and can identify the virus even earlier than antigen/antibody tests. In resource-limited settings like Nigeria, national testing algorithms often utilize rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) performed in a serial or parallel testing scheme to confirm status at the point of care. Additionally, monitoring tests such as CD4 cell counts and viral load assays are essential post-diagnosis tools. Viral load testing measures the amount of active virus in the blood and is the gold standard for assessing the effectiveness of antiretroviral therapy.

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) and Management

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) has revolutionized the management of HIV, transforming what was once considered a fatal diagnosis into a manageable chronic condition. ART involves the daily use of a combination of HIV medicines (typically three drugs from at least two different drug classes) designed to suppress viral replication to undetectable levels. Major classes of antiretroviral medications include Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs), Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs), Protease Inhibitors (PIs), Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs), and Entry Inhibitors. By suppressing the viral load to undetectable levels, ART prevents progressive immune destruction, reduces the risk of opportunistic infections, improves life expectancy, and eliminates sexual transmission of the virus (Undetectable = Untransmittable, or U=U). Nursing management centers heavily on promoting lifelong adherence to the therapeutic regimen, monitoring for adverse drug reactions (such as metabolic disturbances or renal toxicity), managing side effects, and providing ongoing psychosocial support. Nurses must educate patients on the severe risks of treatment interruption, which can lead to viral rebound, clinical deterioration, and the development of drug-resistant viral strains.

Prevention and Nursing Interventions

Prevention of HIV and comprehensive nursing interventions form the cornerstone of curbing the epidemic at both individual and community levels. Primary prevention strategies include promoting consistent and correct condom use, offering pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) to uninfected individuals at high risk, and providing post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) following occupational or non-occupational exposures. For pregnant women living with HIV, prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) programs—which include maternal ART, safe delivery practices, and infant prophylaxis—are vital to ensure infants are born free of HIV. Secondary and tertiary nursing interventions focus on providing holistic care to diagnosed individuals. Nurses play a pivotal role in delivering client-centered counseling, advocating for safe sex practices, screening for tuberculosis and sexually transmitted infections (STIs), managing opportunistic infections, and linking patients to community support networks. Furthermore, nurses actively work to dismantle social stigma, which remains a formidable barrier to testing, treatment retention, and overall health-seeking behavior in many communities across Nigeria.
