Basics of HIV and AIDS

Introduction to HIV and AIDS

Human Immunodeficiency Virus, commonly known as HIV, is a lentivirus that attacks the body's immune system, specifically destroying vital white blood cells known as CD4+ T cells. Without these cells, the human body struggles to fight off everyday infections and cancers.

Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome, or AIDS, represents the final, most advanced stage of an untreated HIV infection. A person reaches this stage when their CD4 count drops dangerously low or when they develop specific severe opportunistic illnesses. It is vital to remember that not everyone with HIV has AIDS, but everyone with AIDS contracted HIV first. Early detection and modern medical treatment can keep a person living with HIV healthy for decades, preventing the onset of AIDS entirely.

Pathophysiology and Mechanism of Infection

Once HIV enters the human body, it seeks out cells bearing surface receptor proteins known as CD4, predominantly helper T lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells.

  1. Binding and Fusion: The viral envelope attaches to the host cell CD4 receptor and co-receptors, fusing the viral membrane with the cell membrane.
  2. Reverse Transcription: The virus releases its single-stranded RNA inside the cell and uses an enzyme called reverse transcriptase to convert it into double-stranded DNA.
  3. Integration: Another viral enzyme, integrase, inserts this newly formed viral DNA into the host cell's own genetic blueprint within the nucleus.
  4. Replication and Budding: The host cell machinery manufactures new viral proteins, which assemble and bud off from the cell surface to infect other healthy cells, eventually destroying the host cell in the process.

Modes of Transmission

HIV is transmitted exclusively through specific bodily fluids that contain high concentrations of the virus or infected immune cells. These fluids include blood, semen, pre-seminal fluid, rectal fluids, vaginal fluids, and breast milk.

  • Sexual Contact: Unprotected vaginal, anal, or oral sex with an infected partner remains the leading global route of transmission.
  • Blood Exposure: Sharing contaminated needles, syringes, or drug injection equipment poses a very high risk. Accidental needle-stick injuries in healthcare settings also occur.
  • Perinatal Transmission: An infected mother can pass the virus to her child during pregnancy, childbirth, or through breastfeeding.

Casual contact such as hugging, sharing utensils, swimming in the same pool, or mosquito bites does not transmit HIV.

Stages of HIV Infection

The natural progression of untreated HIV infection unfolds across three distinct clinical phases:

  1. Acute HIV Infection: Occurring two to four weeks after exposure, patients experience flu-like symptoms such as fever, fatigue, and swollen lymph nodes. Viral replication is extremely high during this period.
  2. Chronic HIV Infection (Clinical Latency): The virus remains active but multiplies at very low levels. Many individuals experience no symptoms at all during this stage, which can last for a decade or longer without medications.
  3. Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS): The most severe stage characterized by a CD4 count falling below 200 cells/mm³ or the appearance of defining opportunistic infections.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

As the immune system deteriorates, clinical signs become more pronounced and varied. Opportunistic infections—pathogens that normally do not trouble healthy immune systems—take advantage of the failing defenses.

Common early signs include persistent generalized lymphadenopathy, unexplained weight loss, recurring fevers, night sweats, and chronic diarrhea. Oral candidiasis, commonly known as thrush, often appears as a fungal indicator of declining immunity.

Advanced disease brings severe opportunistic conditions such as Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), Kaposi's sarcoma (a purplish skin lesion tumor), cytomegalovirus, and HIV-associated wasting syndrome.

Diagnosis and Laboratory Testing

Accurate laboratory testing is essential for confirming an HIV diagnosis and guiding ongoing clinical care. Modern diagnostic protocols rely on several key assays:

  • Combination Antibody/Antigen Tests: These laboratory blood tests look for both HIV antibodies and viral proteins called p24 antigens, detecting infection earlier than antibody tests alone.
  • Rapid Antibody Tests: Point-of-care fingerstick tests or oral fluid swabs that provide results within minutes by detecting antibodies against HIV.
  • Viral Load Testing: Quantitative PCR tests that measure the exact number of viral copies in a milliliter of blood, vital for monitoring how well therapy works.
  • CD4 T-Cell Count: Measures immune system strength and dictates when opportunistic infection prophylaxis is required.

Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) and Management

The cornerstone of modern HIV management is Antiretroviral Therapy (ART). ART involves taking a combination of daily medications from different drug classes to block various steps in the viral life cycle.

These classes include reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors, integrase strand transfer inhibitors, and entry inhibitors. By suppressing viral replication to undetectable levels, ART preserves immune function, prevents progression to AIDS, and drastically reduces the risk of transmitting the virus to others.

A primary nursing goal is promoting strict adherence to the medication regimen. Missing doses allows the virus to mutate and develop drug resistance, rendering treatment ineffective.

Prevention and Nursing Interventions

Preventing new infections requires a multi-faceted public health and clinical approach. Nurses play a crucial role in education, screening, and support.

  • Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP): Daily medication taken by HIV-negative individuals at high risk to prevent acquiring the virus.
  • Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP): Emergency antiretroviral medications started within 72 hours of a potential occupational or non-occupational exposure.
  • Undetectable = Untransmittable (U=U): Educating patients that maintaining an undetectable viral load through ART completely prevents sexual transmission.

Nursing care also encompasses psychosocial support, counseling on safe sex practices, monitoring for medication side effects, and linking patients to community resources to reduce stigma.
