Antiretroviral Drug Therapy

Introduction and Goals of Antiretroviral Therapy

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) consists of a combination of medications that suppress human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) replication. The primary objective is to reduce the viral load to an undetectable level, which halts disease progression and prevents transmission to sexual partners. Achieving viral suppression allows the immune system to recover, measured by an increasing CD4 cell count, thereby preventing opportunistic infections and prolonging life. In Nigerian clinical practice, making ART universally accessible through national health programs has transformed HIV from a fatal diagnosis into a manageable chronic condition.

Classes of Antiretroviral Drugs and Mechanisms of Action

Antiretroviral medications target different stages of the HIV life cycle. Using a combination of drugs from multiple classes prevents the virus from easily mutating and developing resistance.

  • Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs): Act as faulty building blocks that prematurely terminate the viral DNA chain.
  • Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs): Bind directly and allosterically to the reverse transcriptase enzyme, halting its activity.
  • Protease Inhibitors (PIs): Block the protease enzyme, preventing the cleavage of viral proteins into mature, infectious virions.
  • Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs): Prevent the integration of reverse-transcribed viral DNA into the host cell's chromosomes.
  • Entry and Fusion Inhibitors: Block the attachment or fusion of the virus to the host CD4 cell membrane.

Standard First-Line and Second-Line Regimens in Nigeria

National guidelines in Nigeria, aligned with World Health Organization recommendations, prioritize dolutegravir (DTG) as the backbone of preferred first-line regimens. A typical first-line combination for adults and adolescents consists of Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF) plus Lamivudine (3TC) plus Dolutegravir (DTG). This regimen offers high potency, a high genetic barrier to resistance, and a favorable safety profile.

When a first-line regimen fails—confirmed by persistent viral load elevation—patients are switched to a second-line regimen. Second-line regimens typically substitute the anchor drug with a ritonavir-boosted protease inhibitor, such as Atazanavir/ritonavir or Lopinavir/ritonavir, combined with a new optimized dual-NRTI backbone.

Patient Assessment and Baseline Investigations

Before initiating antiretroviral therapy, a comprehensive clinical evaluation establishes the patient's baseline health status and screens for opportunistic infections like tuberculosis or cryptococcal meningitis. Baseline laboratory investigations are essential to guide drug selection and monitor future toxicity:

  • CD4 Cell Count: Assesses the degree of immune suppression and helps determine the urgency of prophylaxis against opportunistic infections.
  • Plasma Viral Load: Quantifies baseline viremia and serves as the reference point for assessing treatment success.
  • Renal Function Tests: Serum creatinine and estimated creatinine clearance are vital, especially when prescribing tenofovir.
  • Liver Function Tests and Hepatitis Serology: Evaluates baseline hepatic health and checks for coinfections with hepatitis B or C.

Monitoring Efficacy and Identifying Toxicity

Routine laboratory monitoring ensures that ART is working safely and effectively. Viral load testing is the gold standard for measuring treatment efficacy, performed typically at 6 months after initiation, at 12 months, and annually thereafter if suppressed. A viral load above 1,000 copies/mL on two consecutive tests indicates treatment failure.

Monitoring for drug toxicity involves regular clinical check-ins and targeted blood tests. Common toxicities include renal impairment from tenofovir, central nervous system side effects such as insomnia or vivid dreams from efavirenz, and metabolic alterations like dyslipidemia or insulin resistance associated with older protease inhibitors.

Managing Side Effects and Drug Interactions

Antiretroviral medications can interact with various drugs, including herbal remedies, anti-tuberculosis medications, and contraceptives. For instance, rifampicin—used in tuberculosis treatment—can significantly reduce blood levels of certain PIs and NNRTIs, requiring dose adjustments or alternative agents like dolutegravir taken twice daily.

Managing side effects proactively prevents unnecessary treatment interruption. Mild gastrointestinal upset can often be managed by taking medications with food or adjusting the time of administration, whereas severe hypersensitivity reactions, such as severe rash or lactic acidosis, require immediate drug substitution and medical evaluation.

Promoting Medication Adherence and Retention in Care

Lifelong adherence of at least 95% is required to suppress viral replication successfully and prevent the emergence of drug-resistant viral strains. Suboptimal adherence allows viral replication to resume under selective drug pressure, leading to treatment failure.

Nursing interventions to support adherence include assessing readiness before initiation, identifying potential barriers such as transport costs or stigma, and utilizing peer support groups or mobile phone reminders. Addressing mental health issues, particularly depression, is equally crucial for maintaining long-term engagement in HIV care clinics.
