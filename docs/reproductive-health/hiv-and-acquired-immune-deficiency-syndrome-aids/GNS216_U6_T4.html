Antiretroviral Drug Therapy

Introduction to Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) and HIV Lifecycle

Human Immunodeficiency Virus, commonly known as HIV, is a retrovirus that primarily targets the body's immune system, specifically the CD4+ T lymphocytes. Once the virus enters a host cell, it uses an enzyme called reverse transcriptase to convert its viral RNA into DNA. This newly formed viral DNA then integrates into the host cell's own genetic material using the integrase enzyme, forcing the cell to manufacture more copies of the virus. Finally, the protease enzyme cuts these newly made viral proteins so they can assemble into mature, infectious viral particles.

Antiretroviral Therapy, or ART, involves taking a combination of daily medications to block different stages of this viral replication cycle. Because HIV mutates very rapidly when exposed to a single drug, patients must take a combination of at least three drugs from different classes simultaneously. This multi-pronged approach is known as combination antiretroviral therapy (cART), and it prevents the virus from developing resistance to the medicines.

Goals and Benefits of ART

The primary clinical goal of Antiretroviral Therapy is to suppress viral replication to undetectable levels in the patient's blood. Achieving and maintaining an undetectable viral load protects the immune system, allowing CD4 cell counts to recover and preventing the onset of opportunistic infections that define acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).

Beyond individual health, ART offers a massive public health benefit known as Undetectable equals Untransmittable (U=U). When a person living with HIV maintains a consistently suppressed viral load through proper adherence, the risk of sexually transmitting the virus to an HIV-negative partner drops to zero. Furthermore, scaling up ART prevents vertical transmission from mother to child during pregnancy, childbirth, and breastfeeding.

Classes of Antiretroviral Drugs and Their Mechanisms

  • Nucleoside/Nucleotide Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NRTIs): Act as decoy building blocks that trick the reverse transcriptase enzyme into stopping the formation of viral DNA.
  • Non-Nucleoside Reverse Transcriptase Inhibitors (NNRTIs): Bind directly to the reverse transcriptase enzyme, changing its shape and blocking its ability to function.
  • Protease Inhibitors (PIs): Block the protease enzyme, preventing it from cutting viral proteins into mature, working pieces.
  • Integrase Strand Transfer Inhibitors (INSTIs): Stop the integrase enzyme from inserting viral DNA into the host cell's human chromosomes.
  • Entry and Fusion Inhibitors: Block HIV from attaching to or entering the surface of the human host cell in the first place.

First-Line and Second-Line ART Regimens

National guidelines, such as those provided by the Federal Ministry of Health in Nigeria, dictate standardized drug combinations to ensure effective and affordable care. A standard first-line regimen for adults and adolescents starting ART typically consists of two NRTIs combined with an INSTI. A very common preferred combination is Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) plus Lamivudine (3TC), paired with Dolutegravir (DTG).

When a first-line regimen fails—usually confirmed by persistent viral load rebound—the healthcare team switches the patient to a second-line regimen. Second-line therapy swaps out the core drug class while retaining two active NRTIs, often transitioning the patient to a ritonavir-boosted protease inhibitor like Atazanavir or Lopinavir, alongside a different NRTI backbone.

Patient Assessment and Initiation of Therapy

Before initiating antiretroviral therapy, nurses and clinicians conduct a thorough baseline assessment. This evaluation includes taking a detailed medical and psychosocial history, assessing readiness for lifelong daily medication, and performing baseline laboratory investigations. Essential baseline labs include a CD4 cell count, a viral load test, renal function tests (such as serum creatinine and estimated creatinine clearance), liver function tests, and screening for co-infections like tuberculosis and viral hepatitis.

Current guidelines recommend same-day ART initiation for eligible patients who are clinically stable and mentally prepared to start on the day of their HIV diagnosis. However, if a patient presents with symptoms of an opportunistic infection like cryptococcal meningitis or active tuberculosis, ART initiation is intentionally delayed to manage the acute infection first and prevent severe inflammatory complications.

Monitoring ART Efficacy and Toxicity

Ongoing monitoring ensures that antiretroviral therapy remains both safe and effective throughout the patient's life. Efficacy is primarily measured using viral load testing, which is typically performed six months after starting ART, again at twelve months, and annually thereafter if the patient remains stable and suppressed.

Toxicity monitoring involves routine laboratory checks and clinical interviews to catch drug side effects early. Nurses screen for bone density loss, changes in kidney filtration rates, liver enzyme elevations, and metabolic shifts like lipid abnormalities. Catching these adverse changes early allows the clinical team to substitute problem drugs before permanent organ damage occurs.

Managing Side Effects and Drug Interactions

Antiretroviral drugs can cause various side effects, ranging from mild nausea and vivid dreams (often associated with Efavirenz) to more severe complications like lactic acidosis or severe rashes. Nurses play a vital role in educating patients on what side effects to expect, reassuring them that many mild symptoms subside after the first few weeks of treatment, and advising on safe symptom relief.

Drug-drug interactions are another critical concern in HIV management. Many ARVs—especially non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors and protease inhibitors—are processed by liver enzymes, leading to interactions with medications like rifampicin for tuberculosis, herbal supplements such as St. John's wort, and certain contraceptives. Thorough medication reconciliation prevents dangerous drops in ARV levels or toxic drug buildups.

Promoting Medication Adherence and Retention in Care

Lifelong adherence—taking at least 95% of prescribed doses on time—is the single most important factor in the success of HIV treatment. Poor adherence leads to viral replication, immune decline, and the emergence of drug-resistant strains of HIV. Nurses employ various strategies to support adherence, including peer support groups, pillboxes, mobile phone reminder alarms, and appointment tracking systems.

Retention in care goes beyond just taking pills; it means keeping clinic appointments and picking up refills on schedule. Community-based pharmacy refill models and multi-month dispensing of medications help reduce the travel and financial burdens on patients, making it easier to stay engaged in long-term chronic care.
