Pathophysiology and Natural Progression of HIV/AIDS

Introduction to HIV and Viral Structure

The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is a lentivirus that attacks the body's immune system, specifically targeting cells that have CD4 receptors on their surface. Structurally, HIV is an enveloped virus containing two identical strands of single-stranded RNA.

Surrounding the viral genetic material is a cone-shaped core made of the p24 capsid protein. Key enzymes required for the virus to multiply are packed inside this core, including reverse transcriptase, integrase, and protease. The outer envelope consists of a lipid bilayer stolen from the host cell membrane, studded with glycoprotein spikes known as gp120 and gp41. These surface proteins act like keys, allowing the virus to recognize, attach to, and enter target immune cells.

Mechanism of Transmission and Cellular Tropism

HIV is transmitted primarily through specific body fluids, including blood, semen, pre-seminal fluid, rectal fluids, vaginal fluids, and breast milk. The virus requires a portal of entry, such as mucosal tissue or broken skin, or direct injection into the bloodstream.

Cellular tropism refers to the specific types of cells a virus prefers to infect. HIV shows a strong tropism for cells expressing CD4 surface molecules, predominantly CD4+ T-lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Infection begins when the viral gp120 protein binds to the CD4 receptor and a co-receptor, either CCR5 or CXCR4. Macrophages and dendritic cells often act as early targets and viral reservoirs, transporting the pathogen from mucosal sites to regional lymph nodes.

Viral Replication Cycle

  • Binding and Fusion: The viral gp120 protein attaches to the CD4 receptor and a co-receptor, fusing the viral envelope with the host cell membrane.
  • Reverse Transcription: Once inside, the viral enzyme reverse transcriptase converts single-stranded viral RNA into double-stranded DNA.
  • Integration: The newly formed viral DNA enters the host cell nucleus, where the integrase enzyme inserts it into the host's own DNA.
  • Transcription and Translation: The host cell machinery reads the integrated viral DNA to manufacture new viral RNA and proteins.
  • Assembly and Budding: Immature viral proteins assemble near the cell membrane, bud off from the host cell, and use the protease enzyme to mature into infectious viral particles.

Natural Stages of HIV Infection

Untreated HIV infection progresses through three distinct clinical phases:

  1. Acute HIV Infection: Occurs 2 to 4 weeks after exposure. Patients experience a rapid surge in viral load and a temporary drop in CD4 counts. Many develop flu-like symptoms such as fever, rash, and swollen lymph nodes.
  2. Clinical Latency (Chronic HIV): The rate of viral replication slows down, and patients may remain asymptomatic for years. The virus continues to actively replicate in lymph nodes, slowly destroying CD4+ T-cells.
  3. Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS): The final, most severe stage. The immune system is severely compromised, leaving the body vulnerable to opportunistic infections and cancers.

WHO Clinical Staging

In resource-limited settings where CD4 cell counts are not always immediately available, the World Health Organization (WHO) uses a clinical staging system based on observable clinical signs and symptoms to guide treatment decisions.

  • Stage 1: Asymptomatic or persistent generalized lymphadenopathy.
  • Stage 2: Mild manifestations such as minor mucocutaneous infections and recurrent upper respiratory tract infections.
  • Stage 3: Advanced symptoms including unexplained chronic diarrhea for over a month, severe weight loss, and pulmonary tuberculosis.
  • Stage 4: Severe, life-threatening conditions defining AIDS, such as Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, toxoplasmosis of the brain, or Kaposi sarcoma.

Immunological Decline and Progression to AIDS

As HIV infection progresses without treatment, the constant destruction of CD4+ T-cells outpaces the body's ability to produce new ones. A healthy adult typically has a CD4 count between 500 and 1,500 cells/mm³.

When the CD4 count drops below 200 cells/mm³, or when the patient develops an AIDS-defining condition regardless of the count, the diagnosis changes from HIV to AIDS. Immune system collapse occurs because CD4 cells act as the master coordinators of the adaptive immune response. Without them, B-cells cannot produce effective antibodies, and killer T-cells fail to recognize infected body cells.

Opportunistic Infections and Malignancies

When cellular immunity fails, organisms that normally do not cause disease in healthy people take advantage of the weakened immune system. These are known as opportunistic infections.

Common opportunistic infections include Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP), cryptococcal meningitis, severe candidiasis (thrush), and disseminated cytomegalovirus (CMV). Reactivation of latent tuberculosis is also common and dangerous. Additionally, the lack of immune surveillance increases the risk of specific cancers, notably Kaposi sarcoma (caused by human herpesvirus 8) and non-Hodgkin lymphoma.
