Pathophysiology and Natural Progression of HIV/AIDS

Introduction to HIV and Viral Structure

Human Immunodeficiency Virus is a retrovirus belonging to the Lentivirus family, characterized by a long incubation period before the onset of symptomatic disease. Structurally, the virion is spherical and enclosed by a lipid bilayer derived from the host cell membrane. Embedded within this envelope are viral glycoproteins, specifically **gp120** and **gp41**, which mediate attachment and entry into host cells. Beneath the envelope lies the viral matrix protein, and centrally positioned is a conical capsid containing two identical single-stranded RNA molecules alongside essential viral enzymes: reverse transcriptase, integrase, and protease. These enzymes are vital for converting viral RNA into host-compatible DNA and integrating it into the human genome.

Mechanism of Transmission and Cellular Tropism

Transmission of HIV occurs primarily through the transfer of specific body fluids, including blood, semen, pre-seminal fluid, rectal fluids, vaginal secretions, and breast milk. In high-prevalence settings like Nigeria, unprotected heterosexual intercourse and mother-to-child transmission remain major routes of spread. Cellular tropism defines which cells the virus infects; HIV exhibits a high affinity for cells bearing **CD4 surface receptors**, predominantly helper T-lymphocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells. Successful viral entry requires not only the CD4 receptor but also a chemokine coreceptor—either **CCR5** or **CXCR4**—which triggers a conformational change allowing the viral and host cell membranes to fuse.

Viral Replication Cycle and CD4+ T-Cell Depletion

Once inside the host cell, the viral life cycle unfolds in a precise sequence: 1. **Binding and Fusion**: The gp120 protein attaches to CD4 and coreceptors, allowing gp41 to mediate membrane fusion. 2. **Reverse Transcription**: Viral RNA is transcribed into complementary DNA by the error-prone enzyme reverse transcriptase. 3. **Integration**: The newly formed viral DNA enters the nucleus, where integrase inserts it into the host's chromosomal DNA as a provirus. 4. **Transcription and Translation**: The host cellular machinery transcribes viral genes, producing viral proteins and RNA transcripts. 5. **Assembly and Budding**: New viral particles assemble at the cell membrane and bud off to infect other cells. This relentless cycle ultimately destroys host CD4+ T-cells through direct viral cytotoxicity, immune system clearance of infected cells, and syncytia formation.

Natural Stages of HIV Infection

Without therapeutic intervention, untreated HIV infection progresses through three distinct clinical phases: - **Acute Infection Stage**: Occurs two to four weeks post-exposure. Patients experience a massive surge in viral replication accompanied by a sharp drop in CD4+ counts. Many present with flu-like symptoms, fever, and lymphadenopathy. - **Clinical Latency Stage**: Also known as chronic HIV infection, this phase can last for over a decade. Viral replication drops to a lower steady-state level, and patients are largely asymptomatic while CD4+ counts gradually decline. - **AIDS Stage**: Characterized by severe immune exhaustion where CD4+ counts fall below 200 cells/mm³ or the manifestation of defining opportunistic illnesses.

WHO Clinical Staging System

The World Health Organization classifies HIV disease progression into four clinical stages to guide resource allocation and clinical management in resource-limited settings where routine CD4 testing may not be immediately available. Stage 1 represents asymptomatic infection or persistent generalized lymphadenopathy. Stage 2 includes mild manifestations such as recurrent upper respiratory tract infections, minor mucocutaneous lesions, and papular pruritic eruptions. Stage 3 involves advanced symptoms like unexplained chronic diarrhea for over a month, severe persistent weight loss, and pulmonary tuberculosis. Stage 4 marks severe clinical disease, defining the transition to AIDS through life-threatening opportunistic infections and malignancies like cryptococcal meningitis or cervical cancer.

Immunological Decline and Progression to AIDS

The transition from chronic infection to AIDS is driven by the steady depletion and functional exhaustion of CD4+ T-lymphocytes. As viral load gradually rises, the rate of CD4+ cell destruction outpaces the bone marrow's capacity for generation. Beyond quantitative loss, surviving T-cells suffer qualitative defects, including impaired proliferative capacity and loss of cytokine production. Concurrently, chronic immune activation leads to generalized immune system exhaustion and lymphoid tissue fibrosis. Once the CD4+ count drops below the critical threshold of **200 cells per microliter**, cell-mediated immunity collapses, rendering the individual vulnerable to opportunistic pathogens that a healthy immune system easily controls.

Opportunistic Infections and Malignancies

When cell-mediated immunity fails, patients become susceptible to a spectrum of opportunistic infections and cancers that rarely afflict immunocompetent individuals. Common complications include *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia, cerebral toxoplasmosis, esophageal candidiasis, and disseminated tuberculosis or cryptococcosis. Viral-driven malignancies also emerge due to loss of immune surveillance, most notably **Kaposi sarcoma** caused by human herpesvirus 8, and aggressive non-Hodgkin lymphomas. Recognizing these pathologies is critical for timely clinical intervention and patient survival in advanced disease stages.
