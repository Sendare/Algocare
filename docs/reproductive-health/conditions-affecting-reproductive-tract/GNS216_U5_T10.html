Vaginal Inflammation

Introduction to Vaginal Inflammation

Vaginal inflammation, clinically termed vaginitis, is one of the most frequent reasons women seek gynecological care across primary health clinics. It involves irritation, swelling, and often infection of the vaginal mucosa and surrounding tissues. While many cases are easily managed with appropriate antimicrobial therapy, failure to identify the specific underlying cause can lead to recurrent infections and secondary complications. In the Nigerian clinical setting, healthcare providers frequently encounter patients self-medicating with over-the-counter antiseptics or herbal douches, which often worsen symptoms by stripping away protective barriers.

Normal Vaginal Flora and Physiology

The healthy vagina maintains a delicate ecological balance that protects against invading pathogens. Lactobacilli, commonly known as Döderlein's bacilli, dominate this environment. These beneficial bacteria ferment glycogen from shed epithelial cells to produce lactic acid, maintaining an acidic pH typically between 3.8 and 4.2. This low pH creates a hostile environment for opportunistic bacteria and fungi. Disruptions to estrogen levels, systemic antibiotic use, or alterations in local hygiene practices can rapidly deplete these protective bacteria, setting the stage for infection.

Classification and Common Types of Vaginitis

Vaginitis primarily stems from three distinct etiologies, each requiring specific diagnostic approaches and treatments.

  • Bacterial Vaginosis (BV): Caused by an overgrowth of anaerobic bacteria like *Gardnerella vaginalis* alongside a significant reduction in protective lactobacilli.
  • Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC): Commonly known as a yeast infection, driven by the proliferation of fungal species such as *Candida albicans*.
  • Trichomoniasis: A sexually transmitted infection caused by the flagellated protozoan parasite *Trichomonas vaginalis*.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Understanding predisposing factors helps clinicians identify root causes and counsel patients on prevention. Risk factors vary by the specific condition but frequently overlap.

  • Hormonal Fluctuations: Pregnancy, use of combined oral contraceptives, and menopause alter glycogen availability and pH.
  • Pharmacological Agents: Broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy eliminates normal protective flora, frequently triggering fungal overgrowth.
  • Immunocompromised States: Uncontrolled diabetes mellitus and HIV infection predispose individuals to recurrent candidiasis.
  • Behavioral Practices: Frequent vaginal douching, use of scented soaps, wearing tight non-breathing synthetic underwear, and multiple sexual partners increase vulnerability.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Accurate diagnosis requires a combination of thorough nursing history and objective clinical testing. Nurses prepare patients for pelvic examinations and assist clinicians with bedside diagnostics. Key assessment steps include measuring vaginal pH using indicator strips, performing a wet mount microscopy preparation with normal saline and potassium hydroxide (KOH) to identify clue cells, hyphae, or motile trichomonads. The whiff test, releasing a strong amine odor upon adding KOH to vaginal discharge, strongly indicates bacterial vaginosis.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Treatment protocols depend entirely on the confirmed microbiological diagnosis. Prescribing incorrect medications can prolong suffering and foster antimicrobial resistance.

  • Bacterial Vaginosis: Treated with systemic or topical nitroimidazole agents, primarily oral or gel metronidazole or clindamycin.
  • Candidiasis: Managed using topical azole antifungals (such as clotrimazole creams or suppositories) or a single oral dose of fluconazole.
  • Trichomoniasis: Requires systemic treatment with metronidazole or tinidazole, with the critical requirement that sexual partners must be treated simultaneously to prevent reinfection.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Education

Effective nursing care extends beyond administering medications to empowering patients through targeted education. Nurses must advise patients to complete the entire course of antibiotics or antifungals, even if symptoms resolve early. Instruction should focus on proper hygiene: wiping from front to back after elimination, avoiding scented feminine hygiene sprays, douches, and bubble baths that disrupt vaginal flora. Patients prescribed oral metronidazole must be explicitly warned to completely avoid alcohol consumption during treatment and for 24 hours afterward to prevent severe disulfiram-like adverse reactions.

Complications and Preventive Strategies

Untreated or recurrent vaginal inflammation can progress to more serious health issues, particularly in pregnant women where infections can trigger preterm labor, premature rupture of membranes, and low birth weight infants. In non-pregnant individuals, chronic inflammation increases susceptibility to acquiring sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, due to compromised mucosal integrity. Preventive strategies center on maintaining a healthy lifestyle, managing underlying systemic illnesses like diabetes, avoiding unnecessary antibiotic use, and fostering open reproductive health communication.
