Vaginal Inflammation

Normal Vaginal Physiology and Flora

The vagina is not a sterile environment; it naturally maintains a delicate balance of microorganisms that protect against infection. The dominant organism in a healthy, reproductive-age vagina is Lactobacillus. These friendly bacteria feed on glycogen produced by the vaginal walls and break it down into lactic acid. This process keeps the vaginal pH acidic, typically between 3.8 and 4.2. This acidic shield stops harmful bacteria and yeast from multiplying out of control. When this internal balance gets disturbed—by antibiotics, hormonal shifts, or frequent douching—the protective flora drops, paving the way for inflammation and infection.

Common Types of Vaginitis

Vaginal inflammation, commonly called vaginitis, usually stems from one of three main conditions. Nurses must understand how to tell them apart based on their underlying causes:

  • Bacterial Vaginosis (BV): Caused by an overgrowth of mixed anaerobic bacteria, especially *Gardnerella vaginalis*, replacing normal lactobacilli.
  • Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VYC): Commonly known as a yeast infection, triggered by an overgrowth of *Candida* fungus, most often *Candida albicans*.
  • Trichomoniasis: A sexually transmitted infection caused by a microscopic single-celled parasite called *Trichomonas vaginalis*.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Various everyday triggers and systemic factors disrupt vaginal health. Knowing these risks helps nurses guide patients toward better self-care. Common factors include:

  1. Antibiotic Use: Wipes out good lactobacilli along with bad bacteria, often leading to yeast overgrowth.
  2. Hormonal Changes: Pregnancy, oral contraceptives, and menopause alter glycogen levels and vaginal tissue thickness.
  3. Frequent Douching: Washes away protective bacteria and raises vaginal pH.
  4. Uncontrolled Diabetes: High sugar levels in bodily fluids encourage fungal growth.
  5. Sexual Activity: Introducing new partners or semen can alter the vaginal chemical balance.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Although different types of vaginitis share common signs like irritation, each condition presents distinct clues during a clinical assessment.

  • Bacterial Vaginosis: Features a thin, gray-white discharge with a distinct, unpleasant fishy odor, which often worsens after sexual intercourse. Itching is usually mild or absent.
  • Candidiasis: Presents with thick, white, cottage-cheese-like discharge that does not smell bad. Patients experience intense vulvar itching, burning, and redness.
  • Trichomoniasis: Produces yellow-green, frothy discharge that may have a foul odor, accompanied by severe local irritation, dysuria, and sometimes strawberry spots on the cervix.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Tests

Accurate treatment depends on finding the exact cause of the inflammation, because antifungal drugs will not cure a bacterial or parasitic infection. Nurses assist providers by gathering a thorough health history—asking about sexual partners, hygiene habits, and recent medications—and preparing supplies for a pelvic exam.

Key diagnostic tests include measuring vaginal fluid pH with indicator paper, performing a wet mount (mixing discharge with saline or potassium hydroxide on a microscope slide to look for yeast, clue cells, or moving trichomonads), and performing the whiff test (adding potassium hydroxide to discharge to release a fishy amine odor).

Medical Treatment and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological therapy directly targets the specific causative organism identified during assessment.

  • Bacterial Vaginosis: Treated with prescription antibiotics such as metronidazole or clindamycin, taken orally or applied as a vaginal gel.
  • Candidiasis: Managed with antifungal medications, ranging from over-the-counter azole creams (like clotrimazole) to single-dose oral fluconazole.
  • Trichomoniasis: Requires systemic treatment with oral metronidazole or tinidazole. A critical nursing note for trichomoniasis is that sexual partners must be treated simultaneously, even if they have no symptoms, to prevent reinfection.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Education

Beyond administering medications, nurses play a vital role in educating patients on hygiene practices that promote healing and prevent future episodes. Patients should be advised to wear breathable, cotton-crotch underwear and avoid tight, synthetic pants that trap moisture. Proper wiping technique—from front to back after using the toilet—prevents fecal contamination of the vaginal area. Nurses must strongly discourage douching, scented feminine hygiene sprays, and bubble baths, which strip away protective natural flora and irritate delicate mucosal tissues.
