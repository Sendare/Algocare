Vulval Varicose Veins

Introduction and Definition of Vulval Varicose Veins

Vulval varicose veins refer to the dilation, elongation, and tortuosity of the superficial venous system of the labia majora and minora. While less common than lower extremity varicosities, they affect an estimated four percent of pregnant individuals. These vascular changes typically develop during the second or third trimester when hemodynamic shifts are most pronounced. Most cases are benign and resolve spontaneously within six weeks following childbirth, but recognizing the condition early prevents unnecessary alarm for the patient and guides appropriate clinical management.

Anatomy and Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

Pregnancy creates a unique hemodynamic environment that predisposes pelvic vessels to engorgement. Maternal blood volume increases by up to fifty percent, while progesterone levels induce smooth muscle relaxation within vessel walls. Concurrently, the enlarging gravid uterus exerts direct mechanical compression on the inferior vena cava and iliac veins. This obstruction elevates downstream venous pressure in the pelvis, forcing blood backward into the pudendal venous plexus and causing the delicate vulvar veins to dilate.

Etiology and Risk Factors

  • Multiparity: Successive pregnancies weaken pelvic floor structures and vascular elasticity, increasing recurrence rates.
  • Family History: Inherited weakness in vein wall integrity or congenital valve defects predisposes individuals.
  • Prolonged Standing: Occupations requiring long hours of standing aggravate pelvic venous stasis through gravity.
  • Previous Varicosities: Pre-existing lower limb varicose veins or history of deep vein thrombosis heighten vulnerability.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Patients frequently report a heavy, aching, or full sensation in the perineum, which often worsens as the day progresses or after prolonged standing. Visual inspection reveals distinct, bluish, tortuous swellings on one or both labia. Intercourse may become uncomfortable, leading to dyspareunia. Some individuals experience a localized throbbing or burning discomfort. While alarming in appearance, severe sharp pain is uncommon unless a complication such as thrombosis or rupture occurs.

Nursing Assessment and Physical Examination

A thorough assessment requires careful, sensitive inspection of the perineal region with the patient in the lithotomy or modified Sims position, ensuring privacy and informed consent. The nurse evaluates the size, distribution, and color of the varicosities, checking for skin changes, tenderness, or signs of localized inflammation. Palpation helps determine if the veins are soft and compressible or firm and tender, which might indicate superficial thrombophlebitis. Documenting baseline findings aids in tracking progression throughout the remainder of the pregnancy.

Conservative Management and Patient Education

Management focuses on symptom relief and preventing progression through lifestyle adjustments. Instruct patients to avoid prolonged standing or sitting without breaks. Elevating the pelvis with pillows while lying down promotes venous drainage. Specialized vulvar support garments or snug-fitting supportive underwear can alleviate pressure. Warm baths and mild analgesics like paracetamol offer localized comfort when approved by the primary provider. Routine physical activity, such as walking, enhances overall circulation without straining the pelvic floor.

Intrapartum Care and Delivery Considerations

A common clinical concern is the risk of hemorrhage during childbirth due to tissue tearing over engorged veins. Fortunately, massive hemorrhage is rare, but careful handling is vital. During the second stage of labor, uncontrolled explosive pushing should be avoided when possible. Care providers monitor for accidental rupture of these vessels. If an episiotomy is necessary, surgeons take extreme care to avoid cutting directly through large varicosities to prevent excessive bleeding. Adequate lighting and preparedness for rapid suturing are standard precautions in the delivery room.

Potential Complications and Postpartum Follow-Up

While spontaneous resolution is the norm, complications can occasionally arise. Superficial thrombophlebitis may develop, characterized by localized hard, painful cords with surrounding erythema. Severe vessel rupture, though dramatic in presentation, is managed effectively with direct pressure. During postpartum checks, nurses assess the perineum for involution of the varicosities. If significant engorgement persists beyond six weeks, referral to a vascular specialist for sclerotherapy or further evaluation is indicated for persistent discomfort.
