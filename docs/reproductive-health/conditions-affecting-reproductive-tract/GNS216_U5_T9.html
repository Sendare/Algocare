Vulval Varicose Veins

Introduction and Definition of Vulval Varicose Veins

Vulval varicose veins are enlarged, tortuous, and dilated blood vessels located on the labia majora, labia minora, and surrounding perineal structures. They are essentially varicose veins of the female external genitalia. While they can occur independently, they are most frequently diagnosed during pregnancy due to unique haemodynamic shifts. Many women first notice a soft swelling or feel a heavy, dragging sensation in the perineal region. Although alarming in appearance, these dilated vessels are usually benign and tend to resolve spontaneously following childbirth. However, understanding their presentation helps nurses reassure anxious mothers and differentiate them from other pelvic conditions like inguinal hernias or Bartholin gland cysts.

Anatomy and Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

The development of vulval varicosities is heavily driven by the anatomical and hormonal alterations of pregnancy.

  • Blood Volume: Total maternal blood volume increases by up to 50 percent to support the growing foetus, placing extra stretch on all vascular walls.
  • Venous Pressure: The enlarging uterus mechanically compresses the inferior vena cava and pelvic veins, obstructing smooth blood return from the lower limbs and perineum.
  • Hormonal Influence: Elevated circulating progesterone relaxes smooth muscle within the walls of blood vessels, causing them to dilate and lose their baseline tone.
  • Valvular Incompetence: As the veins stretch, the delicate one-way valves inside them fail to close properly, leading to backward pooling of blood.

Together, these factors create a high-pressure environment in the pelvis that forces superficial perineal vessels to bulge outward.

Etiology, Risk Factors, and Clinical Manifestations

Not every pregnant woman develops vulval varicose veins, but certain predisposing factors increase individual vulnerability. A strong family history of varicose veins suggests a genetic weakness in vein wall integrity. Multiparity—having multiple previous pregnancies—stretches pelvic tissues and vessels repeatedly, making varicosities more likely and more severe with each subsequent gestation. Prolonged standing, heavy lifting, and advanced maternal age further compound the risk by worsening pelvic congestion.

Patients typically report a range of local symptoms that fluctuate throughout the day:

  • A heavy, full, or aching sensation in the perineum, often worse by evening.
  • Visible, bluish, tortuous clusters of swollen tissue on one or both sides of the vulva.
  • Discomfort or a dull pain during prolonged walking or standing.
  • Dyspareunia, or pain during sexual intercourse, due to tissue congestion.
  • Occasional localized itching or irritation from skin stretching.

Nursing Assessment and Physical Examination

A thorough nursing assessment requires both skilled communication and careful physical inspection. Nurses should establish a comfortable, private environment because patients often feel embarrassed discussing perineal changes. Ask targeted questions about pain intensity, swelling duration, and aggravating activities like prolonged standing.

During the physical examination, explain each step clearly and maintain patient dignity. With the patient in the lithotomy position, inspect the vulva for asymmetric swelling, bluish discolouration, and protruding vascular clusters. Palpate gently to assess tenderness, warmth, and whether the swelling is compressible. Nurses must also inspect the lower extremities, as vulval varicosities frequently occur alongside great saphenous vein insufficiency or leg varicosities.

Conservative Management and Comfort Measures

Management for uncomplicated vulval varicosities is primarily conservative, focusing on symptom relief and preventing the pooling of blood in the pelvis. Nurses play a vital role in educating patients on self-care strategies that can be easily integrated into daily life.

  • Positional Relief: Encourage frequent rest periods with the pelvis elevated or lying in the left lateral position to take pressure off the inferior vena cava.
  • Support Garments: Recommend specialized perineal supports or compression shorts designed to apply gentle, even pressure to the vulva.
  • Activity Modification: Advise patients to avoid standing or sitting in one position for extended periods and to take short, regular walks to promote venous return.
  • Hydration and Bowel Care: Prevent straining during bowel movements by encouraging high fibre intake and adequate fluids, as straining worsens pelvic pressure.

Intrapartum Care and Delivery Considerations

As pregnancy reaches term, both the patient and the maternity team naturally wonder about the safety of vaginal delivery. A common misconception is that vulval varicosities make vaginal birth impossible and automatically mandate a caesarean section. In reality, most women with vulval varicosities deliver vaginally without major incident.

During labor, midwives and nurses monitor the veins closely. Care must be taken during pelvic examinations and the application of internal monitors to avoid trauma to the fragile vessels. Clinicians generally avoid unnecessary episiotomies near varicose clusters to prevent heavy bleeding. If an episiotomy or laceration is necessary, careful suturing ensures hemostasis, as dilated vessels bleed more profusely when cut.

Potential Complications and Postpartum Follow-Up

Although most cases resolve smoothly, nurses must remain vigilant for rare complications. The most serious acute risk during pregnancy or childbirth is vessel rupture and severe hemorrhage due to trauma. Another potential issue is superficial thrombophlebitis, where a blood clot forms inside the dilated superficial vein, causing localized pain, redness, and a firm, tender cord-like swelling.

In the postpartum period, rapid hormonal shifts and the sudden relief of pelvic compression cause vulval varicosities to shrink dramatically. Nurses should inspect the perineum during postnatal checks to confirm involution. Most varicosities regress significantly within six weeks after delivery. If residual vessels persist and cause ongoing pain or cosmetic distress, the patient can be referred for specialized vascular assessment later on.
