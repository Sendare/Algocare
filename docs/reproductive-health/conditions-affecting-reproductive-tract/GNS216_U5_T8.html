Vulval Tumour

Introduction to Vulval Tumours

Vulval tumours encompass a diverse group of growths affecting the external female genitalia, including the labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, and perineal body. As future nursing professionals in Nigeria, understanding these growths is critical because patients often present late due to cultural stigma, self-medication, or misdiagnosis of vulval lesions as common infections like vulvovaginitis or sexually transmitted diseases. Vulval tumours can be benign, such as cysts, fibromas, and condylomata, or malignant, with squamous cell carcinoma being the most prevalent histologic type. Because the vulva is highly visible, patient education and early detection can drastically improve prognosis and reduce morbidity. Nurses play a foundational role in creating a safe, non-judgmental environment that encourages women to seek timely medical attention for persistent vulval lumps, chronic itching, or unexplained bleeding. Comprehensive care requires a thorough grasp of the anatomical structures involved, common pathological classifications, and the psychosocial burdens associated with malignancies of the reproductive system. By bridging the gap between community health education and clinical intervention, nurses can significantly decrease the delayed presentation rates commonly observed in resource-limited settings.

Anatomical and Physiological Background

The vulva is a complex anatomical region richly supplied by blood vessels, lymphatic drainage pathways, and nerve endings. Understanding its anatomy is essential for staging vulval malignancies and anticipating surgical complications. The lymphatic drainage of the vulva is predominantly superficial inguinal and deep femoral lymph nodes, which dictates the primary routes of metastasis for malignant vulval tumours. The labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, and vestibule each have unique tissue compositions, ranging from keratinized stratified squamous epithelium on the outer labia to non-keratinized epithelium in mucosal areas. Physiological changes, such as hormonal fluctuations during puberty, pregnancy, and menopause, can influence tissue resilience and the presentation of benign lesions like cysts or hypertrophy. Furthermore, the rich innervation of the vulva means that even small tumours can cause significant pain, dyspareunia, or chronic pruritus, drastically affecting a patient's physical and psychological well-being. Nursing students must appreciate that lymphatic mapping is crucial because regional lymph node status is one of the single most important prognostic factors in vulval cancer. Knowing these pathways helps nurses accurately assess for groin swellings, lymphedema, and post-operative healing complications following surgical resections.

Classification: Benign versus Malignant Tumours

Vulval tumours are broadly categorized into benign and malignant lesions, each requiring vastly different management strategies. Benign tumours include inclusion cysts, Bartholin's gland cysts, hidradenomas, fibromas, lipomas, and condylomata acuminata. These lesions are typically slow-growing, well-circumscribed, and non-invasive, though they can cause discomfort, infection, or cosmetic concerns. Conversely, malignant tumours are characterized by uncontrolled cellular proliferation, tissue invasion, and the potential for metastasis. While squamous cell carcinoma accounts for the vast majority of malignancies, other less common malignant tumours include malignant melanoma, extramammary Paget's disease, basal cell carcinoma, and sarcomas. Differentiating between benign and malignant growths requires thorough clinical evaluation, as conditions like chronic vulvar dystrophy or lichen sclerosus can sometimes mimic or precede malignant transformations. Nurses must understand these classifications to provide accurate information to patients, alleviate unnecessary anxiety when a benign diagnosis is confirmed, and emphasize the seriousness of adhering to treatment plans when a malignancy is identified. Recognizing the distinct clinical behaviours of these two categories ensures that nursing interventions align appropriately with medical goals.

Aetiology and Risk Factors

The aetiology of vulval tumours varies depending on whether the lesion is benign or malignant. Benign cysts and tumours often arise from obstructed ducts, local trauma, or chronic inflammation. For malignant vulval tumours, particularly squamous cell carcinoma, two distinct pathways are recognized: one associated with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, commonly seen in younger women, and another independent of HPV, which typically arises in older women with background chronic inflammatory skin conditions such as lichen sclerosus or vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN). Other documented risk factors include smoking, immunosuppression (such as HIV-positive status, which is highly relevant in clinical practice across Nigeria), advanced age, and a history of other anogenital squamous neoplasias. Understanding these risk factors enables nursing students to identify vulnerable populations, advocate for HPV vaccination, and promote smoking cessation programs. Nurses are uniquely positioned to educate communities on managing chronic inflammatory vulvar conditions proactively to prevent malignant progression, emphasizing regular self-examinations and prompt reporting of persistent vulvar changes.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Assessment

Clinical presentation of vulval tumours can vary widely based on the size, location, and malignancy status of the growth. Many patients present with a distinct vulvar lump, mass, or ulcer that fails to heal. Chronic pruritus (itching), burning, localized pain, dyspareunia, and abnormal bleeding or blood-stained discharge are also frequent complaints. In advanced cases, patients may notice groin swelling due to lymph node metastasis, weight loss, or constitutional symptoms. During patient assessment, nurses must demonstrate high levels of cultural sensitivity, empathy, and professional decorum, as examination of the genitalia can cause intense anxiety and embarrassment. The nursing assessment should include a comprehensive health history focusing on the duration of symptoms, previous infections, obstetric history, and risk factors like smoking or immunosuppression. Physical examination requires proper positioning (lithotomy position with adequate lighting and privacy) to thoroughly inspect and palpate the vulva, perineum, and inguinal lymph nodes. Documenting the exact size, borders, texture, and tenderness of any lesion provides baseline data that assists the multidisciplinary healthcare team in formulating an accurate diagnostic and treatment plan.

Diagnostic Investigations and Biopsy

Accurate diagnosis of a vulval tumour relies on clinical visualization, colposcopy, and definitive histological confirmation through a biopsy. When a suspicious lesion, pigmented spot, or non-healing ulcer is identified, a punch biopsy is typically performed under local anesthesia. Nursing responsibilities during a vulval biopsy include preparing the procedure tray, reassuring the anxious patient, explaining each step of the procedure, and ensuring proper labeling and transport of the tissue specimen to the pathology laboratory. Histopathological examination determines whether the lesion is benign, premalignant (such as vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia), or malignant, and identifies the specific cell type and depth of invasion. In cases of confirmed malignancy, further diagnostic investigations such as cystoscopy, proctoscopy, chest radiography, and pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT) scans may be ordered to stage the disease and check for distant metastasis. Nurses must educate patients on the importance of these staging investigations, manage any associated procedure-related anxiety, and provide clear post-procedure instructions regarding wound care and pain management following the biopsy.

Medical and Surgical Management

The medical and surgical management of vulval tumours depends heavily on the histological diagnosis, stage, and overall health status of the patient. Benign tumours and cysts may be managed conservatively if asymptomatic, or treated via simple excision, marsupialization (for Bartholin's cysts), or laser ablation. Conversely, malignant tumours typically require a multidisciplinary approach involving surgery, radiation therapy, and sometimes chemotherapy. Surgical options for malignant vulval tumours range from wide local excision (for early-stage lesions) to radical vulvectomy with sentinel lymph node biopsy or complete inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy (for advanced disease). Radiation therapy and chemoradiation may be utilized preoperatively to shrink large tumours, or postoperatively to eradicate residual microscopic disease and reduce recurrence rates. As a nurse, understanding these treatment modalities is vital for anticipating post-operative needs, managing drains, monitoring wound healing in an area prone to infection due to its proximity to the urethra and anus, and administering supportive therapies. Collaborative care ensures that both oncological clearance and functional preservation of urinary and sexual organs are prioritized whenever possible.

Nursing Care and Patient Support

Holistic nursing care and patient support are paramount for women undergoing treatment for vulval tumours, given the sensitive nature of the diagnosis. Pre-operative nursing care involves thorough education, psychological support, addressing body image concerns, and preparing the patient for the physical changes associated with vulvar surgery. Post-operatively, meticulous wound care is essential to prevent surgical site infections; this includes perineal hygiene, Sitz baths, keeping the area dry, and monitoring surgical drains for output volume and character. Pain management must be prioritized using prescribed analgesics, keeping in mind that perineal wounds can make sitting, walking, and micturition extremely painful. Nurses should also watch for signs of complications such as wound dehiscence, lymphedema of the lower extremities, and deep vein thrombosis. Beyond physical care, nurses must offer compassionate psychological support, encourage open communication, and involve partners or family members in counseling with the patient's consent. Providing referrals to psycho-oncology support groups, sexual health counselors, and social workers helps patients navigate the complex emotional recovery and restore their quality of life.

Complications and Prognosis

Complications associated with the treatment of vulval tumours can be short-term or long-term, significantly impacting the patient's overall prognosis and rehabilitation. Short-term complications include wound breakdown (dehiscence), surgical site infections, cellulitis, urinary tract infections due to catheterization, and acute bleeding. Long-term complications frequently involve chronic lymphedema of the legs, vulvar scarring, dyspareunia, sensory loss or neuropathic pain in the perineal region, and psychological distress related to altered body image and sexual dysfunction. The prognosis of vulval tumours is primarily determined by the stage of the disease at diagnosis, the presence and extent of regional lymph node metastasis, histological grade, and tumour depth of invasion. Early detection through community awareness and prompt nursing intervention translates to significantly higher survival rates and less radical, de-escalated surgical treatments. Conversely, late-stage presentations carry a guarded prognosis, requiring aggressive multimodality therapies. As healthcare advocates in Nigeria, nurses must champion early screening, dispel myths surrounding reproductive tract cancers, and provide long-term follow-up care to monitor for disease recurrence and manage chronic treatment side effects.
