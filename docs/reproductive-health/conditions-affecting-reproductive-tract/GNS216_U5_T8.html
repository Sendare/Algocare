Vulval Tumour

Introduction and Overview of Vulval Tumours

Vulval tumours are abnormal growths that develop on the external female genitalia, which include the labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, and perineal region. These growths can be non-cancerous or cancerous. While they are relatively rare compared to other gynaecological cancers, they demand careful attention from nurses because patients often delay seeking care due to embarrassment or mistaking the symptoms for common infections like yeast or dermatitis. Early recognition directly improves treatment outcomes and spares patients from extensive, disfiguring surgeries.

Classification: Benign versus Malignant Growths

Understanding the difference between benign and malignant vulval lesions guides clinical management. Benign growths do not spread to other parts of the body, whereas malignant tumours are invasive and can metastasize.

  • Benign Tumours: Includes conditions like cysts, lipomas, fibromas, and condylomata acuminata. These are typically slow-growing and well-defined.
  • Malignant Tumours: Squamous cell carcinoma accounts for the vast majority of vulval cancers. Other less common types include melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and adenocarcinoma of the Bartholin's gland.

Aetiology and Associated Risk Factors

The development of vulval malignancies often follows different pathways depending on the patient's age and health history. Chronic inflammatory skin conditions of the vulva, such as lichen sclerosus, significantly increase the risk of malignant transformation over time. Additionally, persistent infection with high-risk strains of the human papillomavirus plays a major role in younger women, leading to precursor lesions known as vulval intraepithelial neoplasia. Smoking is another independent risk factor that impairs local immune response and works synergistically with HPV to promote cancerous cell changes.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment

Patients with vulval tumours present with a variety of localized complaints. A persistent vulval pruritus that does not respond to standard anti-fungal or anti-inflammatory creams is a classic warning sign. Nurses must look out for visual and tactile changes during physical assessment.

  • Common Symptoms: Long-standing itching, chronic pain, soreness, and burning.
  • Visible Findings: A raised lump, an ulcerated sore, a wart-like growth, or a patch of skin that has changed color to white, red, or dark brown.

Diagnostic Investigations and Biopsy

Definitive diagnosis requires a physical examination, often aided by a magnifying device called a colposcope to inspect skin patterns. However, a tissue biopsy is the gold standard for confirming malignancy and determining the exact histological subtype. The nurse plays a vital role in preparing the patient for this procedure by explaining what will happen, ensuring local anaesthetic is ready, and providing reassurance. Imaging studies such as pelvic MRI, CT scans, or chest radiographs may also be ordered to check for regional lymph node involvement and distant metastasis if cancer is confirmed.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment strategies depend heavily on the stage of the tumour, its size, and the patient's general health status. Surgery remains the primary treatment modality for most vulval cancers. Procedures range from a wide local excision for small, early-stage lesions to radical vulvectomy for extensive disease. Lymph node dissection of the groin is often performed to assess or prevent regional spread. For advanced cases or when surgery is unsafe, radiation therapy combined with chemotherapy may be used to shrink the tumour or provide palliative relief.

Nursing Care, Wound Management, and Patient Support

Postoperative nursing care for patients undergoing vulval surgery focuses on meticulous wound hygiene, pain control, and emotional support. The vulval region has a rich blood supply and a high risk of bacterial contamination due to proximity to the urethra and anus. Nurses must perform regular wound assessments for signs of dehiscence, infection, or hematoma formation. Sitz baths, sterile perineal care, and keeping the area dry are standard nursing interventions. Because these surgeries alter body image and sexual function, providing a compassionate, non-judgmental environment for psychological support is just as important as physical wound care.

Complications and Prognosis

Patients treated for vulval tumours may face both short-term and long-term complications. Immediate risks include wound breakdown, infection, and severe postoperative pain. Long-term complications frequently involve chronic lymphedema of the lower limbs due to lymph node removal, scarring, and sexual dysfunction resulting from tissue loss. The overall prognosis is closely tied to the stage at diagnosis. When detected early before lymph node metastasis occurs, survival rates are high. However, advanced disease with positive nodes carries a significantly guarded prognosis, making routine screening and early reporting of symptoms critical.
