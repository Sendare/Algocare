Vulval Tumour

Introduction and Anatomical Context

Vulval tumours encompass a diverse group of growths affecting the external female genitalia, which includes the labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, and vestibular glands. While gynecological malignancies often focus internally on the cervix or endometrium, vulval lesions are external, making them visually accessible yet frequently delayed in presentation due to social stigma or self-treatment with over-the-counter anti-fungal creams. Understanding the distinct anatomical boundaries of the vulva helps clinicians map lymphatic drainage, which primarily flows to the inguinal and femoral lymph nodes, dictating how these growths metastasize.

Classification of Benign and Malignant Lesions

Growths on the vulva are categorized into benign conditions and malignant neoplasms. Benign lesions do not invade surrounding tissues or metastasize, whereas malignant tumours show aggressive cellular behavior.

  • Benign Tumours: Includes cysts (such as Bartholin's gland cysts), fibromas, lipomas, and condylomata acuminata caused by human papillomavirus.
  • Malignant Tumours: Dominated by squamous cell carcinoma, which accounts for over ninety percent of cases. Other rare forms include melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and sarcomas.

Aetiology and Risk Factors

The development of vulval malignancies follows two distinct pathways. The first pathway is linked to high-risk human papillomavirus infection, particularly HPV type 16, which is more common in younger women and associated with vulval intraepithelial neoplasia. The second pathway is independent of HPV, occurring more frequently in older women with chronic inflammatory conditions such as lichen sclerosus, which causes chronic irritation, scratching, and cellular turnover that can drive malignant transformation.

  • HPV Infection: Associated with younger cohorts and smoking.
  • Chronic Dermatoses: Lichen sclerosus and lichen planus act as chronic precursors.
  • Immunosuppression: Conditions that reduce immune surveillance increase susceptibility.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Assessment

Persistent vulval pruritus (itching) is the most common presenting symptom of vulval cancer, often leading to months of self-medication before diagnosis. Visible or palpable abnormalities include raised lesions, ulcers, wart-like growths, or patches of altered skin pigmentation (white, red, or dark areas). Patients may also report localized pain, burning, bleeding, or foul-smelling discharge if the lesion has ulcerated and become infected. A thorough physical assessment requires adequate lighting, a chaperone, and careful inspection of all vulval structures, noting the exact size, borders, and fixation of any lesion, alongside palpation of the groin for lymphadenopathy.

Diagnostic Workup and Biopsy

Definitive diagnosis requires a tissue biopsy of the suspicious lesion. Before performing a biopsy, local anesthetic is administered, and the area is inspected using a colposcope to identify optimal biopsy sites, particularly areas with atypical vascular patterns. Punch biopsy instruments are commonly used to obtain a core sample that includes both epithelium and superficial stroma, allowing the pathologist to assess the depth of invasion. Further staging investigations, such as pelvic magnetic resonance imaging or computed tomography scans, may be ordered if the lesion is large or clinically invasive, helping to evaluate regional lymph node involvement and distant metastasis.

Surgical and Medical Management

Treatment strategies depend on the stage of the tumour, patient health status, and histological subtype. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of therapy for localized vulval cancer.

  • Wide Local Excision: Performed for early-stage lesions, removing the tumour with a clear margin of healthy tissue.
  • Radical Vulvectomy: Involves removing a substantial portion or all of the vulva, often paired with inguinofemoral lymphadenectomy for advanced cases.
  • Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy: Used in selected early cases to map and sample the first draining node, sparing patients unnecessary full node dissections and reducing lymphedema risk.
  • Radiotherapy and Chemotherapy: Employed as primary treatment for unresectable tumours, as neoadjuvant therapy to shrink large lesions before surgery, or as adjuvant therapy for positive margins and node-positive disease.

Nursing Care and Postoperative Support

Postoperative nursing care for patients undergoing vulval surgery focuses on wound healing, pain management, and psychological support. The vulval region has high vascularity and moisture, creating a high risk for wound infection and breakdown. Nurses must perform meticulous wound care, keeping the surgical site clean and dry, and utilizing techniques such as sitz baths or gentle irrigation after micturition. Urinary catheterization is often maintained for several days to prevent urine from contaminating surgical incisions. Because vulval surgery alters body image and sexual function, providing a compassionate, non-judgmental environment for the patient to express emotional distress is an integral component of holistic nursing recovery.

Complications and Prognosis

Prognosis for vulval tumours is heavily dependent on the stage at diagnosis, particularly the presence and extent of lymph node metastasis. When detected early while confined to the vulva, survival rates are high. However, if cancer spreads to the inguinofemoral lymph nodes, prognosis declines significantly. Short-term complications of treatment include wound dehiscence, infection, and deep vein thrombosis, whereas long-term issues involve chronic lower limb lymphedema, dyspareunia, and altered urinary stream direction. Routine postoperative follow-up is critical for early detection of local recurrence.
