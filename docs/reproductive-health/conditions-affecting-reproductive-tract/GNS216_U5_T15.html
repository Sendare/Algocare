Cervical Polyps

Introduction and Definition of Cervical Polyps

Cervical polyps are common, hyperplastic, benign growths that originate from the surface of the endocervix or ectocervix. They typically appear as finger-like, smooth, red or purplish projections ranging from a few millimeters to several centimeters in size. Most cervical polyps are attached by a thin stalk, known as a peduncle, protruding through the external cervical os into the vaginal canal. While the vast majority of these lesions are benign, a small percentage can undergo malignant transformation, making proper clinical evaluation essential during routine gynecological examinations.

Pathophysiology and Risk Factors

The exact pathophysiology of cervical polyps remains multifactorial, closely linked to chronic localized inflammation, hormonal stimulation, and localized congestion of cervical blood vessels. Elevated estrogen levels, whether endogenous during pregnancy or exogenous via hormone therapies, stimulate proliferation of the cervical epithelium. Chronic infection, such as cervicitis, also recruits inflammatory mediators that promote tissue overgrowth. Risk factors predisposing women to cervical polyps include: - **Hormonal Fluctuations**: Peak incidence occurs in multiparous women aged 40 to 50 experiencing perimenopausal estrogen surges. - **Chronic Cervical Inflammation**: Persistent bacterial, viral, or fungal infections of the cervix. - **Local Vascular Congestion**: Increased venous pressure within the cervical stroma leading to localized edema and tissue projection.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Many cervical polyps are entirely asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during a routine speculum examination or Pap smear. When symptoms do occur, they stem from the extreme vascularity and fragility of the mucosal surface. The most common presenting feature is abnormal uterine bleeding, which requires careful clinical differentiation from more serious endometrial pathologies. - **Intermenstrual Bleeding**: Spotting or light bleeding occurring between regular menstrual cycles. - **Postcoital Bleeding**: Spotting or bleeding triggered by physical contact during sexual intercourse. - **Postmenopausal Bleeding**: Unexpected bleeding occurring after the cessation of menses, requiring immediate investigation. - **Excessive Leukorrhea**: Increased white or yellowish mucous vaginal discharge caused by chronic inflammatory irritation.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures

A comprehensive nursing assessment involves gathering a detailed gynecological and menstrual history, specifically noting the timing and triggers of any vaginal bleeding. During a pelvic examination, the nurse assists the clinician by preparing equipment for a direct visual inspection using a bivalve speculum. Cervical polyps typically manifest as smooth, red, cherry-like growths extending from the os. Diagnostic confirmation relies on clinical visualization, gentle palpation to assess the peduncle attachment, and targeted removal followed by mandatory histopathological examination to rule out malignancy.

Medical and Surgical Management

Medical treatment is generally ineffective for resolving established cervical polyps, making simple surgical removal the gold standard of management. Polyp removal, or polypectomy, is a quick, minor outpatient procedure usually performed without anesthesia in the clinic setting. The clinician gently grasps the base of the peduncle with ring forceps and twists or snips the polyp off its attachment point. Silver nitrate or electrocautery may be applied briefly to the stalk base to achieve hemostasis if minor bleeding persists.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Post-procedure nursing care focuses on monitoring for complications such as hemorrhage or infection, and providing clear discharge instructions. The nurse instructs the patient to expect mild cramping and light spotting for a day or two following polypectomy. To prevent ascending pelvic infections, patients are advised to avoid sexual intercourse, tampon use, and douching for a specified recovery period, usually one to two weeks. Furthermore, the nurse emphasizes the importance of keeping follow-up appointments to review biopsy results and maintain routine cervical cancer screening.
