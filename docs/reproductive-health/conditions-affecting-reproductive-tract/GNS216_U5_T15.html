Cervical Polyps

Introduction and Definition of Cervical Polyps

Cervical polyps are common, benign growths that develop on the surface of the cervix or within the endocervical canal. These finger-like or cherry-like projections vary in size, usually measuring just a few millimeters to a couple of centimeters. They arise from hyperplastic tissue growth of the cervical mucosa and can be single or multiple. While most polyps are completely harmless and non-cancerous, any abnormal growth in the reproductive tract still requires proper evaluation to rule out malignancy or other conditions.

Causes and Risk Factors

The exact cause of cervical polyps is not fully understood, but several contributing factors are linked to their development. Chronic inflammation of the cervix, prolonged hormonal stimulation (particularly high estrogen levels), and local congestion of the cervical blood vessels play major roles in hyperplastic growth.

  • Hormonal fluctuations: High circulating estrogen levels during reproductive years or pregnancy frequently stimulate tissue growth.
  • Chronic cervicitis: Ongoing irritation or infection of the cervix triggers an inflammatory response that can lead to polyp formation.
  • Age: Women in their reproductive years, particularly those who have given birth and those who are perimenopausal, experience a higher incidence.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Many women with cervical polyps experience no symptoms at all, discovering the growths only during a routine speculum examination. When symptoms do occur, they are typically related to the vascular nature of the polyps.

Common signs include intermenstrual bleeding, abnormal vaginal discharge, postcoital bleeding, and heavy menstrual bleeding. A common clinical misconception is that heavy bleeding always originates from the uterine cavity; however, a fragile cervical polyp can easily bleed in response to minor friction or contact during intercourse or pelvic exams.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures

Discovering cervical polyps relies heavily on direct visualization during a physical examination. During a speculum examination, a nurse or doctor typically sees a red, purple, or pink projection protruding from the external os. To confirm the diagnosis and ensure patient safety, several steps are taken:

  1. Visual inspection: Observing the size, color, and attachment site of the polyp using a speculum.
  2. Bimanual examination: Assessing the size and mobility of the uterus and ruling out deeper pelvic pathology.
  3. Biopsy and removal: Sending the removed tissue to pathology to definitively rule out malignancy.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management of symptomatic or large cervical polyps involves simple surgical removal, a procedure known as a polypectomy. This minor procedure can often be performed directly in the clinic without general anesthesia. The clinician gently grasps the base of the polyp with forceps and twists or snips it off. The base may then be cauterized to prevent bleeding. Asymptomatic polyps may sometimes be left alone and monitored, but removal is generally preferred to relieve bothersome bleeding and allow for pathological testing.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Following a polypectomy, nursing care focuses on monitoring for complications, providing comfort, and educating the patient. Nurses must instruct the patient to expect mild cramping and light spotting for a couple of days. Vital signs are monitored if the procedure was more extensive, and comfort measures are offered.

Patients should be advised to avoid sexual intercourse, tampon use, and douching for a short period specified by the provider to allow the cervix to heal properly. The nurse also stresses the importance of attending follow-up appointments to review pathology results.
