Cancer of the Cervix

Introduction and Definition of Cervical Cancer

Cervical cancer is a malignant neoplasm originating from the cells of the cervix, the lower cylindrical part of the uterus that connects to the vagina. Globally and within West Africa, it remains a major public health concern, disproportionately affecting women in low- and middle-income countries due to limited access to routine screening programmes. The vast majority of cases develop within the **squamocolumnar junction**—often referred to as the transformation zone—where the stratified squamous epithelium of the ectocervix meets the simple columnar epithelium of the endocervix. Understanding this transition area is crucial because persistent oncogenic viral infections selectively target these dividing epithelial cells, initiating dysplasia that can progress to invasive carcinoma if left undetected.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Persistent infection with high-risk types of the **Human Papillomavirus (HPV)**, particularly genotypes 16 and 18, is the primary causative agent responsible for over ninety percent of cervical cancer cases. HPV is a sexually transmitted DNA virus that disrupts normal cellular regulatory proteins, specifically p53 and retinoblastoma proteins, leading to uncontrolled cellular proliferation. Several environmental and lifestyle factors cofactor with persistent HPV infection to increase malignant progression: - **Early age at first intercourse**: Immature cervical epithelium is more vulnerable to oncogenic insults. - **Multiple sexual partners**: Increases cumulative exposure to varied HPV strains. - **Immunosuppression**: Conditions like HIV reduce viral clearance, accelerating dysplasia. - **Multiparity**: Hormonal shifts and physical trauma during childbirth promote cellular changes. - **Tobacco smoking**: Carcinogens concentrate in cervical mucus, damaging local DNA.

Pathophysiology and Histological Types

The development of cervical cancer follows a slow, predictable continuum from normal epithelium to precancerous changes known as **cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN)**, graded from CIN 1 to CIN 3, before becoming invasive. Viral integration into host DNA forces the expression of oncoproteins E6 and E7, which deactivate tumor suppressor genes and allow damaged cells to evade apoptosis. Histologically, cervical cancers divide into two primary categories: 1. **Squamous cell carcinoma**: Accounts for approximately 85% to 90% of all cases, arising directly from the ectocervical epithelial lining. 2. **Adenocarcinoma**: Makes up about 10% to 15% of cases, originating within the mucus-secreting glandular cells of the endocervical canal, and has been noted to be slightly more challenging to detect via standard surface scrapings.

Clinical Manifestations and Staging

Early-stage cervical cancer is frequently asymptomatic, which underscores the absolute necessity of routine screening. As the lesion grows, structural fragility and vascular invasion produce characteristic clinical signs. The hallmark symptom is **abnormal vaginal bleeding**, encompassing post-coital bleeding, intermenstrual spotting, and increasingly heavy or prolonged menses in post-menopausal women. Patients may also report foul-smelling, blood-tinged vaginal discharge caused by tumor necrosis and secondary bacterial colonization. In advanced stages, local tumor extension compresses adjacent pelvic structures. Ureteral obstruction leads to hydronephrosis and uremia, while invasion of the pelvic wall or sacral plexus causes intractable lower back and radiating leg pain. The FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) staging system relies heavily on clinical examination, cystoscopy, proctoscopy, and advanced imaging such as MRI to determine the extent of local and distant spread.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Screening programs aim to identify pre-invasive lesions long before symptoms manifest. The **Papanicolaou (Pap) smear** cytology remains a cornerstone, though Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) is widely utilized in resource-limited primary health settings across Nigeria due to its immediate results and low cost. When an abnormality is identified, confirmatory diagnostic procedures follow. - **Colposcopy**: Magnified visualization of the cervix using acetic acid and Lugol's iodine to highlight dysplastic epithelium. - **Cervical Biopsy**: Directed tissue sampling from abnormal areas identified during colposcopy to provide definitive histological diagnosis. - **Endocervical Curettage**: Sampling of the endocervical canal when the transformation zone is not fully visible. - **Imaging studies**: Chest X-ray, pelvic MRI, or CT scans to assess distant metastasis or local lymph node involvement.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment modalities for cervical cancer depend strictly on the FIGO staging, the patient's general health status, and their desire for future fertility. For micro-invasive disease (Stage IA), conservative surgical options such as **cone biopsy (conization)** or simple hysterectomy may suffice. For early invasive stages (Stage IB to IIA), radical hysterectomy—involving the removal of the uterus, cervix, upper vagina, and surrounding parametrial tissue—along with pelvic lymph node dissection is standard. In locally advanced disease (Stage IIB through IVA), surgery loses efficacy, and **concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT)** becomes the treatment of choice. This protocol combines external beam radiation therapy, brachytherapy (internal radioactive sources placed directly into the uterine cavity), and radiosensitizing chemotherapy regimens such as cisplatin. Advanced metastatic disease (Stage IVB) is managed primarily with palliative systemic chemotherapy and pain management.

Nursing Care, Symptom Management, and Patient Support

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with cervical cancer addresses physical symptoms, emotional distress, and complex treatment side effects. Nurses monitor for acute complications during radiation and chemotherapy, including bone marrow suppression, gastrointestinal distress, and radiation cystitis or proctosigmoiditis. Pain management following the WHO analgesic ladder is paramount, especially for patients with nerve root compression. Beyond physical care, nurses provide empathetic psychological support. A diagnosis of reproductive organ cancer often carries profound emotional trauma, body image disturbance, and fears surrounding sexual health and fertility loss. Nurses facilitate open communication, encourage support groups, and coordinate palliative referrals when disease progression limits curative options.

Prevention, Vaccination, and Public Health Strategies in Nigeria

Primary prevention of cervical cancer centers on **prophylactic HPV vaccination** targeted at young adolescent girls before sexual debut, typically between ages 9 and 14. Quadrivalent and nonavalent vaccines offer robust protection against high-risk viral strains. Integrating these vaccines into national immunization schedules is a vital strategy for reducing future incidence. Secondary prevention relies on scaling up screening programs using a "screen-and-treat" approach with VIA or HPV DNA testing, particularly in rural Nigerian communities where cytology laboratories are scarce. Public health campaigns must also combat cultural stigmas, misinformation, and financial barriers that hinder women from seeking timely gynecological care.
