Cancer of the Cervix

Introduction and Definition of Cervical Cancer

Cancer of the cervix is a malignant neoplasm that arises from the cells lining the lower part of the uterus, known as the cervix. It typically begins as a slow-growing pre-cancerous condition before invading deeper tissues. Globally and locally in Nigeria, it remains a major public health concern, largely affecting women in their prime years. Early detection offers a high cure rate, making understanding this disease crucial for nursing practice.

Anatomy and Pathophysiology of the Cervix

The cervix consists of two main parts: the ectocervix, visible during a speculum examination and covered by squamous cells, and the endocervix, a canal lined by glandular cells. The meeting point of these two cell types is called the squamocolumnar junction, or the transformation zone.

Pathologically, almost all cervical cancers develop right inside this transformation zone. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus alters the DNA of these cells, leading to dysplasia. Over time, these abnormal cells multiply uncontrollably, break through the basement membrane, and invade surrounding pelvic structures or spread through the lymphatic system.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Persistent infection with high-risk strains of the Human Papillomavirus, particularly types 16 and 18, is the primary cause of cervical cancer. While HPV is a common sexually transmitted infection, most immune systems clear it naturally. When the virus persists, however, it drives malignant transformation.

Several co-factors increase the likelihood of persistent infection and cancer development:

  • Early onset of sexual activity: Immature cervical cells in young teenagers are more vulnerable to viral uptake.
  • Multiple sexual partners: Increases exposure risk to various HPV strains.
  • Multiparity: Having many full-term pregnancies places physical stress on cervical tissues and correlates with hormonal shifts.
  • Immunosuppression: Conditions like HIV reduce the body's ability to clear the virus.
  • Cigarette smoking: Chemical byproducts accumulate in cervical mucus and weaken local cellular immunity.

Clinical Manifestations and Staging

In its earliest stages, cervical cancer is usually asymptomatic, which highlights the necessity of routine screening. As the tumor grows and invades surrounding tissue, clinical signs begin to appear.

Common signs and symptoms include:

  • Abnormal vaginal bleeding: Intermenstrual bleeding, post-coital bleeding, or bleeding after menopause.
  • Unusual vaginal discharge: Watery, blood-tinged, foul-smelling, or purulent discharge.
  • Pelvic or lower back pain: Indicates advanced disease affecting pelvic nerves or sidewalls.
  • Urinary symptoms: Hematuria or difficulty urinating if the bladder is involved.

Staging follows the FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics) system, which categorizes the cancer from Stage I (confined strictly to the cervix) to Stage IV (spread beyond the pelvis to distant organs like the lungs or liver).

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Screening tests detect pre-cancerous changes before they become malignant. The traditional Pap smear (cytology) examines scraped cells from the cervix under a microscope, while HPV testing detects high-risk viral DNA directly. Visual Inspection with Acetic Acid (VIA) is an affordable alternative widely used in low-resource settings, where the cervix is painted with vinegar to highlight abnormal white patches.

When screening tests return abnormal results, diagnostic confirmation requires a colposcopy (magnified visual examination of the cervix) combined with targeted biopsy of suspicious areas. Histological examination of the tissue sample provides the definitive diagnosis.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment plans depend heavily on the stage of the cancer, the patient's overall health, and her desire to preserve fertility.

  • Surgical options: Early-stage cancers may be treated with conservative procedures like loop electrosurgical excision procedure (LEEP) or cone biopsy, or more radical surgeries such as a radical hysterectomy (removal of the uterus, cervix, and surrounding tissues) and pelvic lymph node dissection.
  • Radiation therapy: Can be delivered externally or internally (brachytherapy) and is often combined with chemotherapy for locally advanced disease.
  • Chemotherapy: Drugs like cisplatin are used alongside radiation to sensitize cancer cells or to treat distant metastases.

Nursing Care and Patient Support

Nurses play a vital role across the continuum of care for patients with cervical cancer. Preoperatively, nursing interventions focus on emotional support, explaining procedures, and addressing anxiety regarding body image and sexuality. Postoperatively, nurses monitor for hemorrhage, infection, and urinary retention due to bladder nerve disruption.

For patients undergoing radiation or chemotherapy, supportive care is crucial. Nurses manage side effects such as fatigue, nausea, radiation cystitis, and proctitis. Compassionate communication helps patients cope with the emotional burden of a cancer diagnosis, while discharge education emphasizes proper follow-up schedules and recognizing red-flag symptoms like heavy bleeding or severe pain.

Prevention, Vaccination, and Public Health Strategies in Nigeria

Preventing cervical cancer requires a multi-layered public health approach. Primary prevention focuses on prophylactic HPV vaccination targeted at young adolescent girls before they become sexually active. Secondary prevention relies on nationwide screening programs, utilizing low-cost methods like VIA and HPV DNA testing in primary health centers.

In Nigeria, challenges such as cultural taboos, limited access to healthcare facilities, and low awareness contribute to high mortality rates. Nurses act as community educators by dispelling myths, promoting vaccine uptake in schools and clinics, and organizing outreach screening camps to ensure early detection and treatment for women in rural and urban areas alike.
