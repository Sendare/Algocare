Cervicitis

Introduction and Definition of Cervicitis

Cervicitis is an inflammation of the uterine cervix, the lower cylindrical portion of the uterus that opens into the vagina. This condition typically involves an inflammatory response of the columnar epithelium of the endocervix, the squamous epithelium of the ectocervix, or both. It holds significant clinical relevance in women's health because it often serves as a primary port of entry for ascending reproductive tract infections. When pathogens breach the cervical barrier, they can travel upward to cause pelvic inflammatory disease, endometritis, and long-term reproductive complications such as tubal factor infertility or ectopic pregnancy. In many resource-limited settings across Nigeria, cervicitis is frequently encountered in outpatient gynecology clinics, making accurate identification and management vital for primary healthcare nursing practice.

Etiology and Common Causative Pathogens

The etiology of cervicitis spans infectious and non-infectious categories. Infectious causes are predominantly sexually transmitted infections, while non-infectious origins stem from mechanical or chemical irritation.

  • Chlamydia trachomatis: An obligate intracellular bacterium responsible for a high proportion of mucopurulent cases.
  • Neisseria gonorrhoeae: A Gram-negative diplococcus that induces a robust polymorphonuclear leukocyte response.
  • Trichomonas vaginalis: A flagellated protozoan that can cause punctate hemorrhages on the ectocervix.
  • Herpes Simplex Virus: Can cause painful ulcerations on the cervical epithelium alongside systemic viral symptoms.
  • Mycoplasma genitalium: An emerging intracellular bacterium increasingly linked to persistent or recurrent lower genital tract inflammation.
  • Non-infectious triggers: Intrauterine devices, barrier contraceptives, latex allergies, or exposure to douching products and spermicides.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

When pathogens or irritants attack the cervical epithelium, local immune cells release pro-inflammatory cytokines, triggering vasodilation, edema, and a heavy influx of neutrophils. This cellular influx accounts for the classic sign of mucopurulent discharge. The friable, inflamed sub-epithelial blood vessels easily rupture upon contact, leading to post-coital or intermenstrual bleeding. Patients may report symptoms ranging from completely asymptomatic presentations to abnormal vaginal discharge, dyspareunia, and dysuria. A clinical hallmark during speculum examination is cervical motion tenderness and easy friability, where the tissue bleeds readily when gently brushed with a swab.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough nursing assessment begins with a detailed sexual and gynecological history, noting the character of vaginal discharge, timing of abnormal bleeding, and last menstrual period. During physical assessment, the nurse prepares the patient for a bimanual and speculum pelvic examination. Diagnostic evaluation relies heavily on visualization and laboratory testing. Clinicians collect endocervical swabs for nucleic acid amplification tests to detect chlamydia and gonorrhea. Wet mount preparations can identify trichomoniasis or candidiasis. In busy Nigerian public health facilities, syndromic management protocols are often deployed when laboratory testing is delayed or unavailable, treating patients based on a constellation of predictable clinical signs.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological management focuses on eradicating causative pathogens and breaking the chain of transmission. For chlamydial infections, oral azithromycin or doxycycline is standard. Gonococcal infections require combination therapy, typically ceftriaxone injection paired with azithromycin, to combat rising antibiotic resistance patterns. If Trichomonas vaginalis is identified, oral metronidazole is prescribed. A critical component of medical management is partner treatment; without concurrent treatment of all sexual partners, reinfection rates remain exceptionally high. Nurses must reinforce adherence to medication schedules and advise temporary abstinence until treatment courses are fully completed and symptoms resolve.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Left untreated, ascending upper genital tract infections can lead to chronic pelvic pain, pelvic inflammatory disease, tubal scarring, and subsequent ectopic pregnancy or infertility. For pregnant women, untreated cervicitis increases the risk of premature rupture of membranes, preterm labor, and neonatal conjunctivitis or pneumonia. Nursing interventions center on patient advocacy, education on safe sexual practices, and emotional support. Because sexually transmitted infections carry social stigma in many communities, nurses must maintain strict confidentiality, provide a non-judgmental environment, and offer clear guidance on barrier contraception use to protect future reproductive health.
