Cervicitis

Introduction and Definition of Cervicitis

Cervicitis is defined as an acute or chronic inflammation of the uterine cervix, specifically targeting the columnar epithelium of the endocervical canal. As a prevalent condition encountered in gynecological practice across Nigeria, it is most frequently associated with sexually transmitted infections (STIs), though non-infectious causes also exist. Understanding cervicitis is vital for nursing students because untreated cervical inflammation can ascend to the upper reproductive tract, leading to significant morbidity such as pelvic inflammatory disease, chronic pelvic pain, and potential infertility. Nurses play a frontline role in identifying risk factors, recognizing clinical signs, and providing culturally sensitive health education to patients presenting with reproductive tract symptoms. The condition can manifest with subtle symptoms or remain entirely asymptomatic, especially in women infected with Chlamydia trachomatis, making routine screening and thorough nursing assessment indispensable components of holistic female healthcare delivery in diverse clinical settings.

Etiology and Common Causative Pathogens

The etiology of cervicitis is broadly divided into infectious and non-infectious categories. Infectious causes are predominantly sexually transmitted pathogens, with Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae being the most common bacterial agents globally and in regional Nigerian populations. Other infectious microorganisms include Trichomonas vaginalis, Mycoplasma genitalium, and Herpes simplex virus (HSV), which can cause distinct ulcerative or inflammatory changes. Non-infectious etiologies include mechanical or chemical irritation from foreign bodies such as barrier contraceptives, latex condoms, diaphragms, or intrauterine devices, as well as allergic reactions to spermicides, douches, or feminine hygiene products. Identifying the specific causative organism is crucial for guiding targeted antimicrobial therapy and halting the transmission cycle. Nurses must take a comprehensive sexual and product-use history to help differentiate between infectious transmission vectors and localized irritants.

Pathophysiology of Cervical Inflammation

The pathophysiology of cervicitis involves an inflammatory response mounted by the mucosal tissue of the cervix in reaction to microbial invasion or physical and chemical trauma. When pathogens adhere to or penetrate the columnar epithelial cells of the endocervix, they trigger the release of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines. This biochemical signaling cascade results in localized vasodilation, increased capillary permeability, and a heavy influx of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (neutrophils) into the infected mucosal tissue. Clinically, this massive cellular recruitment manifests as cervical erythema, edema, and mucosal friability, meaning the tissue easily bleeds upon gentle contact, such as during a speculum examination or coitus. In chronic cervicitis, a persistent lymphocytic and plasma cell infiltrate replaces the acute neutrophilic response, potentially leading to squamous metaplasia or glandular distortion. Nurses understanding this inflammatory process can better anticipate why patients experience post-coital bleeding and mucopurulent discharge.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Clinical manifestations of cervicitis vary depending on the severity and causative agent, though many individuals remain asymptomatic. When symptoms do occur, the most hallmark presentations include abnormal vaginal discharge, which is often purulent or mucopurulent (yellowish or greenish), and intermenstrual or post-coital bleeding due to cervical friability. Patients may also complain of dyspareunia (pain during sexual intercourse), lower abdominal discomfort, and dysuria if concurrent urethral involvement is present. During a speculum examination, healthcare providers commonly observe cervical erythema, edema, and visible mucopurulent exudate oozing from the os. Because these symptoms overlap significantly with other lower reproductive tract infections like vaginitis or pelvic inflammatory disease, a detailed nursing and medical evaluation is essential to establish an accurate diagnosis and prevent misdirected treatment regimens.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Nursing assessment for a client with suspected cervicitis begins with taking a thorough gynecological, obstetric, and sexual history, noting symptoms such as abnormal discharge, dyspareunia, and bleeding patterns. Nurses must approach the patient with empathy, maintaining privacy and confidentiality to build trust. Diagnostic evaluation relies heavily on a physical speculum examination to inspect the cervix for erythema, edema, and friability, alongside the collection of cervical swabs. Diagnostic testing includes nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) for Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae, wet mount preparations for trichomoniasis or bacterial vaginosis, and pelvic examination to rule out uterine tenderness indicative of upper tract involvement. Nurses play an essential role in preparing the patient for the speculum examination, explaining each step of the procedure, labeling specimens correctly, and ensuring safe handling to prevent contamination and diagnostic errors.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical and pharmacological management of cervicitis is tailored to the specific causative organism identified through diagnostic testing. If an STI such as Chlamydia trachomatis is confirmed or strongly suspected, treatment typically involves single-dose azithromycin or a course of doxycycline. For Neisseria gonorrhoeae, combination therapy using intramuscular ceftriaxone is standard practice to combat growing antibiotic resistance patterns. If Trichomonas vaginalis is identified, oral metronidazole or tinidazole is prescribed. In cases where pathogens are not immediately identified, empiric treatment covering both chlamydia and gonorrhea is often initiated promptly, especially in high-prevalence settings, to prevent complications. An essential component of pharmacological management is partner treatment; sexual partners within the last 60 days must be evaluated and treated simultaneously to prevent immediate reinfection. Nurses are responsible for administering medications, educating clients on correct dosage regimens, and emphasizing the necessity of completing the full course of therapy.

Potential Complications if Left Untreated

If left untreated, cervicitis can lead to several severe and long-term reproductive complications. The primary risk is the ascent of the infectious pathogen through the internal os into the upper reproductive tract, resulting in pelvic inflammatory disease (PID). PID can cause chronic pelvic pain, tubo-ovarian abscesses, and permanent scarring of the fallopian tubes, significantly increasing the future risk of ectopic pregnancy and tubal factor infertility. Additionally, persistent inflammation and mucosal disruption compromise the local immunological barrier, thereby facilitating the acquisition and transmission of Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) and other viral STIs. In pregnant women, untreated cervicitis associated with chlamydia or gonorrhea can cause adverse obstetrical outcomes, including premature rupture of membranes, preterm labor, chorioamnionitis, and neonatal complications such as conjunctivitis or pneumonia. Recognizing these severe risks underscores why aggressive screening, prompt treatment, and comprehensive follow-up care are fundamental nursing priorities.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Education

Nursing interventions for cervicitis focus on holistic patient care, symptom management, and extensive health education. Nurses must advise clients to abstain from sexual intercourse until treatment is successfully completed and follow-up cultures confirm pathogen eradication, usually seven days after single-dose therapy or upon finishing a multi-day regimen. Education should include proper medication adherence, emphasizing that symptoms may resolve before the infection is fully cleared. Nurses must counsel patients on safe sex practices, including the consistent and correct use of barrier methods like male and female condoms to reduce future infection risks. Furthermore, nurses should promote regular gynecological check-ups and screening, explain the importance of treating sexual partners, and offer non-judgmental counseling regarding reproductive health and hygiene practices, such as avoiding harsh douches or chemical feminine sprays that can disrupt normal vaginal flora and cause non-infectious irritation.
