Vulval Inflammation

Introduction to Vulval Inflammation

Vulval inflammation, commonly referred to as vulvitis, is a localized inflammatory condition affecting the external female genitalia. This includes the labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, and the vaginal opening. Because the vulva has sensitive mucosal and cutaneous tissues, it reacts strongly to irritants, infections, and systemic skin disorders. While vulvitis can occur alone, it frequently overlaps with vaginitis (inflammation of the vagina) because of the close anatomical proximity between the two structures. Nurses must understand that vulvitis is not a disease in itself, but rather a clinical sign pointing to an underlying infectious, allergic, or inflammatory trigger.

Etiology and Common Causes of Vulvitis

The causes of vulval inflammation are diverse, ranging from simple hygiene products to complex dermatological conditions. Clinicians generally group these triggers into infectious, irritant, allergic, and systemic categories.

  • Infectious agents: Yeast infections like *Candida albicans*, sexually transmitted infections such as herpes simplex virus, human papillomavirus, and bacterial vaginosis.
  • Irritants: Harsh soaps, bubble baths, synthetic underwear, scented pantyliners, laundry detergents, and tight clothing that traps moisture.
  • Allergic reactions: Contact dermatitis triggered by spermicides, latex condoms, feminine wipes, or topical medications.
  • Dermatological conditions: Lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, and eczema which cause chronic skin changes and intense pruritus.
  • Hormonal shifts: Low estrogen levels during menopause can cause atrophic changes, making the vulval tissue thin, dry, and easily inflamed.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Patients presenting with vulvitis describe a distinct set of uncomfortable symptoms that significantly impact their daily lives. The clinical presentation varies slightly depending on the root cause, but common signs include:

  1. Pruritus: Persistent, intense itching of the external genitalia is the hallmark symptom of almost all forms of vulvitis.
  2. Erythema and Edema: Visual inspection often reveals noticeable redness and swelling of the labia and surrounding tissues.
  3. Pain and Burning: A stinging sensation, particularly when passing urine or during physical movement and sexual intercourse (dyspareunia).
  4. Excoriation: Scratch marks, skin thickening (lichenification), or raw weeping areas caused by repeated scratching.
  5. Abnormal Discharge: While the discharge originates from the vagina, it often pools in the vulval folds, contributing to irritation and skin breakdown.

Diagnostic Assessment and Investigations

Diagnosing the exact cause of vulvitis requires a careful history, a thorough physical examination, and targeted laboratory tests. Nurses assist the healthcare provider by preparing the patient and gathering necessary specimen collection tools.

The physical examination involves inspecting the vulva, perineum, and perianal region under good lighting, noting the distribution of redness, lesions, or skin changes. Diagnostic tests commonly include:

  • Wet mount microscopy: Mixing a vaginal and vulval discharge sample with saline or potassium hydroxide to check for yeast cells, trichomonads, or clue cells.
  • Swab cultures: Collecting samples to test for bacterial, viral, or sexually transmitted pathogens.
  • Skin patch testing: Used if allergic contact dermatitis is suspected from soaps or topical products.
  • Vulval biopsy: A small tissue sample taken under local anesthesia if chronic skin conditions, precancerous changes, or persistent lesions do not respond to standard treatment.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management of vulvitis focuses on relieving discomfort, treating the primary cause, and restoring the natural skin barrier of the vulva. Medical treatment depends entirely on the diagnostic findings: antifungal creams for candidiasis, antibiotics for bacterial infections, or low-potency topical corticosteroids for inflammatory skin conditions.

Nursing care centers on comfort measures and patient advocacy. Nurses should encourage sitz baths using plain warm water to soothe irritated tissue and promote cleanliness. Applying cool compresses can temporarily dull intense itching. It is vital to instruct patients to keep the vulval area dry and avoid scratching, as scratching breaks the skin and leads to secondary bacterial infections. Nurses also monitor for treatment effectiveness and provide emotional support, as chronic vulval conditions often cause significant distress and anxiety.

Patient Education and Preventive Care

Preventing recurrent vulvitis relies heavily on empowering patients with correct hygiene and lifestyle habits. Nurses play a crucial education role by clearing up common misconceptions about feminine hygiene products.

Patients should be advised to wash the vulva using only plain water or mild, unscented cleansers, avoiding harsh soaps and douching entirely, as douching destroys the protective vaginal flora. Wearing breathable, 100% cotton underwear and avoiding tight trousers or nylon shapewear helps reduce moisture accumulation. Additionally, wiping from front to back after using the toilet prevents fecal bacteria from contaminating the vulval and vaginal areas. Changing wet swimsuits or sweaty workout clothes promptly further minimizes the risk of microbial overgrowth.
