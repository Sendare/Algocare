Vulval Inflammation

Introduction to Vulval Inflammation

Vulval inflammation, clinically termed vulvitis, refers to the irritation, redness, and swelling of the external female genitalia. This condition is not a standalone disease but rather a symptom complex arising from various dermatological, infectious, or allergic triggers. Because the vulva possesses a warm, moist microenvironment and sensitive mucosal tissue, it is uniquely vulnerable to external irritants and opportunistic pathogens. In Nigerian clinical practice, patients presenting with vulval discomfort often delay seeking care due to cultural stigma or self-medication with harsh traditional concoctions, which frequently exacerbates the inflammatory process. Understanding this condition requires a systematic approach to identifying underlying etiologies rather than merely providing symptomatic relief.

Etiology and Common Causes of Vulvitis

The triggers for vulval inflammation span several distinct categories, ranging from infectious agents to contact dermatitis. Clinicians categorize these causes to guide targeted therapy.

  • Infectious Agents: Candida albicans (yeast), bacterial vaginosis, Trichomonas vaginalis, herpes simplex virus, and human papillomavirus.
  • Contact Irritants: Perfumed soaps, synthetic underwear, laundry detergents, feminine hygiene sprays, and topical herbal preparations.
  • Dermatological Conditions: Lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, eczema, and psoriasis affecting the anogenital region.
  • Hormonal Factors: Estrogen deficiency during menopause leading to atrophic vulvitis and thinning of the mucosal epithelium.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Patients experiencing vulvitis present with a constellation of localized symptoms that can severely impact daily comfort and quality of life.

  • Pruritus: Persistent, intense itching is the most common hallmark symptom, often worsening at night.
  • Erythema and Edema: Visible redness and swelling of the labia and surrounding structures due to localized vasodilation and fluid exudation.
  • Pain and Burning: Stinging sensations, particularly when urine contacts inflamed, abraded mucosal surfaces during micturition.
  • Excoriation and Fissures: Linear breaks in the skin caused by scratching, which can weep serum or bleed.
  • Atypical Discharge: Altered vaginal or vulvar secretions depending on the underlying infectious or inflammatory etiology.

Diagnostic Assessment and Investigations

Accurate diagnosis relies on a thorough clinical history and physical examination, supplemented by targeted laboratory investigations. The nurse plays a pivotal role in preparing the patient, ensuring privacy, and collecting specimens correctly.

  • History Taking: Inquire about hygiene practices, new soaps, contraceptive use, sexual history, and the duration of symptoms.
  • Inspection: Direct visualization using adequate lighting to assess the pattern of erythema, presence of lesions, ulcers, or lichenification.
  • High Vaginal Swab (HVS): Collected for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity to identify bacterial or fungal pathogens.
  • Wet Mount Preparation: Saline and potassium hydroxide (KOH) preparations to check for yeast hyphae, clue cells, or trichomonads under a microscope.
  • Vulvar Biopsy: Indicated for persistent, non-healing lesions or suspected vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN) to rule out malignancy.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management of vulvitis targets the underlying cause while providing aggressive symptomatic relief to break the itch-scratch cycle. Medical treatment typically involves antifungal creams, topical corticosteroids for non-infectious dermatoses, or specific antimicrobial therapy.

Nursing care focuses on comfort measures and patient advocacy. Sitz baths using plain warm water help soothe irritated tissues and cleanse the area gently. Applying cool compresses can temporarily numb nerve endings and reduce acute pruritus. Nurses must instruct patients to keep the vulvar area clean and dry, avoid scratching despite the urge, and adhere strictly to prescribed medication regimens. In tropical climates like Nigeria, promoting loose, breathable cotton clothing is vital to reduce local humidity and prevent fungal proliferation.

Patient Education and Preventive Care

Empowering patients with correct hygiene and self-care knowledge is the cornerstone of preventing recurrent vulvitis. Many cases stem from well-intentioned but misguided hygiene practices. Nurses should explicitly advise patients to avoid douching, as it disrupts the normal vaginal microbiome and protective acidic pH. Vulvar cleansing should be restricted to the external structures using plain water or mild, unfragranced cleansers, wiping strictly from front to back after elimination to prevent enteric pathogen contamination. Additionally, patients should be educated on recognizing early signs of irritation so they can seek prompt medical attention before secondary skin breakdown occurs.

Potential Complications

Unmanaged or chronic vulvitis can lead to progressive physical and psychological sequelae. Persistent scratching breaks the skin barrier, allowing secondary bacterial infections like cellulitis or impetigo to establish. Over time, chronic inflammation and repeated trauma can cause lichenification—thickening and leathery hardening of the vulvar skin—making the area permanently prone to fissuring and severe pain during sexual intercourse (dyspareunia). Furthermore, the relentless discomfort, sleep disturbance from nocturnal itching, and visible skin changes frequently contribute to significant psychological distress, anxiety, and intimacy issues for the patient.
