Vulval Inflammation

Introduction to Vulval Inflammation

Vulval inflammation, commonly referred to as vulvitis, is a prevalent clinical presentation encountered in gynecological and primary health nursing care across Nigeria. It represents an inflammatory response of the external female genitalia, encompassing the labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, and the vestibule. Because the vulvar skin is sensitive and continuously exposed to a warm, moist environment, friction, and various chemical agents, it is highly susceptible to acute and chronic irritative or infectious processes. For nursing students, understanding vulval inflammation is essential for providing holistic, empathetic, and culturally sensitive care to female patients. Patients presenting with vulvar discomfort often experience significant psychological distress, anxiety, and embarrassment due to the sensitive nature of the symptoms. Furthermore, cultural beliefs and stigmatization surrounding reproductive health in certain Nigerian communities may delay presentation to healthcare facilities, occasionally leading to chronicity and secondary infections. Nurses play a frontline role in establishing a therapeutic, non-judgmental rapport, conducting systematic physical assessments, and differentiating between infectious vulvitis, dermatological conditions, and systemic diseases. Recognizing the multi-factorial nature of vulvitis enables nurses to advocate effectively for comprehensive patient evaluations and individualized care plans, bridging the gap between clinical intervention and patient comfort.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Vulva

A thorough grasp of the anatomy and physiology of the vulva is fundamental for nursing students evaluating vulval health. The vulva is richly supplied by blood vessels, lymphatic drainage systems, and sensory nerve endings, making the area extremely sensitive to trauma, inflammation, and pain. The skin of the labia majora contains hair follicles and sebaceous glands, while the labia minora consist of non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium devoid of hair follicles but rich in sebaceous and sweat glands. This structural composition creates a microenvironment that is prone to moisture retention, friction, and maceration. Physiologically, the vulvar tissues maintain a delicate barrier function supported by normal acidic pH levels (though slightly higher than the vaginal vault, ranging around 4.0 to 5.0 in adjacent areas) and a resident microflora primarily composed of lactobacilli that inhibit pathogenic colonization. Hormonal influences, particularly estrogen during reproductive years, maintain mucosal thickness, glycogen content, and tissue elasticity. Conversely, hypoestrogenic states such as pre-pubescence and post-menopause lead to thinning of the vulvar epithelium, reduced glandular secretions, decreased elasticity, and increased susceptibility to trauma, dryness, and inflammatory conditions. Understanding these physiological nuances assists nurses in identifying why certain age groups or hormonal states are predisposed to specific types of vulvitis.

Etiology and Common Causes of Vulvitis

The etiology of vulvitis is diverse, requiring nurses to categorize causes systematically to plan effective interventions. Common causes can be broadly grouped into infectious, irritative/allergic, and dermatological categories. Infectious vulvitis frequently stems from sexually transmitted infections (STIs) such as Candida albicans (candidiasis), Trichomonas vaginalis, herpes simplex virus, and human papillomavirus. Non-STI infections can also occur due to bacterial vaginosis overgrowth or poor perineal hygiene leading to secondary bacterial invasion by streptococci or staphylococci. Irritative and allergic vulvitis arise from contact with exogenous substances, including perfumed soaps, bubble baths, synthetic underwear, tight clothing, scented sanitary pads, detergents, and topical contraceptives or spermicides. In tropical climates like Nigeria, high ambient temperatures and humidity promote excessive sweating, leading to intertrigo and chemical irritation from trapped moisture and urine. Furthermore, chronic dermatological conditions such as lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, contact dermatitis, and psoriasis frequently manifest primarily on the vulva, causing persistent inflammation, architectural changes, and intense pruritus. Identifying the precise etiology is crucial because treating infectious vulvitis with topical steroids intended for contact dermatitis would exacerbate the underlying pathology, emphasizing the need for accurate differential nursing assessments.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Clinical manifestations of vulvitis vary depending on the underlying etiology, duration, and severity of the inflammatory process. The cardinal symptoms reported by patients typically include severe pruritus (itching), burning, soreness, and localized pain of the external genitalia. Physical examination by a trained nurse or clinician often reveals visible signs of inflammation, such as erythema (redness), edema (swelling), excoriations resulting from continuous scratching, and sometimes vesiculation, ulceration, or fissuring of the skin and mucous membranes. In cases of infectious candidiasis, a thick, white, cottage-cheese-like discharge may be present, though it originates primarily from the vagina and pools in the vulvar vestibule. Conversely, trichomoniasis may present with a frothy, yellowish-green discharge accompanied by a foul odor and intense vulvovaginal irritation. Dermatological conditions like lichen sclerosus may display classic porcelain-white plaques, atrophy, and resorption of the labial architecture over time. Patients frequently report that symptoms worsen during micturition (dysuria) due to urine coming into contact with inflamed, excoriated skin, or during sexual intercourse (dyspareunia). Nurses must document these findings meticulously, noting the exact distribution, color, and character of lesions, as this information guides medical diagnosis and evaluates the ongoing effectiveness of therapeutic interventions.

Diagnostic Assessment and Investigations

Diagnostic assessment and investigations for vulvitis require a systematic, sensitive, and sterile approach to determine the precise causative agent and rule out underlying systemic or malignant conditions. The diagnostic process begins with a comprehensive nursing and medical history, exploring the onset, duration, hygiene practices, sexual history, use of potential irritants, and previous treatments. Physical examination involves inspecting the vulva, perineum, and perianal region under adequate lighting, noting any lesions, discharge, or structural changes. To identify infectious etiologies, healthcare providers collect high vaginal and vulvar swab specimens for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity (MC&S). A wet mount preparation (saline and 10% potassium hydroxide or KOH wet mount) is frequently performed in clinic settings to visualize fungal hyphae, spores, trichomonads, or clue cells indicative of bacterial vaginosis. Measurement of vaginal pH can also assist in differential diagnosis. If a chronic, non-healing lesion or atypical plaque is observed, particularly in older women, a vulvar biopsy is mandatory to rule out vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN) or squamous cell carcinoma. Nurses assist patients throughout these procedures by explaining each step to reduce anxiety, ensuring privacy, positioning the patient comfortably in the lithotomy position, and properly labeling and transporting specimens to the laboratory.

Medical and Nursing Management

Medical and nursing management of vulvitis focuses on eradicating the underlying cause, relieving distressing symptoms, promoting tissue healing, and preventing recurrences. Medical treatment is etiology-specific: antifungal agents (such as topical or oral azoles) are prescribed for candidiasis, antibiotics or antiparasitics (like metronidazole) for bacterial and trichomonal infections, and topical corticosteroids or calcineurin inhibitors for inflammatory dermatoses like lichen sclerosus or contact dermatitis. In cases of severe edema, cool sitz baths or cold compresses applied for 15-20 minutes provide immediate symptomatic relief. Nursing management centers on implementing these medical orders while delivering compassionate, individualized patient care. Nurses administer prescribed medications, monitor for adverse effects, and instruct patients on proper application techniques for topical creams and ointments. Pain management is prioritized; mild analgesics may be administered, and barrier ointments (such as zinc oxide or petroleum jelly) can be applied to protect raw skin from urine during micturition. Nurses also maintain meticulous documentation of the healing progress, assess the patient's coping mechanisms, and collaborate closely with multidisciplinary teams, including gynecologists and dermatologists, to ensure comprehensive patient recovery.

Patient Education and Preventive Care

Patient education and preventive care form the cornerstone of long-term management and recurrence prevention in vulval inflammation. Nurses hold a pivotal responsibility in imparting accurate health literacy regarding perineal hygiene and self-care practices. Patients should be advised to wash the vulvar area gently using plain, warm water or mild, non-perfumed cleansers, avoiding harsh soaps, bubble baths, feminine hygiene sprays, and scented wipes that disrupt the natural protective barrier and alter pH. Proper wiping technique—from front to back after defecation—is emphasized to prevent the transfer of enteric pathogens like Escherichia coli to the vulvovaginal region. Clothing choices significantly impact vulvar health; nurses should educate patients to wear loose-fitting, 100% cotton underwear and avoid tight trousers, leggings, or synthetic undergarments that trap heat and moisture. Wet swimwear or damp workout clothes should be changed promptly. For postmenopausal women experiencing atrophic changes, discussing the use of prescribed vaginal estrogen creams or non-hormonal moisturizers can prevent dryness and friction-induced micro-tears. Empowering patients with these practical preventive strategies fosters self-management, reduces the frequency of acute flare-ups, and enhances overall reproductive health and well-being.

Potential Complications

While acute vulvitis is often self-limiting or easily treated, failing to manage the condition effectively or ignoring persistent symptoms can lead to significant complications. One of the primary local complications is secondary bacterial infection, resulting from breaks in the skin barrier caused by scratching excoriated areas, which can progress to cellulitis or localized abscess formation. Chronic, unrelenting pruritus and pain severely disrupt sleep patterns, impair daily functioning, and contribute to psychological distress, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. In chronic inflammatory dermatoses like untreated lichen sclerosus, scarring can lead to permanent architectural distortion, such as labial fusion, clitoral resorption, and stenosis of the vaginal introitus (introital stenosis), which subsequently causes severe dyspareunia and mechanical sexual dysfunction. Furthermore, chronic inflammation and certain viral etiologies (such as persistent high-risk HPV strains) can increase the long-term risk of malignant transformation into vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN) or squamous cell carcinoma. Nurses must remain vigilant during follow-up assessments to identify early signs of these complications, provide timely referrals to specialized gynecological services, and offer psychological support to patients coping with the chronic sequelae of vulval disease.
