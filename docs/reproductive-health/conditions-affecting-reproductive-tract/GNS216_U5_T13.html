Rectovaginal Fistula

Introduction and Definition of Rectovaginal Fistula

A rectovaginal fistula is an abnormal epithelialized tract that establishes a connection between the rectum and the posterior wall of the vagina. This abnormal opening allows gas and fecal matter to continuously pass from the bowel into the vaginal vault, creating a distressing and socially isolating condition for the affected woman. In low-resource settings, this condition carries a profound psychological burden due to persistent malodor and physical discomfort. The pathophysiology centers on a breakdown of the structural integrity separating the lower gastrointestinal and reproductive tracts, disrupting normal pelvic organ compartmentalization.

Anatomy and Pathophysiology of the Anovaginal Septum

The anovaginal septum is a thin, fibrous partition separating the anterior rectal wall from the posterior vaginal wall. Structurally, it relies on the integrity of the perineal body and surrounding fascial planes for support. When this septum is compromised by trauma, ischemia, or chronic inflammation, the mucosal layers of the rectum and vagina can adhere and epithelialize. Once the tract epithelializes, spontaneous closure becomes extremely rare, requiring surgical intervention to excise the tract and re-establish separate tissue planes.

Etiology and Risk Factors in Local Practice

  • Obstetric Trauma: Prolonged or obstructed labor is the leading cause in many developing regions, often resulting from pressure necrosis.
  • Surgical Complications: Accidental lacerations during perineorrhaphy, episiotomy extensions, or pelvic surgeries can damage the septum.
  • Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Conditions like Crohn's disease cause chronic inflammation and deep tissue ulceration.
  • Malignancy and Radiation: Advanced pelvic cancers or therapeutic radiation therapy cause tissue breakdown and ischemic necrosis.
  • Infections: Severe pelvic abscesses or perineal sepsis can erode through the tissue walls.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment

Patients typically present with complaints of passing flatus or feces through the vagina, a symptom known as flatus vaginalis or fecal incontinence per vaginam. A careful nursing history should explore the onset of symptoms, particularly in relation to recent childbirth, pelvic surgery, or inflammatory bowel flare-ups. Physical examination requires a gentle and empathetic approach, utilizing a sterile speculum and digital rectal examination to visualize or palpate the opening along the posterior vaginal wall. Nurses must document the size, location, and condition of surrounding tissues, as inflammation must subside before any definitive repair can be planned.

Diagnostic Evaluation

While physical examination often confirms the diagnosis, imaging and specialized tests help map out complex tracts. A methylene blue dye test can be performed by placing a soaked gauze in the vagina and instilling dye into the rectum to visualize leakage. Endosonography or transrectal ultrasound provides detailed images of the sphincter muscles and the rectovaginal septum. Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is reserved for complex cases associated with Crohn's disease, recurrent fistulas, or suspected malignancy.

Preoperative Nursing Care and Bowel Preparation

Preoperative care focuses on optimizing tissue health and ensuring a clean surgical field. Nurses administer prescribed bowel preparations, low-residue diets, and sometimes prophylactic antibiotics to reduce the fecal bacterial load. Nutritional support is vital, especially for patients who are malnourished or anemic from chronic disease. Emotional support is equally critical, as patients often experience deep anxiety, shame, and depression regarding their condition. Nurses must offer reassurance, explain every step of the preparation, and validate their feelings.

Surgical Interventions and Intraoperative Care

  • Transvaginal Repair: Accessing and closing the fistula tract from the vaginal side; often used for low rectovaginal fistulas.
  • Transperineal Repair: Involving an incision through the perineal body to provide wide exposure of the septum and sphincter muscles.
  • Transanal Repair: Approaching the defect through the rectal lumen, sometimes using flap advancements.
  • Abdominal Approach: Used for complex fistulas associated with Crohn's disease or diverticular disease, occasionally requiring a temporary diverting colostomy.

Postoperative Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Postoperative management requires meticulous wound care to protect the surgical repair. Nurses must monitor vital signs, assess perineal dressings for excessive bleeding or drainage, and maintain strict perineal hygiene. Stool softeners are routinely administered to prevent straining during defecation, which can disrupt the suture line. Patients are generally instructed to avoid rectal temperatures, enemas, or digital examinations. Early mobilization is encouraged, but activities that place excessive tension on the perineum should be avoided.

Complications and Long-Term Prognosis

While many primary repairs are successful, potential complications include fistula recurrence, wound dehiscence, rectovaginal stenosis, and persistent fecal or urinary incontinence. Success rates depend heavily on the underlying cause, tissue vascularity, and surgical technique. Long-term rehabilitation may involve pelvic floor muscle exercises (Kegel exercises) to restore sphincter tone. With proper surgical care, adequate nutritional support, and patient compliance with postoperative restrictions, the prognosis for full recovery and return to normal social and physical activities is generally favorable.
