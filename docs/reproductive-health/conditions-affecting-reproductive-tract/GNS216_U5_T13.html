Rectovaginal Fistula

Introduction and Definition of Rectovaginal Fistula

A rectovaginal fistula (RVF) represents an abnormal epithelialized tract connecting the lumen of the rectum to the posterior wall of the vagina. This condition results in the unintended passage of flatus and fecal matter through the vaginal vault, creating profound physical, social, and psychological distress for the affected woman. In low-resource settings across sub-Saharan Africa, including Nigeria, the condition carries a heavy social stigma due to persistent fecal incontinence and odor. Understanding the anatomy of this abnormal communication is fundamental for nursing assessment and effective patient advocacy.

Anatomy and Pathophysiology of the Anovaginal Septum

The anovaginal septum is a delicate fibrous and muscular partition separating the lower rectum from the posterior vaginal wall. Pathophysiologically, any process that compromises the integrity of this tissue layer—whether through ischemic necrosis, direct trauma, or chronic inflammation—can lead to fistula formation. Once epithelial cells line the compromised tract from both mucosal surfaces, spontaneous closure becomes virtually impossible, necessitating surgical intervention to restore anatomical separation.

Etiology and Risk Factors in Nigerian Practice

In Nigerian clinical practice, the etiology of rectovaginal fistulas differs somewhat from high-income nations. While obstetric trauma remains a leading cause globally, prolonged and obstructed labor is a particularly dominant factor in rural settings, often overlapping with obstetric fistula presentations. Other significant causes include:

  • Obstetric Trauma: Severe perineal lacerations (third and fourth degree) during vaginal delivery, especially with inadequate repair or wound breakdown.
  • Surgical Complications: Accidental intraoperative injury during gynecological or colorectal procedures, or staple line dehiscence after low anterior resection.
  • Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Crohn's disease can cause transmural inflammation leading to fistula tracts.
  • Malignancy and Radiation: Pelvic radiation therapy or advanced rectal/cervical cancers causing tissue necrosis.
  • Infections and Foreign Bodies: Perirectal abscess rupture or retained surgical sponges.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Assessment

Patients with a rectovaginal fistula present with a constellation of symptoms that can profoundly impact daily functioning. Nurses must conduct a sensitive, thorough history and physical assessment.

  • Flatus per Vagina: The involuntary passage of gas through the vagina, often the earliest and most distressing symptom.
  • Fecal Incontinence: Leakage of liquid or solid stool from the vagina, varying with stool consistency.
  • Vaginal Discharge: Chronic, foul-smelling, or purulent discharge resulting from contamination.
  • Recurrent Vaginitis: Persistent vaginal infections or irritation due to altered local flora and moisture.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Investigative Procedures

Confirming the diagnosis and mapping the exact location, size, and number of fistula tracts requires targeted investigations. A careful physical examination using a speculum and digital rectal examination is the first step. Additional diagnostic tools include methylene blue dye tests, where dye is instilled into the rectum while a white gauze is placed in the vagina. Anovaginal ultrasound, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and barium enema or fistulography may be utilized to delineate complex tracts and assess sphincter integrity before planning corrective surgery.

Preoperative Nursing Care and Optimization

Preoperative preparation aims to optimize tissue health, clear the bowel, and provide psychological support. Nurses play a crucial role in ensuring the surgical field is as clean and viable as possible. Bowel preparation regimens, nutritional support, and smoking cessation counseling are standard elements. For complex or recurrent fistulas, a temporary diverting colostomy may be performed weeks before the definitive repair to allow acute inflammation to subside and local tissues to heal.

Surgical Interventions and Intraoperative Considerations

Surgical closure depends on the fistula's size, location, and underlying etiology. Common approaches include trans-rectal, trans-vaginal, and perineal flap procedures. The core surgical principles require meticulous mobilization of tissue layers without tension, layered closure to prevent overlapping suture lines, and excision of the fibrous fistula tract. Intraoperative nursing responsibilities include maintaining precise instrument counts, positioning the patient safely (often in lithotomy or jackknife position), and protecting pressure points during prolonged pelvic procedures.

Postoperative Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Postoperative management focuses on protecting the surgical repair, managing pain, and promoting comfort. Nurses must monitor for signs of hemorrhage, infection, or wound dehiscence. Stool softeners are routinely prescribed to ensure soft, painless bowel movements and prevent straining against the suture line. Catheter care is maintained to prevent urinary retention and contamination. Patient education on perineal hygiene, activity restrictions, and recognizing early warning signs of complications is vital for successful long-term recovery.

Complications and Long-term Prognosis

While many rectovaginal fistula repairs are successful on the first attempt, complications can occur. These include fistula recurrence, persistent fecal or urinary incontinence, rectovaginal stenosis, and dyspareunia (painful intercourse). The long-term prognosis depends heavily on the initial etiology, tissue quality, and surgical expertise. Nurses provide ongoing psychological support, counseling, and referrals for pelvic floor rehabilitation to help patients regain confidence and return to normal daily activities.
