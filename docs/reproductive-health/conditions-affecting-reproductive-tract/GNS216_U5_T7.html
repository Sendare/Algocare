Vulval Cyst

Introduction and Definition of Vulval Cysts

A vulval cyst is a fluid-filled sac or pouch that forms on or beneath the skin of the external female genitalia. These swellings can develop from various glands or structures in the perineal region. While many are harmless and painless at first, they can grow large enough to cause discomfort during sitting, walking, or sexual intercourse. Nurses encounter these lumps frequently in gynecology clinics, and understanding their basic nature helps calm anxious patients who often fear malignancy upon first discovery.

Types and Causes of Vulval Cysts

Different structures in the vulva give rise to distinct types of cysts:

  • Bartholin duct cyst: Forms when the main duct of the Bartholin gland becomes blocked, causing fluid to back up and swell the gland.
  • Epidermal inclusion cyst: Develops when trapped surface skin cells multiply beneath the dermis, often following trauma, childbirth tearing, or episiotomy repair.
  • Mucous cyst: Arises from misplaced embryonic tissue or minor mucous glands, containing clear or thick secretions.
  • Hydrocele of the canal of Nuck: Occurs when peritoneal fluid tracks down into an unclosed embryonic pouch near the round ligament.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment

The clinical presentation of a vulval cyst depends largely on its size and whether infection is present. Small cysts are frequently asymptomatic and discovered purely by chance during routine physical examinations. Larger cysts produce a noticeable, painless swelling on one side of the labia. Patients may report a feeling of heaviness, local pressure, or pain during physical activities. If a cyst becomes infected—turning into an abscess—the area grows red, hot, extremely tender, and may cause fever or difficulty walking.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosing a vulval cyst starts with a thorough visual and physical examination of the perineum. The nurse assists the healthcare provider by ensuring patient privacy, proper positioning in the lithotomy position, and good lighting. Palpation helps determine the size, mobility, and tenderness of the mass. While most cysts are diagnosed clinically, imaging such as ultrasound may be used to differentiate solid tumors from fluid-filled sacs. Biopsies are reserved for atypical, persistent, or hard masses to rule out malignancy, especially in older women.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management strategies range from conservative observation to surgical intervention based on symptoms:

  • Watchful waiting: Small, asymptomatic cysts require no treatment beyond reassurance and hygiene guidance.
  • Sitz baths: Warm water soaks help relieve mild discomfort and may encourage spontaneous drainage.
  • Incision and drainage (I&D): Provides immediate relief for painful abscesses, though recurrence is common if the cavity is not managed properly.
  • Word catheter: A small balloon-tipped tube placed into an incised Bartholin cyst to keep the tract open for several weeks, allowing a new epithelialized duct to form.
  • Marsupialization: A surgical procedure where the cyst wall is stitched open to the exterior mucosa to create a permanent drainage pouch.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Holistic nursing care focuses on pain relief, infection control, and emotional support. Nurses administer prescribed analgesics and antibiotics promptly. Providing clear instructions on how to take sitz baths safely helps reduce local inflammation. Nurses must educate patients on proper perineal hygiene—wiping from front to back and wearing loose cotton underwear. Crucially, nurses instruct patients to avoid squeezing or attempting to pop the cyst at home, as this introduces bacteria and worsens inflammation.
