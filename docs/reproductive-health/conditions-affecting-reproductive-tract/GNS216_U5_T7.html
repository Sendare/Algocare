Vulval Cyst

Introduction and Definition of Vulval Cysts

A vulval cyst is a fluid-filled sac or pouch that develops on the external female genitalia, collectively known as the vulva. These lesions can arise from various structures, including sweat glands, hair follicles, or embryonic remnants, and they range significantly in size from a tiny, asymptomatic pea to a mass large enough to cause discomfort during ambulation or sexual intercourse. While the vast majority of these growths are entirely benign, their presence often causes considerable anxiety for patients due to their intimate location. In many clinical settings across Nigeria, women may present late with these swellings because of cultural stigma or fear of gynaecological examinations, making early, sensitive assessment crucial for reassurance and timely intervention.

Anatomical Considerations of the Vulva

The vulva encompasses several distinct anatomical landmarks that dictate where specific types of cysts develop. Understanding this topography helps in accurately localising lesions during physical examination. Key structures include: - **Mons pubis**: The rounded fatty tissue overlying the pubic symphysis, occasionally prone to sebaceous cysts. - **Labia majora**: The larger outer skin folds containing adipose tissue and sweat glands, common sites for epidermal inclusion cysts. - **Labia minora**: The thin, hairless inner folds flanking the vaginal vestibule. - **Bartholin's glands**: Mucus-secreting glands situated posteriorly on each side of the vaginal opening, notorious for ductal obstruction. - **Skene's glands**: Periurethral glands located near the lower end of the urethra.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The formation of vulval cysts generally stems from ductal obstruction, glandular retention, or traumatic implantation of surface epithelium. When the duct of a mucus-secreting or sweat gland becomes blocked by debris, thick secretions, or local inflammation, the fluid continues to produce behind the obstruction, causing the gland to distend. Alternatively, epidermal inclusion cysts form when surface skin cells become trapped beneath the dermal layer following minor trauma, episiotomy scars, or surgical procedures, continuing to shed keratin into a closed pocket. Chronic irritation and poor perineal hygiene can exacerbate these blockages by promoting localized inflammation and infection.

Types and Classification of Vulval Cysts

Vulval cysts are categorised based on their tissue of origin. Recognising these distinct types guides appropriate clinical management. - **Bartholin's duct cyst**: Arises from blockage of the Bartholin's gland duct, usually presenting as a unilateral swelling at the lower third of the labia majora. - **Epidermal inclusion cyst**: Small, firm, slow-growing nodules containing cheesy keratin material, often found on the labia majora following minor skin trauma. - **Sebaceous cyst**: Formed by blocked oil-producing sebaceous glands, characterized by a small punctum on the overlying skin. - **Hydrocele of the canal of Nuck**: A rare developmental anomaly involving persistent fluid in the processus vaginalis, extending into the labium majus.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Assessment

Clinical manifestations depend heavily on the size and secondary infection status of the lesion. Many small cysts remain completely asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally during routine pelvic examinations. Larger cysts can cause a sensation of heaviness, local irritation, dyspareunia, or mechanical difficulty during walking. If a cyst becomes secondarily infected—transforming into an abscess—the patient will report acute, throbbing pain, localized erythema, oedema, and systemic fever. During assessment, a thorough history should capture symptom duration, pain levels, and any history of previous vulval trauma or infections, followed by a gentle, chaperoned physical inspection.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Differential Diagnosis

Diagnosing a vulval cyst relies primarily on careful visual and tactile inspection during a pelvic examination. The clinician assesses size, tenderness, mobility, and exact anatomical location. While most diagnoses are clinically evident, diagnostic uncertainty requires ruling out other conditions such as inguinal hernias, lipomas, hidradenitis suppurativa, or malignancies like vulvar carcinoma—especially in postmenopausal women with irregular, fixed masses. Ultrasonography is occasionally utilized to differentiate solid masses from cystic fluid collections, and tissue biopsy remains the gold standard if malignant degeneration is suspected.

Medical and Surgical Nursing Management

Management strategies vary depending on whether the cyst is asymptomatic, acutely inflamed, or chronically recurrent. Small, asymptomatic cysts require no active treatment beyond routine hygiene and observation. Warm sitz baths can relieve mild discomfort. When a Bartholin's cyst becomes abscessed, incision and drainage may be performed, though simple drainage has a high recurrence rate. Consequently, procedures like word catheter placement or marsupialization—suturing the cyst wall open to create a permanent drainage pouch—are standard surgical interventions. Nursing care involves pre-operative reassurance, post-operative pain management, maintaining perineal hygiene, and monitoring for signs of haemorrhage or wound infection.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

Although vulval cysts are typically benign and carry an excellent overall prognosis, certain complications can arise if left untreated or managed improperly. The most frequent complication is secondary bacterial infection resulting in abscess formation, severe pain, and cellulitis. In recurrent Bartholin's cysts, chronic discomfort can significantly impact a patient's psychological wellbeing and sexual health. Surgical excision carries minor risks of wound dehiscence, haematoma formation, dyspareunia from scar tissue, and recurrence. Reassurance regarding the benign nature of most cysts helps alleviate patient anxiety and supports a positive recovery trajectory.

Patient Education and Holistic Nursing Care

Holistic nursing care for women with vulval cysts centers on empathy, education, and destigmatisation. Nurses should instruct patients on proper perineal hygiene—such as wiping from front to back and avoiding harsh chemical soaps or tight synthetic underwear that trap moisture and irritate ducts. Education should also emphasize recognizing early signs of infection, such as increasing redness or throbbing pain, so patients seek prompt care before an abscess forms. Creating a safe, non-judgmental environment is particularly vital in Nigerian clinical settings to encourage open communication and dispel myths linking vulval growths to promiscuity or untreatable curses.
