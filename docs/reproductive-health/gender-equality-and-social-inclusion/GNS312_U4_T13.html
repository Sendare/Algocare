Management of Gender Based Violence

Understanding Gender-Based Violence in Clinical Practice

Gender-based violence (GBV) encompasses any harmful act directed against an individual based on socially ascribed gender roles and inequalities. While it disproportionately affects women and girls, men and boys can also be victims. In Nigerian clinical settings, healthcare providers frequently encounter survivors presenting with somatic complaints rather than disclosing abuse directly. Recognizing the root dynamics of power and control is essential for moving beyond routine physical treatment to holistic care. Many survivors carry deep-seated fear, stigma, and economic dependency that complicate simple discharge planning. A trauma-informed approach shifts the clinical mindset from asking 'What is wrong with you?' to 'What happened to you?'.

Forms and Manifestations of Abuse

- **Physical Abuse**: Acts resulting in bodily harm, ranging from minor bruising to severe trauma, fractures, and strangulation marks. - **Sexual Violence**: Non-consensual sexual contact, marital rape, forced prostitution, and reproductive coercion. - **Emotional and Psychological Abuse**: Verbal assaults, constant humiliation, isolation from family and friends, and severe intimidation. - **Economic Abuse**: Restricting access to financial resources, withholding money, or sabotaging employment and educational opportunities. - **Harmful Traditional Practices**: Child marriage, female genital mutilation, and widowhood rites prevalent in certain regions.

Clinical Assessment and Forensic Evidence Collection

Conducting a clinical assessment for a survivor of GBV requires meticulous attention to both immediate medical stabilization and legal documentation. You must perform a head-to-toe physical examination in a private, secure room, ensuring the patient gives explicit informed consent before every step. Document all injuries using precise anatomical descriptions, measurements, and body maps rather than vague terms like 'bad bruises.' If sexual assault is suspected within the standard 72-hour window, a standardized rape kit must be utilized to preserve biological evidence. Never clean, suture, or dress wounds that may compromise forensic specimens until collection is complete, provided the patient is hemodynamically stable.

Immediate Medical Management and Prophylaxis

Medical priorities after acute violence focus on life stabilization, treating traumatic injuries, and preventing long-term health consequences such as sexually transmitted infections and unwanted pregnancy. Offer emergency contraception as soon as possible, ideally within 120 hours of unprotected intercourse. Initiate Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV ideally within 72 hours of exposure, accompanied by baseline serological testing. Provide prophylactic antibiotic therapy for common bacterial infections like chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis according to local public health guidelines. Administer tetanus toxoid booster shots if the physical assault involved lacerations or contamination with soil or metal.

Psychological First Aid and Trauma-Informed Care

Survivors frequently experience acute stress reactions, dissociation, severe anxiety, and panic upon entering a healthcare facility. Psychological First Aid (PFA) provides humane, supportive, and practical assistance without forcing individuals to recount details of their trauma prematurely. Grounding techniques help patients regain emotional control when they feel overwhelmed or disconnected from their bodies. Never judge, blame, or question the survivor's choices or delayed disclosure. Creating a safe, predictable environment restores a sense of personal agency that the abusive encounter stripped away.

Legal Frameworks and Mandatory Reporting

Healthcare professionals practicing in Nigeria must navigate complex legal landscapes regarding gender-based violence, including the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act. Understanding state-specific laws helps nurses protect patients' rights while fulfilling statutory duties. While mandatory reporting laws vary by jurisdiction and patient age—such as mandatory notification for minors or severe felonies—patient autonomy and informed consent regarding adult disclosures must be balanced carefully against legal requirements. Breaching confidentiality without legal backing can destroy trust and place the survivor in greater physical danger. Always consult hospital legal units or social work departments when navigating complex reporting mandates.

Multi-Sectoral Referral Pathways and Community Support

Comprehensive management of GBV extends far beyond the walls of a hospital ward. Effective recovery requires a robust multi-sectoral referral network involving law enforcement, legal aid organizations, social welfare services, and safe shelter providers. In Nigeria, coordinating care through established GBV taskforces and sexual assault referral centers (SARCs) ensures survivors receive seamless legal, psychological, and medical support. Nurses act as vital bridges, connecting vulnerable patients to community NGOs and vocational rehabilitation programs that foster long-term independence and healing.
