Management of Gender Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence

Gender-based violence (GBV) represents a critical public health and human rights violation deeply entrenched in societal power imbalances and unequal gender norms. As nursing students in Nigeria, understanding GBV is essential because nurses are frequently the first healthcare professionals to encounter survivors in emergency departments, maternal clinics, and community health centers. GBV encompasses any harmful act directed against an individual based on their gender, resulting in physical, sexual, mental, or economic harm or suffering. It disproportionately affects women and girls, although men and boys can also be victims. In the Nigerian context, cultural factors, traditional practices, economic dependence, and structural inequalities often compound vulnerability and hinder reporting. Nurses must recognize that GBV is not merely a private domestic issue, but a systemic emergency requiring a compassionate, non-judgmental, and professional response. Healthcare settings serve as safe havens where survivors can seek medical attention, emotional support, and guidance without fear of stigma or secondary victimization. By approaching GBV with cultural sensitivity and clinical competence, nursing professionals can bridge the gap between emergency care and long-term recovery, upholding the fundamental ethical duty to protect and advocate for vulnerable populations.

Forms and Manifestations of GBV

Gender-based violence manifests in diverse and distressing forms across Nigerian communities, reflecting complex intersections of culture, economics, and power dynamics. Physical violence involves intentional use of physical force causing injury, pain, or disability, ranging from slaps and beatings to acid baths and mutilation. Sexual violence includes rape, attempted rape, forced marriage, sexual coercion, and harassment, often compounded by cultural taboos surrounding sexual purity and consent. Emotional and psychological violence comprises verbal abuse, constant humiliation, isolation, and threats, which severely erode a survivor's self-esteem and mental stability. Economic violence involves denying a partner access to financial resources, withholding basic necessities, or sabotaging educational and employment opportunities to maintain total dependency. Furthermore, harmful traditional practices such as female genital mutilation (FGM), child marriage, widowhood rites, and forced levirate marriage are prevalent manifestations of GBV deeply rooted in customary beliefs. Recognizing these overlapping forms is crucial for nurses during patient triage and history-taking, as survivors rarely present with just one isolated issue. Comprehensive assessment requires an acute awareness of these manifestations to provide tailored clinical interventions and accurate documentation.

Psychosocial and Physical Consequences of GBV

The consequences of gender-based violence are profound, multifaceted, and long-lasting, affecting every domain of a survivor's life. Physically, survivors may suffer from acute trauma such as lacerations, fractures, internal bleeding, and traumatic brain injury, alongside chronic conditions like pelvic pain, gastrointestinal disorders, and unwanted pregnancies. Sexual violence carries severe reproductive health complications, including sexually transmitted infections (STIs), HIV transmission, obstetric fistulae, and recurrent urinary tract infections. Psychologically, the burden is immense, characterized by post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), severe depression, generalized anxiety, phobias, substance abuse, and suicidal ideation. Survivors often experience profound feelings of guilt, shame, and self-blame reinforced by stigmatizing societal attitudes. Socially, the impact manifests as alienation from family, breakdown of support systems, loss of employment, and disruption of education. For Nigerian nursing students, identifying these overlapping consequences is vital because survivors may seek care for vague somatic complaints like chronic headaches or abdominal pain rather than disclosing the underlying abuse. A holistic nursing assessment must therefore look beyond physical wounds to address the invisible mental and social scars inflicted by GBV.

Clinical Assessment and Forensic Evidence Collection

Clinical assessment and forensic evidence collection require meticulous precision, extreme sensitivity, and strict adherence to legal protocols. When a survivor of sexual or physical assault presents to a healthcare facility, the nurse's primary goal is to establish rapport, ensure physical safety, and obtain informed consent before initiating any examination. The clinical examination must be conducted in a private, secure environment, with a chaperone present if desired by the patient. Nurses must perform a head-to-toe physical assessment, documenting all injuries, bruises, lacerations, and tenderness using standard body maps and precise descriptive language rather than unverified conclusions. Forensic evidence collection involves gathering biological samples such as blood, saliva, hair, and swabs (vaginal, oral, or anal) using standardized rape kits or evidence collection protocols, ensuring an unbroken chain of custody. Every item collected must be properly labeled, sealed, and stored securely to ensure admissibility in a court of law. Throughout this process, nurses must avoid judgmental language, respect patient autonomy, and explain every step of the procedure to prevent re-traumatization and foster a sense of control for the survivor.

Immediate Medical Management and Post-Exposure Prophylaxis

Immediate medical management of GBV survivors, particularly those experiencing sexual assault, is time-sensitive and critical for preventing life-altering complications. Emergency care prioritizes life stabilization, treatment of acute injuries, and prophylaxis against infections and unwanted pregnancies. Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV must be initiated ideally within 72 hours of exposure to significantly reduce the risk of seroconversion; this involves a multi-drug antiretroviral regimen that requires patient counseling regarding compliance and potential side effects. Emergency contraception (EC) should be offered promptly, ideally within 120 hours of unprotected intercourse, to prevent unwanted pregnancy. Additionally, prophylaxis for other sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, syphilis, and hepatitis B must be administered according to national health guidelines. Tetanus toxoid immunization should be updated if wound contamination is present. Nurses play a pivotal role in administering these medications, educating survivors on the importance of completing full treatment courses, scheduling necessary follow-up appointments, and monitoring for adverse reactions or psychological distress during this critical window of vulnerability.

Psychological First Aid and Trauma-Informed Care

Psychological First Aid (PFA) and trauma-informed care form the bedrock of emotional healing for survivors of gender-based violence. PFA is a humane, supportive response to a fellow human being who is suffering and who may need support; it focuses on reducing initial distress and fostering adaptive coping without forcing individuals to share their trauma details. Core elements of PFA include providing practical care and comfort, assessing needs and concerns, helping people address basic needs, listening actively without pressuring to talk, and connecting survivors with social support and information. Trauma-informed care shifts the clinical perspective from asking 'What is wrong with you?' to 'What happened to you?', emphasizing safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, and empowerment. Nurses trained in these principles avoid re-traumatizing survivors through intrusive questioning or dismissive attitudes. Instead, they create a calm, predictable environment where survivors regain a sense of agency and control over their healthcare decisions. Integrating PFA and trauma-informed principles ensures that nursing interventions heal both the physical body and the wounded psyche.

Legal Frameworks and Mandatory Reporting in Nigeria

Navigating the legal frameworks and mandatory reporting requirements surrounding GBV in Nigeria is a crucial responsibility for healthcare professionals. Nigeria has enacted significant legislative measures, notably the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act (2015) at the federal level, alongside various state-level equivalents, which criminalize physical, psychological, sexual, and economic abuse, as well as harmful traditional practices. Nurses must understand their legal obligations regarding confidentiality versus mandatory reporting. While patient confidentiality is a cornerstone of nursing ethics, national laws and institutional policies often mandate the reporting of child abuse, statutory rape, and severe criminal offenses to law enforcement or child protection agencies. Furthermore, accurate medical documentation serves as legal evidence in court proceedings; nurses must record observations objectively, avoiding subjective assumptions or speculations. Collaborating with legal aid organizations and law enforcement ensures that survivors receive comprehensive justice while maintaining their dignity and rights throughout the legal process. Understanding these laws protects nurses from legal liability and empowers them to advocate effectively for justice on behalf of survivors.

Multi-Sectoral Referral Pathways and Community Support

Managing gender-based violence effectively requires a multi-sectoral referral pathway and robust community support networks, as no single healthcare provider or agency can address all dimensions of trauma alone. The multi-sectoral approach coordinates services across medicine, law enforcement, legal aid, social welfare, and psychological counseling. Nurses act as central navigators within this network, identifying survivor needs and facilitating seamless referrals to specialized agencies such as shelters, legal aid clinics, and mental health specialists. Establishing clear, functional local referral pathways prevents survivors from experiencing secondary victimization through repeated, disjointed storytelling across different institutions. In addition to institutional referrals, community-based support groups, traditional and religious leaders, and non-governmental organizations play a vital role in dismantling stigma, raising awareness, and fostering safe environments for survivors. For nursing students, building collaborative relationships with community stakeholders ensures holistic continuity of care, empowering survivors to rebuild their lives with dignity, resilience, and comprehensive support systems.
