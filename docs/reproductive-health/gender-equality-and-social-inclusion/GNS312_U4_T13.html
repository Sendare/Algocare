Management of Gender Based Violence

Understanding Gender-Based Violence in Nursing Practice

Gender-based violence (GBV) refers to harmful acts directed at an individual based on their biological sex or socially constructed gender identity. While it affects people of all genders, women and girls bear a disproportionate burden globally. In clinical settings, nurses frequently encounter victims whose injuries or somatic complaints mask the underlying trauma of abuse. Recognizing that GBV extends far beyond physical assault is essential for holistic care; it encompasses structural, economic, and psychological harm. As frontline healthcare providers, nurses occupy a trusted position that often allows patients to disclose abuse when they feel safe. Providing a non-judgmental atmosphere directly impacts whether a survivor seeks ongoing medical and legal support.

Forms and Clinical Presentations of Abuse

Abuse manifests in diverse ways, requiring nurses to look past surface-level complaints. The common categories include:

  • Physical abuse: Infliction of bodily pain or injury, presenting as fractures, bruises, or burns.
  • Sexual abuse: Non-consensual sexual contact, often accompanied by genital trauma or sexually transmitted infections.
  • Psychological abuse: Emotional torment, verbal threats, and isolation leading to severe anxiety or depression.
  • Economic abuse: Control over financial resources to maintain dependency and limit the victim's autonomy.

Patients rarely walk in stating they have been abused. Instead, they present with vague somatic complaints such as chronic headaches, pelvic pain, insomnia, or unexplained gastrointestinal distress. A thorough head-to-toe assessment helps correlate physical findings with inconsistent or evasive patient histories.

Clinical Assessment and Forensic Evidence Collection

When a survivor of sexual or physical assault presents for care, clinical assessment serves a dual purpose: treating immediate injuries and preserving forensic evidence. Nurses must follow strict chain-of-custody protocols to ensure collected evidence remains legally admissible.

  1. Consent: Obtain informed consent specifically for forensic examination and evidence collection.
  2. Documentation: Accurately record verbatim statements using quotation marks, detailing injuries on body maps with precise measurements.
  3. Evidence Collection: Use standardized kits to gather clothing fibers, biological samples, and swabs without contaminating the scene.
  4. Storage: Seal, label, and store collected items securely according to institutional policy.

Failing to document details objectively can compromise legal proceedings later, compounding the injustice faced by the survivor.

Immediate Medical Management and Post-Exposure Prophylaxis

Emergency medical care takes absolute precedence over forensic collection if the patient is unstable. Once stabilization is achieved, clinicians must address the severe biological risks associated with sexual assault and trauma.

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV must be initiated as quickly as possible, ideally within 72 hours of exposure, to reduce transmission risk. Emergency contraception should be offered within 120 hours to prevent unwanted pregnancy. Additionally, healthcare providers administer syndromic treatment for sexually transmitted infections like gonorrhea, chlamydia, and trichomoniasis, alongside hepatitis B vaccination and tetanus boosters where indicated. Routine wound care, pain management, and thorough physical stabilization complete the urgent medical intervention phase.

Psychological First Aid and Trauma-Informed Care

Trauma-informed care shifts the clinical mindset from asking 'What is wrong with this patient?' to 'What happened to this patient?' Psychological First Aid (PFA) provides humane, supportive assistance without forcing survivors to retell traumatic details before they are ready.

Nurses build safety, trust, and collaboration by validating the patient's feelings and restoring a sense of personal control. Avoid probing questions, victim-blaming language, or rushing the patient through clinical procedures. Creating a calm environment lowers acute distress and helps prevent long-term psychological sequelae like post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Empathy and active listening form the bedrock of effective emotional stabilization during acute crisis recovery.

Legal Frameworks and Reporting Obligations

Nurses practicing in Nigeria must navigate complex legal landscapes regarding gender-based violence, including the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act. This legislation provides robust legal protection against domestic violence, rape, harmful traditional practices, and emotional abuse.

Balancing patient confidentiality with legal reporting obligations requires careful clinical judgment. While patient privacy is a fundamental ethical tenet, statutory laws or institutional guidelines may mandate reporting certain violent crimes, particularly involving minors or incapacitated adults. Nurses must understand local legislation to comply with legal duties while keeping the survivor's safety and informed consent at the center of decision-making whenever statutory exceptions allow.

Multi-Sectoral Referral Pathways and Community Support

No single healthcare provider or agency can meet all the complex needs of a GBV survivor. Effective management relies on a coordinated multi-sectoral referral network involving medical services, legal aid, social welfare, law enforcement, and safe shelter providers.

Nurses act as critical linkage coordinators, connecting survivors with social workers, legal advocates, and community-based support groups. Establishing clear institutional referral pathways ensures that patients do not get lost between departments or dropped from care. Community engagement and education further help dismantle stigma, challenge victim-blaming attitudes, and encourage early reporting, ultimately fostering an environment where survivors can heal and rebuild their lives.
