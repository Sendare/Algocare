Gender Based Violence in Humanitarian Settings

Introduction and Contextual Realities

Gender-based violence in humanitarian settings refers to any harmful act directed against an individual based on socially ascribed gender differences. During conflicts, natural disasters, or displacement crises, the social fabric collapses, existing community protections vanish, and rule of law breaks down. This environment creates a landscape where physical, sexual, and psychological abuse thrives with near-total impunity. For nursing professionals deployed to internally displaced person camps or emergency field hospitals in Nigeria, recognizing this reality means shifting away from viewing violence as isolated criminal acts. Instead, it must be understood as a predictable, systemic emergency health threat that requires an immediate, trauma-informed clinical response just like trauma from a motor vehicle accident or a cholera outbreak.

Vulnerability Factors and Risk Drivers in Crises

Displacement disproportionately exposes certain groups to heightened protection risks. Economic destitution forces individuals into survival sex or transactional relationships just to secure food rations. Overcrowded camp layouts featuring poorly lit latrines, segregated bathing blocks, and miles-long walks for firewood create spatial traps where physical assaults routinely happen. Adolescent girls, women traveling alone, pregnant individuals, and marginalized gender identities face compounding layers of discrimination that restrict their mobility and negotiation power. Furthermore, the psychological strain of displacement often worsens pre-existing substance abuse and domestic tension within households, turning temporary shelters into dangerous environments.

Types and Manifestations of GBV in Emergencies

  • Sexual Violence: Encompasses rape, attempted rape, forced concubinage, and coerced sexual acts perpetrated during raids or under threats of withholding aid.
  • Physical Assault: Involves beating, mutilation, or acid attacks resulting from domestic disputes, communal clashes, or targeted intimidation.
  • Emotional and Psychological Abuse: Involves severe verbal degradation, forced isolation from family networks, threats of abandonment, and destruction of personal identification documents.
  • Harmful Traditional Practices: Includes accelerated child marriages arranged to secure bride price for family survival or female genital mutilation resurgence in unstable areas.
  • Denial of Resources and Opportunities: Involves withholding humanitarian assistance, food rations, or employment based on sex or refusal of sexual advances.

Health Consequences for Survivors

The physical and psychological fallout of gender-based violence in crises is profound and long-lasting. Immediate trauma care often deals with lacerations, bone fractures, blunt force abdominal trauma, and unwanted pregnancies. Sexually transmitted infections, including HIV and Hepatitis B, present high transmission risks due to forced, unprotected intercourse. Chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, fistulas, and gastrointestinal distress frequently plague survivors long after the acute event has passed. On the psychological front, post-traumatic stress disorder, severe depression, acute anxiety, and suicidal ideation are common. Stigmatization by family members and local communities often compounds these symptoms, driving survivors into social isolation and preventing them from seeking timely medical intervention.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Nurses working in triage or outpatient departments must maintain a high index of suspicion because survivors rarely present by directly stating they experienced violence. Instead, they manifest vague somatic complaints, chronic pain, sleeping disorders, or exhibit defensive behaviors. Establishing an empathetic, non-judgmental environment is vital before asking screening questions. Direct, gentle inquiries should be framed within normal health intake protocols—such as asking every patient about safety at home. Clinicians must observe for discrepancies between the history given and the physical injuries observed, while taking care not to interrogate or pressure the patient into disclosing details they are not yet ready to share.

Clinical Management and Immediate Care Protocols

Immediate clinical management centers on stabilization, emergency contraception, infection prophylaxis, and psychological first aid. If a patient presents within 120 hours of sexual assault, emergency contraceptive pills should be offered to prevent unintended pregnancy. Post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV must be initiated as soon as possible, ideally within 72 hours, alongside presumptive treatment for common sexually transmitted infections like chlamydia, gonorrhea, and trichomoniasis. Documenting injuries requires meticulous, objective language, recording verbatim statements without drawing legal conclusions. Nurses must perform physical examinations only with informed consent, ensuring the patient retains control over every step of the physical assessment.

Ethical Considerations, Confidentiality, and Safety

Confidentiality is a life-saving clinical requirement, not merely an administrative rule. In fragile humanitarian settings where gossip spreads quickly, a breached medical record can result in severe social ostracization, honor-based violence, or retribution against the survivor. Nurses must store physical charts securely and restrict electronic data sharing. Informed consent must be secured before conducting any examination, administering medication, or sharing details with multidisciplinary response teams. Furthermore, nurses must never force a survivor to report an incident to law enforcement against their will, as forced reporting strips away their remaining autonomy and increases future danger.

Referral Pathways and Inter-Agency Collaboration

No single nurse or healthcare facility can manage the complex needs of a gender-based violence survivor alone. Comprehensive recovery demands a functioning, multi-sectoral referral pathway involving medical care, psychosocial counseling, legal assistance, and safe shelter protection. Nurses serve as critical frontline connectors who map available local services and guide survivors safely through the referral network. Establishing functional coordination among humanitarian actors ensures survivors do not have to recount their traumatic history repeatedly to different agencies, which minimizes re-traumatization and closes gaps in continuity of care.

Prevention Strategies and Community-Based Support

Clinical treatment alone cannot stop the cycle of violence; active community engagement and primary prevention are essential. Nurses can participate in community outreach by working with trusted local leaders, women's groups, and youth networks to challenge harmful gender norms and raise awareness of available health services. Creating safe spaces within displacement camps gives women and girls a venue for peer support, skills training, and confidential reporting. Engaging men and boys as active allies in dismantling toxic masculinity helps transform community attitudes, fostering a protective environment that discourages violence before it occurs.
