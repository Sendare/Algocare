Gender Based Violence in Humanitarian Settings

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence in Humanitarian Settings

Gender-based violence (GBV) represents a pervasive, global crisis that escalates exponentially during humanitarian emergencies such as natural disasters, conflicts, and epidemics. In these fragile environments, the breakdown of social structures, displacement, and the collapse of the rule of law create an atmosphere of lawlessness and impunity. For nursing students operating within resource-limited or emergency settings in Nigeria and globally, recognizing GBV as a critical public health and human rights emergency is paramount. Humanitarian settings exacerbate existing gender inequalities, making women, girls, and marginalized populations disproportionately vulnerable to various forms of abuse. As frontline healthcare providers, nurses are often the first point of professional contact for survivors. Understanding the epidemiological landscape of GBV during crises allows nursing professionals to approach care with heightened clinical suspicion, empathy, and preparedness. Comprehensive care begins with acknowledging that GBV is entirely preventable and that every survivor has the right to safe, dignified, and non-judgmental medical and psychological assistance. Nurses must remain vigilant, recognizing that emergency contexts disrupt standard protective mechanisms, thereby necessitating a proactive stance in identifying, documenting, and responding to cases of violence. By contextualizing GBV within humanitarian frameworks, nurses can better advocate for vulnerable populations, challenge systemic impunity, and contribute effectively to multidisciplinary humanitarian response teams dedicated to upholding human dignity and safety in crisis zones.

Vulnerability Factors and Risk Drivers in Crises

Vulnerability to gender-based violence in humanitarian settings is driven by a complex interplay of systemic, environmental, and individual factors. When communities are displaced by conflict or natural disasters, traditional support systems disintegrate, leaving individuals without their usual social safety nets. Economic instability is a major risk driver; lack of livelihoods forces individuals, particularly women and adolescent girls, into desperate survival strategies such as transactional sex or hazardous labor where they face exploitation. Furthermore, overcrowded living conditions in camps for internally displaced persons (IDPs), inadequate lighting in sanitation facilities, and poorly secured distribution sites for food and non-food items create physical environments ripe for exploitation and abuse. Marginalized groups, including persons with disabilities, ethnic minorities, and individuals with diverse sexual orientations or gender identities, face compounded vulnerabilities due to prevailing discrimination and restricted access to protection mechanisms. Structural gender inequality remains the foundational driver, normalising power imbalances that subjugate women and girls. For nursing professionals, understanding these specific risk drivers is essential for conducting thorough risk assessments and designing community-level interventions. By identifying who is most at risk—such as unaccompanied minors, pregnant women, or female-headed households—nurses can help tailor targeted preventive measures and ensure that clinical spaces are equipped to offer safe, specialized care to those arriving from high-risk environments.

Types and Manifestations of GBV in Emergencies

Gender-based violence manifests in diverse, devastating ways during emergencies, reflecting the multi-dimensional nature of power abuses in crisis settings. Sexual violence, including rape, attempted rape, and sexual assault, frequently spikes during conflicts as a weapon of war or due to general lawlessness. Intimate partner violence (IPV) also intensifies under the immense psychological and economic stress experienced by families in displacement. Non-physical forms of abuse, such as psychological and emotional abuse, coercion, and severe harassment, are equally pervasive and deeply damaging to the mental health of survivors. Additionally, humanitarian crises often exacerbate harmful traditional practices, including forced and early child marriage, where desperate families marry off young daughters in exchange for bride price or perceived protection. Denial of resources, services, and opportunities—such as withholding food aid or humanitarian assistance unless sexual favors are granted—represents another critical form of coercion prevalent in camp settings. Human trafficking and modern slavery also surge as criminal networks exploit the desperation and displacement of vulnerable populations. For nurses, recognizing these varied manifestations is critical, as survivors may present with physical injuries, psychological trauma, or subtle somatic complaints that mask the underlying abuse. A comprehensive clinical understanding ensures that nurses do not overlook non-physical or structural abuses during patient intake and assessment.

Health and Psychosocial Consequences for Survivors

The health and psychosocial consequences of gender-based violence in humanitarian settings are profound, long-lasting, and affect every dimension of a survivor's well-being. Physically, survivors may experience acute traumatic injuries, lacerations, fractures, and severe internal damage. In cases of sexual violence, there is an immediate risk of acquiring sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV, as well as unwanted pregnancies that carry high maternal morbidity and mortality risks in resource-constrained environments. Chronic physical conditions such as pelvic inflammatory disease, chronic pain, and gastrointestinal disorders frequently manifest long after the acute event. Psychosocially, survivors often suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), severe depression, profound anxiety, panic attacks, and suicidal ideation. The stigma associated with GBV in many communities frequently leads to social ostracization, abandonment by family members, and severe isolation, which compounds the psychological trauma. For nursing students, understanding these multifaceted consequences underscores the necessity of holistic, trauma-informed nursing care. Nurses must look beyond immediate physical wounds to evaluate the psychological and social status of the patient. Providing empathetic, comprehensive care helps mitigate the long-term sequelae of trauma, supports mental health recovery, and aids survivors in reclaiming their health and agency despite the adversity of their humanitarian environment.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of GBV Survivors

Nursing assessment and identification of GBV survivors require exceptional clinical skill, acute observation, and deep empathy, particularly within chaotic humanitarian settings. Survivors frequently do not disclose abuse spontaneously due to fear of stigma, retaliation, or disbelief by authority figures. Therefore, nurses must proactively yet sensitively screen patients for GBV, especially when presenting with unexplained physical injuries, recurrent gynecological complaints, chronic pain, or symptoms of severe psychological distress. Assessment must be conducted in a private, secure, and confidential space to ensure the patient feels safe. The nurse should use clear, non-judgmental language, allowing the survivor to speak at their own pace without interruption or victim-blaming questions. Documenting findings accurately is vital for both clinical continuity and potential legal procedures; however, documentation must strictly adhere to the patient's consent and confidentiality guidelines. Nurses must observe for non-verbal cues of trauma, such as avoidance of eye contact, trembling, or defensive body postures. By maintaining a trauma-informed approach, nursing professionals validate the survivor's experience, establish vital therapeutic trust, and lay the foundation for effective clinical management and supportive multidisciplinary interventions tailored to the survivor's unique needs in a crisis.

Clinical Management and Immediate Care Protocols

Clinical management and immediate care protocols for GBV survivors in humanitarian settings are time-sensitive and critical to preventing severe health complications. For survivors of sexual assault presenting within the acute window (typically up to 72 hours, though timelines vary by protocol), immediate interventions include emergency contraception to prevent unwanted pregnancy, and post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) to minimize the risk of HIV transmission. Presumptive treatment for other sexually transmitted infections and tetanus vaccination for physical trauma must also be administered promptly. Wound care, forensic evidence collection (if required and consented to by the survivor, and if trained personnel are available), and meticulous documentation of physical injuries are essential components of the clinical protocol. Beyond acute medical treatment, psychological first aid (PFA) must be offered to stabilize the survivor and address immediate emotional distress. Nurses must ensure that clinical protocols prioritize the survivor's bodily autonomy and consent at every single step. The care environment must be healing rather than re-traumatizing. By following evidence-based clinical guidelines, nurses significantly reduce the physical and psychological morbidity associated with gender-based violence, offering survivors a compassionate and professional pathway toward recovery.

Ethical Considerations, Confidentiality, and Safety

Ethical considerations, confidentiality, and safety form the bedrock of all nursing interactions with GBV survivors in humanitarian settings. In crisis zones, where gossip, insecurity, and social breakdown are common, maintaining strict confidentiality is not merely professional etiquette—it is a life-or-death matter for the survivor. Breaching confidentiality can expose the survivor to severe retaliation, stigma, or physical harm from perpetrators or community members. Nurses must adhere to the core principles of the survivor-centered approach: safety, confidentiality, respect, and non-discrimination. Every action and intervention must be guided by informed consent, meaning the survivor is fully informed of their options and retains absolute autonomy over decisions regarding medical treatment, reporting, and referrals. Mandatory reporting laws must be navigated carefully, balancing legal obligations with the absolute safety and best interests of the survivor, particularly in volatile humanitarian contexts where formal legal systems may be compromised. Nurses must continuously evaluate safety risks, collaborating with protection officers if physical threats persist. By prioritizing ethical integrity, nurses empower survivors, restore their agency, and ensure that healthcare spaces remain sanctuaries of trust, safety, and human rights.

Referral Pathways and Inter-Agency Collaboration

No single nurse or healthcare facility can manage the complex, multifaceted needs of GBV survivors in isolation, making effective referral pathways and inter-agency collaboration indispensable. In humanitarian settings, a functional referral network connects health services with psychosocial support, legal assistance, safe shelter, and security protection services. Nurses must be thoroughly familiar with local referral maps and service providers operating within their specific displacement camp or host community. When a survivor requires specialized services outside the nurse's scope—such as legal aid or long-term mental health counseling—the nurse facilitates a safe, coordinated transfer of care. Crucially, inter-agency collaboration requires strict adherence to data protection protocols, ensuring that identifying information is never shared without explicit, informed consent from the survivor. Regular coordination meetings among humanitarian actors (health, protection, legal, and camp management) help identify service gaps, streamline response protocols, and ensure a unified approach to survivor care. For nursing students, mastering referral pathways means recognizing the limits of clinical care and understanding how to leverage multidisciplinary networks to provide holistic, seamless support that addresses the total welfare of the survivor in crisis.

Prevention Strategies and Community-Based Support in Humanitarian Action

While acute clinical management is essential, effective response to gender-based violence in humanitarian settings must be complemented by robust prevention strategies and community-based support. Prevention requires addressing root causes by transforming harmful gender norms, promoting gender equality, and engaging community leaders, men, and boys as active allies in protection. Nurses play a vital role in community-based outreach, health education, and sensitization campaigns within displaced populations. By conducting workshops on reproductive health, rights, and available services, nurses help dispel myths, reduce stigma, and encourage early health-seeking behavior. Establishing community support networks, women-friendly spaces, and peer-led support groups empowers community members to recognize risks, look out for vulnerable peers, and establish informal protective mechanisms. Community engagement ensures that interventions are culturally appropriate, accepted, and sustainable over time. For nursing students, understanding prevention shifts the clinical paradigm from reactive treatment to proactive community health advocacy. By embedding prevention into daily nursing practice and humanitarian operations, professionals contribute to building resilient communities capable of preventing violence, protecting vulnerable members, and fostering a culture of safety, dignity, and mutual respect.
