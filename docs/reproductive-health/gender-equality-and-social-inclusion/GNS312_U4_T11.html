Gender Based Violence in Humanitarian Settings

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence in Humanitarian Settings

Gender-based violence refers to any harmful act directed against an individual based on socially ascribed gender roles and inequalities. In humanitarian crises such as natural disasters, armed conflicts, and forced displacement, the normal protective structures of society break down completely. This collapse of the rule of law, combined with mass panic and disrupted community networks, creates an environment where violence thrives with near-total impunity.

Displaced populations living in overcrowded camps, makeshift shelters, or informal settlements face heightened exposure to physical and sexual attacks. Basic survival needs like food, clean water, and shelter become scarce, creating transactional dynamics where desperate individuals are exploited by those controlling aid distribution. Recognizing that humanitarian emergencies do not create gender-based violence, but rather dramatically amplify preexisting inequalities and power imbalances, is foundational for effective clinical nursing response.

Vulnerability Factors and Risk Drivers in Crises

  • Displacement and Loss of Shelter: Living in temporary, insecure spaces lacking locks, adequate lighting, or privacy walls exposes women and girls to opportunistic attacks.
  • Economic Desperation: Extreme poverty and lack of livelihoods force individuals into survival sex or dangerous transactional relationships to secure daily meals.
  • Separation from Family Support: Unaccompanied minors, separated elderly individuals, and single female-headed households lose traditional family protection networks.
  • Disrupted Infrastructure: Walking long distances alone through isolated areas to collect firewood, water, or relief items presents constant physical danger.

Types and Manifestations of GBV in Emergencies

Gender-based violence takes many distinct forms during humanitarian crises, each causing profound physical and psychological trauma. Nurses must recognize these varied presentations because survivors rarely volunteer a full history upfront.

  1. Sexual Violence: Includes rape, attempted rape, and forced sexual acts committed during raids on villages or within unsafe camp zones.
  2. Physical Assault: Non-sexual beatings and bodily harm inflicted by intimate partners, family members, or armed actors due to domestic disputes or perceived disobedience.
  3. Early and Forced Marriage: Families marrying off young daughters prematurely as a desperate coping strategy to reduce household mouths to feed or to secure bride price.
  4. Denial of Resources and Opportunities: The withholding of life-saving humanitarian assistance, food rations, or medical care to coerce individuals into compliance.

Health and Psychosocial Consequences for Survivors

The aftermath of gender-based violence extends far beyond initial injuries, creating a complex web of medical and mental health crises. Physically, survivors face the immediate risks of traumatic lacerations, internal bleeding, unwanted pregnancies, and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. Chronic pelvic pain, gastrointestinal distress, and untreated injuries frequently plague those who cannot access timely care.

Mental health impacts are equally severe. Survivors commonly experience severe depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and suicidal ideation. Stigma, community ostracization, and victim-blaming often compound this psychological suffering, driving survivors into isolation. A nurse must approach every clinical interaction understanding that physical healing cannot happen while emotional and social wounds remain unaddressed.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of GBV Survivors

Identifying survivors requires a high index of suspicion because patients rarely present by stating they experienced violence. Instead, they visit clinics for vague physical complaints like chronic headaches, abdominal pain, or unexplained injuries. Nurses should provide a private, confidential setting before asking any sensitive screening questions.

When assessing a patient, use gentle, non-judgmental language and observe non-verbal cues such as excessive anxiety, withdrawal, or flinching. Avoid direct interrogation; rather, ask open-ended questions about safety at home or stress levels. Common misconceptions lead some nurses to wait for patients to report assault on their own—meanwhile, proactive, compassionate inquiry saves lives by opening the door to critical care.

Clinical Management and Immediate Care Protocols

Immediate clinical care for a survivor of sexual or physical violence follows a strict priority timeline. The absolute first step is stabilizing life-threatening physical injuries and ensuring basic physical safety. Following stabilization, the nurse must administer emergency medical interventions within specific time windows.

  • Emergency Contraception: Must be offered within 120 hours (5 days) of unprotected sexual assault to prevent unwanted pregnancy.
  • Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP): Must be initiated within 72 hours of exposure to significantly reduce the risk of HIV transmission.
  • Wound Care and Prophylactic Antibiotics: Treat lacerations, screen for sexually transmitted infections, and administer tetanus boosters as indicated.
  • Forensic Evidence Collection: If legal action is desired and trained personnel are present, document and collect physical evidence using standardized kits without delaying life-saving medical treatment.

Ethical Considerations, Confidentiality, and Safety

Providing care in humanitarian settings requires strict adherence to core ethical principles. Confidentiality is paramount; sharing a survivor's identity or details of their trauma without explicit, informed consent can endanger their life and destroy community trust in health services. Nurses must never share patient information with family members, community leaders, or unauthorized aid workers.

Furthermore, the principle of 'Do No Harm' dictates that every action must prioritize the physical and emotional safety of the survivor. Survivors must retain complete autonomy over their decisions, including whether to accept treatment, report the crime, or engage with legal services. Forcing a survivor to take legal action against their will re-traumatizes them and violates their fundamental human rights.

Referral Pathways and Inter-Agency Collaboration

No single nurse or health clinic can provide all the specialized services a survivor needs. Effective care depends on a functioning referral pathway—an organized network of local and international agencies coordinating medical, psychological, legal, and shelter services.

When a survivor requires services beyond the clinic's capacity, such as safe housing or legal aid, the nurse facilitates a safe referral. This process requires explicit consent from the survivor and adherence to secure transfer protocols that do not expose their identity. Inter-agency collaboration ensures that the survivor receives comprehensive, wraparound support without having to retell their traumatic story repeatedly to different organizations.

Prevention Strategies and Community-Based Support

While clinical treatment is essential, stopping gender-based violence requires robust community-level prevention. Nurses work alongside community leaders, youth groups, and women's associations to challenge harmful cultural norms and raise awareness about survivor rights.

Prevention activities include establishing safe spaces for women and girls, organizing community education dialogues on gender equality, and training local volunteers to recognize early warning signs of abuse. Engaging men and boys as allies in these educational campaigns is equally critical to shifting patriarchal attitudes. Community-based support networks create protective shields that deter perpetrators and foster an environment where dignity and safety prevail.
