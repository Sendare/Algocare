Gender Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence

Gender-Based Violence (GBV) refers to any harmful act directed against an individual based on their socially ascribed gender roles and inequalities. While men and boys can experience GBV, women and girls bear the vast majority of the global and local burden due to deeply entrenched power imbalances. In the Nigerian context, this violence cuts across all socioeconomic, religious, and ethnic divides, manifesting in domestic settings, workplaces, and educational institutions. Nurses must recognize that GBV is fundamentally a human rights violation and a major public health emergency, rather than a private family matter.

Types and Manifestations of GBV

Gender-based violence takes multiple intersecting forms that extend far beyond physical battering. Nurses encounter survivors presenting with injuries resulting from diverse abusive behaviors:

  • Physical violence: Inflicting bodily harm through hitting, slapping, burning, or acid throwing.
  • Sexual violence: Forced sexual intercourse, rape, attempted rape, and non-consensual sexual touching.
  • Psychological abuse: Constant intimidation, verbal insults, isolation from family and friends, and threats of harm.
  • Economic abuse: Withholding financial support, denying access to employment, or seizing a partner's earnings.
  • Harmful traditional practices: Child marriage, female genital mutilation, and widowhood rites.

Socio-Cultural Risk Factors in Nigeria

Understanding the local drivers of GBV is essential for holistic nursing care in Nigeria. Patriarchy remains a foundational pillar in many communities, promoting male dominance and female subjugation. Cultural norms often normalize domestic discipline, making it difficult for survivors to speak out without facing social stigma or victim-blaming. Economic vulnerability compounds these risks, as poverty and lack of financial independence trap many women in abusive environments. Additionally, weak enforcement of protective laws creates a culture of impunity for perpetrators.

Health Consequences and Clinical Presentation

The physical and psychological toll of GBV is extensive. Survivors frequently present with acute injuries like lacerations, fractures, bruises, and traumatic brain injuries. Sexual violence introduces risks of unwanted pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. Beyond acute trauma, chronic somatic complaints are common, such as unexplained pelvic pain, gastrointestinal disorders, and chronic headaches linked to sustained stress. Mental health struggles frequently manifest as depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and suicidal ideation.

Nursing Assessment and Identification

Nurses are often the first healthcare professionals to interact with survivors, making identification critical. Routine screening should be integrated into clinical encounters, especially in emergency departments, antenatal clinics, and general outpatient units. Nurses must look for red flags such as injuries inconsistent with the provided history, partner dominance during consultations, or excessive anxiety. Creating a private, safe, and confidential environment is paramount. Direct, non-judgmental questioning encourages disclosure without making the survivor feel interrogated or blamed.

Immediate Management and Emergency Care

Immediate care focuses on stabilization, treating acute physical trauma, and preventing secondary health complications. For sexual assault survivors, time-sensitive interventions are critical. Emergency contraception should be offered within 120 hours to prevent pregnancy. Post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV must be initiated as soon as possible, ideally within 72 hours of exposure. Forensic evidence collection requires meticulous documentation, chain of custody maintenance, and specific consent from the survivor. Nurses must prioritize trauma-informed care, ensuring the patient retains control over all medical decisions.

Psychosocial Support and Counseling

Medical treatment alone is insufficient for recovery; psychological rehabilitation is vital. Nurses provide basic psychological first aid by offering empathy, active listening, and reassurance without pressuring survivors to share details before they are ready. Referral to professional mental health counselors and social workers is essential for long-term recovery. Support groups connect survivors with peers, reducing feelings of isolation and shame. Empowering the survivor involves discussing safety planning, rebuilding self-esteem, and exploring community support resources.

Legal Frameworks and Mandatory Reporting

Nurses must navigate legal obligations and protect patient confidentiality. In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act provides robust legal protection against various forms of abuse, though implementation varies by state. Healthcare providers must balance patient confidentiality with mandatory reporting laws, especially regarding minors and vulnerable adults. Accurate and objective documentation in the medical record is crucial, as clinical notes frequently serve as legal evidence in court proceedings. Nurses should collaborate closely with legal aid organizations and law enforcement when authorized by the survivor.

Prevention Strategies and Community Advocacy

Ending gender-based violence requires proactive community engagement and education. Nurses play a crucial role in primary prevention by conducting community awareness programs that challenge harmful gender norms and promote gender equality. Advocacy involves working with local leaders, religious bodies, and youth groups to foster safe environments. Educational campaigns in schools teach young people about respectful relationships, consent, and conflict resolution. By addressing the root causes at the community level, nurses contribute to stopping violence before it starts.
