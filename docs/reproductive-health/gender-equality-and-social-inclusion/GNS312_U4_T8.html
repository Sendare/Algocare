Gender Based Violence

Introduction and Scope of Gender-Based Violence

Gender-based violence (GBV) encompasses any harmful act directed against an individual based on socially ascribed gender roles and inequalities. While men and boys can experience abuse, women and girls disproportionately bear the burden globally. In clinical practice, recognizing that GBV transcends physical boundaries is vital. It is a profound violation of fundamental human rights and a major public health crisis with deep-rooted systemic drivers.

Types and Manifestations of Abuse

GBV is rarely limited to physical strikes. Clinical encounters require an awareness of its diverse forms: - **Physical violence**: Inflicting bodily harm through hitting, choking, burning, or weapon use. - **Sexual violence**: Non-consensual sexual contact, forced marriage, marital rape, and reproductive coercion. - **Psychological abuse**: Chronic intimidation, verbal degradation, isolation, and threats of abandonment. - **Economic abuse**: Withholding financial resources, restricting employment, or sabotaging career opportunities. - **Harmful traditional practices**: Female genital mutilation, child betrothal, and widowhood rites.

Socio-Cultural Context and Risk Factors in Nigeria

In many Nigerian communities, patriarchal social structures normalize male dominance and female subjugation. Cultural norms often frame domestic disputes as private family matters rather than crimes, discouraging disclosure. Economic dependency, high poverty rates, and traditional beliefs surrounding inheritance and bride price further entrench vulnerability. Recognizing these localized dynamics helps explain why survivors frequently face intense stigmatization when attempting to leave abusive environments.

Health Consequences and Clinical Presentation

The physiological and mental sequelae of abuse are often chronic and multifaceted. Survivors may present with unexplained somatic complaints, recurrent pelvic pain, traumatic injuries, or unwanted pregnancies. Sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, are common clinical concerns following sexual assault. Psychologically, survivors frequently manifest depression, severe anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and somatic panic attacks. Nurses must look beyond acute injuries to appreciate this broader symptom constellation.

Nursing Assessment and Identification

Routine screening in clinical settings normalizes conversations around safety and abuse. Nurses should conduct assessments in a private, confidential space without family members present. Ask direct, non-judgmental questions regarding safety at home. Watch for discrepancies between physical injuries and the history provided, delayed presentation for medical care, and visible signs of distress. Establishing trust is the cornerstone of successful disclosure.

Immediate Management and Emergency Care

Emergency care prioritizes stabilizing acute life-threatening injuries and preventing further harm. For sexual assault survivors presenting within the golden window (typically 72 hours), immediate interventions include emergency contraception, baseline STI prophylaxis, and forensic evidence collection using a standardized kit. Document all physical findings meticulously with body maps and direct quotes when possible. Ensure the survivor understands all proposed medical procedures before proceeding.

Psychosocial Support and Counseling

Trauma-informed care shifts the question from "What is wrong with you?" to "What happened to you?" Psychological first aid helps stabilize acute emotional distress through active listening, reassurance, and validation. Nurses must avoid victim-blaming language and respect patient autonomy at every step. Referral to specialized mental health professionals, social workers, and local support networks facilitates long-term recovery.

Legal Frameworks and Reporting in Nigeria

Nigeria's legal landscape regarding GBV includes the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act, which criminalizes various forms of domestic and sexual abuse at the federal level. Nurses must understand their institutional policies and legal obligations regarding documentation and evidence preservation. While patient confidentiality is paramount, mandatory reporting laws or court subpeonas may dictate how clinical information is shared with legal authorities.

Prevention Strategies and Community Advocacy

Stopping GBV requires upstream interventions that challenge gender inequality and transform social norms. Nurses play a crucial role as community educators, collaborating with local leaders, schools, and grassroots organizations. Advocacy campaigns that promote gender equity, challenge harmful traditional practices, and educate youth on respectful relationships are essential. Empowering communities creates a protective network that deters violence before it occurs.
