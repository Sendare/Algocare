Gender Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence

Gender-Based Violence (GBV) is a critical public health and human rights issue affecting individuals globally, with profound implications in Nigeria. GBV refers to any harmful act directed against an individual based on their gender, stemming from socially ascribed differences, power imbalances, and unequal power relations between men and women. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding GBV is essential because nurses are frequently the first point of clinical contact for victims seeking medical attention. GBV disproportionately affects women and girls, although men and boys can also be survivors. In the Nigerian context, GBV manifests within various settings, including domestic environments, educational institutions, workplaces, and conflict zones. Recognizing the broader definition of GBV goes beyond physical abuse; it encompasses a wide range of human rights violations that threaten physical, mental, sexual, and reproductive health. As future healthcare professionals, nurses must approach GBV not merely as a private family matter, but as a severe systemic issue that requires a compassionate, evidence-based, and professional healthcare response. Nurses must dismantle personal biases and adopt a survivor-centered approach that prioritizes the safety, confidentiality, and autonomy of the individual seeking care. Public health data underscores that untreated or unaddressed GBV leads to chronic illnesses, psychological trauma, and even mortality. Therefore, mastering the foundational concepts of GBV equips Nigerian nursing students to identify, support, and advocate effectively for vulnerable populations within clinical and community settings.

Types and Manifestations of Gender-Based Violence

Gender-Based Violence manifests in multiple interrelated forms that inflict physical, sexual, psychological, and economic harm upon survivors. Physical violence involves acts of bodily harm, such as slapping, beating, kicking, acid baths, and mutilation, which can lead to permanent disability or death. Sexual violence includes rape, attempted rape, forced sexual acts, unwanted sexual advances, and child sexual abuse, often compounded by cultural stigmas in Nigeria. Psychological and emotional violence is characterized by verbal abuse, constant humiliation, intimidation, isolation, threats of harm, and coercive control, which severely erodes the victim's self-esteem and mental stability. Economic violence involves restricting a partner's access to financial resources, preventing them from obtaining employment, or sabotaging their educational and career opportunities to maintain total dependency. Furthermore, harmful traditional practices prevalent in certain Nigerian communities—such as female genital mutilation (FGM), forced child marriages, early marriages, and abusive widowhood rites—represent deeply institutionalized forms of GBV. Recognizing these diverse manifestations is crucial for nursing assessment, as survivors often present with a combination of physical injuries and non-specific psychological symptoms. Each type of violence carries distinct clinical indicators that require targeted nursing interventions, comprehensive documentation, and a multidisciplinary approach to ensure holistic care and recovery for the affected individual.

Root Causes and Socio-Cultural Risk Factors in Nigeria

Understanding the root causes and socio-cultural risk factors of Gender-Based Violence in Nigeria requires examining deeply entrenched patriarchal structures, cultural norms, and economic inequalities. Patriarchy establishes a social system where men hold primary power and dominate roles in political leadership, moral authority, social privilege, and control of property. This dynamic fosters a culture of male entitlement and female subordination, making women more susceptible to abuse. Socio-cultural norms often normalize domestic violence, viewing it as a private family matter or a legitimate method of discipline rather than a criminal offense. Religious and traditional misinterpretations are frequently invoked to justify the subjugation of women and girls, discouraging them from reporting abuse. Economic vulnerability is another significant risk factor; poverty, lack of formal education, and financial dependency trap many Nigerian women in abusive relationships because they lack the resources to live independently. Additionally, systemic failures, such as corruption, weak legal enforcement, and stigmatization by law enforcement agencies, create a climate of impunity for perpetrators. Societal silence and victim-blaming further deter survivors from seeking help. For nursing students, contextualizing these risk factors is essential for delivering culturally sensitive care, avoiding judgmental attitudes, and understanding why a survivor may exhibit hesitation in leaving an abusive environment or reporting their abuser to authorities.

Health Consequences and Clinical Presentation

Gender-Based Violence inflicts devastating physical, reproductive, and psychological consequences on survivors, often presenting with complex clinical pictures. Physical manifestations include acute trauma such as lacerations, fractures, contusions, burns, and traumatic brain injury, as well as chronic conditions like gastrointestinal disorders, musculoskeletal pain, and headaches. Sexual and reproductive health consequences are particularly profound, encompassing unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, obstetric fistulas, sexually transmitted infections (STIs) including HIV, and chronic pelvic pain. Psychologically, survivors frequently present with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), severe depression, anxiety disorders, sleep disturbances, suicidal ideation, and substance abuse as coping mechanisms. In clinical settings in Nigeria, survivors may present with vague somatic complaints—such as chronic fatigue, abdominal pain, or insomnia—when they are unable or afraid to disclose the true cause of their trauma. Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion, recognizing that repeated visits for unexplained injuries or chronic pain syndromes are major red flags for ongoing abuse. Comprehensive clinical assessment requires looking beyond the obvious physical wounds to evaluate the holistic well-being of the patient. Accurate documentation of all clinical findings is critical not only for immediate medical treatment but also for preserving forensic evidence should the survivor choose to pursue legal action against the perpetrator in the future.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of Survivors

Identifying survivors of Gender-Based Violence requires acute clinical observation, excellent communication skills, and adherence to a strict survivor-centered approach. Nurses must create a safe, private, and confidential environment that encourages patients to disclose abuse without fear of judgment, exposure, or further harm. Universal screening—asking all patients about abuse regardless of visible injuries—is a recommended best practice in emergency and outpatient departments. Nurses should watch for behavioral indicators such as excessive anxiety, reluctance to make eye contact, hesitation when speaking in the presence of a partner, and inappropriate or defensive explanations for injuries. When conducting interviews, nurses must use open-ended, non-judgmental questions like 'Do you feel safe at home?' or 'Has anyone ever hurt or threatened you?' and listen actively without interrupting or blaming the victim. It is vital to respect the survivor's autonomy; pressuring a patient to disclose details or report to the police before they are ready can re-traumatize them and compromise their safety. The nurse's role is to validate the survivor's experience, affirm that the violence is never their fault, and offer a supportive presence. Effective nursing identification bridges the gap between acute medical intervention and comprehensive long-term recovery, ensuring that vulnerable individuals do not slip through the cracks of the healthcare system.

Immediate Management and Emergency Care

Immediate management and emergency care for survivors of Gender-Based Violence—particularly survivors of sexual assault—require a rapid, standardized, and compassionate clinical response within a strict golden window (typically within 72 hours for sexual assault). The primary nursing priority is stabilization of life-threatening physical injuries, airway management, and control of severe bleeding. For sexual assault survivors, emergency care must include prompt administration of Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP) to prevent HIV infection, emergency contraception to prevent unwanted pregnancy, and prophylaxis for other sexually transmitted infections such as chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis. Forensic evidence collection must be performed meticulously using a standardized rape kit (where available), ensuring a strict chain of custody if the survivor consents. Pain management, wound care, and psychological first aid are critical components of the immediate intervention package. Nurses must ensure privacy, provide clean clothing, and offer a bath or shower (while preserving evidence if forensic examination is pending). Documentation must be objective, factual, and detailed, using the patient's exact words where possible without making subjective legal conclusions. Collaborating closely with physicians, laboratory scientists, social workers, and law enforcement (with patient consent) ensures that the survivor receives comprehensive emergency care that addresses both medical survival and legal justice needs.

Psychosocial Support and Counseling

Psychosocial support and counseling are indispensable components of holistic care for survivors of Gender-Based Violence, facilitating long-term emotional recovery and resilience. Following emergency medical care, survivors often grapple with intense trauma, shame, guilt, and depression that require specialized psychological intervention. Nurses provide initial psychological first aid by offering a calm presence, active listening, reassurance of safety, and practical assistance. For ongoing support, survivors should be referred to professional mental health counselors, psychiatric nurses, or clinical psychologists trained in trauma-informed therapy, such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) or Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR). In Nigeria, community-based support groups play a vital role, allowing survivors to share experiences, reduce feelings of isolation, and rebuild self-worth in a safe, non-judgmental peer environment. Family counseling may also be necessary, though only with the explicit, informed consent of the survivor, ensuring that family interventions do not place the patient at risk of secondary victimization or further abuse. Nurses must also prioritize self-care and debriefing to prevent compassion fatigue and secondary traumatic stress when caring for trauma survivors over extended periods. Through empathetic counseling and structured psychosocial referrals, nurses empower survivors to regain control over their lives and successfully transition from victimhood to survivorship.

Legal Frameworks and Mandatory Reporting in Nigeria

Navigating the legal frameworks and mandatory reporting guidelines regarding Gender-Based Violence in Nigeria is a critical professional responsibility for nurses. Nigeria operates under a federal system where laws vary by state, but significant national legislation provides protections, notably the Violence Against Persons (Prohibition) (VAPP) Act passed in 2015. The VAPP Act criminalizes various forms of GBV, including physical violence, emotional abuse, harmful traditional practices, and economic abuse, prescribing stiff penalties for offenders. Nurses must be familiar with local legal mechanisms, including the availability of free legal aid, protection orders, and specialized gender desks within the Nigerian Police Force. Regarding mandatory reporting, ethical and legal obligations often clash or require delicate balancing; while nurses are advocates for patient autonomy and confidentiality, certain jurisdictions or institutional policies mandate reporting specific cases of severe assault, child abuse, or rape to law enforcement or child protection agencies. In cases involving minors or incapacitated adults, mandatory reporting laws override standard confidentiality rules to ensure immediate protection. Nurses must document all findings objectively, securely store forensic samples, and guide survivors on legal options without coercing them. Understanding these laws empowers Nigerian nursing students to protect their patients' legal rights while adhering to professional codes of ethics and statutory requirements.

Prevention Strategies and Community Advocacy

Prevention strategies and community advocacy are essential for eradicating Gender-Based Violence at its root, shifting the focus from reactive clinical treatment to proactive societal transformation in Nigeria. Nurses play a pivotal role as public health advocates, educators, and community leaders who can drive awareness campaigns and challenge harmful gender norms. Primary prevention strategies include conducting educational workshops in schools, markets, and religious centers to promote gender equality, respectful relationships, and zero tolerance for violence. Secondary prevention involves early identification and intervention for at-risk individuals, while tertiary prevention focuses on rehabilitation and preventing the recurrence of abuse among survivors. Community advocacy requires collaborating with local chiefs, religious leaders, youth groups, and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) to dismantle patriarchal beliefs and harmful traditional practices like early marriage and FGM. Nurses can also advocate for stronger institutional policies, better-funded shelter systems, and improved training for healthcare workers and law enforcement personnel. By engaging communities in open dialogues about consent, human rights, and mental health, nursing students and professionals help build a safer society. Empowering communities with knowledge creates a supportive environment where survivors feel encouraged to speak out, perpetrators are held accountable, and the generational cycle of gender-based violence is ultimately broken.
