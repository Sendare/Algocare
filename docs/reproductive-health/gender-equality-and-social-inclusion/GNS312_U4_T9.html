Forms of Gender Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence in Nursing

Gender-Based Violence (GBV) is a critical public health and human rights issue that nursing students in Nigeria must fully understand. GBV refers to harmful acts directed at an individual based on their gender, stemming from socially unequal power relationships. In clinical and community healthcare settings across Nigeria, nurses are frequently the first point of contact for survivors. Recognizing the various manifestations of GBV is essential because victims often present with vague physical complaints, psychological distress, or injuries that do not match their provided history. Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion, create a safe, non-judgmental environment, and approach every patient with trauma-informed care. Understanding the root causes, including cultural norms and systemic inequalities, helps nursing professionals provide empathetic support without victim-blaming. Furthermore, Nigeria's healthcare framework requires nurses to not only treat acute medical needs but also document findings accurately for potential legal proceedings, ensure patient confidentiality, and facilitate multi-disciplinary referrals to social workers, law enforcement, and psychological support services. By mastering the fundamentals of GBV, nursing students prepare themselves to act as effective advocates, educators, and frontline defenders of vulnerable populations within diverse healthcare delivery systems.

Physical Violence

Physical violence is perhaps the most outwardly visible form of gender-based violence, characterized by any act of physical force that causes injury, pain, or harm. For nursing students in Nigeria, identifying physical violence involves recognizing patterns of trauma such as unexplained bruises, fractures, burns, lacerations, or repeated visits to emergency departments. Perpetrators often use physical aggression to establish control, intimidate, and instill fear in the victim. During clinical assessments, nurses must carefully document the location, size, shape, and color of all physical injuries using body maps and photographic evidence where hospital policy and patient consent allow. It is vital to separate the patient from accompanying family members or partners during the history-taking process, as abusers often monitor or speak on behalf of the victim. Nursing care goes beyond wound dressing and pain management; it requires assessing immediate safety risks, developing a safety plan with the patient, and connecting them with legal and shelter resources. Understanding the physical manifestations of abuse equips nurses to intervene early, prevent escalating harm, and potentially save lives within clinical and community healthcare environments.

Emotional Violence

Emotional and psychological violence involves behaviors that psychologically harm a person, lower their self-esteem, or induce fear through intimidation, humiliation, and constant verbal abuse. While it leaves no physical scars, emotional violence can be deeply debilitating, leading to anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and suicidal ideation. Nigerian nursing students must recognize that emotional abuse frequently coexists with physical and sexual violence and is often used to wear down a victim's independence. Manifestations include insults, isolation from friends and family, threats of abandonment, controlling behavior regarding clothing or movement, and constant surveillance. Patients experiencing emotional abuse may present with chronic somatic complaints like headaches, insomnia, or gastrointestinal issues linked to prolonged stress. In nursing assessment, active listening and validating the patient's emotional experience are critical. Nurses must avoid minimizing the patient's pain and instead provide a reassuring environment that fosters trust. Documenting psychological symptoms accurately and offering counseling referrals helps address the invisible wounds of gender-based violence, promoting holistic healing and recovery.

Sexual Violence

Sexual violence encompasses any sexual act, attempt to obtain a sexual act, or other act directed against a person's sexuality using coercion, by any person regardless of their relationship to the victim. For nursing professionals, managing cases of sexual assault requires specialized, sensitive, and urgent clinical interventions. Victims may present to emergency departments or clinics following rape, attempted rape, or coerced sexual acts. The immediate priorities for the nurse include ensuring physical safety, providing psychological first aid, preventing sexually transmitted infections (STIs) through prophylaxis, offering emergency contraception when indicated, and collecting forensic evidence meticulously if the assault occurred within the legal time frame. Nigerian nurses must approach sexual violence survivors with absolute empathy, avoiding judgmental language or victim-blaming queries. Maintaining chain of custody for forensic specimens is vital for any subsequent legal action. Furthermore, comprehensive care involves arranging for psychological counseling, ongoing follow-up for infection screenings, and linking the survivor with specialized support networks and legal aid services designed to protect their rights and dignity.

Economic and Financial Abuse

Economic and financial abuse is a subtle yet pervasive form of gender-based violence where the abuser controls the victim's access to economic resources, employment opportunities, and financial independence. This form of abuse restricts the victim's ability to support themselves, forcing dependency and making it extremely difficult for them to leave an abusive environment. Examples include withholding money, forbidding the partner from seeking employment or education, sabotaging current job performance, taking control of earnings, or running up debt in the partner's name. Nursing students in Nigeria must recognize that financial dependency is a primary barrier keeping many individuals trapped in abusive relationships. During assessments, nurses should pay attention to signs of financial restriction, such as a patient lacking money for transport to follow-up visits, inability to pay for prescribed medications, or showing extreme anxiety regarding the cost of healthcare. Nursing interventions involve connecting patients with social welfare programs, economic empowerment initiatives, and community support groups that help build self-reliance and facilitate safe exits from abusive households.

Harmful Traditional Practices

Harmful traditional practices (HTPs) refer to cultural, religious, or traditional norms that violate the fundamental human rights, health, and bodily integrity of women and girls. In various regions of Nigeria, prevalent HTPs include Female Genital Mutilation (FGM), child or forced marriage, breast ironing, widowhood rites, and accusations of witchcraft. These practices are deeply entrenched in patriarchal structures and are often defended under the guise of cultural preservation. Nursing students must develop cultural competence combined with a strong human rights framework to identify and address HTPs without alienating communities. FGM, for instance, can lead to severe immediate complications such as hemorrhage, shock, and infection, as well as long-term issues like chronic pain, dyspareunia, obstetric complications, and psychological trauma. Nurses play a crucial role in providing specialized care for complications arising from HTPs, educating communities on the adverse health impacts, and advocating for policy enforcement against these practices. Compassionate, non-judgmental nursing care combined with community-based advocacy helps protect vulnerable girls and women from ongoing cultural violence.

Clinical Identification and Nursing Assessment

Clinical identification and nursing assessment form the cornerstone of effective intervention for survivors of gender-based violence. Because victims may not spontaneously disclose abuse due to fear, shame, or stigma, nurses must utilize routine screening techniques and recognize clinical red flags. Red flags include frequent emergency room visits, vague somatic complaints, injuries inconsistent with the given history, delayed seeking of medical care, and an overbearing partner who insists on answering for the patient. A thorough assessment involves conducting private interviews in a secure environment, using open-ended questions like 'How are things at home?' or 'Do you feel safe in your relationship?'. Nurses must document all findings objectively, recording direct patient quotes without subjective bias, as medical records serve as critical legal documents. Safety assessment is vital: determining if weapons are present, if children are at risk, and establishing an emergency exit plan. Finally, nursing care requires coordinating referrals to social services, legal aid, safe houses, and psychological counseling, ensuring a holistic, multidisciplinary approach to patient recovery and empowerment.
