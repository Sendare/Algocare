Forms of Gender Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence in Nursing

Gender-based violence (GBV) represents any harmful act directed against an individual based on socially ascribed gender roles and inequalities. Within the healthcare setting, nurses frequently encounter victims whose physical and psychological ailments stem directly from systemic abuse. Recognizing this dynamic is vital because clinical symptoms often mask underlying interpersonal violence. Victims may present repeatedly with vague somatic complaints, defensive injuries, or chronic anxiety disorders without disclosing the root cause. Understanding that GBV cuts across socioeconomic, religious, and educational strata prevents clinical bias and ensures that care remains objective, empathetic, and trauma-informed.

Physical Violence

Physical violence involves any intentional act causing injury, pain, or bodily harm through the use of physical force. In clinical practice, nurses must look for specific injury patterns that suggest interpersonal assault rather than accidental trauma.

  • Contusions and Welts: Frequently found on the head, neck, arms, and torso in varying stages of healing.
  • Fractures: Common in the facial bones, ribs, and wrists due to defensive positioning during attacks.
  • Bites and Burns: Distinctive thermal or dental marks that clearly indicate deliberate infliction rather than everyday mishaps.
  • Abdominal Trauma: Blunt force trauma to the abdomen, which poses an immediate internal hemorrhage risk, particularly in pregnant women.

Emotional and Psychological Abuse

Emotional abuse inflicts mental anguish through verbal assaults, intimidation, humiliation, and constant surveillance. Although it leaves no visible bruises, its toll manifests through psychosomatic disorders, severe depression, and suicidal ideation. Perpetrators systematically erode the victim's self-esteem until independent decision-making feels impossible. Nurses should pay close attention to behavioral cues during consultations, such as a partner answering all questions on the patient's behalf or extreme hesitation from the patient before speaking. Documenting specific behavioral observations is just as critical as charting physical wounds.

Sexual Violence

Sexual violence encompasses any non-consensual sexual contact, attempted sexual contact, or acts directed against a person's sexuality using coercion, force, or threats. In many cultural contexts, profound stigma surrounds sexual assault, causing victims to delay seeking medical attention.

Immediate nursing priorities focus on stabilizing physical injuries, providing prophylactic treatment against sexually transmitted infections, offering emergency contraception when indicated, and carefully preserving forensic evidence. Non-judgmental communication creates a safe space, helping to mitigate the acute psychological trauma experienced by survivors.

Economic and Financial Abuse

Economic abuse involves controlling a partner's ability to acquire, use, and maintain financial resources, thereby creating total dependency. Perpetrators may forbid employment, confiscate earnings, deny access to basic household funds, or run up debt in the victim's name. This form of abuse directly traps individuals in dangerous environments because leaving requires financial means that have been systematically withheld. Nurses assessing socio-economic vulnerability should recognize that financial control is a powerful tool of coercion used to maintain dominance within households.

Harmful Traditional Practices

Harmful traditional practices refer to cultural, religious, or traditional behaviors that violate fundamental human rights and cause physical or psychological harm. Examples include child marriage, female genital mutilation (FGM), and severe widowhood rites. These practices are often deeply entrenched in community norms, making intervention delicate. Nurses working in rural or traditional settings must provide culturally sensitive yet firm health education regarding the physiological complications of these practices, such as chronic pelvic pain, obstetric fistulae, and severe psychological trauma.

Clinical Identification and Nursing Assessment

Effective clinical identification relies on maintaining high suspicion, creating private spaces for interviews, and asking direct, non-judgmental screening questions. Universal screening ensures that no patient is singled out based on prejudice while allowing hidden abuse to come to light. Nurses must document findings objectively, using exact quotes from the patient rather than subjective interpretations. Ensuring patient confidentiality and adhering to local legal frameworks regarding mandatory reporting safeguard both the patient's well-being and institutional integrity.
