Forms of Gender Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence in Nursing

Gender-based violence (GBV) is any harmful act directed against a person's will based on socially ascribed differences between males and females. As nurses, encountering victims of GBV is common in emergency departments, maternity wards, and general outpatient clinics. Recognizing that GBV crosses all socio-economic, religious, and cultural boundaries is vital for delivering unbiased care.

Victims often present with vague physical complaints rather than disclosing abuse immediately. A nurse must look beyond surface injuries to understand the broader social and psychological context of a patient's health. Creating a safe, non-judgmental environment encourages patients to share their experiences when they feel ready.

Physical Violence

Physical violence involves any intentional act causing injury, pain, or bodily harm through the use of physical force. In clinical settings, nurses frequently treat injuries resulting from pushing, slapping, beating, choking, or the use of weapons.

Common physical presentations include:

  • Contusions and Hematomas: Bruises in various stages of healing, especially on the head, neck, breasts, and abdomen.
  • Fractures: Broken bones resulting from blunt force trauma or defensive postures.
  • Burns: Injuries caused by scalding liquids, hot objects, or chemical agents.
  • Traumatic Brain Injuries: Concussions or head trauma resulting from severe blows.

Emotional and Psychological Abuse

Emotional and psychological abuse inflicts mental anguish through verbal assaults, intimidation, humiliation, and isolation. While it leaves no visible scars, the internal damage profoundly impacts a victim's mental health and recovery.

  • Verbal Degradation: Constant insults, name-calling, and public humiliation designed to erode self-worth.
  • Isolation: Restricting contact with family, friends, and support systems to increase dependence.
  • Threats and Intimidation: Threatening harm to the victim, their children, or their pets to maintain control.
  • Gaslighting: Manipulating the victim into doubting their own sanity, memory, or perception of reality.

Sexual Violence

Sexual violence encompasses any sexual act, attempt to obtain a sexual act, or other act directed against a person's sexuality using coercion, force, or manipulation. This form of abuse violates bodily autonomy and carries severe physical and psychological consequences.

Nurses must handle sexual assault survivors with extreme sensitivity, utilizing specialized forensic protocols when appropriate. Potential complications include sexually transmitted infections, unwanted pregnancies, severe genital trauma, and profound post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Establishing trust and avoiding re-traumatization during examinations are core nursing responsibilities.

Economic and Financial Abuse

Economic abuse occurs when one partner has total control over the other's financial resources, restricting their ability to acquire and maintain independence. This form of control traps victims by removing the financial means required to leave an abusive environment.

Common tactics include withholding money for basic needs, forbidding employment, confiscating earnings, and running up debt in the victim's name. A patient facing financial abuse may struggle to pay for necessary medications, transport to follow-up visits, or basic hospital supplies.

Harmful Traditional Practices

Harmful traditional practices are culturally or socially accepted behaviors that violate fundamental human rights and cause severe physical and psychological harm, disproportionately affecting women and girls. Nurses practicing in diverse communities must recognize these practices to provide appropriate care and education.

  • Female Genital Mutilation (FGM): Partial or total removal of external female genitalia for non-medical reasons, leading to chronic pain, infections, and obstetric complications.
  • Child Marriage: Forcing underage girls into marital unions, interrupting education and resulting in early pregnancy risks like obstetric fistula.
  • Honor Killings: Acts of violence committed against family members perceived to have brought dishonor upon the family name.

Clinical Identification and Nursing Assessment

Effective clinical identification requires routine screening for GBV in all female patients, regardless of whether visible injuries are present. Nurses should conduct interviews in private, away from partners or family members who might intimidate the patient.

Using direct, non-judgmental screening questions helps uncover hidden abuse. Documenting findings objectively using the patient's own words, alongside clear body maps and photographs when permitted, is essential for legal and clinical continuity. Finally, nurses must connect patients with local support networks, social workers, and shelters.
