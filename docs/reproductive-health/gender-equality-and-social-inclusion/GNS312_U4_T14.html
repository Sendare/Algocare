Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act (VAPP Act)

Introduction and Historical Background of the VAPP Act

Before the enactment of the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act in 2015, Nigeria relied heavily on the colonial-era Criminal Code and Penal Code to handle cases of assault and domestic violence. These older laws had major loopholes, often ignoring domestic violence, spousal battery, and marital rape. Activists, civil society organizations, and human rights lawyers pushed for years to create a dedicated law that addresses modern forms of violence, particularly against women and girls. Signed into law in May 2015, the VAPP Act marked a major turning point. It shifted the legal focus from merely punishing general crimes to specifically protecting individuals from all forms of interpersonal violence, abuse, and harmful cultural practices.

Core Objectives and Scope of the Legislation

The primary goal of the VAPP Act is to eliminate violence in private and public life, prohibit all forms of violence against persons, and maximize protection and effective remedies for victims while punishing offenders. Unlike older legislation that viewed domestic matters as private family affairs, the VAPP Act explicitly brings domestic abuse into the realm of criminal law. Its scope is comprehensive, covering offenses committed by family members, intimate partners, employers, and strangers alike. It applies strictly within the Federal Capital Territory (FCT) Abuja, but state governments across Nigeria are encouraged to domesticate the law so it can apply in their respective states.

Definitions and Categorization of Offenses

The VAPP Act breaks down violence into distinct legal categories to ensure proper prosecution. The main categories include physical violence, psychological abuse, sexual violence, harmful traditional practices, and economic abuse. By defining these terms clearly, the law removes ambiguity that often allowed perpetrators to escape justice. For instance, economic abuse—such as forcefully depriving a partner or dependent of financial sustenance—is explicitly recognized as a criminal act. Understanding these categories helps healthcare workers and law enforcement officers properly identify and document different forms of abuse during patient intake.

Physical, Psychological, and Emotional Violence Provisions

Physical violence under the Act involves any act of battery, physical restraint, or willful causing of bodily injury. Psychological and emotional violence, though harder to prove, are treated with equal seriousness. This includes verbal assaults, constant intimidation, stalking, harassment, and threats of harm that destroy a person's mental well-being. Victims often present to healthcare facilities with vague somatic complaints, anxiety, or depression stemming from psychological abuse. Nurses must recognize that emotional trauma is just as real and legally actionable under the VAPP Act as a physical broken bone.

Sexual Violence and Harmful Traditional Practices

The VAPP Act provides robust protection against sexual violence, explicitly criminalizing rape, attempted rape, sexual assault, and forced financial or marital isolation related to sex. Crucially, the Act criminalizes marital rape, a major milestone in Nigerian legal history. Furthermore, it prohibits harmful traditional practices that violate human dignity. These include female genital mutilation (FGM), forced isolation of widows, harmful widowhood rites, forced marriages, and girl-child betrothal. Nurses working in rural and urban communities must be vigilant in identifying victims of these harmful cultural practices and connecting them with legal and social services.

Rights and Protections of Victims and Survivors

Victims and survivors under the VAPP Act are granted specific rights to ensure their safety, dignity, and recovery. These include the right to medical attention, psychological counseling, legal aid, and temporary shelter. Law enforcement agencies are mandated to treat victims with respect and protect them from further intimidation by perpetrators. Additionally, the Act allows courts to issue protection orders that legally prevent an abuser from contacting or coming near the survivor. Healthcare professionals play a vital role in upholding these rights by ensuring safe, confidential environments where victims can disclose abuse without fear of judgment.

Penalties, Sentencing, and Legal Enforcement

The VAPP Act prescribes clear penalties for offenders, ranging from heavy monetary fines to lengthy prison sentences depending on the severity of the crime. For example, offenses like rape carry severe mandatory prison terms, while lesser forms of assault or emotional abuse carry fines or shorter jail times. The law also mandates the creation of a Sex Offender Register to track convicted perpetrators and prevent them from working in sensitive environments like schools or hospitals. Judges are empowered to order convicts to pay compensation to the victims for medical expenses and rehabilitation.

The Role of Nurses and Healthcare Providers in VAPP Act Implementation

Nurses are frequently the first professional contact for victims of abuse seeking medical help. Consequently, nursing staff hold a frontline responsibility in identifying signs of physical, sexual, or emotional trauma. Healthcare providers must maintain meticulous medical records, take clear photographs of injuries with proper consent, and fill out forensic medical report forms accurately. These documents often serve as vital evidence in court during the prosecution of offenders. Beyond clinical documentation, nurses provide empathetic counseling, ensure patient privacy, and collaborate with social workers and law enforcement to guarantee the safety of survivors.
