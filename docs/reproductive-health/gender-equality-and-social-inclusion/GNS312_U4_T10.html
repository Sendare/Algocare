Types of Gender Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence in Nursing Practice

Gender-based violence (GBV) is a major public health crisis and a severe violation of human rights. It encompasses any harmful act directed against an individual based on their socially ascribed gender roles and inequalities. In clinical practice, nurses encounter survivors across emergency departments, antenatal clinics, and general outpatient wards. Recognizing the signs of GBV requires moving beyond physical injuries to understand the broader social and structural power imbalances that trap victims. Healthcare facilities often represent the first safe haven a survivor reaches, making the nurse's role in early identification and non-judgmental care vital to recovery and safety.

Domestic and Intimate Partner Violence

Domestic violence occurs within the private sphere of the home, typically involving family members or cohabitants. A specific, highly prevalent subset of this is intimate partner violence (IPV), which involves physical aggression, psychological abuse, sexual coercion, and controlling behaviors by a current or former romantic partner. IPV rarely starts with physical blows; it usually begins with subtle emotional degradation, isolation from support networks, and financial control. Nurses must look past visible bruises to identify patterns of coercion and fear that dictate the victim's daily life.

Physical, Emotional, and Psychological Abuse

Abuse takes many forms that leave deep marks without always showing broken bones.

  • Physical Abuse: Involves any intentional use of physical force resulting in pain, injury, or impairment, ranging from slapping and burning to severe beating.
  • Emotional Abuse: Involves constant insults, belittling, public humiliation, and threats intended to destroy a person's self-esteem.
  • Psychological Abuse: Involves instilling terror, threatening loved ones or pets, and severe isolation from friends and family to make the victim completely dependent.

Sexual Violence and Exploitation

Sexual violence includes any sexual act, attempt to obtain a sexual act, or other act directed against a person's sexuality using coercion, regardless of their relationship to the victim. This category covers rape, attempted rape, forced marriage, child sexual abuse, and human trafficking for sexual exploitation. Survivors often experience profound trauma, fear of social stigma, and risks of sexually transmitted infections or unwanted pregnancies. Clinical response requires immense sensitivity, adherence to forensic evidence collection protocols, and trauma-informed care principles.

Harmful Traditional Practices and Reproductive Coercion

Certain forms of gender-based violence are deeply rooted in cultural customs and traditions. These include female genital mutilation (FGM), child or forced early marriage, and forced widowhood rites. Additionally, reproductive coercion involves behaviors intended to maintain power and control by controlling a partner's reproductive outcomes, such as sabotaging birth control or forcing pregnancy. Nurses practicing in regions where these traditions persist must approach care with cultural awareness while firmly advocating for patient autonomy and bodily integrity.

Socio-Cultural Vulnerability Factors in Nigeria

In Nigeria, gender-based violence is heavily influenced by patriarchal social structures, economic dependency, and cultural norms that normalize male dominance. Poverty often traps women and girls in abusive environments because they lack financial resources to leave. Furthermore, legal loopholes and weak enforcement of protective laws can leave survivors unprotected. Cultural beliefs that view domestic disputes as private family matters discourage outside intervention and silence victims who fear being ostracized by their communities.

Clinical Identification and Holistic Nursing Response

Nurses play a frontline role in detecting and supporting GBV survivors. Because victims rarely disclose abuse spontaneously, nurses must routinely screen patients using gentle, direct questioning in private settings. A holistic nursing response requires treating physical injuries, offering psychological first aid, preserving forensic evidence if sexual assault occurred, and connecting the patient to social workers and legal advocates. Above all, nurses must respect patient autonomy, ensuring the survivor retains control over decisions regarding their care and reporting.
