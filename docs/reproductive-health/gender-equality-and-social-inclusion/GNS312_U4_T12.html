Prevention of Gender Based Violence

Understanding Gender-Based Violence

Gender-based violence refers to harmful acts directed at an individual based on their biological sex or socially constructed gender identity. Rooted in systemic inequality, abuse of power, and harmful cultural norms, it disproportionately affects women and girls globally. In Nigerian clinical practice, healthcare providers frequently encounter survivors across emergency departments, antenatal clinics, and general outpatient wards. Recognizing these acts requires looking beyond physical injuries to understand the social structures that permit such mistreatment.

Forms and Manifestations of Abuse

Violence manifests in multiple overlapping ways within domestic and public spaces. Clinicians must recognize these distinct categories during history taking:

  • Physical violence: Infliction of bodily harm through hitting, slapping, burning, or weapon use.
  • Sexual violence: Non-consensual sexual contact, attempted or completed rape, and forced reproductive coercion.
  • Emotional and psychological abuse: Constant belittling, isolation from support networks, threats, and intimidation.
  • Economic abuse: Controlling access to financial resources, withholding money, or sabotaging employment opportunities.

Levels of Prevention

Public health frameworks divide intervention into three distinct tiers to stop violence before it starts and mitigate harm after occurrence:

  1. Primary prevention: Stopping violence before it occurs by shifting social norms, educating communities, and promoting gender equality.
  2. Secondary prevention: Immediate, targeted interventions for individuals at high risk or early detection through routine clinical screening.
  3. Tertiary prevention: Long-term rehabilitation, specialized medical treatment, psychological counseling, and legal support for established survivors to prevent recurrence and chronic disability.

Clinical Screening and Assessment

Routine screening in healthcare settings transforms emergency rooms and clinics into safe havens for disclosure. Because survivors rarely volunteer abuse histories spontaneously due to shame, fear, or stigma, nurses must use universal screening approaches. Ask direct, non-judgmental questions in a private setting away from accompanying family members or partners. Observe for behavioral cues such as overly dominant partners, evasiveness, delayed presentation of injuries, or somatic complaints lacking clear organic pathology.

Survivor-Centric Clinical Management

Care provision must revolve around the survivor's autonomy, safety, and emotional well-being. A survivor-centric model mandates that the nurse prioritizes informed consent before any examination or diagnostic test. Avoid victim-blaming language or intrusive questioning about why the assault happened. Document objective physical findings meticulously using body maps and verbatim quotes when appropriate, as these records may later serve as critical legal evidence.

Multi-Sectoral Referral Pathways

No single healthcare professional can manage the complex aftermath of gender-based violence alone. Effective intervention relies on a coordinated multi-sectoral network connecting medical facilities with legal aid, social welfare agencies, psychological services, and law enforcement. Nurses serve as critical linkage points, guiding survivors through referral networks while ensuring confidentiality and avoiding revictimization across different institutional touchpoints.
