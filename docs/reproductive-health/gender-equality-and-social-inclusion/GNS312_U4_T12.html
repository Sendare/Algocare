Prevention of Gender Based Violence

Understanding Gender-Based Violence and Its Root Causes

Gender-based violence (GBV) refers to harmful acts directed at an individual based on their biological sex or gender identity. It is rooted in structural inequalities, unequal power relations between men and women, and deeply entrenched social norms. In many communities, harmful traditional practices and patriarchal beliefs normalize control, dominance, and aggression. Nurses must recognize that GBV is not merely a private family dispute or a random act of crime, but a major public health emergency and a serious violation of human rights. Social determinants such as poverty, lack of educational opportunities, economic dependence, and community acceptance of violence further trap survivors in unsafe environments. Addressing these root causes requires community education, economic empowerment, and active challenging of gender stereotypes.

Forms and Manifestations of Abuse

Gender-based violence takes multiple interconnected forms, stretching far beyond physical assaults. Nurses should be familiar with these categories to accurately identify distress during clinical encounters:

  • Physical violence: Acts resulting in bodily harm, pain, or impairment, such as hitting, kicking, burning, or acid attacks.
  • Sexual violence: Any non-consensual sexual contact, attempted sexual contact, or acts directed against a person's sexuality using coercion or force.
  • Emotional and psychological abuse: Constant insults, belittling, threats of abandonment, intimidation, and isolation from friends and family.
  • Economic violence: Controlling access to financial resources, preventing a partner from obtaining employment, or withholding money for basic needs.
  • Harmful traditional practices: Actions deeply rooted in custom, such as child marriage, female genital mutilation, and forced widowhood rituals.

Health Consequences and Clinical Presentation

Survivors of GBV frequently present to healthcare facilities with vague, unexplained symptoms rather than disclosing abuse immediately. Physical injuries can range from acute bruises, fractures, and lacerations to chronic pain syndromes, gastrointestinal disorders, and traumatic brain injuries. Sexual violence carries severe reproductive health consequences, including unintended pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) transmission, and obstetric fistulas. Psychologically, survivors suffer from high rates of depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), substance abuse, and suicidal ideation. Healthcare workers must look beyond the immediate physical complaint and maintain a high index of suspicion when treating patients with recurrent, unexplained injuries or chronic distress.

Prevention Levels: Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary

Effective reduction of GBV relies on a public health prevention framework spanning three distinct levels:

  1. Primary prevention: Stopping violence before it starts by shifting social norms, promoting gender equality, educating youth about respectful relationships, and dismantling patriarchal attitudes.
  2. Secondary prevention: Early identification and rapid response for individuals experiencing abuse. This involves routine clinical screening, providing emergency contraception, offering post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) for HIV, and treating acute injuries.
  3. Tertiary prevention: Long-term rehabilitation and support to minimize the lasting impact of trauma. This includes ongoing psychological counseling, legal advocacy, safe housing support, and reintegration programs.

The Role of Nurses in Screening and Identification

Nurses occupy a frontline position for identifying hidden abuse because they spend the most direct time with patients. Routine screening should be integrated into general nursing assessments, especially in emergency departments, antenatal clinics, and mental health units. When screening, nurses must ensure absolute privacy, conducting interviews away from family members, friends, or suspected partners. Asking direct, compassionate, and non-judgmental questions creates a safe space for disclosure. A common clinical mistake is asking leading questions in front of an accompanying partner, which immediately silences the survivor due to fear of retaliation. Documenting findings objectively using direct quotes and body maps is critical for both medical continuity and potential legal proceedings.

Survivor-Centric Clinical Management

Providing care to a survivor requires a strict survivor-centric approach, which prioritizes the patient's rights, needs, wishes, and safety above all else. Healthcare providers must practice active listening without displaying shock, blame, or moral judgment. Informed consent is mandatory before performing any physical examination, diagnostic test, or evidence collection. Nurses must never pressure a patient to report to the police if the patient feels unready or unsafe to do so, as forcing legal action can strip away their remaining sense of control. Empathy, validation of their feelings, and reassuring them that the abuse was never their fault form the bedrock of emotional healing.

Legal Frameworks and Multi-Sectoral Referral Pathways

No single profession can tackle gender-based violence alone; recovery and justice require a strong multi-sectoral approach. Nurses work within a network that includes medical services, legal aid organizations, law enforcement, social welfare agencies, and safe shelters. Understanding local laws protecting victims of domestic violence, assault, and child abuse enables nurses to guide patients accurately. Effective referral pathways ensure that once a nurse identifies abuse, the patient is smoothly connected to psychological support, safe accommodation, and legal counsel without having to retell their traumatic story repeatedly to every new service provider.
