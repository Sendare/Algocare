Prevention of Gender Based Violence

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence (GBV)

Gender-Based Violence (GBV) is a critical public health and human rights issue globally, with profound implications in the Nigerian context. It encompasses any harmful act directed against an individual based on their gender, rooted in gender-based power inequalities, social norms, and unequal power dynamics. GBV disproportionately affects women and girls, though men and boys can also be victims. For nursing students, understanding GBV is essential because nurses are frequently the first point of contact for survivors within the healthcare system. Recognizing the systemic nature of GBV allows healthcare professionals to look beyond physical injuries and address the underlying social vulnerabilities of patients. GBV does not discriminate by age, social status, or education level, meaning nurses must maintain high vigilance across all clinical settings, from emergency departments to community health centers. Addressing GBV requires dismantling stigma, ensuring confidentiality, and viewing safety as an integral component of holistic patient care. By approaching GBV with clinical empathy and scientific rigor, nurses contribute significantly to reducing morbidity and mortality associated with interpersonal violence. Education on this topic empowers nursing professionals to act as advocates, educators, and compassionate caregivers for vulnerable populations.

Root Causes and Social Determinants of GBV

The persistence of Gender-Based Violence is driven by complex root causes and social determinants that vary across different cultural and regional settings in Nigeria. Patriarchal structures, traditional gender roles, and deeply ingrained cultural beliefs often normalize dominance and control over women. Social determinants such as poverty, economic dependency, lack of formal education, and inadequate legal enforcement further exacerbate vulnerability to abuse. When individuals lack financial autonomy, escaping an abusive environment becomes exceedingly difficult, trapping them in cycles of violence. Furthermore, community tolerance of harmful practices, victim-blaming attitudes, and institutional corruption create barriers to reporting and seeking justice. Understanding these determinants helps nursing students recognize that GBV is not an isolated private matter, but a structural issue requiring multifaceted interventions. Nurses must be aware of how cultural contexts influence a survivor's willingness to disclose abuse or seek help. By contextualizing a patient's experience within these social determinants, nurses can provide more empathetic, non-judgmental care and connect patients with appropriate community-level support systems that address both health and socioeconomic vulnerabilities.

Forms and Manifestations of Gender-Based Violence

Gender-Based Violence manifests in multiple interrelated forms, each causing severe physical, psychological, and social harm. Physical violence includes hitting, slapping, burning, and mutilation, which often brings survivors to emergency wards. Sexual violence encompasses rape, attempted rape, forced marriage, and sexual harassment, carrying heavy stigmas and risks of unwanted pregnancies and sexually transmitted infections, including HIV. Emotional and psychological violence involves verbal abuse, constant humiliation, isolation, and threats, which erode the victim's self-esteem and mental stability. Economic violence restricts a person's access to financial resources, employment opportunities, or basic survival needs, ensuring total dependency on the abuser. Additionally, harmful traditional practices such as female genital mutilation (FGM), child marriage, and widowhood rites represent institutionalized forms of GBV prevalent in various communities. Nurses must be trained to identify the subtle and overt indicators of these diverse manifestations. Recognizing that abuse is rarely limited to a single form helps clinicians perform thorough assessments and document injuries accurately for both medical and legal purposes.

Health and Psychosocial Impact on Survivors

The impact of Gender-Based Violence on survivors extends far beyond immediate physical injuries, resulting in long-lasting health and psychosocial consequences. Physically, survivors may suffer from chronic pain, traumatic brain injuries, unwanted pregnancies, unsafe abortions, gynecological disorders, and sexually transmitted infections. Psychosocially, the trauma frequently manifests as depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), substance abuse, and suicidal ideation. Stigma and societal victim-blaming often compound these psychological burdens, leading to social isolation and a reluctance to seek professional help. For pregnant women, GBV poses severe risks, including miscarriage, premature labor, low birth weight, and maternal mortality. Nurses caring for these patients must recognize that psychological wounds often take longer to heal than physical ones and require sustained, compassionate mental health support. Understanding these diverse impacts enables nursing professionals to anticipate complications, conduct comprehensive head-to-toe assessments, and provide holistic care that addresses both the body and the mind of the survivor.

Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Prevention Strategies

Preventing Gender-Based Violence requires a comprehensive public health approach categorized into primary, secondary, and tertiary prevention strategies. Primary prevention aims to stop GBV before it occurs by challenging harmful gender norms, educating communities, promoting gender equality, and empowering women and girls. Secondary prevention focuses on early identification and prompt intervention for individuals who have recently experienced abuse, utilizing routine screening and emergency medical care to mitigate immediate harm. Tertiary prevention involves managing the long-term consequences of GBV, providing rehabilitation, psychological counseling, legal support, and reintegration programs for survivors to prevent re-victimization and chronic disability. Nurses play active roles across all three levels, from conducting community health education campaigns (primary) and screening patients in emergency rooms (secondary) to supporting long-term rehabilitation and support groups (tertiary). Implementing these strategies in an integrated manner ensures that communities move from merely treating victims to actively dismantling the root causes of violence.

The Role of Nurses in GBV Screening and Assessment

Nurses occupy a critical position in healthcare settings for identifying and assessing victims of Gender-Based Violence through routine screening. Because survivors may not voluntarily disclose abuse due to fear, shame, or threats, nurses must integrate sensitive, confidential screening questions into standard intake procedures. Effective screening requires creating a private, safe, and non-judgmental environment where the patient feels secure and understood. Nurses should ask direct, open-ended questions and observe for non-verbal cues, unexplained injuries, frequent emergency visits, or somatic complaints with no clear organic cause. Proper clinical assessment also involves documenting findings meticulously, including direct quotes from the patient and detailed descriptions of physical trauma, which may be needed for legal documentation. However, nursing assessment must always prioritize the safety and autonomy of the survivor, ensuring that screening does not inadvertently place the patient at greater risk of retaliation from the abuser.

Survivor-Centric Care and Clinical Management

Survivor-centric care is the gold standard in clinical management for individuals who have experienced Gender-Based Violence. This approach places the rights, needs, and wishes of the survivor at the center of all clinical decisions, emphasizing dignity, confidentiality, autonomy, and non-discrimination. Clinicians must practice trauma-informed care, recognizing how past trauma affects a patient's behavior and health, and avoiding re-traumatization during examinations. Clinical management includes comprehensive physical examinations, treatment of injuries, emergency contraception, prophylaxis against sexually transmitted infections (STIs) and HIV, and psychological first aid. Crucially, the nurse must obtain informed consent before any procedure or examination, allowing the survivor to maintain control over their own body. Nurses should never judge, blame, or pressure survivors into making decisions they are not ready for, such as reporting to the law enforcement authorities. Providing a compassionate, listening ear combined with expert clinical skill helps restore a sense of agency and promotes emotional and physical healing.

Legal Frameworks and Multi-Sectoral Referral Pathways

Addressing Gender-Based Violence effectively requires robust legal frameworks and seamless multi-sectoral referral pathways. In Nigeria, legal instruments such as the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act provide critical legal protections and criminalize various forms of abuse. However, laws alone are insufficient without operational referral pathways that connect health facilities with social welfare agencies, legal aid organizations, police services, and shelter providers. Nurses must understand their local referral network to ensure that survivors receive comprehensive support beyond medical treatment. A multi-sectoral approach ensures that once a nurse stabilizes a patient medically, the survivor can be safely connected to legal counsel for protection orders, social workers for safe housing, and psychological experts for counseling. Effective coordination among these sectors prevents survivors from having to retell their traumatic stories repeatedly to different agencies. Nursing students must learn how to navigate these referral networks to advocate successfully for holistic justice and recovery for every survivor in their care.
