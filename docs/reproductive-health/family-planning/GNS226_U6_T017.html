Diaphragm

Anatomical Overview and Definition

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped, thin, skeletal muscle that separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity, acting as the primary muscle of respiration. In the context of reproductive health, however, the term refers to a barrier method of contraception. A contraceptive diaphragm is a shallow, dome-shaped cup made of silicone or latex with a flexible, circular spring rim. It is designed to fit snugly inside the vaginal canal, covering the cervix to prevent sperm from entering the upper reproductive tract. Unlike the respiratory muscle, this barrier device requires proper sizing by a trained clinician and must always be used alongside a chemical spermicide to ensure high contraceptive efficacy. Understanding this distinction is vital for nursing documentation and client communication in family planning clinics across Nigeria.

Mechanism of Action and Contraceptive Efficacy

The contraceptive diaphragm operates through a dual mechanism involving both mechanical obstruction and chemical destruction. Mechanically, the dome stretches across the vaginal vault, creating a physical wall directly in front of the external cervical os. Chemically, the spermicide applied to the inner dome before insertion immobilizes spermatozoa on contact, drastically reducing their ability to swim through the cervical canal into the uterus.

In clinical practice, the effectiveness of the diaphragm depends heavily on consistent and correct use. Typical use failure rates are higher than perfect use rates due to factors such as incorrect placement, failure to use spermicide with every act of intercourse, or early removal following ejaculation.

Types, Rim Designs, and Sizing

  • Coil spring diaphragm: Features a soft, bending spring suitable for women with good pelvic muscle tone and no cystocele.
  • Arching spring diaphragm: Bends in a straight line, creating an arc that makes it easier to insert, especially for women with a retroverted uterus or mild cystocele.
  • Pulsator or flat spring diaphragm: Contains a thin, delicate spring that offers less structural tension, ideal for sensitive vaginal tissues.
  • Arcing-style single-size devices: Newer silicone options designed to fit a wide range of women without requiring an internal pelvic examination for sizing.

Client Selection, Indications, and Contraindications

Selecting an appropriate candidate for a diaphragm requires a thorough reproductive health history and pelvic examination.

Indications

  • Women seeking a non-hormonal, reversible method of birth control.
  • Individuals who prefer coitus-dependent contraception rather than daily pills.
  • Lactating mothers who wish to avoid hormonal contraceptives that might affect breast milk supply.

Contraindications

  • Active urinary tract infections or frequent recurrent cystitis.
  • Known allergy to latex, silicone, or nonoxynol-9 spermicides.
  • History of toxic shock syndrome.
  • Severe pelvic organ prolapse, cystocele, or rectocele that prevents proper rim retention.

Nursing Assessment and Fitting Procedure

During the initial clinical visit, the nurse performs a bimanual pelvic examination to assess the size, shape, and position of the uterus, as well as the tone of the pelvic floor muscles. Sizing rings or a fitting kit containing graduated diaphragm sizes are used to measure the internal distance from the posterior fornix to the inferior surface of the pubic symphysis. The correct size is the largest diaphragm that comfortably fits into the vaginal vault without causing pressure, bulging, or displacement during straining. The nurse must allow the client to practice inserting and removing the demonstration device in the clinic under direct supervision to ensure confidence and competence before discharge.

Client Education on Insertion, Removal, and Spermicide

Educating the client on correct day-to-day handling is critical for success. The client should wash her hands thoroughly before handling the device. Approximately one teaspoon of spermicidal cream or jelly is placed inside the dome and around the rim. The diaphragm can be inserted up to six hours before coitus. To insert, the client squeezes the opposite sides of the rim together and pushes the device downward and backward into the vaginal canal until it rests securely behind the pubic bone, covering the cervix.

The device must remain in place for at least six hours following the last act of intercourse to ensure all sperm are neutralized. However, it should not be left in place for a total duration exceeding twenty-four hours to minimize infection risks. To remove, the client hooks her index finger under the rim and pulls downward and outward.

Care, Maintenance, and Lifespan

Proper maintenance ensures the longevity and structural integrity of the barrier device. After removal, the diaphragm should be washed with mild soap and warm water, rinsed thoroughly, and allowed to air dry completely before storage. Petroleum-based lubricants, baby oil, or body lotions must never be applied to latex diaphragms, as oil breaks down the rubber material and causes microscopic tears. Once dry, the device should be stored in its plastic container in a cool, dry place away from direct sunlight. The nurse must instruct the client to inspect the diaphragm regularly by holding it up to the light and stretching the rubber to check for pinholes, cracks, or thinning spots. A latex diaphragm typically lasts for one to two years, whereas silicone versions can last up to several years with careful handling.

Complications and Re-fitting Indications

While generally safe, diaphragm use can sometimes lead to localized complications. The most serious, though rare, risk is toxic shock syndrome, associated with prolonged retention of the device during menses or beyond recommended time limits. Frequent urinary tract infections can also occur due to mechanical pressure on the bladder neck.

Furthermore, the diaphragm size is not permanent. The nurse must inform the client that a re-fitting is necessary following significant weight fluctuation of more than five kilograms, after a full-term vaginal delivery, or following any pelvic or uterine surgery.
