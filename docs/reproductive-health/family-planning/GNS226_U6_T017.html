Diaphragm

Anatomy and Overview of the Diaphragm

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped, thin silicone or latex cup with a flexible rim designed to fit snugly over the cervix. As a barrier method of contraception, its primary job is to physically block sperm from entering the upper reproductive tract and reaching an ovum. Unlike male or female condoms, the diaphragm requires a prescription and must be used alongside a chemical spermicide to ensure reliable protection against pregnancy. It sits deep inside the vaginal canal, resting between the posterior fornix and the pubic bone, completely covering the external os of the cervix.

Types and Sizing Considerations

  • Arcing spring: Features a hinge that bends in a specific direction, making it easier to insert for women with poor pelvic muscle tone or a retroverted uterus.
  • Coil spring: Has a round, flexible rim without a hinge, suitable for women with firm pelvic floor muscles.
  • Flat spring: Similar to the coil spring but features a thinner, flatter rim.
  • Single-size devices: Newer silicone diaphragms are manufactured in one universal size designed to fit most women, eliminating traditional fitting procedures.

Clinical Fitting and Initial Assessment

For traditional diaphragms, a healthcare provider must perform a bimanual pelvic examination to determine the correct size. The clinician uses their fingers to measure the distance from the posterior fornix to the inferior surface of the pubic bone, then selects the matching rim diameter, usually ranging from 55mm to 95mm.

A common clinical mistake is prescribing a diaphragm that is too small, which can easily dislodge during intercourse, or too large, causing pressure, discomfort, or urinary retention due to urethral compression. Following weight fluctuations of 10 pounds or more, childbirth, or pelvic surgery, the client must be refitted to ensure the device remains effective.

Client Instruction on Insertion and Removal

Proper technique ensures the diaphragm stays in place during sexual activity.

  1. Preparation: Wash hands with soap and water, then inspect the clean diaphragm against a light source for holes, cracks, or thin spots.
  2. Spermicide application: Apply about one tablespoon of spermicidal cream or jelly inside the dome and along the rim.
  3. Insertion: Compress the opposite sides of the rim together and push it deep into the vagina, sliding it downward and backward along the posterior wall until it tucks securely behind the pubic bone.
  4. Verification: Feel with a fingertip to confirm the cervix is covered and the dome is in place.
  5. Removal: Hook a finger over the front rim, pull downward and outward, then wash the device with mild soap and warm water.

Timing Guidelines and Spermicide Rules

Timing is critical for barrier contraceptive success. The diaphragm can be inserted up to six hours before sexual intercourse. If more than six hours pass before intercourse occurs, or if intercourse is repeated, fresh spermicide must be applied into the vagina without removing the device.

Crucially, the diaphragm must remain in place for at least six hours after the last act of sexual intercourse to allow enough time for all sperm cells to be neutralized by the spermicide. However, the total wear time should never exceed 24 hours total to prevent bacterial overgrowth and associated health risks.

Care, Maintenance, and Lifespan

Taking good care of a diaphragm extends its usable life and prevents infections. After removal, wash the device using warm water and mild, unscented soap, then pat it completely dry with a clean towel. Allow it to air-dry thoroughly before storing it in its protective plastic case away from direct sunlight and extreme heat.

Avoid using petroleum jelly, baby oil, or other oil-based lubricants with latex diaphragms, as oil rapidly degrades latex rubber, causing it to weaken and tear. Silicone diaphragms are compatible with oil-based products, but checking manufacturer recommendations is always best. A well-maintained latex diaphragm typically lasts for one to two years, while silicone versions can last up to several years.

Advantages, Limitations, and Complications

The diaphragm offers hormone-free birth control that women control independently without altering their systemic endocrine balance. It can be inserted ahead of time and does not interrupt sexual spontaneity as much as barrier methods applied right before intercourse.

On the downside, it offers no protection against sexually transmitted infections and requires regular motivation and comfort touching one's own genitals. Potential complications include urinary tract infections due to pressure from the rim on the urethra, and rarely, Toxic Shock Syndrome (TSS) if the device is left in the vagina past the recommended 24-hour limit.
