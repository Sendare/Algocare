Intrauterine Device (IUD)

Introduction and Overview of Intrauterine Devices

An intrauterine device (IUD) is a small, T-shaped contraceptive frame inserted into the uterine cavity to provide highly effective, reversible long-acting contraception. Worldwide, it stands as one of the most widely utilized birth control methods due to its reliability and low maintenance. Users appreciate that once inserted, the device requires no daily action, offering continuous protection for years depending on the specific model. Nursing professionals play a pivotal role in demystifying this contraceptive option, addressing common myths about infertility, and guiding clients through informed reproductive choices in busy clinical environments.

Mechanisms of Action of Copper and Hormonal IUDs

Although both device categories occupy the same anatomical space, copper IUDs and hormonal IUDs function through distinct physiological pathways.

  • Copper IUD (Non-hormonal): Copper ions released locally create a sterile inflammatory reaction inside the endometrium. This environment is toxic to both sperm and ova, impairing sperm motility and preventing fertilization.
  • Hormonal IUD: Progestin (levonorgestrel) is continuously eluted directly into the uterine cavity. This thickens cervical mucus to block sperm passage, thins the endometrium to prevent implantation, and occasionally suppresses ovulation.

Types of IUDs Available in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Public and private reproductive health facilities across Nigeria stock varying configurations of contraceptive devices depending on procurement policies and donor support.

  • Copper T 380A: The most ubiquitous non-hormonal model provided in government primary health centers and family planning clinics, offering efficacy for up to twelve years.
  • Levonorgestrel-Releasing IUDs: Brands such as Mirena or Kyleena are increasingly available in private hospitals and specialized clinics, though cost can limit accessibility for lower-income clients.

Clinicians must verify the specific lifespan of the inserted device during counseling, as leaving a device past its approved duration increases failure rates.

Client Selection, Contraindications, and Clinical Evaluation

Thorough clinical screening using evidence-based medical eligibility criteria ensures safe contraceptive delivery. Nurses must take a detailed menstrual, obstetric, and sexual history.

  • Absolute Contraindications: Current pregnancy, unexplained abnormal uterine bleeding, active pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), current cervical or uterine malignancy, and distorted uterine cavity anatomy.
  • Screening for STIs: In settings with high prevalence, ruling out chlamydia and gonorrhoea prior to insertion is vital to prevent introducing pathogens into the upper reproductive tract.

Bimanual examination and speculum inspection are mandatory steps to assess uterine position, size, and cervical health before proceeding.

Nursing Procedures for IUD Insertion and Removal

Infection prevention and gentle technique are paramount during intrauterine procedures. The nurse assists the clinician or performs the insertion under strict aseptic conditions.

  1. Preparation: Perform bimanual exam, insert a speculum, and cleanse the cervix with an antiseptic solution like chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine.
  2. Sounding: Gently pass a uterine sound through the cervical os to measure cavity depth and direction.
  3. Loading and Insertion: Load the device into its sterile introducer tube, advance it to the measured depth, and release the arms inside the fundus.
  4. Trimming: Cut the polyethylene threads, leaving approximately 3 centimeters visible in the vaginal vault.

Removal is generally simpler: apply steady, gentle traction on the exposed threads with ring forceps. Never yank forcefully if resistance is encountered.

Client Education, Management of Side Effects, and Follow-Up

Clients often experience mild cramping and irregular spotting immediately following insertion. Reassuring the client that these symptoms typically subside within a few weeks promotes adherence. Copper IUD users frequently notice heavier or more painful menses, whereas hormonal users often experience lighter periods or amenorrhea.

Teach clients how to check their own device threads by inserting a clean finger into the upper vagina to feel for the strings, particularly after menstruation. Schedule the first routine follow-up visit after the first menses or between four to six weeks post-insertion to confirm correct placement and address lingering concerns.

Recognition and Management of IUD Complications

Although rare, potential complications require prompt clinical identification by nursing personnel.

  • Expulsion: The device slips partially or completely out of the uterus, usually within the first few months. Clients may notice missing strings or hard plastic poking through the cervix.
  • Perforation: The device pushes through the myometrium into the peritoneal cavity during insertion. Severe pelvic pain or missing strings warrants an immediate ultrasound.
  • Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID): Highest risk occurs within the first twenty days post-insertion if bacteria were introduced during the procedure. Presenting signs include fever, purulent discharge, and deep dyspareunia.
