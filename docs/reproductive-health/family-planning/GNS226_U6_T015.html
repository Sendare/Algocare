Intrauterine Device (IUD)

Introduction and Overview of Intrauterine Devices

An intrauterine device (IUD) is a small, T-shaped contraceptive device made of plastic or a combination of plastic and metal that a trained healthcare provider places inside the uterine cavity. It is classified as a long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) method, meaning it offers continuous protection against pregnancy for several years yet allows for immediate return to fertility once removed. IUDs are among the most effective family planning methods available globally and in Nigerian healthcare facilities because they require very little daily action from the user after the initial insertion procedure.

Mechanisms of Action: Copper vs. Hormonal IUDs

Although both copper and hormonal IUDs sit inside the same organ, they prevent pregnancy through different biological pathways:

  • Copper IUD (e.g., ParaGard): Copper ions are toxic to sperm. They create a sterile inflammatory reaction inside the endometrium that impairs sperm motility, damages sperm cells, and prevents fertilization.
  • Hormonal IUD (e.g., Mirena, Kyleena): These devices release small amounts of the progestin levonorgestrel directly into the uterus. This thickens the cervical mucus to block sperm entry, thins the uterine lining to prevent implantation, and sometimes suppresses ovulation.

Types of IUDs Available in Nigerian Clinical Practice

In Nigerian primary and secondary health care settings, the choice of IUD usually depends on procurement, cost, and client preference. The two major categories are non-hormonal copper-bearing devices and progestin-releasing systems.

  1. Copper T 380A: The most widely distributed IUD in public health clinics and donor-supported programs. It is highly effective and approved for up to 10 to 12 years of continuous use.
  2. Levonorgestrel-Releasing IUDs: Brands like Mirena are increasingly available in private hospitals and specialized clinics. They are often chosen by women who suffer from heavy menstrual bleeding and painful periods, as the hormone drastically reduces menstrual blood loss over time.

Client Selection, Contraindications, and Clinical Evaluation

Thorough clinical screening before IUD insertion protects the client from pelvic infections and ensures safe use. The nurse must review the client's medical, reproductive, and sexual history, followed by a bimanual exam and speculum examination to check uterine size, position, and rule out current infections.

  • Absolute Contraindications: Pregnancy, active pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), unexplained vaginal bleeding, current cervical or uterine cancer, and severe distortion of the uterine cavity.
  • Precautions: History of ectopic pregnancy, current sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and severe dysmenorrhea (for copper IUDs).

Nursing Procedures for Insertion and Removal

The insertion procedure requires strict aseptic technique to prevent introducing bacteria into the sterile uterine cavity. The nurse assists the physician, midwife, or nurse practitioner by preparing equipment, comforting the client, and monitoring vital signs.

  • Step 1: Perform a bimanual exam and insert a speculum to visualize the cervix.
  • Step 2: Clean the cervix with an antiseptic solution and gently grasp the anterior lip with a tenaculum to straighten the uterocervical angle.
  • Step 3: Use a uterine sound to measure the depth of the uterine cavity.
  • Step 4: Load the IUD into its inserter tube, pass it into the fundus, deploy the arms, and withdraw the tube.
  • Step 5: Trim the nylon threads to about 2 to 3 centimeters protruding from the external os.

Removal is generally simpler: the nurse gently pulls on the visible strings with ring forceps using steady, continuous traction.

Client Education, Follow-Up, and Side Effects Management

Effective counseling reduces discontinuation rates caused by preventable anxiety. Clients must be taught how to feel for their IUD strings vaginally after each menstrual period without pulling on them. Common side effects include mild cramping and spotting in the first few weeks. Copper IUD users often experience heavier or more painful periods, whereas hormonal users usually experience lighter periods or absence of menstruation. Nurses should reassure clients that these changes are generally harmless unless accompanied by severe pain, fever, or heavy bleeding.

Recognition and Management of IUD Complications

Although safe, IUDs carry potential complications that nurses must quickly recognize.

  • Expulsion: The IUD slips out of the uterus partially or completely, most commonly within the first three months.
  • Perforation: The device pushes through the uterine wall during insertion, requiring surgical removal.
  • Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID): The risk of infection is slightly elevated only during the first 20 days post-insertion due to potential introduction of bacteria from the lower genital tract.
  • Ectopic Pregnancy: If pregnancy occurs with an IUD in place, the chance that it is outside the uterus is higher, which is a medical emergency.
