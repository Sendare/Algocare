Implants

Introduction to Contraceptive Implants

Contraceptive implants are small, flexible rods or capsules placed subdermally, usually in the inner aspect of the upper non-dominant arm. As a form of long-acting reversible contraception, they offer continuous protection against pregnancy for periods ranging from three to five years depending on the specific product. Their high efficacy stems from user independence; once inserted, the client does not need to remember daily pills or barrier methods. This makes them a prominent choice within national family planning initiatives across primary healthcare centres in Nigeria.

Mechanism of Action and Pharmacology

Contraceptive implants release a progestin hormone continuously into the interstitial fluid, which is then absorbed into the systemic circulation. This steady pharmacological profile achieves pregnancy prevention through three distinct primary pathways:

  • Cervical mucus thickening: Creates an impenetrable barrier that prevents sperm penetration into the upper reproductive tract.
  • Endometrial suppression: Alters the lining of the uterus, making it hostile to blastocyst implantation.
  • Ovulation inhibition: Suppresses the mid-cycle surge of luteinizing hormone, stopping the release of an oocyte from the ovary in most cycles.

Client Selection and Medical Eligibility Criteria

Assessing a client for contraceptive implants requires referencing the World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use (WHO MEC). Most women are eligible, including breastfeeding mothers, adolescents, and women living with HIV.

Conditions categorized as WHO MEC Category 3 or 4—where the theoretical or proven risks outweigh the advantages—include current breast cancer, acute liver disease, unexplained vaginal bleeding prior to evaluation, and active thromboembolic disorders. A thorough medical history must rule out these contraindications before any procedure is scheduled.

Pre-Insertion Counseling and Assessment

Counseling bridges the gap between client expectations and clinical reality, focusing heavily on expected changes in menstrual bleeding patterns. Many discontinuation cases stem from surprise over unscheduled spotting or amenorrhea. The provider should explain the insertion procedure, confirm the absence of pregnancy, and ensure informed consent. Establishing good rapport alleviates anxiety and encourages the client to return if they experience problems or desire removal.

Insertion and Removal Techniques

Implant insertion and removal are minor surgical procedures requiring strict aseptic technique. The insertion site is on the inner arm, about 8 to 10 centimeters above the medial epicondyle of the humerus, avoiding major nerves and blood vessels. Local anesthetic creates a skin wheal, and the trocar or preloaded applicator places the rod subdermally just beneath the dermis. Deep placement into muscle must be avoided, as it complicates future localization and extraction. Removal requires a small incision near the tip of the rod, gentle pushing toward the incision, and extraction with sterile forceps.

Managing Side Effects and Common Client Concerns

Progestin-only implants alter normal endometrial tissue stability, resulting in diverse bleeding profiles ranging from infrequent spotting to complete amenorrhea. These changes are benign and non-pathological. Reassurance during follow-up visits resolves most anxieties. Mild local pain, bruising, or transient headaches can be managed with simple analgesics. If persistent heavy bleeding causes anemia or distress, short-term courses of ibuprofen or a brief cycle of oral estrogen may be prescribed after proper clinical review.

Post-Insertion Care and Follow-Up Protocols

After insertion, the arm wound is covered with a pressure bandage to minimize bruising and hematoma formation. The client is instructed to keep the area clean and dry for at least 48 hours. Routine return visits are not strictly mandatory unless problems arise, but clients are encouraged to return if they experience persistent pain, signs of infection such as pus or spreading redness, or if they wish to have the implant removed. Documentation in the client's health card must include the brand, date of insertion, expiry date, and lot number.
