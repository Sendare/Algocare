Implants

Introduction to Contraceptive Implants

Contraceptive implants are small, flexible rods or capsules containing synthetic progestin that a trained healthcare provider inserts subcutaneously, usually in the inner aspect of the upper arm. Once in place, the device slowly and continuously releases the hormone into the bloodstream, offering safe and highly effective long-acting reversible contraception (LARC). Unlike daily pills or barrier methods that demand constant user compliance, implants provide reliable protection for several years depending on the specific product. This set-and-forget nature makes them particularly attractive for women seeking an extended break between pregnancies or a definitive end to childbearing without surgical sterilization. In resource-constrained settings, expanded access to implants has significantly lowered the incidence of unintended pregnancies and closely spaced births, contributing to improved maternal and child health outcomes across communities.

Mechanism of Action and Pharmacology

The contraceptive efficacy of progestin-only implants stems from a three-pronged pharmacological action. First, the steady local and systemic release of progestin thickens the cervical mucus, creating a formidable barrier that impedes sperm transport and penetration into the upper reproductive tract. Second, the hormone suppresses the normal mid-cycle surge of luteinizing hormone (LH), which consistently prevents ovulation in the majority of users. Third, progestin induces thinning and atrophy of the endometrial lining, making the uterine environment completely hostile to blastocyst implantation should fertilization somehow occur. Because these devices rely exclusively on progestin, they lack the cardiovascular and thrombogenic risks associated with estrogen-containing contraceptives, making them suitable for a wide array of medically complex patients.

Types of Implants Available in Nigeria

  • Jadelle: A two-rod system composed of silicone elastomer, releasing levonorgestrel and offering effective contraceptive protection for up to five years.
  • Implanon NXT: A single, flexible ethylene vinyl acetate rod containing etonogestrel, pre-loaded in a sterile disposable applicator, approved for up to three years of use.
  • Norplant: An older six-rod levonorgestrel-releasing system that is largely phased out in modern clinical practice, though clients who had it inserted years ago may still present for removal.

Client Selection and Medical Eligibility Criteria

Assessing a client for an implant requires referencing the World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria (WHO MEC) for contraceptive use. Most women, including adolescents, nulliparous individuals, and breastfeeding mothers, fall into MEC Category 1 or 2, meaning there are no restrictions or the advantages generally outweigh the theoretical risks. Medical conditions that place a client into Category 3 or 4—such as current breast cancer, unexplained vaginal bleeding before a definitive diagnosis, severe active liver disease, or active venous thromboembolism—require alternative contraceptive methods. A common misconception is that clients with high blood pressure or diabetes cannot use implants; in reality, progestin-only implants are safely categorized as MEC 1 or 2 for most uncomplicated cases.

Pre-Insertion Counseling and Assessment

Thorough counseling before insertion establishes realistic expectations and promotes long-term satisfaction. Nurses must discuss expected changes in bleeding patterns—ranging from infrequent spotting to prolonged spotting or amenorrhea—as this is the leading reason for premature discontinuation. The provider should also review the procedure itself, the exact site of placement, potential minor bruising or scarring, and the exact lifespan of the chosen device. A focused medical history and bimanual pelvic examination, if indicated, rule out pregnancy and pelvic pathology. Obtaining informed consent is mandatory, ensuring the client understands that the implant can be removed safely at any point if they experience intolerable side effects or wish to conceive.

Insertion and Removal Techniques

Implant insertion and removal are minor surgical procedures requiring strict aseptic technique. The nurse or clinician positions the client comfortably, cleans the inner upper arm with antiseptic solution, and injects a local anesthetic such as 1% lidocaine intradermally. For single-rod systems like Implanon NXT, the applicator needle is inserted just under the dermis and advanced along the subdermal plane before sliding the obturator and withdrawing the rod. For two-rod systems like Jadelle, a small V-shaped incision allows each rod to be placed in a fan-like distribution. Removal requires locating the distal tip of the rod by palpation, administering local anesthesia, making a tiny incision near the end, and gently pushing the rod forward until it can be grasped with sterile mosquito forceps.

Managing Side Effects and Common Client Concerns

Addressing client concerns empathetically is vital for continuation rates. Aside from altered bleeding patterns, clients may report localized bruising, swelling, minor pain at the insertion site, weight gain, headaches, or mood swings. Mild analgesic medications and reassuring the client that bruising resolves within a week effectively manage local site reactions. For troublesome, prolonged, or heavy bleeding, short-term courses of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or a brief course of oral estrogen can stabilize the endometrium. Nurses must validate the client's experiences rather than dismissing them, offering timely clinical interventions or scheduling early follow-up visits when side effects become distressing.

Post-Insertion Care and Follow-Up Protocols

Proper post-insertion care prevents wound infection and ensures the client knows how to monitor the site. The insertion area must remain clean, dry, and covered with a pressure bandage for 24 hours to minimize bruising and swelling, followed by a smaller adhesive bandage for a few days. Clients should be instructed to check for the implant periodically by gently touching the arm without digging or manipulating the rods excessively. A routine follow-up visit is typically scheduled at 4 to 6 weeks to check wound healing, address any early side effects, and confirm client satisfaction. Clear instructions must be provided on when to return immediately, such as if an infection develops, if the rod pushes through the skin, or if pregnancy is suspected.
