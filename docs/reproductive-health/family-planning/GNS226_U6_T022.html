Counselling in Family Planning

Introduction to Family Planning Counselling

Family planning counselling is an interactive, empowering dialogue between a healthcare provider and a client or couple. It bridges the gap between available contraceptive technology and personal reproductive goals. Rather than pushing specific methods, effective sessions help individuals make voluntary, well-informed decisions regarding family size, spacing of pregnancies, and overall reproductive health. In busy Nigerian primary health centres or tertiary clinics, this counselling serves as the cornerstone for reducing maternal mortality, preventing unintended pregnancies, and promoting child survival.

Core Principles and Ethics in Reproductive Counselling

Reproductive healthcare delivery rests on strict ethical pillars that safeguard client dignity.

  • Autonomy: Clients retain the absolute right to make their own decisions without coercion, pressure, or judgment from the nurse.
  • Confidentiality: All personal and medical disclosures remain strictly private, encouraging open dialogue especially among adolescents or unmarried clients fearful of community stigma.
  • Informed Choice: Clients must receive unbiased, accurate details regarding the efficacy, risks, and proper use of every available method before selecting one.
  • Beneficence and Non-maleficence: Providers must act in the best interest of the client, ensuring the selected contraceptive method poses minimal health risks relative to potential benefits.

The GATHER Approach

The GATHER acronym provides a systematic, step-by-step framework widely utilized in reproductive health settings to structure counselling sessions effectively.

  1. G - Greet: Welcome the client respectfully, establish rapport, and ensure privacy.
  2. A - Ask: Inquire about reproductive goals, past contraceptive use, and medical history.
  3. T - Tell: Provide clear, balanced information regarding suitable contraceptive methods.
  4. H - Help: Assist the client in selecting the method that best fits their lifestyle and health profile.
  5. E - Explain: Detail how to use the chosen method, potential side effects, and warning signs.
  6. R - Return: Schedule follow-up visits and encourage the client to return whenever needed.

Communication Techniques and Active Listening

Successful counselling relies heavily on interpersonal communication skills. Nurses must balance verbal dialogue with non-verbal cues, such as maintaining an open posture, nodding, and avoiding crossed arms, which can signal disapproval or impatience. Active listening involves repeating or paraphrasing client statements to confirm understanding and validate emotions. Asking open-ended questions—such as "What have you heard about contraceptive injections?" instead of closed questions like "Do you want injections?"—encourages clients to express deep-seated fears, cultural beliefs, or partner pressures that might otherwise remain hidden.

Assessing Client Needs and Medical Eligibility

Before any contraceptive method is dispensed, the nurse must conduct a thorough health and lifestyle assessment. This includes taking a detailed obstetric, gynecological, and medical history. Globally recognized guidelines, such as the World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use (WHO MEC), categorize conditions into four distinct numerical classes to determine safety. For instance, a condition rated Category 1 poses no restriction for use, whereas Category 4 represents an unacceptable health risk. Nurses utilize these categories to screen for contraindications like severe hypertension, active liver disease, or thromboembolic disorders.

Presenting Contraceptive Options and Informed Choice

Presenting options requires absolute neutrality. Nurses must explain both barrier methods (male and female condoms), hormonal methods (oral pills, injectables, implants), intrauterine devices (IUDs), and permanent methods (tubal ligation, vasectomy). Information regarding each method should cover effectiveness, mechanism of action, duration of protection, and potential side effects. The nurse must avoid presenting only modern long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) while ignoring client preferences for short-term methods or traditional options. True informed choice means the client weighs the facts and makes the final selection independently.

Addressing Myths, Misconceptions, and Side Effects

Common myths pose significant barriers to contraceptive uptake in many communities. Misconceptions—such as believing injectables cause permanent infertility, IUDs migrate to the heart, or pills accumulate in the stomach—must be addressed with patience and empathy. The nurse should never dismiss client fears as ignorance. Instead, validate their concern, provide clear scientific facts using simple language, and discuss expected physiological side effects (like temporary amenorrhea with injectables or initial spotting with implants) so clients are not caught off guard and driven to discontinue use prematurely.

Special Considerations in Nigerian Clinical Settings

Providing reproductive counselling in Nigeria requires navigating diverse cultural, religious, and socio-economic dynamics. Spousal consent is frequently cited by clients as a major factor; while legal consent rests with the individual, involving supportive partners can improve compliance and marital harmony where culturally appropriate. Additionally, economic constraints mean clients may favor subsidized or free public sector supplies over costly private options. Language barriers also necessitate using local languages or trusted interpreters to ensure true comprehension, bridging the gap between clinical terminology and everyday understanding.
