Barrier Methods

Introduction and Mechanism of Action

Barrier methods of contraception work on a simple physical principle: keeping sperm and egg apart. By placing a physical obstruction inside the reproductive tract or over the genitalia, these devices block the ascent of spermatozoa into the upper female reproductive tract, preventing fertilization. Unlike hormonal methods that alter biochemistry or intrauterine devices that modify the uterine environment, barriers act purely mechanically. Most modern barriers are constructed from latex, polyurethane, or silicone. Some also incorporate chemical agents to immobilize sperm. Because they do not alter the systemic endocrine system, they have very few medical contraindications, making them an accessible option for individuals who cannot or choose not to use hormonal contraceptives.

Types of Mechanical Barriers: Condoms and Intravaginal Devices

Mechanical barriers are divided into external devices worn on the genitalia and internal devices placed inside the vagina.

  • Male Condoms: Sheaths rolled over the erect penis before any genital contact, trapping ejaculate and preventing direct contact.
  • Female Condoms: Loose-fitting polyurethane or nitrile pouches with flexible rings at both ends; the closed end sits over the cervix while the open ring remains outside the vagina.
  • Diaphragms and Cervical Caps: Shallow dome-shaped silicone cups filled with spermicide and fitted securely over the cervix to block the internal os.
  • Contraceptive Sponges: Polyurethane foam devices saturated with spermicide that simultaneously trap sperm, block the cervical os, and absorb seminal fluid.

Chemical Barriers and Spermicides

Spermicides are chemical agents designed to immobilize and destroy spermatozoa on contact. The most common active ingredient used worldwide is nonoxynol-9. Available as creams, gels, foams, films, and suppositories, chemical barriers are rarely used alone due to low typical-use efficacy. Instead, nurses instruct clients to use them as adjuncts with diaphragms, cervical caps, or condoms to boost protection.

A common clinical concern involves tissue irritation. Frequent application of nonoxynol-9 can cause micro-abrasions in the vaginal and rectal epithelium. Paradoxically, these mucosal disruptions can actually increase the risk of acquiring sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, by providing a portal of entry for pathogens.

Efficacy Rates: Perfect Use Versus Typical Use

Understanding the difference between perfect use and typical use is vital when counseling patients about barrier methods. Perfect use measures effectiveness when the device is used correctly and consistently with every single act of intercourse. Typical use reflects real-world human behavior—accounting for delayed application, slippage, breakage, or failure to use a barrier during every encounter.

Prevention of Sexually Transmitted Infections

A major clinical advantage of certain barrier methods is protection against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including HIV, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and trichomoniasis. Latex and polyurethane male and female condoms provide an effective impermeable cover that stops the exchange of infectious bodily fluids and genital skin lesions.

However, nurses must clarify that not all barriers offer equal protection. Diaphragms, cervical caps, and sponges cover only the cervix and leave the vaginal walls and external genitalia exposed. Therefore, these intravaginal devices offer little to no protection against infections like human papillomavirus (HPV) or herpes simplex virus (HSV), which transmit via skin-to-skin contact in unprotected areas.

Nursing Assessment, Selection, and Patient Education

Selecting the right barrier method requires a personalized nursing assessment. The nurse evaluates the client's medical history, dexterity, lifestyle, and preferences. For prescription devices like diaphragms and cervical caps, physical fitting by a trained clinician is required to ensure the correct size is chosen. If a client gains or loses significant weight, gives birth vaginally, or undergoes second-trimester abortion, the pelvic floor changes, necessitating a new fitting.

Patient education must be clear and practical. Nurses should demonstrate correct application using anatomical models, have the client return the demonstration, and emphasize critical safety points such as checking expiration dates, storing items away from excessive heat, and avoiding oil-based lubricants with latex products.
