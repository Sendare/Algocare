Oral Contraceptives

Mechanism of Action and Types of Oral Contraceptives

Oral contraceptives are among the most reliable methods of reversible birth control when taken correctly. Understanding how they work helps nurses address client concerns and encourage adherence.

  • Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs): Contain synthetic estrogen and progestin. Their primary action is suppressing ovulation by inhibiting the release of follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone from the pituitary gland. They also thicken cervical mucus to block sperm entry and alter the endometrium to prevent implantation.
  • Progestin-Only Pills (POPs): Often called the 'minipill', these contain only progestin. They primarily thicken cervical mucus and suppress ovulation in about half of cycles. They are ideal for clients who cannot take estrogen, such as breastfeeding mothers or those with a history of blood clots.

Non-Contraceptive Health Benefits

Beyond preventing pregnancy, oral contraceptives offer substantial medical benefits for many individuals. Hormonal control can regulate erratic menstrual cycles, significantly reduce menstrual blood loss, and alleviate dysmenorrhea or painful cramping. Clients with polycystic ovary syndrome or endometriosis frequently find relief from symptoms. Additionally, long-term use of combined oral contraceptives lowers the lifetime risk of developing ovarian and endometrial cancers.

Contraindications and Risk Factors

Safety screening is a vital nursing duty because certain medical conditions make hormonal contraception dangerous. The World Health Organization uses medical eligibility criteria to guide these choices.

  • Thromboembolism: History of deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism is an absolute contraindication for estrogen.
  • Cardiovascular Health: Uncontrolled hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and stroke history rule out combined pills.
  • Liver Disease: Severe cirrhosis or liver tumors prevent safe hormone metabolism.
  • Migraines: Migraine headaches with aura in clients over thirty-five years old prohibit estrogen use due to stroke risk.

Client Assessment and Screening

Before prescribing or dispensing oral contraceptives, nurses must perform a thorough baseline assessment. This starts with an accurate blood pressure measurement, as estrogen can elevate blood pressure. A detailed medical and family history screens for clotting disorders, liver disease, and undiagnosed abnormal uterine bleeding. Routine pelvic examinations or routine blood tests are generally not mandatory before starting pills, but checking pregnancy status is essential.

Initiation Protocols and Patient Counselling

Starting birth control at the right time ensures immediate protection and builds client confidence. The 'quick start' method involves taking the first pill on the day of the clinic visit, provided pregnancy is ruled out. Alternatively, the 'sunday start' or 'first day of menses' approaches are traditional. Nurses must counsel clients that backup contraception, such as condoms, is necessary for the first seven days if starting mid-cycle. Clear education about daily pill adherence at the same time each day is the cornerstone of preventing unintended pregnancy.

Managing Missed Doses and Side Effects

Life happens, and clients frequently forget pills. Management depends on how many pills are missed and the type of pill. For combined pills, missing one pill requires taking it immediately and continuing the pack, with no backup needed. Missing two or more combined pills requires active pill-taking plus backup contraception and emergency contraception consideration. Common minor side effects include breakthrough bleeding, breast tenderness, and mild nausea, which often resolve after the first three months of use.

Nursing Responsibilities and Warning Signs

Nurses play a vital monitoring role by teaching clients to recognize serious complications. Teaching the 'ACHES' acronym helps clients recall critical warning signs that require immediate medical evaluation: Abdominal pain indicating liver or gallbladder issues, Chest pain or shortness of breath suggesting a blood clot, severe Headaches pointing to stroke risk, Eye problems like blurred vision, and Severe leg pain signaling deep vein thrombosis. Regular follow-up visits allow nurses to assess ongoing tolerance and monitor blood pressure.
