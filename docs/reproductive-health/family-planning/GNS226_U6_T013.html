Injectable Contraceptives

Introduction and Overview of Injectable Contraceptives

Injectable contraceptives are highly effective, reversible hormonal methods administered via intramuscular or subcutaneous injection. They provide reliable pregnancy prevention without requiring daily adherence, making them a popular choice across diverse healthcare settings. In the Nigerian context, injectables are frequently accessed in primary healthcare clinics, maternal and child health centers, and family planning units due to their privacy and ease of use. These methods rely on progestin to suppress ovulation, alter cervical mucus to prevent sperm penetration, and thin the endometrial lining. Because they do not interfere with coitus and remain discreet, they help clients overcome cultural or domestic barriers often associated with other contraceptive modalities.

Mechanisms of Action

The contraceptive efficacy of progestin-only injectables stems from three distinct physiological pathways:

  • Suppression of Ovulation: Progestin exerts negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, effectively blocking the mid-cycle surge of luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH).
  • Cervical Mucus Alteration: The hormone induces thick, viscous cervical mucus, creating a formidable biochemical barrier that impedes sperm migration into the upper reproductive tract.
  • Endometrial Atrophy: Continuous progestin exposure transforms the endometrium, rendering it thin and unsuitable for blastocyst implantation should fertilization somehow occur.

Types of Injectable Contraceptives Available in Nigeria

Two primary formulations dominate public health and commercial sectors across Nigeria:

  1. Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA): Commonly known by the brand name Depo-Provera, administered as an intramuscular injection of 150 mg every 3 months (13 weeks).
  2. DMPA-SC (Subcutaneous): Marketed as Sayana Press, delivered in a pre-filled Uniject system at a lower dose of 104 mg subcutaneously every 3 months, which allows for potential self-injection training.
  3. Norethisterone Enanthate (NET-EN): Known as Noristerat, given as an intramuscular injection of 200 mg every 2 months (8 weeks).

Eligibility and Client Assessment (WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria)

Before administering an injectable, nurses utilize the World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria for Contraceptive Use (WHO MEC) to screen clients safely. Most women can use progestin-only injectables safely, categorized mostly as WHO MEC Category 1 (no restriction) or Category 2 (advantages generally outweigh risks). However, conditions such as active breast cancer, severe liver disease, unexplained vaginal bleeding requiring evaluation, and severe, poorly controlled hypertension place clients in higher risk categories. A thorough history covering cardiovascular risk, metabolic status, and reproductive goals ensures safe method selection without unnecessary clinical barriers.

Administration Techniques and Schedules

Proper technique ensures optimal drug absorption and minimizes local complications at the injection site. For intramuscular DMPA, shake the vial gently, aspirate into a syringe, and inject deeply into the ventrogluteal or deltoid muscle. Crucially, instruct the client not to massage the injection site afterward, as massage can accelerate systemic absorption and shorten the effective duration of the hormone. For subcutaneous formulations like Sayana Press, inject into the anterior thigh or abdomen using the pre-filled device. Always calculate and document the exact return date on the client's appointment card, emphasizing a grace period if applicable but stressing adherence to the target date.

Side Effects and Common Misconceptions

Progestin-only injectables induce predictable alterations in menstrual bleeding patterns, which frequently cause anxiety if clients are unprepared. Amenorrhea, irregular spotting, or prolonged light bleeding are normal physiological responses to endometrial thinning. Other reported effects include mild weight gain, mood fluctuations, and temporary headaches. In Nigerian clinical practice, widespread myths persist—such as beliefs that injectables cause permanent infertility, accumulate in the head, or lead to congenital malformations in future pregnancies. Proactive counseling dispels these misconceptions, reassuring clients that side effects are non-pathological and fertility returns after discontinuation.

Nursing Management and Client Counseling

Effective nursing management centers on tailored interpersonal communication and clinical vigilance. During counseling, explore the client's lifestyle, reproductive intentions, and tolerance for potential bleeding changes. Provide clear written and verbal instructions regarding the return appointment schedule. Address warning signs that necessitate prompt reporting, such as severe unilateral leg pain (suggesting thromboembolism, though rare) or signs of local allergic reactions. Creating a supportive, non-judgmental environment empowers clients to voice concerns, switch methods if dissatisfaction arises, and adhere successfully to their chosen contraceptive regimen.

Return to Fertility and Long-Term Considerations

Clients discontinuing injectables to achieve pregnancy should be informed about a potential delay in the return of ovulation and normal menstrual cycles. Unlike barrier methods or oral pills, progestin levels decline gradually as the depot clears from the body; ovulation may take an average of 4 to 10 months (and sometimes up to a year) to resume after the last DMPA injection. This delay is temporary and uniform regardless of how long the client used the method. For long-term users, nurses should periodically review bone mineral density considerations, reassuring them that any temporary reduction in bone density largely recovers after discontinuation.
