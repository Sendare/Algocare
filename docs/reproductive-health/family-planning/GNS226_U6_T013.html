Injectable Contraceptives

Introduction and Types of Injectable Contraceptives

Injectable contraceptives are hormonal methods given by intramuscular or subcutaneous injection to prevent pregnancy. They are highly effective, discreet, and reversible, making them a popular choice for many clients seeking family planning. In Nigeria, two primary products are widely available through public clinics, patent medicine vendors, and hospitals.

  • Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA): Given as an intramuscular injection every three months, or 13 weeks. A subcutaneous version, often known as Sayana Press, allows for self-injection or easier administration in community settings.
  • Nanthate (NET-EN): Given as an intramuscular injection every two months, or 8 weeks, providing a shorter duration of action.

Mechanisms of Action

Progestin-only injectables work through multiple synergistic pathways to prevent conception. Understanding these physiological actions helps nurses reassure clients who worry about how the drug works inside their bodies.

  1. Inhibition of ovulation: The steady release of progestin suppresses the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis, preventing the surge of luteinizing hormone necessary for an egg to be released from the ovary.
  2. Thickening of cervical mucus: Progestin alters the consistency of mucus at the cervix, creating a physical barrier that blocks sperm from entering the upper reproductive tract.
  3. Endometrial thinning: The lining of the uterus becomes thin and atrophic, making it unsuitable for the implantation of a fertilized ovum even if fertilization somehow occurs.

Client Eligibility and Medical Assessment

Before administering an injectable contraceptive, nurses must screen clients using the WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria (MEC) to ensure the method is safe. Most women can use progestin-only injectables safely, but certain medical conditions require careful evaluation.

  • Category 1: A condition for which there is no restriction for the use of the contraceptive method.
  • Category 2: A condition where the advantages of using the method generally outweigh the theoretical or proven risks.
  • Category 3: A condition where the theoretical or proven risks usually outweigh the advantages of using the method.
  • Category 4: A condition that represents an unacceptable health risk if the contraceptive method is used, such as current breast cancer or severe uncontrolled hypertension.

Administration Techniques and Schedules

Proper administration guarantees effectiveness and minimizes local tissue reactions. Intramuscular injections are usually given in the ventrogluteal or deltoid muscle, while subcutaneous forms go into the abdomen or thigh.

A common clinical mistake is massaging the injection site after giving DMPA. Nurses should instruct clients not to rub or massage the area, as vigorous rubbing can speed up the absorption rate of the hormone and shorten its duration of effectiveness in the body.

Timing is critical for continued protection. If a client returns late for their repeat injection, additional backup methods like condoms or emergency contraception may be required depending on how many weeks have passed past the due date.

Side Effects and Managing Misconceptions

Side effects are the leading reason clients discontinue injectable contraceptives. Preparing clients beforehand builds trust and improves continuation rates.

A frequent misconception in many Nigerian communities is that injectables cause permanent infertility. Nurses must clearly explain that temporary delays in the return of fertility are normal, but the drug does not cause permanent sterility.

Nursing Management and Client Counseling

Comprehensive counseling empowers clients to choose the right method and manage expectations successfully. A good counseling session covers how the method works, expected side effects, warning signs, and the exact return date for the next dose.

Nurses should maintain a non-judgmental attitude and address cultural myths openly. Documenting the exact injection date and batch number in the client's card and clinic register ensures continuity of care and prevents missed appointments. Always give the client a physical appointment card with the next due date clearly written.

Return to Fertility and Long-Term Considerations

Clients planning future pregnancies often ask how long it takes to conceive after stopping injectables. While contraceptives are reversible, delayed return to fertility is well-documented with DMPA. Ovulation may not resume for several months after the last injection wears off, with an average delay of four to ten months.

Long-term use can also lead to a temporary reduction in bone mineral density. However, studies show that bone density recovers fully after discontinuation. Nurses should encourage adequate dietary calcium and vitamin D intake for all clients, especially adolescents whose bones are still developing.
