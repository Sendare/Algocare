Reasons for Occurrence of Harm

Defining Harm and Adverse Events in Clinical Settings

Patient harm in healthcare refers to any unintended physical or psychological injury resulting from or contributed to by medical care, rather than the underlying disease process itself. In reproductive health and maternity care, this can range from minor drug side effects to severe surgical complications, birth trauma, or maternal morbidity.

Clinicians categorize these incidents to understand how and why they happened. An adverse event is harm caused by medical management rather than the patient's condition. While some adverse events are completely unavoidable due to the inherent risks of a procedure, many stem from clinical mistakes or broken processes. Understanding the difference between disease progression and care-induced injury is the first step toward building a safer healthcare environment.

Human Factors and Cognitive Errors

Even the most dedicated midwives and doctors make mistakes because human beings have natural cognitive limits. Human factors studies how humans interact with their working environment, tools, and each other. When fatigue, stress, and heavy workloads combine, our brains take shortcuts that lead to errors.

  • Confirmation bias: Looking only for evidence that supports your initial guess about a patient while ignoring clues that point to a different diagnosis.
  • Fixation error: Locking onto one plan or diagnosis and failing to adapt when the patient's condition clearly changes.
  • Alert fatigue: Ignoring critical monitor alarms because too many false or meaningless alarms go off during a shift.
  • Memory lapses: Forgetting a step in a routine procedure because of sudden interruptions or extreme tiredness.

Systemic and Organizational Failures

Blaming an individual nurse for a mistake often misses the deeper root cause. Most errors happen because of flawed systems, poor policies, or weak leadership within the health facility. In the Swiss Cheese Model of accident causation, a hospital has multiple layers of defense, such as checklists, double-checks, and standard protocols.

However, every layer has holes—representing system weaknesses. When these holes line up across different departments due to staff shortages, broken supply chains, or rushed shift handovers, an error slips through all defenses and reaches the patient. Organizational failures create the daily conditions that make personal mistakes almost inevitable.

Communication Breakdowns and Handoff Errors

Clear communication is a vital lifeline in healthcare. Many catastrophic events in labor wards and clinics start with a simple misunderstood instruction or missing piece of information during a shift change. When midwives hand over care to obstetricians without a structured format, critical details like rising blood pressure or decreasing urine output get lost.

Using standardized tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) helps prevent these gaps by giving team members a predictable way to share vital facts. Hierarchical barriers also silence junior nurses who notice danger signs but feel too intimidated to speak up against senior doctors.

Technological and Equipment-Related Causes

Modern healthcare relies heavily on machines, from infusion pumps delivering oxytocin to electronic health records and fetal monitors. When medical technology fails or is misused, it can cause direct patient harm. Common issues include poorly designed user interfaces that confuse nurses, confusing button layouts, and equipment maintenance backlogs.

Furthermore, relying blindly on technology without clinical judgment creates risks. If a nurse trusts a malfunctioning monitor instead of assessing the patient physically, signs of fetal distress can be missed entirely. Regular biomedical checks and thorough staff training on new devices are necessary to prevent technology from becoming a hazard.

Identifying and Reporting Near Misses

A near miss is an unplanned event that did not reach the patient because of timely intervention or pure luck—for example, a nurse catches a wrong dose of magnesium sulfate just before it enters the IV line. Near misses are powerful learning opportunities because they expose system weaknesses before actual harm occurs.

Unfortunately, many healthcare facilities suffer from a culture of blame where staff hide near misses out of fear of punishment. To fix this, hospitals must build a just culture that encourages open reporting. A just culture separates honest human mistakes from reckless behavior, ensuring staff feel safe speaking up about vulnerabilities in their units.

Strategies for Prevention and Mitigation

Preventing harm requires a proactive approach that targets both human behavior and system design. Facilities reduce risks by standardizing clinical protocols, conducting regular safety briefings, and implementing safety checklists for high-risk procedures like cesarean sections or blood transfusions.

Mitigation also involves transparent communication with patients and families when things go wrong. Offering an honest explanation and a sincere apology preserves trust and supports emotional healing. Ultimately, patient safety is not achieved by expecting perfection from healthcare workers, but by designing systems that make it easy to do the right thing and hard to make a mistake.
