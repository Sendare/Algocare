Reasons for Occurrence of Harm

Defining Harm and Adverse Events in Reproductive Healthcare

Clinical harm in maternal and reproductive healthcare encompasses physical, psychological, or emotional injury resulting from medical management rather than the underlying disease process. An adverse event is an unintended injury caused by healthcare intervention that leads to prolonged hospital stay, disability, or death at the time of discharge. These events are distinct from unavoidable complications, such as the inherent risks of a ruptured uterus in an unmanaged obstructed labor where timely intervention was impossible.

In Nigerian obstetric wards, frequent adverse events include severe postpartum hemorrhage, iatrogenic bladder injuries during emergency cesarean sections, and birth asphyxia resulting from delayed fetal monitoring. Recognizing the boundary between preventable harm and unavoidable pathology forms the foundation of clinical accountability and quality improvement.

Human Factors, Fatigue, and Cognitive Errors

Healthcare delivery relies heavily on human cognition, which is inherently vulnerable to error. Human factors engineering examines the relationship between human operators, the tools they use, and the working environment. In under-resourced maternity units across West Africa, extreme staff shortages often lead to chronic nurse fatigue, cognitive overload, and tunnel vision.

  • Confirmation bias: Interpreting ambiguous fetal heart monitor readings as reassuring because the delivery room is already overwhelmed.
  • Slips and lapses: Executing an intended action incorrectly due to distraction, such as drawing the wrong concentration of oxytocin.
  • Fixation error: Persisting with a failing resuscitation strategy despite clear clinical indicators to escalate care.

Systemic and Organizational Failures

While individual mistakes capture immediate attention, most catastrophic outcomes in reproductive health originate deep within organizational structures. The Swiss Cheese Model illustrates that harm occurs when multiple systemic defenses fail simultaneously, allowing a hazard to reach the patient.

Organizational latent conditions include chronic stockouts of essential commodities like blood products and magnesium sulfate, absent maintenance schedules for suction machines, and punitive institutional cultures that discourage reporting. When a postpartum mother hemorrhages to death because the blood bank lacks power and blood bags are un-screened, the root cause is systemic, not merely the failure of the attending midwife to stop the bleeding.

Communication Breakdowns and Handoff Errors

The transition of care between shifts or departments represents a high-risk window for patient harm. In maternity units, incomplete handovers frequently cause missed diagnoses and delayed interventions. Information is often passed verbally in chaotic environments without structured formatting.

Key drivers of handoff errors include interruptions, omission of critical clinical parameters (such as a rising pulse rate indicating early shock), and lack of shared mental models between doctors and midwives. Implementing standardized communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) provides a reliable framework to ensure vital reproductive health data is accurately transmitted.

Technological and Equipment-Related Causes

Modern reproductive healthcare relies heavily on medical devices, ranging from infusion pumps and vacuum extractors to electronic fetal monitors and radiant warmers. Equipment-related harm usually stems from device malfunction, lack of preventive maintenance, or user unfamiliarity.

In many Nigerian healthcare facilities, biomedical engineering support is scarce. Staff may use ultrasound machines or vacuum extractors with degraded batteries or uncalibrated pressure gauges. Furthermore, alarm fatigue poses a severe risk; when monitors beep incessantly due to loose sensor leads, nurses may silence alarms that signify true fetal distress.

Identifying and Reporting Near Misses

A near miss is an unplanned event that did not result in clinical harm, only by chance or timely intervention. For example, a nurse draws up magnesium sulfate for an eclamptic patient, notices a label discrepancy just before administration, and discards the dose. No patient harm occurred, but the pathway to potential injury was active.

Near misses offer invaluable safety intelligence because they highlight system vulnerabilities before a catastrophic adverse event occurs. However, if an institution maintains a punitive culture where reporting errors leads to harassment or suspension, staff will conceal near misses, depriving the facility of opportunities for preventative learning.

Strategies for Prevention and Mitigation

Mitigating patient harm requires shifting from blame-oriented cultures to proactive safety systems. A just culture balances individual accountability with recognition that human error is driven by flawed systems. It encourages transparent reporting while distinguishing between honest mistakes, at-risk behavior, and reckless disregard.

Practical mitigation strategies in reproductive health include implementing surgical safety checklists prior to cesarean sections, establishing rapid response obstetric teams, standardizing drug dosages, and ensuring regular equipment servicing. By designing processes that make it easy for nurses to do the right thing and difficult to make mistakes, facilities drastically improve maternal and neonatal survival rates.
