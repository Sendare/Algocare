Antepartum Haemorrhage

Definition and Clinical Significance of Antepartum Haemorrhage

Antepartum haemorrhage (APH) is defined as bleeding from or into the genital tract occurring from 24 weeks of pregnancy up until the birth of the baby. It is a critical obstetric emergency that threatens the survival of both the mother and the fetus. Even when the volume of blood loss appears small, it can quickly escalate into profound hypovolemic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and emergency fetal distress. Nursing staff must treat any bleeding in the second half of pregnancy with immediate urgency, as the maternal body can compensate initially before crashing rapidly.

Main Causes: Placenta Praevia and Abruptio Placentae

The two primary and most dangerous pathological causes of antepartum haemorrhage are placenta praevia and abruptio placentae. Together, they account for the vast majority of severe bleeding episodes. Other, less common causes include local lesions of the cervix or vagina, such as polyps, severe cervicitis, or carcinoma, as well as vasa praevia where fetal vessels run across the internal os.

Placenta Praevia: Classification and Clinical Presentation

Placenta praevia happens when the placenta implants in the lower uterine segment, near or covering the internal cervical os.

  • Grade I (Low-lying): The placenta reaches the lower segment but does not reach the os.
  • Grade II (Marginal): The placental edge dips down to the margin of the internal os.
  • Grade III (Partial): The placenta partially covers the internal os.
  • Grade IV (Complete): The placenta completely covers the internal os.

The hallmark clinical feature of placenta praevia is painless bright red vaginal bleeding, typically occurring after 28 weeks of gestation. The uterus is usually soft, relaxed, and non-tender. A crucial clinical rule: Never perform a digital vaginal examination if placenta praevia is suspected or unruled out, as poking a finger through the cervix can provoke catastrophic, unstoppable hemorrhage.

Abruptio Placentae: Types and Clinical Presentation

Abruptio placentae is the premature separation of a normally implanted placenta from the uterine wall before the delivery of the fetus.

  • Revealed abruption: Blood tracks down between the membranes and the uterine wall and escapes visibly through the vagina.
  • Concealed abruption: Blood is trapped behind the placenta, causing severe internal accumulation, high uterine tension, and disproportionate pain without much external bleeding.
  • Mixed abruption: A combination where some blood escapes and some remains trapped.

The classic presentation includes dark red vaginal bleeding accompanied by constant, severe abdominal or back pain. The uterus feels firm, tense, or 'woody' to the touch. Fetal distress or absence of fetal heart tones can occur quickly depending on the degree of separation.

Nursing Assessment and Initial Diagnostics

When a pregnant woman presents with bleeding, the nurse must rapidly evaluate her hemodynamic stability without wasting time on detailed history-taking if she is unstable.

  • Check maternal vital signs immediately to watch for falling blood pressure and rising pulse.
  • Assess the characteristics of the bleeding: color, amount, and presence of clots or pain.
  • Palpate the abdomen gently for tenderness and uterine tone, remembering never to do a vaginal exam.
  • Monitor fetal heart rate patterns continuously.
  • Assist with an urgent ultrasound to check placental location and wellbeing.
  • Collect blood samples for full blood count, blood grouping, and crossmatching.

Emergency Nursing Management and Resuscitation

Immediate resuscitation follows the standard ABCDE approach, prioritizing maternal stabilization alongside fetal monitoring. Large-bore intravenous lines must be established quickly to infuse crystalloids and prepare for urgent blood transfusion. Oxygen therapy is administered to maintain fetal oxygenation. If the hemorrhage is massive, or if the fetus is in distress, emergency caesarean section is usually the definitive treatment. Nurses must ensure continuous hemodynamic monitoring, strict fluid balance recording via an indwelling catheter, and psychological reassurance for the terrified mother and her family.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Antepartum haemorrhage carries high rates of morbidity and mortality for both mother and child.

  • Maternal complications: Hypovolemic shock, acute kidney injury from prolonged hypotension, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), emergency hysterectomy, and psychological trauma.
  • Fetal complications: Fetal hypoxia, intrauterine growth restriction, prematurity complications, and fetal death.

Prompt nursing recognition of early shock signs—such as tachycardia, tachypnea, and delayed capillary refill—can prevent these catastrophic outcomes.

Health Education and Preventive Care

Preventive education plays a vital role in reducing the incidence and severity of complications related to APH. Nurses must counsel pregnant women during antenatal visits on warning signs, instructing them to report any vaginal spotting or bleeding immediately. Women should be advised to avoid sexual intercourse in late pregnancy if low-lying placenta has been diagnosed on scan. Emphasizing regular antenatal attendance ensures early detection of risk factors like abnormal placental lie through routine ultrasound screening.
