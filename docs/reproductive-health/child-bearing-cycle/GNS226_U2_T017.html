Antepartum Haemorrhage

Definition and Overview of Antepartum Haemorrhage

Antepartum haemorrhage, commonly abbreviated as **APH**, is defined as bleeding from or into the genital tract occurring from the **20th week of gestation** up to the birth of the baby. In many developing healthcare settings, including Nigeria, it remains a leading contributor to severe maternal morbidity and perinatal mortality. Bleeding before twenty weeks is typically classified under early pregnancy bleeding, such as abortion or ectopic pregnancy, whereas APH specifically addresses the second and third trimesters. The total volume of blood loss can vary dramatically from spotting noticed on undergarments to massive, life-threatening haemorrhage that precipitates rapid cardiovascular collapse. It is primarily categorized into two major obstetric entities—**placenta praevia** and **abruptio placentae**—though vasa praevia and local genital tract lesions also account for a small proportion of cases.

Causes and Etiology of Antepartum Haemorrhage

Understanding the underlying pathology of APH requires identifying the anatomical source of bleeding. The causes are traditionally grouped into placental and non-placental origins. - **Placenta Praevia**: The placenta implants abnormally in the lower uterine segment, encroaching upon or completely covering the internal cervical os. - **Abruptio Placentae**: The premature separation of a normally implanted placenta from the uterine wall before delivery of the fetus. - **Vasa Praevia**: Fetal vessels run across or near the internal cervical os unprotected by placental tissue or Wharton's jelly, leaving them vulnerable to rupture when membranes rupture. - **Local Tract Lesions**: Cervical polyps, carcinoma of the cervix, severe cervicitis, or acute vulvovaginal varicosities that bleed independently of placental attachment sites.

Placenta Praevia: Classification and Clinical Features

Placenta praevia presents a classic clinical picture characterized by **painless, bright red vaginal bleeding** that often occurs unexpectedly during rest or sleep in the third trimester. The bleeding is painless because the lower uterine segment stretches and thaws without causing painful uterine contractions. Traditionally, placenta praevia is classified into four grades based on ultrasound findings and digital examination: 1. **Grade I (Low-lying)**: The placenta extends into the lower uterine segment but does not reach the internal os. 2. **Grade II (Marginal)**: The placental edge reaches the margin of the internal os but does not cover it. 3. **Grade III (Partial)**: The placenta partially covers the internal cervical os. 4. **Grade IV (Complete)**: The placenta completely covers the internal cervical os. **Exam Pitfall**: Never perform a digital vaginal examination in a patient with unexplained third-trimester bleeding until placenta praevia has been completely ruled out by ultrasound. A digital exam can disrupt the placenta and trigger catastrophic, unmanageable haemorrhage.

Abruptio Placentae: Types and Clinical Features

Unlike placenta praevia, abruptio placentae typically manifests with **painful vaginal bleeding** accompanied by uterine tenderness, hypertonus, and backache. The blood may escape externally through the vagina, termed revealed haemorrhage, or remain trapped behind the placenta, known as concealed haemorrhage. Often, a combination of both occurs. When blood infiltrates the myometrium, the organ becomes bruised and rigid, a clinical state historically referred to as a **Couvelaire uterus**. Risk factors strongly associated with placental abruption include chronic hypertension, pre-eclampsia, abdominal trauma, cocaine use, and rapid decompression of the uterus, such as following the rupture of membranes in polyhydramnios.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis in APH

Immediate nursing assessment in APH focuses on stabilizing the mother while concurrently evaluating fetal well-being. Nurses must rapidly assess maternal vital signs looking for early signs of hypovolemic shock, such as tachycardia, tachypnea, pallor, and hypotension. The color, consistency, and estimated volume of vaginal blood loss should be documented accurately. Abdominal palpation is performed with extreme caution: a soft, non-tender uterus points toward placenta praevia, whereas a firm, board-like, painful uterus suggests abruptio placentae. Fetal monitoring using cardiotocography helps detect signs of fetal distress secondary to hypoxia. Diagnostic confirmation relies heavily on bedside or departmental transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasonography, alongside baseline laboratory investigations including complete blood count, clotting profile, and blood typing with cross-matching.

Emergency Nursing Management and Resuscitation

Managing acute antepartum haemorrhage requires a swift, multi-disciplinary emergency response focused on restoring circulatory volume and ensuring fetal delivery if indicated. - **IV Access**: Immediately insert two large-bore peripheral intravenous cannula (16G or 18G) to facilitate aggressive fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion. - **Blood Products**: Send blood samples urgently for typing, cross-matching, and coagulation studies. Administer warmed crystalloids and cross-matched blood as ordered. - **Positioning**: Place the woman in the left lateral tilt position to relieve aortocaval compression by the gravid uterus and optimize uteroplacental perfusion. - **Oxygen Therapy**: Administer humidified oxygen via a face mask to maximize fetal oxygenation. - **Monitoring**: Insert an indwelling urinary catheter connected to a urometer to accurately monitor hourly urine output as an indicator of renal perfusion.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Antepartum haemorrhage carries significant risks for both mother and child. Maternal complications depend heavily on the volume of blood lost and the underlying cause. **Hypovolemic shock** is an immediate threat, which can progress to acute kidney injury from renal tubular necrosis. Massive blood loss and tissue factor release can also trigger **disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)**, particularly in severe abruptio placentae. Emergency surgical interventions like caesarean sections carry risks of surgical site infection, postpartum haemorrhage, and hysterectomy. For the fetus, complications stem from chronic or acute hypoxia, resulting in intrauterine growth restriction, prematurity, fetal distress, low birth weight, and perinatal demise.

Health Education and Preventive Care for Pregnant Women

Preventive strategies and targeted health education play a vital role in mitigating the impact of antepartum haemorrhage. During antenatal clinic visits, nurses must educate pregnant women to recognize warning signs such as any episode of vaginal bleeding, persistent abdominal pain, or sudden uterine tightening, and to report immediately to a health facility rather than waiting. Women with identified risk factors, such as previous uterine scars or low-lying placentas noted on mid-trimester scans, require closer supervision and avoidance of strenuous activities or sexual intercourse in late pregnancy. Furthermore, promoting adequate nutritional intake, iron and folic acid supplementation, and early booking for antenatal care helps optimize maternal baseline health to better tolerate unexpected blood loss.
