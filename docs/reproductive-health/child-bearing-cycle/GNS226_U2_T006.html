Foetal Circulation

Introduction and Purpose of Foetal Circulation

Inside the womb, a foetus cannot breathe air or eat food by mouth. Instead, the unborn baby relies entirely on the mother for oxygen and nutrients, and for getting rid of waste products. The foetal circulation system is specially designed to handle this unique environment. Because the lungs and liver are not yet fully working, nature creates temporary blood vessels and shortcuts called shunts. These shortcuts route oxygen-rich blood straight to the vital organs like the brain and heart, while bypassing the resting lungs and liver. Understanding this setup is the first step to seeing how a baby survives and grows before birth.

Key Anatomical Structures and Shunts

To direct blood properly through the tiny body, the foetus uses three major structures that usually close up after birth.

  • Ductus venosus: A shunt that connects the umbilical vein to the inferior vena cava, allowing oxygenated blood to bypass the liver's capillary beds.
  • Foramen ovale: An opening between the right atrium and the left atrium that lets oxygen-rich blood flow straight into the left side of the heart.
  • Ductus arteriosus: A blood vessel connecting the pulmonary artery directly to the descending aorta, pulling blood away from the fluid-filled lungs.

Pathway of Oxygenated Blood from the Placenta

Life-giving blood starts its journey in the placenta, where it picks up oxygen and food from the maternal blood supply. This fresh blood travels inside a single umbilical vein toward the foetus's abdomen. As it reaches the liver, a large portion of it flows through the ductus venosus to merge with the inferior vena cava, mixing slightly with used blood but staying rich in oxygen. This mixed blood enters the right side of the heart. Thanks to the foramen ovale, much of this bright red blood shoots straight across into the left atrium, moves to the left ventricle, and pumps out through the aorta to nourish the brain and upper body first.

Deoxygenated Blood Journey and Return to Placenta

After the upper body and organs use up the oxygen and nutrients, the blood becomes deoxygenated and full of waste. This used blood collects in the superior vena cava and flows down into the right atrium, then into the right ventricle. Instead of going entirely to the lungs, most of this dark blood enters the pulmonary artery and is diverted through the ductus arteriosus straight into the descending aorta. From the aorta, the blood travels down through the body to supply the lower organs. Finally, two umbilical arteries branch off from the internal iliac arteries, carrying the waste-filled blood back to the placenta to be cleaned by the mother's body.

Physiological Adaptations in the Foetal Lungs and Liver

The liver and lungs do not perform their adult jobs before birth, but they still receive just enough blood to grow and develop. The liver receives a small amount of blood directly from the umbilical vein to support its rapid cell division and blood-forming duties, while the ductus venosus handles the rest. The lungs are filled with fluid and have very high resistance in their blood vessels, which naturally pushes blood away via the ductus arteriosus. This design prevents the immature lung tissue from being overloaded with high-pressure blood while still allowing structural growth.

Circulatory Changes at Birth and Cord Clamping

The moment a baby is born and takes its first breath, the entire circulatory system undergoes a dramatic makeover. When the umbilical cord is clamped, the low-resistance placenta is suddenly disconnected, causing the baby's systemic blood pressure to rise sharply. At the same time, air fills the lungs, causing the fluid inside to clear out and the blood vessels in the lungs to relax and open wide. This sudden drop in lung pressure draws a massive rush of blood from the heart into the pulmonary arteries. These immediate physical changes set the stage for the temporary shunts to close permanently.

Transition from Foetal to Neonatal Circulation

Once breathing begins and blood flow shifts, the temporary foetal structures quickly shut down and turn into permanent connective tissue ligaments.

  • Foramen ovale: Claps shut because pressure in the left atrium becomes higher than in the right atrium, eventually sealing completely.
  • Ductus arteriosus: Constricts and closes due to higher oxygen levels and a drop in local hormone-like substances, turning into the ligamentum arteriosum.
  • Ductus venosus: Collapses and becomes the ligamentum venosum within the liver.
  • Umbilical vessels: The umbilical vein becomes the ligamentum teres (round ligament of the liver), and the umbilical arteries become the medial umbilical ligaments.

Common Clinical Implications and Congenital Anomalies

Sometimes, the wonderful transition from foetal to neonatal circulation does not go smoothly. If the ductus arteriosus fails to close after birth, it remains open as a condition called patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), allowing oxygenated blood to leak from the aorta back into the pulmonary artery. Another common issue is an open foramen ovale that fails to seal properly, known as an atrial septal defect. Nurses must monitor newborn vital signs closely, watching for breathing trouble, abnormal heart murmurs, or bluish skin color, which can signal that the normal circulatory changes are delayed or incomplete.
