Foetal Circulation

Introduction and Purpose of Foetal Circulation

Within the safe environment of the womb, the developing foetus cannot breathe air or ingest nutrients independently. The foetal circulatory system solves this physiological challenge by relying entirely on the maternal-foetal interface. The placenta functions as the temporary lung, gut, and kidney for the growing child. Oxygen and vital nutrients diffuse from maternal blood into the foetal circulation, while metabolic waste products and carbon dioxide travel in the reverse direction.

Because the foetal lungs are collapsed, fluid-filled, and possess high vascular resistance, they do not participate in gas exchange. Instead, the cardiovascular system is uniquely wired to bypass non-functioning organs and prioritize high-oxygen blood delivery to the most critical control centers, specifically the brain and the myocardium. Understanding these embryonic pathways is essential for managing neonatal transition at delivery.

Key Anatomical Structures and Shunts

To redirect blood efficiently, the foetal circulatory plan incorporates three distinct vascular structures that typically close shortly after birth:

  • Ductus Venosus: A specialized vessel that connects the umbilical vein directly to the inferior vena cava, allowing a large portion of oxygenated blood to bypass the hepatic sinusoidal network.
  • Foramen Ovale: An anatomical flap-like opening situated in the interatrial septum, permitting blood to flow freely from the right atrium straight into the left atrium.
  • Ductus Arteriosus: A muscular blood vessel linking the pulmonary trunk directly to the descending aorta, diverting blood away from the unexpanded pulmonary bed.

Pathway of Oxygenated Blood from the Placenta

Oxygen-rich blood leaves the placenta via a single, large umbilical vein, carrying an oxygen saturation of roughly 80 percent. As this blood approaches the foetal liver, a substantial fraction of it enters the ductus venosus, bypassing the hepatic microcirculation to merge directly with the inferior vena cava. Here, it mixes with deoxygenated blood returning from the lower limbs and lower trunk.

Upon entering the right atrium, the incoming stream from the inferior vena cava is preferentially directed by the crista dividens straight through the foramen ovale into the left atrium. From the left atrium, the blood flows into the left ventricle and out through the ascending aorta. This specific routing ensures that the coronary arteries and carotid vessels, supplying the heart and brain, receive the highest possible oxygen concentration available in foetal life.

Deoxygenated Blood Journey and Return to Placenta

Blood returning from the head and upper extremities enters the superior vena cava and empties into the right atrium. Unlike the streaming pattern of the inferior vena cava, this deoxygenated blood crosses the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle and enters the pulmonary trunk. Because pulmonary resistance is extraordinarily high, only a tiny fraction nourishes the lung tissue itself; the vast majority crosses the ductus arteriosus into the descending aorta.

After supplying the lower body organs and peripheral tissues, this blood—now depleted of oxygen and laden with waste—enters the two umbilical arteries. These vessels originate from the internal iliac arteries and transport the deoxygenated blood back to the placenta for reoxygenation and waste disposal.

Circulatory Changes at Birth and Cord Clamping

The moment an infant is born and takes its first breath, a dramatic hemodynamic transformation unfolds. Clamping the umbilical cord abruptly removes the low-resistance placental vascular bed, causing a sharp spike in systemic vascular resistance. Simultaneously, lung expansion introduces oxygen, a potent vasodilator, which causes pulmonary vascular resistance to plummet.

As pulmonary blood flow surges, pressure in the left atrium rises above that of the right atrium. This pressure gradient physically slams the flap of the foramen ovale shut, isolating the systemic and pulmonary circulations. Within hours to days, increased oxygen tension and local chemical changes trigger the muscular constriction and permanent anatomical closure of the ductus arteriosus and ductus venosus.

Common Clinical Implications and Congenital Anomalies

Failures in normal circulatory transition can lead to significant neonatal pathology. If the ductus arteriosus fails to close after birth, it results in patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), a condition frequently seen in preterm infants. The resulting left-to-right shunt causes excessive pulmonary blood flow and potential heart failure.

Conversely, certain congenital heart defects rely on keeping these foetal structures open to sustain life. For instance, in critical cyanotic heart lesions like transposition of the great arteries, clinicians administer prostaglandin infusions precisely to prevent the ductus arteriosus from closing before surgical intervention can be performed.
