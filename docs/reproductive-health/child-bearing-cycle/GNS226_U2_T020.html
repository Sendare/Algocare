Anaemia in Pregnancy

Introduction and Definition of Anaemia in Pregnancy

Anaemia during gestation remains a major global public health challenge, with a particularly high burden in sub-Saharan Africa. According to the World Health Organization, a pregnant woman is diagnosed with anaemia when her hemoglobin concentration falls below 11.0 g/dL at sea level. During the first and third trimesters, the threshold drops slightly to 10.5 g/dL due to the maximal expansion of plasma volume. In resource-constrained settings like Nigeria, malaria, nutritional deficiencies, and late booking for antenatal care compound this baseline vulnerability. Failing to recognize and treat low hemoglobin levels early directly compromises oxygen delivery to both maternal tissues and the developing fetoplacental unit.

Physiological Haemodilution and Normal Haematological Changes

Pregnancy induces profound cardiovascular and hematological adaptations designed to meet the metabolic demands of the fetus and protect the mother against acute blood loss at delivery. Plasma volume increases dramatically by about 40 to 50 percent, whereas red blood cell mass increases by only 20 to 30 percent. This disproportionate expansion results in physiological haemodilution, often termed the anaemia of pregnancy. Because plasma increases more than red blood cells, laboratory values show a apparent drop in hemoglobin and hematocrit concentrations, even though total iron-carrying capacity remains adequate. Recognizing this as a normal physiological variant prevents unnecessary and aggressive interventions for mild dilutional drops.

Classification and Etiology of Anaemia in Pregnancy

Nutritional deficiencies represent the leading cause of gestational anaemia, with iron deficiency accounting for the vast majority of cases globally. Folate and vitamin B12 deficiencies also contribute, particularly where diets lack green leafy vegetables and animal protein. Beyond nutrition, infectious diseases like malaria and hookworm infestation heavily drive the etiology in tropical regions, causing hemolysis and chronic blood loss. Genetic hemoglobinopathies, such as sickle cell disease and thalassemia traits, further complicate the clinical picture in endemic populations.

  • Iron Deficiency Anaemia: Caused by depleted iron stores due to fetal demand and inadequate dietary intake; typically microcytic and hypochromic.
  • Megaloblastic Anaemia: Results from folic acid or vitamin B12 deficiency, impairing DNA synthesis and producing large, immature red blood cells.
  • Hemolytic Anaemia: Accelerated destruction of red blood cells, frequently triggered by acute malaria infection or autoimmune conditions.
  • Inherited Haemoglobinopathies: Conditions like sickle cell anaemia that worsen during pregnancy due to increased metabolic stress and vaso-occlusive crises.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment in Pregnant Women

The clinical presentation of anaemia depends on its severity and the speed of onset. Mild cases often remain asymptomatic, discovered only during routine antenatal laboratory screening. As hemoglobin levels drop further, compensatory mechanisms fail, leading to generalized fatigue, pallor of the conjunctiva and nail beds, and exertional dyspnoea. Tachycardia, palpitations, and dizziness frequently occur as the cardiovascular system works harder to maintain tissue oxygenation. In severe cases, patients may present with angular stomatitis, koilonychia, and signs of high-output cardiac failure. Comprehensive nursing assessment involves checking vital signs, inspecting mucous membranes, and screening for dietary habits, pica, and gastrointestinal symptoms.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Findings

Confirming the diagnosis and identifying the specific etiology of gestational anaemia requires targeted laboratory testing. The initial screening tool is the hemogram, which measures hemoglobin concentration and hematocrit. Red blood cell indices, including mean corpuscular volume, help differentiate between microcytic, normocytic, and macrocytic anemias. Serum ferritin serves as the gold standard for assessing iron stores, though levels can be confounded by concurrent systemic inflammation or infection. Additional tests may include peripheral blood smears, reticulocyte counts, and specific screening for malaria parasites or hemoglobin electrophoresis where indicated.

Maternal and Fetal Complications of Anaemia

Uncorrected maternal anaemia poses serious risks to both the mother and the developing fetus throughout gestation, labor, and the puerperium. Mothers face an increased susceptibility to infections, postpartum hemorrhage, and poor tolerance of blood loss during delivery, which elevates maternal mortality rates. Severe anaemia can also precipitate congestive heart failure. For the fetus and neonate, chronic hypoxia increases the likelihood of intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, preterm birth, and fetal distress. Neonates born to severely anemic mothers often enter life with depleted iron stores, predisposing them to early infancy developmental delays.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Management strategies focus on correcting hemoglobin deficits, replenishing body stores, and treating underlying root causes. Oral iron supplementation is the first-line therapy for mild to moderate iron deficiency anaemia, typically prescribed alongside folic acid. Intravenous iron preparations are reserved for patients with severe anaemia late in gestation, intolerance to oral tablets, or malabsorption syndromes. When malaria or helminthic infections contribute to the etiology, concurrent antimalarial treatment and deworming medications must be administered safely according to gestational age guidelines. In cases of severe, acute anaemia near term, blood transfusions may be necessary to stabilize the mother prior to delivery.

Nursing Care Plan and Health Education in Antenatal Care

Nurses play a pivotal role in antenatal clinics through systematic assessment, administration of prophylactic treatments, and targeted health education. Antenatal care visits provide an ideal platform to counsel women on incorporating iron-rich local foods, such as green leafy vegetables, beans, and lean meats, into their daily meals. Nurses must also educate clients on enhancing iron absorption by combining these foods with vitamin C sources while avoiding substances like tea or coffee that inhibit absorption. Furthermore, nurses should explain common side effects of iron supplements, such as dark stools and mild constipation, to ensure adherence and prevent clients from discontinuing their medication prematurely.
