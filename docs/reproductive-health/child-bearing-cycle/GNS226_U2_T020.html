Anaemia in Pregnancy

Introduction and Definition of Anaemia in Pregnancy

Anaemia in pregnancy is a major public health concern, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa. It occurs when the total volume of red blood cells is insufficient to meet the body's increased physiological demands during gestation. According to the World Health Organization, a pregnant woman is diagnosed with anaemia when her hemoglobin concentration falls below 11.0 g/dL. In the first and third trimesters, the threshold drops to 11.0 g/dL, while the second trimester allows for a lower cut-off of 10.5 g/dL due to marked fluid expansion.

Failing to manage low hemoglobin levels early can lead to severe complications for both mother and child during childbirth. Nurses play a frontline role in screening expectant mothers during routine antenatal clinic visits to catch these drops before they progress to severe states.

Physiological Haemodilution and Normal Haematological Changes

During pregnancy, a woman's body undergoes dramatic adaptations to support the growing fetus. The most prominent hematological change is plasma volume expansion, which increases by nearly 50%, whereas red blood cell mass only increases by about 20 to 30%. This mismatch creates a state known as physiological haemodilution or pseudoanaemia. Because the fluid portion of the blood increases much faster than the cells, blood tests show a drop in hemoglobin and hematocrit values.

It is vital to distinguish this normal dilutional effect from true pathological nutritional deficiency. In true anaemia, the total red cell mass is genuinely depleted, whereas in physiological haemodilution, the red cells are simply spread out in a much larger pool of fluid.

Classification and Etiology of Anaemia in Pregnancy

Anaemia in expectant mothers stems from several distinct causes, though nutritional deficiencies remain the most widespread culprits.

  • Iron deficiency anaemia: The most common type, resulting from depleted iron stores needed to form hemoglobin for the mother and fetus.
  • Folate deficiency anaemia: Caused by inadequate intake of vitamin B9, which impairs rapid cell division during fetal development.
  • Megaloblastic anaemia: Often linked to severe vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, leading to oversized, immature red blood cells.
  • Hemolytic anaemias: Conditions like sickle cell disease where red blood cells break down prematurely, worsening during pregnancy due to physical stress.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment in Pregnant Women

The clinical presentation of anaemia depends on how fast the hemoglobin levels drop and how low they fall. Mild cases may present with subtle symptoms that women often brush off as normal pregnancy fatigue.

  • General signs: Generalized weakness, chronic fatigue, and lethargy.
  • Cardiovascular signs: Palpitations, tachycardia, and exertional dyspnea as the heart works harder to pump oxygen-poor blood.
  • Integumentary signs: Pallor of the conjunctiva, nail beds, and palm creases.
  • Advanced signs: Pica (craving non-food items like clay or ice), angular stomatitis, and brittle nails.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Findings

Accurate diagnosis requires laboratory tests beyond a simple physical check. The primary screening tool is the hemoglobin estimation test done during the first antenatal visit and repeated around 28 and 36 weeks. In addition to hemoglobin, a complete blood count evaluates red blood cell indices like mean corpuscular volume to classify the specific type of anaemia.

Serum ferritin is another crucial test; it measures stored iron in the body and can confirm iron depletion even before hemoglobin levels drop. Nurses must ensure blood samples are collected properly and explain the purpose of these tests to ease patient anxiety.

Maternal and Fetal Complications of Anaemia

Left untreated, anaemia poses serious risks to both mother and baby throughout pregnancy, labor, and the postpartum period.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Treating anaemia in pregnancy focuses on correcting the root deficiency and restoring normal hemoglobin levels before delivery. For iron deficiency, oral iron supplementation (such as ferrous sulfate or ferrous fumarate) is the standard first-line treatment. Because iron tablets often cause gastrointestinal upset like constipation or dark stools, nurses must counsel patients on how to manage these side effects rather than stopping the medication.

In cases of severe anaemia diagnosed late in pregnancy, or when oral iron is poorly tolerated, parenteral iron (intravenous infusion) or blood transfusion may be necessary. Folic acid supplements are routinely prescribed alongside iron to prevent neural tube defects and support red blood cell production.

Nursing Care Plan and Health Education in Antenatal Care

Comprehensive nursing care involves continuous monitoring, individualized care planning, and robust health education during every antenatal visit. Nurses must assess dietary habits and promote the intake of local, iron-rich foods such as dark green leafy vegetables, beans, and lean meats. Enhancing iron absorption by combining these foods with sources of vitamin C, like citrus fruits or tomatoes, should be explicitly taught.

Furthermore, nurses educate mothers on the importance of adhering to prescribed routine supplements and keeping all antenatal clinic appointments. Early identification of warning signs ensures timely interventions, safeguarding both maternal and neonatal outcomes.
