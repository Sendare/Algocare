High Risk Pregnancy

Introduction and Definition of High-Risk Pregnancy

A high-risk pregnancy is one in which the health or life of the mother, the fetus, or both is threatened by complications arising from pre-existing medical conditions, pregnancy-specific disorders, or environmental and demographic factors. In the context of nursing practice in Nigeria, recognizing these pregnancies early is vital for reducing the high maternal and neonatal mortality rates seen in many resource-limited settings. A high-risk status requires specialized, multidisciplinary care, involving obstetricians, midwives, pediatricians, and community health extension workers. Nursing students must understand that high-risk pregnancies can develop unexpectedly or be identified during the first antenatal care visit through comprehensive history taking and risk scoring. Identifying these women allows for timely referral from primary health care centers to tertiary facilities equipped with comprehensive emergency obstetric care. The definition extends beyond physical illness to include social vulnerabilities such as poverty, lack of access to transportation, and extremes of maternal age, all of which heavily impact pregnancy outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa. By mastering the principles of high-risk pregnancy identification, nursing students can play a frontline role in triaging patients, advocating for timely interventions, and promoting safe motherhood initiatives across Nigerian healthcare institutions.

Risk Factors and Categorization

Risk factors for a high-risk pregnancy are broadly categorized into pre-existing maternal conditions, obstetric history, and current pregnancy complications. Pre-existing medical conditions include chronic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, renal disease, and cardiac disorders like rheumatic heart disease, which place immense physiological stress on the cardiovascular system during gestation. Obstetric history factors involve previous occurrences of recurrent miscarriages, stillbirths, preterm deliveries, uterine rupture, or a history of cesarean sections. Categorization also encompasses demographic and lifestyle elements, such as maternal age under 18 or over 35 years, high parity, malnutrition, and substance use. In Nigeria, infectious diseases such as malaria, HIV/AIDS, and tuberculosis significantly elevate pregnancy risk and demand targeted screening during antenatal booking. Categorizing risk allows healthcare providers to stratify care levels, ensuring that low-risk women deliver in primary centers while high-risk patients are managed in tertiary institutions with neonatal intensive care units. Nursing students must learn to perform systematic risk assessments during every antenatal visit, as dynamic changes can cause a previously low-risk pregnancy to shift into a high-risk category instantly.

Common Medical and Obstetric Complications

Common medical and obstetric complications significantly threaten the wellbeing of both mother and fetus. Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, ranging from gestational hypertension to severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, pose major risks of cerebral hemorrhage, acute renal failure, and placental abruption. Gestational diabetes mellitus introduces risks of fetal macrosomia, birth trauma, shoulder dystocia, and neonatal hypoglycemia, requiring stringent blood glucose monitoring and dietary or insulin management. Obstetric complications also include antepartum hemorrhage from placenta previa or abruptio placentae, both of which can rapidly progress to hypovolemic shock. Furthermore, premature rupture of membranes (PROM) and preterm labor increase the susceptibility to ascending intrauterine infections like chorioamnionitis. In malaria-endemic regions like Nigeria, placental malaria leads to severe maternal anemia and intrauterine growth restriction. Nurses must maintain high clinical vigilance to detect subtle signs of these complications, such as sudden weight gain, visual disturbances, epigastric pain, or abnormal vaginal bleeding, enabling prompt medical intervention and improved maternal-fetal salvage rates.

Maternal and Fetal Assessment Techniques

Maternal and fetal assessment techniques are foundational for monitoring high-risk pregnancies and guiding clinical decision-making. Maternal assessment involves serial blood pressure measurements, urinalysis for proteinuria, blood glucose tracking, and regular laboratory investigations including complete blood counts, clotting profiles, and renal function tests. Fetal surveillance utilizes both non-invasive and invasive diagnostic tools to evaluate fetal well-being in utero. The non-stress test (NST) evaluates fetal heart rate accelerations in response to fetal movement, indicating an intact, healthy central nervous system. Biophysical profile (BPP) scoring combines non-stress test results with real-time ultrasonography to evaluate fetal breathing movements, body movements, muscle tone, and amniotic fluid volume. Ultrasonography is also indispensable for assessing gestational age, localizing the placenta, diagnosing structural anomalies, and monitoring serial fetal growth through estimated fetal weight percentiles and Doppler velocimetry of the umbilical artery. Nurses play a critical role in preparing patients for these procedures, explaining what to expect, interpreting baseline findings, and immediately reporting any non-reassuring fetal heart tracings or abnormal maternal laboratory values to the attending physician.

Nursing Care Plan and Clinical Management

A comprehensive nursing care plan for a high-risk pregnancy focuses on continuous monitoring, symptom management, prevention of complications, and promotion of optimal maternal-fetal outcomes. Nursing diagnoses frequently revolve around deficient fluid volume, risk for injury, ineffective tissue perfusion, and anxiety. Clinical management requires strict adherence to institutional protocols, such as administering antihypertensive medications (e.g., methyldopa or labetalol) for chronic or gestational hypertension, and magnesium sulfate infusions for seizure prophylaxis in pre-eclampsia. Nurses must closely monitor clients receiving magnesium sulfate for signs of toxicity, which include loss of deep tendon reflexes, oliguria, and respiratory depression, always keeping calcium gluconate readily available as an antidote. For women with preterm labor risk, tocolytic therapy and corticosteroid administration for fetal lung maturity are coordinated by the nursing team. Additionally, strict intake and output charting, maternal vital sign checks every four hours or more frequently, and repositioning the patient to the left lateral position to enhance uteroplacental blood flow are essential nursing interventions. Collaborative teamwork with physicians, pharmacists, and laboratory scientists ensures that changes in the patient's clinical trajectory are met with rapid, evidence-based adjustments in care.

Patient Education, Counseling, and Psychosocial Support

Patient education, empathetic counseling, and robust psychosocial support are indispensable components of managing high-risk pregnancies. Women diagnosed with high-risk conditions often experience profound anxiety, fear of fetal loss, depression, and feelings of inadequacy. Nursing students must develop strong therapeutic communication skills to provide a safe space for patients to express their emotional burdens. Education must be tailored to the patient's literacy level and cultural context, explaining the disease process, the rationale for frequent hospital visits, dietary restrictions, and warning signs that require immediate emergency presentation. In Nigeria, involving partners and family members in counseling sessions is crucial, as spousal support heavily influences health-seeking behaviors and compliance with medical treatment plans. Nurses should also link patients with community support groups, medical social workers, and financial aid resources where available to mitigate the stress associated with prolonged hospital stays or expensive investigations. Empowering the patient through knowledge fosters self-care adherence, reduces psychological distress, and strengthens coping mechanisms throughout the antenatal and postpartum journey.

Emergency Interventions and Intrapartum Care

Emergency interventions and specialized intrapartum care are critical during the labor and delivery of high-risk obstetric clients. Intrapartum management requires continuous electronic fetal monitoring or intermittent auscultation according to strict clinical guidelines to detect early signs of fetal distress. Nurses must be fully prepared to handle sudden obstetric emergencies such as postpartum hemorrhage, shoulder dystocia, cord prolapse, and eclamptic seizures. In the event of cord prolapse, the nurse must immediately use sterile gloved fingers to elevate the presenting fetal part off the umbilical cord, place the mother in a knee-chest or exaggerated Sims position, and summon emergency obstetric and anesthetic teams for an urgent cesarean section. For shoulder dystocia, nurses must be skilled in executing maneuvers such as McRoberts maneuver and applying suprapubic pressure while avoiding fundal pressure. Hemorrhage management kits, uterotonic drugs (like oxytocin, misoprostol, and ergometrine), and blood transfusion protocols must be readily accessible in every labor ward. Effective intrapartum coordination, rapid decision-making, and precise execution of emergency drills by the nursing team can decisively avert maternal and neonatal mortality during childbirth complications.
