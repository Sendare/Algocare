Foetal Development

Introduction to Fertilization and Pre-Embryonic Stage

Human life begins at fertilization, the moment a secondary oocyte meets a spermatozoon, typically occurring in the ampulla of the uterine tube. Once the sperm penetrates the outer layers of the egg, a series of chemical changes blocks other sperm from entering, and the genetic material fuses to create a single-celled zygote.

During the first two weeks post-fertilization, known as the pre-embryonic stage, the zygote undergoes rapid mitotic cell division called cleavage as it travels down the uterine tube toward the uterus.

  • Morula: A solid ball of about 16 cells reached roughly three days after fertilization.
  • Blastocyst: The structure that forms by day five, featuring an inner cell mass that will become the embryo and an outer layer called the trophoblast that forms the placenta.
  • Implantation: The blastocyst burrows into the vascular lining of the uterus, the endometrium, around days six to ten, establishing hormonal communication with the mother via human chorionic gonadotropin.

Embryonic Period and Organogenesis

The embryonic period spans from the third week through the eighth week following fertilization. This phase is characterized by organogenesis, the vital process where the three primary germ layers—ectoderm, mesoderm, and endoderm—differentiate and give rise to all major organ systems.

Because major internal organs and external body structures are actively forming, the embryo is extremely sensitive to external damage during these weeks. The neural tube closes to form the brain and spinal cord, the primitive heart begins to beat, and limb buds emerge. Any severe disruption during this window can result in major structural birth defects rather than minor functional issues.

Trimester-by-Trimester Foetal Growth

From the ninth week until birth, the developing human is referred to as a foetus. Growth during this phase focuses on increasing in size, refining organ function, and gaining weight.

  • First Trimester (Weeks 1 to 13): Covers fertilization, implantation, and the entire embryonic period, concluding with recognizable human features and early movement.
  • Second Trimester (Weeks 14 to 27): A period of rapid growth where quickening occurs, skin is covered in vernix caseosa, and the lungs begin to produce surfactant.
  • Third Trimester (Weeks 28 to 40+): Focuses heavily on fat deposition, final maturation of the respiratory and central nervous systems, and positioning for birth.

Placenta, Amniotic Fluid, and Umbilical Cord

Support structures outside the foetus are essential for survival, nutrition, and protection inside the intrauterine environment.

StructurePrimary Clinical Function
PlacentaGas exchange, nutrient transport, waste elimination, and hormone production
Amniotic FluidCushions the foetus, allows free movement, maintains temperature, and aids lung development
Umbilical CordContains two arteries and one vein to connect foetal circulation to the placenta

The placenta acts as a dual-organ of exchange, keeping maternal and foetal blood supplies separate while allowing passive diffusion of oxygen and nutrients. Amniotic fluid is swallowed and inhaled by the foetus, reflecting normal kidney and gastrointestinal function.

Foetal Circulation and Cardiovascular Adaptations

Because the foetal lungs are collapsed and filled with fluid before birth, foetal circulation bypasses the pulmonary circuit entirely. Blood is oxygenated at the placenta rather than the lungs.

Three special shunts redirect blood flow in utero:

  1. Ductus Venosus: Shunts a portion of oxygenated blood from the umbilical vein straight into the inferior vena cava, bypassing the liver.
  2. Foramen Ovale: An opening between the right and left atria that allows oxygen-rich blood to flow directly into the left side of the heart.
  3. Ductus Arteriosus: A blood vessel connecting the pulmonary artery to the descending aorta, diverting blood away from the lungs.

At birth, the baby takes its first breath, pulmonary vascular resistance drops, and these shunts naturally close off.

Assessing Foetal Wellbeing

Clinical monitoring ensures the foetus is developing properly and tolerating the intrauterine environment. Routine checks include measuring fundal height from the pubic symphysis to assess gestational growth, listening for foetal heart tones via Doppler ultrasound, and monitoring foetal movements.

Advanced assessments include the biophysical profile (BPP), which combines ultrasound evaluation of foetal breathing, body movements, muscle tone, and amniotic fluid volume with a non-stress test. These tests help healthcare providers identify potential hypoxia or intrauterine growth restriction early.

Teratogens and Factors Affecting Development

A teratogen is any environmental agent that can cause physical or functional defects in the developing embryo or foetus. Common examples include certain medications, ionizing radiation, maternal infections like rubella or toxoplasmosis, and lifestyle choices such as alcohol consumption and cigarette smoking.

The impact of a teratogen depends on the gestational age at exposure, the dosage of the agent, and the genetic susceptibility of the foetus. Because organ systems form at different times, exposure during the early embryonic period often leads to structural malformations, while exposure later in pregnancy can affect growth and functional maturation.
