Foetal Development

Introduction to Foetal Development and Fertilization

Human development begins with fertilization, the biological process where a secondary oocyte and a spermatozoon fuse, typically occurring in the ampulla of the fallopian tube. Upon penetration of the oocyte membrane, the zona pellucida undergoes a structural alteration to prevent polyspermy, ensuring diploid chromosomal integrity. The resulting single-celled zygote embarks on a journey toward the uterine cavity, undergoing repeated mitotic divisions known as cleavage while maintaining the overall volume of the original cell. - **Zygote**: The initial diploid cell formed immediately following successful syngamy. - **Morula**: A solid ball of sixteen or more blastomeres reached approximately three days post-fertilization. - **Blastocyst**: The differentiated developmental stage comprising an inner cell mass and an outer trophoblast layer, ready for implantation. Implantation into the vascularized endometrium generally occurs around the sixth to tenth day after fertilization. Successful burrowing relies on the proteolytic activity of the syncytiotrophoblast, establishing the foundation for maternal-foetal exchange.

The embryonic period is characterized by rapid cellular differentiation and morphogenesis, rendering the developing structure highly vulnerable to environmental insults. Foetal Growth and Organogenesis (Trimester by Trimester)

Pregnancy is clinically divided into three trimesters, each marking distinct developmental milestones in physical size and physiological capability. The first trimester encompasses fertilization through week thirteen. During this window, foundational organogenesis takes place. By the end of this period, external genitalia begin to differentiate, though ultrasound determination remains challenging due to small size. The second trimester spans weeks fourteen through twenty-seven. This phase is defined by rapid linear growth and weight gain. Skeletal ossification advances visibly on imaging, and quickening—the maternal perception of active foetal movement—typically occurs between weeks eighteen and twenty-two. Surfactant production in the lungs initiates late in this trimester but remains insufficient for independent extrauterine respiration without clinical support. The third trimester runs from week twenty-eight until delivery at term. Subcutaneous fat accumulates rapidly, smoothing out wrinkled skin and rounding contours. Brain development accelerates dramatically, and the immune system acquires passive maternal IgG antibodies, preparing the neonate for life outside the protective intrauterine environment.

Structure and Function of the Placenta, Amniotic Fluid, and Umbilical Cord

The extraembryonic support structures are vital for sustaining intrauterine life, managing nutrition, gas exchange, waste elimination, and physical protection. The placenta functions as a combined endocrine and metabolic organ. Maternal blood pools in intervillous spaces, separated from foetal capillary blood by a thin placental membrane consisting of syncytiotrophoblast, cytotrophoblast, and connective tissue. This barrier facilitates passive diffusion of oxygen and glucose while restricting many large infectious pathogens, though viruses and drugs can cross freely. The placenta also secretes human placental lactogen and progesterone to maintain the pregnancy. The umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta, typically containing two smaller arteries carrying deoxygenated blood and waste products away from the foetus, and one larger vein carrying oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood back to the foetus. This vascular matrix is suspended in Wharton's jelly, a specialized mucous connective tissue that prevents kinking and compression during foetal movement. Amniotic fluid fills the amniotic sac, providing a sterile, thermoregulating cushion. It permits symmetrical musculoskeletal growth and prevents umbilical cord compression. The fluid is largely composed of foetal urine and pulmonary secretions, turned over continuously as the foetus swallows and inhales the liquid.

Foetal Circulation and Cardiovascular Adaptations

Foetal circulation differs fundamentally from postnatal circulation because the lungs and liver are non-functional for respiration and primary metabolic processing before birth. Oxygenated blood returns from the placenta via the umbilical vein, bypassing the hepatic capillary bed largely through the **ductus venosus** to enter the inferior vena cava directly. Upon reaching the right atrium, the path of blood is directed predominantly across the **foramen ovale**—an anatomical opening in the interatrial septum—into the left atrium, thereby bypassing the non-aerated pulmonary circuit. Blood that does enter the right ventricle and pulmonary artery is largely shunted away from the lungs through the **ductus arteriosus**, a vascular conduit connecting the pulmonary trunk directly to the descending aorta. At birth, clamping of the umbilical cord and the first neonatal breath alter pressure gradients dramatically. Systemic vascular resistance rises, pulmonary vascular resistance drops, and functional closure of these three foetal shunts occurs rapidly, establishing permanent adult circulatory patterns.

Methods of Assessing Foetal Wellbeing in Clinical Practice

Clinical evaluation of the intrauterine environment relies on a combination of biophysical, biochemical, and electronic monitoring tools designed to detect hypoxemia and chronic distress early. - **Foetal Heart Rate Monitoring**: Utilizes Doppler auscultation or cardiotocography to evaluate baseline rates, variability, accelerations, and decelerations, providing insight into autonomic nervous system integrity. - **Ultrasonography**: Measures biometric parameters such as biparietal diameter, abdominal circumference, and femur length to estimate weight and evaluate structural anatomy and amniotic fluid volume. - **Biophysical Profile**: Combines real-time ultrasound scoring of foetal breathing movements, gross body movements, tone, and amniotic fluid volume with a non-stress test to assess central nervous system oxygenation. - **Kick Counts**: A maternal self-assessment method where counting daily movements helps screen for overall foetal well-being in outpatient settings.

Common Teratogens and Factors Affecting Foetal Development

Teratogens are exogenous agents, chemicals, infectious pathogens, or physical conditions capable of altering embryonic or foetal development, resulting in structural malformations or functional deficits. The impact of any teratogen depends heavily on the timing of exposure during gestation, the dosage, and the genetic susceptibility of both mother and foetus. During the pre-embryonic period, teratogenic exposure often follows an all-or-nothing rule, either destroying the blastocyst entirely or allowing normal recovery. The embryonic period represents the highest susceptibility window due to active organogenesis, during which exposure can cause major structural anomalies. The foetal period is less prone to structural defects, though exposure can still impair functional maturation, central nervous system growth, and intra-uterine growth restriction. Commonly encountered clinical teratogens include certain medications like antiepileptic drugs and warfarin, maternal infections such as rubella and toxoplasmosis, environmental heavy metals, ionizing radiation, and maternal metabolic disorders like poorly controlled diabetes mellitus.
