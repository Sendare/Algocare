Circulatory Changes at Birth

Introduction to Foetal Circulation

Before birth, the human foetus relies entirely on the placenta for oxygen and nutrients, as the lungs are filled with fluid and non-functional. Blood returning from the placenta is well-oxygenated and flows through the umbilical vein toward the liver. Because the foetal liver does not need massive blood flow for processing yet, much of this oxygen-rich blood bypasses the hepatic circulation entirely through a vessel called the ductus venosus, entering directly into the inferior vena cava.

Inside the heart, foetal circulation features special bypasses designed to route blood away from the resting lungs:

  • Foramen ovale: An opening in the atrial septum that shunts highly oxygenated blood straight from the right atrium into the left atrium.
  • Ductus arteriosus: A muscular blood vessel connecting the pulmonary artery to the descending aorta, diverting blood away from the fluid-filled lungs and into the systemic circulation.

Mixed blood eventually returns to the placenta via two umbilical arteries for waste removal and re-oxygenation. Understanding these specialized pathways is vital for nurses, as failure to transition these structures at birth leads to persistent neonatal circulation problems.

Triggers for Circulatory Transition at Birth

The dramatic shift from foetal to neonatal circulation happens within seconds of birth, triggered by two major events: the baby's first breath and the clamping of the umbilical cord.

When the newborn takes that first breath, the lungs expand and fill with air. This mechanical action drastically reduces pulmonary vascular resistance, allowing blood to rush into the pulmonary capillary bed. At the same time, the sudden removal of the low-resistance placental bed via cord clamping causes systemic vascular resistance to skyrocket. These combined pressure changes—lowering pressure on the right side and raising pressure on the left side of the heart—force the functional closure of foetal shunts. Temperature changes, handling, and exposure to air also stimulate the respiratory center in the brainstem, sustaining effective breathing and sealing the fate of the transitional circulation.

Closure of the Foramen Ovale

The foramen ovale relies on a delicate pressure balance between the heart's upper chambers to stay open or shut. During foetal life, blood returning from the placenta enters the right atrium and pushes the flap of the foramen ovale open, flowing directly into the left atrium.

When the baby takes that crucial first breath and pulmonary blood flow increases, a large volume of blood returns to the left atrium from the lungs. This surge raises pressure in the left atrium higher than the pressure in the right atrium. This pressure reversal slams the flap valve of the foramen ovale shut against the atrial wall. Initially, this closure is functional and reversible if the baby cries hard and strains. Over the next several weeks of life, the tissue fuses permanently, forming the anatomical structure known as the fossa ovalis.

Obliteration of the Ductus Arteriosus

The ductus arteriosus is a muscular shunt that connects the pulmonary artery to the aorta in foetal life. Unlike passive flaps, its closure depends heavily on biochemical signals, specifically oxygen tension and local hormones.

As the newborn breathes room air, the partial pressure of oxygen in the blood rises sharply. This high oxygen concentration, combined with a sudden drop in local prostaglandins produced by the placenta, causes the muscular walls of the ductus arteriosus to constrict and spasm shut. Functional closure typically occurs within the first 12 to 24 hours of life. Over the next few weeks, the constricted vessel undergoes fibrosis and permanent anatomical obliteration, turning into a fibrous band known as the ligamentum arteriosum. In premature infants, low oxygen levels and high prostaglandin levels often keep this vessel open, leading to a condition called patent ductus arteriosus.

Closure of the Ductus Venosus and Umbilical Vessels

With the separation of the placenta at birth, the remaining foetal structures in the lower abdomen and liver undergo rapid functional closure and eventual fibrosis.

  • Umbilical arteries: These muscular vessels constrict immediately upon exposure to higher oxygen levels and cooling, preventing the newborn from bleeding out through the cord. They eventually become the medial umbilical ligaments.
  • Umbilical vein: Once the cord is clamped, the vein empties and collapses. It remains intact as a fibrous cord within the round ligament of the liver, known as the ligamentum teres.
  • Ductus venosus: This shunt within the liver functional closes as blood flow from the placenta ceases. Over days to weeks, it fibroses into the ligamentum venosum.

Nurses should inspect the clamped umbilical stump during routine assessments to ensure there is no secondary bleeding from these vessels.

Establishment of Pulmonary and Systemic Circulation

Once all foetal shunts are closed, the infant's circulatory system operates in a completely parallel, adult-style pattern. The right side of the heart now pumps exclusively deoxygenated blood through the pulmonary trunk into the low-resistance lungs to pick up oxygen. The left side of the heart receives this newly oxygenated blood from the pulmonary veins and pumps it out through the aorta to supply the entire body.

This transition shifts the workload of the heart. During foetal life, the right ventricle was actually thicker and stronger because it pumped against high systemic resistance via the ductus arteriosus. In the weeks following birth, as pulmonary resistance stays low and systemic resistance rises, the left ventricle hypertrophies and becomes the thicker, dominant pumping chamber. Nurses monitoring infant heart sounds will note changes in relative loudness and split heart sounds as this muscular remodeling takes place.

Neonatal Assessment and Delayed Transition Recognition

Not all newborns transition smoothly from foetal to neonatal circulation. When shunts fail to close or pulmonary resistance remains high, persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) or cyanotic heart defects can occur.

Nurses must systematically assess newborns for signs of delayed transition. Key red flags include persistent central cyanosis (blueness of the core, lips, and tongue), tachypnea, grunting, nasal flaring, and abnormal heart murmurs. Pulse oximetry screening on the right hand (pre-ductal) compared to the feet (post-ductal) helps detect abnormal blood mixing across a patent ductus arteriosus. Early recognition allows the healthcare team to intervene with oxygen, warmth, and specialized medications to support the cardiovascular system before permanent tissue damage occurs.
