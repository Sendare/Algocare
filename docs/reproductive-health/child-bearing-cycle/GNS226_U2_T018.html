Multiple Pregnancy

Introduction and Definition of Multiple Pregnancy

A multiple pregnancy occurs when a woman carries two or more fetuses simultaneously within the uterus. The most frequent form is twin gestation, though triplets, quadruplets, and higher-order multiples occur through spontaneous conception or assisted reproductive technologies. Biologically, multiple pregnancies are classified as either dizygotic or monozygotic. Dizygotic pregnancies arise from the fertilization of two separate ova by two different spermatozoa, producing fraternal twins who share roughly half of their genetic material, much like regular siblings. Monozygotic pregnancies originate from a single fertilized ovum that subsequently splits into two or more embryos, resulting in identical siblings with the same genetic makeup. Rising maternal age and fertility treatments have significantly increased the incidence of multiple gestations across obstetric wards in Nigeria, making early identification essential for risk stratification and positive perinatal outcomes.

Zygosity and Placentation

Understanding the placental and membrane configurations of a multiple pregnancy carries more clinical importance than knowing the genetic zygosity alone. Placentation directly dictates fetal risk and complications, particularly in monozygotic twins.

  • Dichorionic Diamniotic (DD): Each fetus has its own placenta and its own amniotic sac. This is typical for all dizygotic and about one-third of monozygotic twins, carrying the lowest risk profile.
  • Monochorionic Diamniotic (MD): The twins share a single placenta but have separate amniotic sacs. This configuration is common in monozygotic twins and requires heightened surveillance for vascular imbalances.
  • Monochorionic Monoamniotic (MM): Both twins share a single placenta and a single amniotic sac. This is the rarest and highest-risk form, carrying severe dangers such as cord entanglement.

An accurate ultrasound scan performed in the first trimester is the gold standard for determining chorionicity by visualizing the twin peak or lambda sign for dichorionic pregnancies, or the T-sign for monochorionic pregnancies.

Diagnosis and Antenatal Assessment

Early and accurate diagnosis of multiple gestation transforms antenatal outcomes by prompting timely specialized care. Clinical suspicion arises when uterine size is significantly greater than the period of amenorrhea, when multiple fetal poles are palpated during Leopold's maneuvers, or when two distinct fetal heart rates are auscultated simultaneously using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler ultrasound. However, definitive confirmation relies on ultrasonography.

Antenatal assessment for women carrying multiples involves frequent clinical visits compared to singleton pregnancies. Serial growth scans are mandatory to monitor individual fetal development and detect intrauterine growth restriction early. In monochorionic pregnancies, bi-weekly or weekly scans from the sixteenth week onward are crucial for identifying early signs of twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome, characterized by polyhydramnios in one sac and oligohydramnios in the other.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Carrying more than one fetus places an extraordinary demand on maternal organ systems and exposes the developing fetuses to unique pathological risks. Mothers face a substantially higher incidence of gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, hyperemesis gravidarum, and postpartum hemorrhage due to uterine overdistention and large placental mass.

On the fetal side, prematurity remains the leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality, as the average duration of a twin gestation is roughly 35 to 37 weeks. Monochorionic pregnancies carry specific, life-threatening complications such as twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome, selective intrauterine growth restriction, and twin reversed arterial perfusion sequence. Additionally, congenital malformations occur at a significantly higher rate in multiple gestations compared to singletons, requiring careful morphological screening during the second trimester.

Antenatal Management and Maternal Nutritional Care

Comprehensive nutritional support and proactive lifestyle modifications form the cornerstone of antenatal management for multiple pregnancies. Because the metabolic rate and nutrient demands increase exponentially with each additional fetus, maternal dietary intake must be adjusted accordingly. Caloric intake should increase by approximately 300 to 500 kilocalories per day for each fetus. Supplementation with iron, folic acid, and calcium is vital to prevent severe maternal anemia and pregnancy-induced bone demineralization, given the high expansion of maternal blood volume.

Rest and physical activity modification are heavily emphasized. Pregnant women carrying multiples are advised to reduce strenuous physical labor and incorporate regular periods of daytime rest, particularly in the third trimester, to improve uteroplacental blood flow and help prevent preterm labor.

Labor, Delivery, and Intrapartum Nursing Care

Intrapartum care for a multiple pregnancy requires a skilled, multidisciplinary team including obstetricians, anaesthetists, neonatologists, and experienced midwives. The mode of delivery is determined by fetal presentation, chorionicity, and gestational age. While a vaginal delivery may be safely accomplished if the first twin presents by the vertex, the second twin requires close intrapartum monitoring via electronic fetal monitoring and immediate ultrasound reassessment of its lie immediately after the birth of the first twin.

Midwives must maintain continuous intravenous access, have oxytocic drugs readily available for active management of the third stage of labor, and ensure that a fully equipped neonatal resuscitation team is present for each infant. Operating theatre staff should remain on standby in case an emergency caesarean section becomes necessary for the second twin due to acute fetal distress or malpresentation.

Postpartum Care and Prevention of Complications

The postpartum period for a mother of multiples is a time of intense physiological recovery and profound psychosocial adjustment. Nursing care focuses on monitoring vital signs, assessing uterine involution, and quantifying lochia to detect early secondary postpartum hemorrhage or retained placental tissue. Because of the heavy blood loss during delivery, hemoglobin levels must be checked, and iron supplementation continued postnatally.

Beyond physical recovery, emotional support is paramount. Parents facing the simultaneous care of two or more newborns are at elevated risk for postpartum depression, exhaustion, and financial strain. Nurses facilitate bonding, provide practical guidance on synchronized feeding and resting schedules, and connect families with social support networks and community health resources before discharge.
