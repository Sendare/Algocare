Multiple Pregnancy

Introduction and Definition of Multiple Pregnancy

A multiple pregnancy occurs when a woman carries two or more fetuses at the same time. The most common type is a twin pregnancy, though triplets, quadruplets, and higher-order multiples also happen. These pregnancies can be spontaneous, resulting from the release of multiple eggs or the splitting of a single fertilized egg, or they can result from assisted reproductive technologies like fertility drugs and IVF.

From a nursing perspective, multiple gestations are classified as high-risk from the very beginning. The maternal body undergoes profound anatomical and physiological adaptations to support more than one growing fetus, which significantly increases cardiovascular, metabolic, and mechanical strain.

Zygosity and Pathophysiology of Twinning

Understanding how twins develop helps nurses anticipate specific fetal risks. Zygosity refers to the genetic makeup of the babies:

  • Dizygotic (fraternal) twins: Develop from two separate eggs fertilized by two separate sperm. They have their own placentas, chorions, and amnions, and are genetically no more identical than regular siblings.
  • Monozygotic (identical) twins: Develop from a single fertilized egg that splits into two. Their specific placenta and sac setup depends on how early the division happens.

Monozygotic twins share a single placenta in many cases, which sets the stage for unique vascular complications like twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome due to shared blood vessels.

Diagnosis and Antenatal Assessment in Multiple Gestation

Early and accurate diagnosis is vital for planning proper antenatal care. While measuring fundal height larger than expected for gestational age or hearing multiple heartbeats can raise suspicion, ultrasound is the definitive diagnostic tool.

During the first trimester scan, sonographers determine chorionicity and amnionicity—whether the fetuses share a placenta or sacs. This distinction guides the entire schedule of subsequent monitoring. Nurses must prepare mothers for more frequent clinic visits, specialized scans to check fetal growth, and close observation for early signs of preterm labor.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Multiple pregnancies carry a substantially higher risk profile for both the mother and the babies compared to singletons.

Antenatal Management and Maternal Nutritional Care

Managing a multiple pregnancy requires a specialized care plan with frequent clinic visits to monitor fetal growth trajectories and screen for maternal complications early.

Nutrition plays a cornerstone role. Because the maternal body is supporting more than one fetus, energy, protein, vitamin, and mineral requirements increase markedly. Nurses must educate mothers on consuming adequate daily calories, along with higher doses of iron and folic acid to prevent severe nutritional anemia. Hydration is also heavily emphasized to prevent uterine irritability and subsequent preterm contractions.

Labor, Delivery, and Intrapartum Nursing Care

Intrapartum care for multiple pregnancies demands intense preparation, as delivery can be unpredictable. The mode of delivery—vaginal versus cesarean section—depends on the presentation of the fetuses, chorionicity, and the presence of complications. If the first twin is in a cephalic presentation, a trial of labor is often considered, whereas breech presentations or monoamniotic twins usually necessitate a cesarean section.

During labor, continuous electronic fetal monitoring for all fetuses is standard practice. The delivery room must be fully staffed, with pediatric or neonatal nursing teams present for each baby, along with equipment ready for neonatal resuscitation.

Postpartum Care and Prevention of Complications

The immediate postpartum period requires vigilant nursing oversight due to the high risk of postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) caused by uterine atony. Nurses must perform frequent checks on fundal height, lochia, and vital signs, administering uterotonic medications as ordered.

Beyond physical recovery, caring for multiple newborns places immense psychological and physical demands on the mother and her support system. Nurses play an instrumental role in facilitating early bonding, supporting breastfeeding or alternative feeding strategies, and connecting families with community resources to prevent exhaustion and postpartum depression.
