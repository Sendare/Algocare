Pregnancy Induced Hypertension

Introduction and Definition of Pregnancy Induced Hypertension

Pregnancy induced hypertension is typically characterized by the de novo development of elevated blood pressure after the twentieth week of gestation in a previously normotensive woman. Diagnostic criteria require a systolic blood pressure reading of 140 mmHg or higher, or a diastolic reading of 90 mmHg or higher, recorded on at least two separate occasions at least four hours apart. Proteinuria is no longer an absolute requirement for the initial definition, as modern clinical guidelines recognize that end-organ dysfunction can occur without urinary protein excretion. This condition represents a major contributor to maternal and perinatal morbidity globally, requiring vigilant monitoring across antenatal care settings in Nigeria. Blood pressure measurement must be performed using a correctly sized cuff while the woman is seated or lying in a semi-recumbent lateral position to avoid inferior vena cava compression.

Classification of Hypertensive Disorders in Pregnancy

Hypertensive disorders during pregnancy encompass several distinct clinical entities, each carrying unique implications for management and prognosis: - **Gestational Hypertension**: New-onset high blood pressure appearing after twenty weeks of pregnancy without proteinuria or systemic markers of end-organ dysfunction. - **Preeclampsia**: Hypertension arising after twenty weeks gestation accompanied by proteinuria or evidence of other systemic end-organ compromise such as thrombocytopenia or impaired liver function. - **Eclampsia**: The onset of grand mal seizure activity in a woman with preeclampsia that cannot be attributed to other neurological conditions. - **Chronic Hypertension**: High blood pressure present before pregnancy or diagnosed before the twentieth week of gestation. - **Chronic Hypertension with Superimposed Preeclampsia**: Worsening blood pressure or new systemic signs developing in a woman with pre-existing chronic hypertension.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

The fundamental pathophysiological event in pregnancy induced hypertension involves abnormal placentation during early gestation. Cytotrophoblasts fail to adequately invade and remodel the maternal spiral arteries, leaving them narrow, muscular, and high-resistance instead of wide, low-resistance conduits. This inadequate remodeling results in placental ischemia and oxidative stress, triggering the release of anti-angiogenic factors into the maternal circulation. These circulating factors cause widespread endothelial cell activation and injury throughout the maternal body. Endothelial damage leads to vasoconstriction, increased vascular permeability, and activation of the coagulation cascade. The resulting hemoconcentration and multi-organ ischemia account for the diverse clinical manifestations observed in the kidneys, liver, brain, and clotting systems.

Risk Factors and Identification

- **Nulliparity**: Women experiencing their first pregnancy carry a significantly higher baseline risk. - **Previous History**: A personal history of preeclampsia in a prior pregnancy strongly predisposes to recurrence. - **Extreme Maternal Age**: Women under eighteen or over thirty-five years old face elevated vulnerability. - **Multiple Gestation**: Carrying twins, triplets, or more increases placental mass and ischemic load. - **Pre-existing Medical Conditions**: Chronic hypertension, renal disease, diabetes mellitus, and autoimmune disorders like systemic lupus erythematosus heighten risk. - **Family History**: A maternal or sister history of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy suggests a genetic predisposition.

Maternal and Fetal Assessment

Comprehensive assessment requires regular monitoring of both mother and fetus to detect early signs of deterioration. Nurses must routinely check blood pressure, assess for visual disturbances such as blurred vision or scotomata, evaluate for persistent frontal headaches, and monitor urine output. Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain is a critical warning sign indicating hepatic stretching and impending liver capsule rupture. Laboratory investigations ordered by the clinician typically include complete blood counts to check platelet levels, serum creatinine, liver enzymes, and 24-hour urine protein or spot protein-to-creatinine ratios. Fetal well-being is evaluated through serial ultrasound scans to assess fetal growth and amniotic fluid volume, alongside cardiotocography and biophysical profiles to rule out acute fetal compromise resulting from reduced placental perfusion.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management strategies depend on gestational age and disease severity. For women with non-severe features at term, delivery is the definitive treatment to prevent maternal and fetal complications. When preeclampsia presents before thirty-four weeks gestation, expectant management in a hospital setting is often pursued to allow for fetal maturation, provided maternal and fetal conditions remain stable. Antihypertensive medications such as oral methyldopa, labetalol, or nifedipine are administered when blood pressure reaches severe thresholds, usually defined as 160/110 mmHg or higher, to reduce the risk of maternal cerebrovascular accidents. Nursing care centers on strict intake and output charting, hourly urine output monitoring via indwelling catheter when necessary, and providing a quiet, low-stimulus environment to minimize the risk of seizure triggers.

Emergency Care for Severe Preeclampsia and Eclampsia

Magnesium sulfate serves as the pharmacological agent of choice for seizure prophylaxis in severe preeclampsia and for acute seizure control during eclampsia. It acts as a central nervous system depressant and a neuromuscular blocker. During administration, nurses must monitor closely for signs of magnesium toxicity, which include loss of deep tendon reflexes, respiratory depression, and oliguria. Calcium gluconate must always be kept immediately available at the bedside as the specific antidote for magnesium toxicity. In the event of an eclamptic seizure, nursing priorities shift to maintaining a patent airway, placing the patient in a lateral recovery position to prevent aspiration, administering supplemental oxygen, and avoiding forced restraint of the limbs during convulsive episodes.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

Unmanaged or rapidly progressive hypertensive disorders in pregnancy can lead to severe, life-threatening complications for both mother and child. Maternal risks include cerebrovascular accidents, pulmonary edema, acute kidney injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and HELLP syndrome—characterized by hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelet count. Fetal complications stem primarily from chronic placental insufficiency, manifesting as intrauterine growth restriction, oligohydramnios, placental abruption, and non-assuring fetal heart rate tracings requiring emergency operative delivery. While most women experience complete blood pressure normalization following delivery, a history of pregnancy induced hypertension increases the long-term risk of developing chronic hypertension and cardiovascular disease later in life.
