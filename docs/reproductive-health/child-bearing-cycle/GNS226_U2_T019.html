Pregnancy Induced Hypertension

Introduction and Classification of Hypertensive Disorders in Pregnancy

Hypertension is one of the most common medical complications during pregnancy, posing serious risks to both the mother and the developing baby. In clinical practice, high blood pressure in pregnancy is not a single condition but a group of disorders with different levels of severity and underlying causes. A blood pressure reading of 140/90 mmHg or higher, recorded on two separate occasions at least four hours apart, is the standard threshold for diagnosis.

  • Gestational Hypertension: High blood pressure that develops after 20 weeks of pregnancy without protein in the urine, returning to normal postpartum.
  • Chronic Hypertension: High blood pressure that is present before pregnancy or is diagnosed before the 20th week of gestation.
  • Preeclampsia: A multi-organ condition characterized by high blood pressure and protein in the urine, or end-organ dysfunction after 20 weeks.
  • Eclampsia: The onset of seizure activity in a woman with preeclampsia that cannot be attributed to other neurological causes.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

The exact origin of pregnancy-induced hypertension remains complex, but abnormal placentation sits at the core of the process. In a healthy pregnancy, the spiral arteries in the uterus widen to supply high blood volume to the placenta. In preeclampsia, these blood vessels fail to widen properly, remaining narrow and causing reduced blood flow and ischemia to the placenta.

This stressed placenta releases harmful inflammatory factors and proteins into the maternal circulation. These circulating toxins cause widespread damage to the inner lining of blood vessels, known as the endothelium. Endothelial dysfunction leads to generalized vasoconstriction, capillary leak, and activation of the clotting system. Consequently, women experience elevated blood pressure, swelling due to fluid shifting out of vessels, and reduced perfusion to vital organs like the kidneys, liver, and brain.

Risk Factors and Vulnerable Populations

Certain clinical and demographic factors increase a woman's chance of developing hypertensive disorders during pregnancy. A first-time pregnancy, carrying twins or multiples, and a personal or family history of preeclampsia carry notable weight. Chronic medical conditions such as preexisting high blood pressure, kidney disease, diabetes, and autoimmune disorders like lupus also elevate risk significantly.

Additional risk factors include advanced maternal age over 35, obesity, and African ancestry due to complex genetic and socioeconomic interactions. Recognizing these triggers early in antenatal care allows nurses to plan increased surveillance, ensuring timely intervention if complications begin to emerge.

Maternal and Fetal Assessment

Routine antenatal assessment serves as the frontline defense in detecting pregnancy-induced hypertension before it escalates. Nurses must meticulously measure blood pressure at every visit, checking for persistent elevations. Asking about subjective symptoms is equally vital; persistent frontal headaches, visual disturbances like blurring or flashing lights, and upper right abdominal pain signal impending severe disease.

Objective screening involves testing urine for protein using dipsticks or 24-hour collections, alongside blood tests to monitor kidney function, liver enzymes, and platelet counts. Fetal well-being is evaluated concurrently through serial ultrasound scans to check growth, Doppler flow studies of the umbilical artery, and cardiotocography to monitor the fetal heart rate.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management strategies depend heavily on gestational age and the severity of the disease. For women with non-severe hypertension, outpatient monitoring, regular blood pressure checks, and close observation of fetal movements are standard. Bed rest is no longer routinely recommended, as it does not prevent preeclampsia and increases the risk of blood clots.

When blood pressure climbs to severe levels (160/110 mmHg or higher), antihypertensive medications such as labetalol, nifedipine, or methyldopa are prescribed to stroke prevention thresholds. The ultimate cure for preeclampsia is delivery of the placenta. If the pregnancy is near term, induction of labor is scheduled. For preterm cases, corticosteroids are administered to accelerate fetal lung maturity while stabilizing the mother.

Emergency Care for Severe Preeclampsia and Eclampsia

Severe preeclampsia and eclampsia represent obstetric emergencies requiring swift, coordinated teamwork. Magnesium sulfate is the drug of choice for preventing and treating convulsions in eclampsia. It acts as a central nervous system depressant and neuromuscular blocker. During administration, nurses must monitor closely for toxicity by checking deep tendon reflexes, respiratory rate, and urine output.

Calcium gluconate must always be kept at the bedside as the specific antidote for magnesium sulfate toxicity. Simultaneously, emergency blood pressure control using intravenous antihypertensives prevents maternal stroke or cardiac failure, while continuous electronic fetal monitoring assesses the baby's response to the crisis.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

Failure to manage hypertensive disorders in pregnancy can lead to catastrophic complications for both mother and child. Maternal risks include placental abruption, stroke, acute kidney injury, pulmonary edema, and HELLP syndrome, which involves hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets. In worst-case scenarios, multi-organ failure results in maternal mortality.

Fetal complications stem primarily from chronic low oxygen and nutrient supply, leading to intrauterine growth restriction, premature birth, and stillbirth. Following delivery, most women see their blood pressure return to normal within weeks. However, women who experience preeclampsia carry an increased lifetime risk of developing chronic hypertension, heart disease, and stroke later in life.
