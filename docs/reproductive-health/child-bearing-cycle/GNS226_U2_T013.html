Heartburn in Pregnancy

Introduction and Physiological Basis

Heartburn, or pyrosis, is an exceptionally common complaint during pregnancy, affecting up to eighty percent of women, particularly during the third trimester. It manifests as a burning sensation rising from the epigastric region toward the sternum or throat. While generally benign, it causes significant discomfort and disrupts sleep and nutrition.

The primary driver of this condition is hormonal and anatomical change. Progesterone, a dominant hormone throughout gestation, causes systemic smooth muscle relaxation. This includes the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), which normally acts as a one-way valve preventing gastric contents from entering the esophagus. When the LES tone decreases, acidic gastric juices easily reflux upward, irritating the unprotected esophageal mucosa. As the uterus enlarges, it physically displaces the stomach, increasing intra-gastric pressure and compounding the tendency for acid to escape upward.

Contributing Etiology and Risk Factors

Several distinct factors elevate a pregnant woman's risk of developing severe pyrosis:

  • Advanced Gestational Age: As the fetus grows, upward mechanical displacement of the stomach increases gastric pressure.
  • Multiparity: Women who have had previous pregnancies often experience earlier and more severe symptoms due to weakened abdominal wall and pelvic floor structures.
  • Dietary Triggers: Consuming spicy, fatty, highly acidic, or caffeinated foods delays gastric emptying and directly irritates the gastric lining.
  • Hiatal Hernia: Pre-existing structural anomalies of the diaphragm exacerbate the anatomical predisposition to acid reflux.

Clinical Manifestations and Subjective Assessment

During routine antenatal visits, nurses must actively screen for gastrointestinal discomfort. Subjective assessment goes beyond a simple inquiry about pain. The nurse should ask about the exact timing, character, and radiation of symptoms.

Typical complaints include a retrosternal burning sensation that worsens after eating, when bending over, or while lying flat. Some women report a sour or bitter taste in the mouth, excessive salivation (water brash), or a chronic dry cough caused by micro-aspiration of gastric acid. Asking about the impact on sleep and daily fluid intake helps determine the severity of the condition and guides appropriate intervention strategies.

Differential Diagnosis and Red Flag Symptoms

Not all upper abdominal pain or burning in pregnancy is simple heartburn. Nurses must differentiate benign reflux from more serious pathologies such as peptic ulcer disease, cholecystitis, or pre-eclampsia (which can cause epigastric pain due to hepatic involvement).

Certain indicators require immediate medical escalation. If a pregnant patient reports dysphagia, odynophagia, persistent vomiting, unexplained weight loss, or gastrointestinal bleeding, further diagnostic workup is mandatory. Epigastric pain accompanied by right upper quadrant tenderness, headache, visual disturbances, and hypertension must be evaluated promptly to rule out severe pre-eclampsia.

Non-Pharmacological Management and Lifestyle Modifications

First-line management of heartburn focuses on behavioral and dietary adjustments to minimize reflux episodes without exposing the fetus to medications.

  • Small, Frequent Meals: Eating five or six small meals instead of three large ones prevents gastric distension and reduces the volume of acid available for reflux.
  • Postural Control: Remaining upright for at least one to two hours after eating uses gravity to keep stomach contents in place. Elevating the head of the bed by six to nine inches helps prevent nighttime reflux.
  • Fluid Timing: Drinking fluids primarily between meals rather than washing down food minimizes gastric volume during digestion.
  • Clothing Choices: Avoiding tight waistbands and constrictive garments prevents external compression of the stomach.

Pharmacological Interventions and Safe Medications

When lifestyle modifications fail to provide adequate relief, safe pharmacological options can be introduced under clinical guidance. Antacids containing aluminum, magnesium, or calcium carbonate are generally considered first-line drug therapy because they neutralize existing gastric acid rapidly.

However, chronic high-dose intake of certain antacids should be monitored; for instance, magnesium-trisilicate-containing products are often avoided in high doses near term, and sodium bicarbonate should be avoided entirely due to the risk of maternal fluid overload and metabolic alkalosis. If antacids prove insufficient, histamine-2 receptor antagonists (like ranitidine alternatives or famotidine) or proton pump inhibitors may be prescribed after risk-benefit analysis by the attending physician.
