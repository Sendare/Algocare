ICT Related Addictions

Understanding ICT-Related Addictions in Youth

Information and Communication Technology (ICT) addiction refers to the problematic, compulsive use of digital devices like smartphones, computers, and gaming consoles that interferes with daily life, relationships, and academic performance. While modern technology is essential for learning and socialization, excessive use can trigger behavioural dependency patterns similar to substance abuse. Adolescents are particularly vulnerable due to ongoing brain development, specifically the early maturation of emotional centres coupled with the delayed development of the prefrontal cortex, which governs impulse control and long-term planning. Nurses must understand that this is not merely poor discipline or a bad habit, but a complex psychological issue requiring professional attention.

Common Forms and Manifestations of Digital Addiction

  • Social Media Addiction: Driven by the constant need for validation through likes, shares, and comments, leading to endless scrolling and fear of missing out (FoMO).
  • Internet Gaming Disorder: Characterized by compulsive playing of online or offline video games at the expense of hygiene, eating, and academic duties.
  • Smartphone Addiction: A general reliance on mobile devices where the phone is checked constantly throughout the day and night, often causing phantom vibration syndrome.
  • Cybersex and Online Pornography: Compulsive viewing of adult content online that distorts normal interpersonal relationship expectations and intimacy.
  • Net-Compulsion: Involves obsessive online gambling, compulsive shopping on e-commerce sites, or excessive trading that drains financial resources.

Neurobiological and Psychological Drivers

Digital platforms are specifically engineered to capture and retain human attention. Every notification, like, or victory in a game triggers a release of dopamine in the brain's reward pathway, creating a reinforcement loop similar to gambling or chemical substances. Psychologically, many youth turn to screens as an escape mechanism to cope with underlying anxiety, low self-esteem, depression, or loneliness. The immediate gratification provided by virtual worlds offers a temporary shield against real-world stressors, eventually creating a vicious cycle where increased screen time worsens the original emotional distress.

Physical and Mental Health Consequences

Prolonged and unregulated ICT use takes a heavy toll on both the body and the mind. Physically, adolescents frequently present with computer vision syndrome, characterized by dry eyes, blurred vision, and headaches from staring at backlit screens for hours. Sedentary behaviour promotes obesity, poor posture, and musculoskeletal issues like repetitive strain injury in the thumbs and wrists. Sleep disruption is exceptionally common; blue light exposure suppresses melatonin production, delaying circadian rhythms and causing chronic sleep deprivation. Mentally, excessive screen time correlates strongly with rising rates of depression, irritability, and social isolation.

Nursing Assessment and Screening

Nurses play a frontline role in identifying problematic technology use during routine health visits. Assessment should be non-judgmental, exploring not just the number of hours spent online, but the context and emotional impact of device use. Useful screening questions examine whether the adolescent lies about screen time, loses track of time, or becomes agitated when devices are removed. Nurses should utilize validated tools like the Internet Addiction Test (IAT) or screen-time tracking features built into modern smartphones to gather objective data. In addition, assessing family dynamics, school attendance, and sleep patterns helps paint a complete picture of the youth's well-being.

Prevention and Digital Hygiene Strategies

Preventing ICT addiction requires cultivating healthy habits known as digital hygiene. Nurses should educate parents and adolescents on setting clear, consistent boundaries around technology use. Practical steps include designating tech-free zones, such as the dining table and the bedroom, and establishing a digital curfew at least one hour before bedtime to protect sleep quality. Encouraging offline hobbies, physical exercise, and face-to-face social interactions helps build a balanced lifestyle. Parents must also model healthy tech habits, as children often imitate adult behaviours regarding device consumption.

Management, Counseling, and Rehabilitation

Managing severe ICT addiction requires a multidisciplinary approach involving nurses, psychologists, and family counselors. Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT) is the gold standard psychological treatment, helping adolescents identify distorted thinking patterns, recognize triggers, and develop healthier coping mechanisms. Rather than enforcing total abstinence—which is unrealistic in a digital world—treatment focuses on harm reduction and regaining self-control. Family therapy is equally vital to improve household communication, restructure boundaries, and address enabling behaviours. In cases with severe comorbid depression or anxiety, medical evaluation for pharmacotherapy may be necessary.
