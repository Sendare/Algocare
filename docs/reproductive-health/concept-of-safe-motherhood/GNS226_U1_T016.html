Estimation of Maternal Mortality

Introduction to Maternal Mortality and Safe Motherhood

Maternal mortality remains one of the most sensitive indicators of healthcare quality and societal equity worldwide. The WHO defines a maternal death as the death of a woman while pregnant or within forty-two days of termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and site of the pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes. Understanding the scale of this issue requires systematic tracking and estimation. Safe motherhood initiatives depend heavily on these baseline figures to direct resources, formulate public health policies, and evaluate the impact of interventions deployed in primary and tertiary healthcare facilities across Nigeria.

Key Definitions and Standard Metrics

Accurate calculation of maternal mortality relies on specific epidemiological metrics.

  • Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR): The number of maternal deaths during a given time period per 100,000 live births during the same time period. This measures the risk of death once a woman becomes pregnant.
  • Maternal Mortality Rate (MMRate): The number of maternal deaths in a given period per 100,000 women of reproductive age (typically 15-49 years). This reflects both the risk of maternal death per pregnancy and the general fertility rate.
  • Lifetime Risk of Maternal Death: The probability that a young woman will eventually die from a maternal cause, taking into account both the maternal mortality ratio and her overall fertility level.

Direct and Indirect Sources of Data Collection

Gathering data on maternal deaths requires tapping into various information channels, categorized into direct and indirect sources.

  • Civil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS): The gold standard system that continuously records births and deaths as they occur in the population.
  • Health Facility Records: Hospital registers, admission logs, and maternal death reviews that capture facility-based mortality.
  • Population Censuses: Periodic national counts that can include specific questions on household deaths.
  • Household Surveys: Sample-based questionnaires administered to communities, such as Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS), to capture retrospective data.

Methods of Estimation: CRVS and Health Facility Data

Civil Registration and Vital Statistics systems provide continuous data, but their effectiveness depends entirely on complete coverage. When every birth and death is legally registered and medical causes of death are certified by trained personnel, MMR calculations are straightforward. However, in many resource-constrained settings, a large proportion of deaths occur at home and go unrecorded. To bridge this gap, health facility data is often utilized. Facility data suffers from selection bias because it only captures women who reach a hospital, ignoring those who die in the community before seeking care.

Methods of Estimation: Household Surveys and Sisterhood Techniques

When vital registration is absent, indirect estimation techniques such as the sisterhood method become invaluable. Developed by the WHO and UNICEF, the sisterhood method asks adult respondents about the survival status of all their adult sisters who reached reproductive age. This clever approach aggregates community data to calculate maternal mortality without requiring massive sample sizes or complex prospective tracking. Similarly, Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) use direct sisterhood modules, asking detailed questions regarding the timing and cause of sisters' deaths to derive national estimates.

Challenges and Limitations in Measuring Maternal Mortality

Accurately measuring maternal mortality is fraught with technical and administrative hurdles. Misclassification is a major barrier; deaths due to indirect causes like cardiac disease or malaria aggravated by pregnancy are frequently miscoded on death certificates. Furthermore, stigma surrounding conditions like septic abortions can lead families to conceal the true cause of death. In many regions, cultural practices dictate prompt burial of the deceased without notifying authorities, resulting in severe underreporting. Navigating these gaps requires triangulation of multiple data sources rather than relying on a single flawed metric.

The Role of Nurses and Midwives in Data Recording

Nurses and midwives sit at the frontline of maternal healthcare delivery and data generation. Every admission, antenatal visit, intrapartum intervention, and discharge documented in clinic registers forms the bedrock of facility-based statistics. Accurate completion of partographs, clear documentation of hemorrhage or hypertensive disorders, and meticulous recording in maternal death surveillance and response (MDSR) forms are professional duties with public health implications. When a nurse fails to document a secondary contributing factor in a clinical record, the true burden of complications becomes invisible to policy planners.
