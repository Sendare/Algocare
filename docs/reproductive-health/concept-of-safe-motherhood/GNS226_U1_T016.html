Estimation of Maternal Mortality

Introduction and Core Definitions of Maternal Mortality

Maternal mortality remains a major public health crisis, particularly in developing nations like Nigeria. Understanding how these deaths are tracked begins with precise definitions set by the World Health Organization. A maternal death is defined as the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of the end of pregnancy, regardless of the duration and site of the pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes.

To measure this burden accurately, statisticians and healthcare planners rely on two primary metrics:

  1. Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR): The number of maternal deaths during a given time period per 100,000 live births during the same period. This measures the obstetric risk associated with pregnancy and childbirth.
  2. Maternal Mortality Rate (MMRTE): The number of maternal deaths in a given period per 100,000 women of reproductive age (usually 15 to 49 years) in the same period. This reflects both the risk of pregnancy and the frequency with which women get pregnant.

A common clinical confusion occurs between direct and indirect obstetric deaths. Direct obstetric deaths result from obstetric complications of the pregnancy, labor, and puerperium, such as postpartum hemorrhage or eclampsia. Indirect obstetric deaths result from previous existing disease or disease that developed during pregnancy and was aggravated by the physiological effects of pregnancy, such as cardiac disease or severe malaria.

Why We Estimate Maternal Mortality

Counting maternal deaths is not just an academic exercise; it is the foundation for saving women's lives. Without reliable data, health ministries cannot allocate scarce resources effectively or target interventions where they are most desperately needed. Estimation helps policymakers track progress toward global health goals, such as reducing preventable maternal mortality.

Furthermore, studying these figures exposes systemic failures in the healthcare delivery system. When a community has a soaring maternal mortality ratio, it usually signals a breakdown in emergency obstetric care, poor referral networks, or severe shortages of skilled birth attendants. By analyzing the trends behind the numbers, nurses and public health workers can advocate for better maternal health policies, infrastructural improvements, and targeted community sensitization programs.

Sources of Data: Direct and Indirect Sources

Gathering data on maternal deaths requires tapping into various information streams, broadly divided into direct and indirect sources.

  • Direct Sources: These capture vital events as they happen or through official registration systems. Examples include Civil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS) systems, hospital records, and institutional registers.
  • Indirect Sources: These are utilized when direct registration is weak or absent. They rely on surveys, censuses, and mathematical modeling to estimate the number of deaths based on related family or community information.

In settings with robust health infrastructure, direct institutional data is preferred. However, in resource-constrained environments where many women deliver at home and deaths go unrecorded, indirect estimation methods become indispensable for painting an accurate national picture.

Civil Registration, Vital Statistics, and Census Approaches

A functioning Civil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS) system is the gold standard for monitoring vital events like births and deaths continuously. When a nation has a strong CRVS system, every maternal death is legally reported, investigated, and recorded. Unfortunately, in many low- and middle-income countries, the CRVS system is incomplete, meaning a large percentage of deaths occur quietly at home and escape official tallying.

To bridge this gap, governments occasionally use population censuses and large-scale household surveys (such as Demographic and Health Surveys). During a census or survey, trained interviewers ask households about recent deaths among women of reproductive age. To catch pregnancy-related deaths without asking awkward or confusing medical questions, surveyors often use a simple screening question known as the pregnancy-related death question: "Did any female member of this household die while pregnant, during delivery, or within two months of childbirth?"

Indirect Estimation: The Sisterhood Method

When neither hospital records nor CRVS systems are reliable, epidemiologists turn to clever indirect techniques, most notably the sisterhood method. Developed to overcome the difficulty of collecting large sample sizes for rare events like maternal death, this survey technique asks adult respondents about the survival of all their sisters who reached reproductive age.

By asking respondents how many sisters they have, how many are dead, and of those dead, how many died during pregnancy, childbirth, or the puerperium, researchers can calculate maternal mortality rates with a smaller sample size. Because respondents report on the survival of siblings who may have died years ago, the sisterhood method provides a retrospective estimate that reflects the average mortality level over a decade prior to the survey, rather than a current annual snapshot.

Challenges in Measuring Maternal Mortality in Nigeria

Measuring maternal mortality accurately in Nigeria presents unique hurdles that skew official statistics. One major barrier is the high prevalence of home deliveries attended by traditional birth attendants or unregulated healers. When a woman dies at home, her death often goes completely undocumented by the formal health system.

Other significant obstacles include:

  • Misclassification of Cause of Death: Stigma surrounding conditions like septic abortion, or cultural practices that bury bodies quickly without an autopsy, lead to inaccurate death certificates.
  • Poor Record Keeping: Many primary and secondary health facilities rely on paper registers that get lost, damaged, or poorly filled out by overworked staff.
  • Fear of Litigation or Blame: Healthcare workers may sometimes alter or omit details in clinical logs out of fear of legal repercussions or disciplinary action.

These combined factors mean that many figures reported in developing nations are statistical estimates and modeling projections rather than exact headcounts.

The Role of Nurses and Midwives in Data Collection

Nurses and midwives sit at the frontline of maternal health data collection. Every time a nurse logs a delivery, records an admission, or fills out an inpatient mortality ticket, they are contributing raw data that feeds into national health statistics. Accurate documentation begins at the bedside.

To improve data quality, nursing personnel must ensure that admission books, partographs, delivery registers, and cause-of-death entries are filled out completely and legibly without shortcuts. When a maternal death occurs, participating in maternal death surveillance and response (MDSR) audits helps healthcare teams review what went wrong clinically, identify avoidable delays, and prevent similar tragedies in the future. Professional integrity in record-keeping transforms messy daily logs into powerful evidence for health reform.
