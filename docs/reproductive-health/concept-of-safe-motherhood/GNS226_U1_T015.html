Causes of Maternal Morbidity

Introduction to Maternal Morbidity and the Safe Motherhood Initiative

Maternal morbidity refers to any short- or long-term health condition attributed to or aggravated by pregnancy and childbirth, negatively impacting a woman's well-being. Unlike maternal mortality, which tracks deaths, morbidity captures the immense burden of 'near-misses' and chronic disabling conditions such as obstetric fistula, chronic pain, and uterine prolapse. Globally and across sub-Saharan Africa, reducing these complications remains a pressing public health priority. The **Safe Motherhood Initiative**, launched internationally in 1987, serves as a landmark framework aimed at reducing maternal mortality and morbidity by improving access to quality reproductive health services. In Nigeria, where health indicators have historically lagged behind global averages, national policies have increasingly integrated the core pillars of this initiative: family planning, antenatal care, skilled attendance at birth, and essential obstetric care. Grasping the scope of maternal morbidity requires looking beyond simple clinical statistics to understand the devastating social and economic ripple effects on families and communities when a mother's health is compromised.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Hemorrhage and Hypertensive Disorders

Direct obstetric complications stem directly from obstetric interventions, omissions, incorrect treatment, or from a chain of events arising during pregnancy, labor, or the puerperium. Among these, **postpartum hemorrhage (PPH)** and **hypertensive disorders of pregnancy** stand out as the leading killers and maimers of women in resource-constrained settings. - **Postpartum Hemorrhage**: Defined traditionally as blood loss exceeding 500 mL following vaginal delivery (or 1000 mL following a caesarean section), PPH is frequently caused by uterine atony, retained placental tissue, or genital tract trauma. Rapid hypovolemic shock can ensue if uterotonics and fluid resuscitation are delayed. - **Hypertensive Disorders**: Conditions such as gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, and eclampsia involve systemic endothelial dysfunction and vasospasm. Severe pre-eclampsia can rapidly progress to **eclampsia**, characterized by generalized tonic-clonic seizures, cerebral hemorrhage, and multi-organ failure if magnesium sulfate therapy is withheld.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Sepsis and Obstructed Labor

Infections and mechanical labor dystocia represent two additional pillars of direct maternal morbidity. **Puerperal sepsis** is a bacterial infection of the genital tract occurring at any time between the rupture of membranes or labor and the 42nd day postpartum. Pathogens frequently ascend from the lower reproductive tract during prolonged rupture of membranes, multiple digital vaginal examinations, or unhygienic delivery practices. Clinical manifestations include pyrexia, lower abdominal pain, and foul-smelling lochia, which can escalate to septic shock. **Obstructed labor** occurs when the presenting part of the fetus cannot progress through the birth canal due to mechanical blockage, despite strong uterine contractions. Cephalopelvic disproportion, malpresentations, and fetal macrosomia are primary contributors. If unrelieved, prolonged pressure leads to ischemic necrosis of adjacent pelvic tissues, culminating in devastating long-term morbidities such as **vesicovaginal fistula (VVF)** or rectovaginal fistula, alongside severe uterine rupture.

Indirect Obstetric Causes: Pre-existing and Co-existing Medical Conditions

Indirect maternal morbidity arises from previously existing diseases or conditions developed during pregnancy that are not direct obstetric causes, but are aggravated by the physiological adaptations of gestation. Pregnancy imposes significant hemodynamic, metabolic, and respiratory demands on the maternal body, unmasking or worsening underlying pathologies. - **Malaria in Pregnancy**: In endemic regions like Nigeria, Plasmodium infection causes severe maternal anemia, placental parasitemia, and low birth weight. Hemolysis and reduced immunity heighten vulnerability. - **Cardiovascular Disease**: Rheumatic heart disease and congenital anomalies strain under increased cardiac output and blood volume, frequently precipitating congestive heart failure. - **Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)**: Immunosuppression increases susceptibility to opportunistic infections and puerperal sepsis, requiring meticulous antiretroviral therapy management. - **Anemia**: Iron deficiency and chronic blood loss diminish physiological reserves, severely limiting the body's tolerance to intrapartum hemorrhage.

Socioeconomic and Cultural Determinants of Maternal Health

Clinical complications rarely occur in a vacuum; they are deeply influenced by socioeconomic, cultural, and structural determinants. In many Nigerian communities, poverty limits financial access to healthcare facilities, drugs, and diagnostic tests, forcing women to rely on under-resourced alternatives. Educational attainment is strongly correlated with health-seeking behavior; women with lower literacy levels may lack awareness of danger signs in pregnancy and possess less autonomy in household decision-making. Cultural norms and patriarchal structures frequently dictate whether a pregnant woman can seek timely medical assistance. In some settings, permission must be sought from male heads of households or traditional elders before visiting a health facility. Additionally, strong reliance on traditional birth attendants (TBAs) for complicated deliveries—often driven by cultural familiarity and financial constraints—frequently delays recognition of obstetric emergencies until irreversible harm has occurred.

Health System Challenges and Delays in Seeking Care

The **Three Delays Model** provides a vital lens for analyzing why maternal morbidity and mortality remain stubbornly high in low-income settings. This framework highlights how structural breakdowns in the health system intersect with human behavior to produce tragic outcomes. | Delay Phase | Primary Description | Contributing Factors | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Phase 1** | Delay in deciding to seek care | Ignorance of danger signs, cultural beliefs, financial constraints, low autonomy | | **Phase 2** | Delay in reaching a medical facility | Poor road infrastructure, lack of transportation, geographical isolation, high transport costs | | **Phase 3** | Delay in receiving adequate care at the facility | Staff shortages, stock-outs of blood and drugs, power outages, poor staff attitude, lack of equipment | Addressing maternal morbidity requires holistic health system strengthening that tackles all three phases simultaneously, rather than focusing solely on clinical treatment inside the hospital walls.

Assessment, Early Identification, and Nursing Interventions

Nurses occupy the front lines of maternal care, bearing the responsibility for early detection and rapid intervention when complications arise. Routine antenatal visits must incorporate thorough risk stratification, blood pressure monitoring, urinalysis for proteinuria, and screening for anemia and infections. Empowering pregnant women with clear, actionable knowledge regarding danger signs—such as vaginal bleeding, severe headaches, visual disturbances, decreased fetal movement, and fluid leakage—enables prompt reporting. In acute intrapartum and postpartum scenarios, nurses must execute rapid clinical triage using standardized tools like the **Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS)** for obstetrics. Swift initiation of intravenous access, collection of blood for cross-matching, monitoring of vital signs, and effective interprofessional communication with medical officers can mean the difference between full recovery and permanent disability or death. Maintaining readiness for emergency drills in every clinical setting ensures that nursing responses remain calm, structured, and life-saving.
