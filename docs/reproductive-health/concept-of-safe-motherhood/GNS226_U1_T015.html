Causes of Maternal Morbidity

Introduction to Maternal Morbidity and the Safe Motherhood Initiative

Maternal morbidity refers to any short- or long-term health condition that results from pregnancy and childbirth, placing a heavy burden on women, families, and healthcare systems, particularly in developing nations like Nigeria. Understanding the scope of maternal morbidity is essential for student nurses as they prepare to deliver holistic, evidence-based care in various clinical settings. To combat high rates of maternal complications and mortality globally, the World Health Organization and international partners launched the Safe Motherhood Initiative in 1987. This global campaign aims to raise awareness about the magnitude of maternal health problems and to ensure that all women receive the necessary care throughout pregnancy, labor, and the postpartum period. For Nigerian nursing students, the Safe Motherhood Initiative serves as a foundational framework emphasizing that maternity care must be accessible, affordable, and of high quality. Key pillars of the initiative include family planning, antenatal care, clean and safe delivery, and essential obstetric care. By integrating these principles into daily nursing practice, healthcare providers can significantly reduce preventable illnesses and long-term disabilities resulting from childbearing. Furthermore, nurses play a vital role in community health education, advocating for women's health rights, and bridging gaps in healthcare delivery at primary, secondary, and tertiary levels.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Hemorrhage and Hypertensive Disorders

Direct obstetric causes are complications directly resulting from obstetric interventions, omissions, incorrect treatment, or from a chain of events arising during pregnancy, labor, or the puerperium. Among these, postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) stands out as a leading cause of severe maternal morbidity and mortality worldwide, particularly in Nigeria. PPH is commonly defined as blood loss exceeding 500 mL following a vaginal birth or 1000 mL following a cesarean section. Uterine atony, retained placental tissues, genital tract lacerations, and coagulation disorders are primary triggers that demand swift nursing and medical intervention. Student nurses must master the signs of excessive bleeding, routinely monitor uterine tone, and administer uterotonic medications such as oxytocin promptly as prescribed. Another major category of direct obstetric causes includes hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, notably pre-eclampsia and eclampsia. These conditions are characterized by new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation, which can rapidly progress to seizures, stroke, renal failure, or HELLP syndrome if left unmanaged. Nurses must diligently check blood pressure, test urine for protein, monitor for visual disturbances or severe headaches, and be prepared to administer magnesium sulfate as an anticonvulsant and antihypertensive agents as indicated. Prompt identification and management of hemorrhage and hypertension are cornerstone competencies for safe maternal nursing practice.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Sepsis and Obstructed Labor

In addition to hemorrhage and hypertension, puerperal sepsis and obstructed labor remain formidable direct causes of maternal morbidity in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria. Puerperal sepsis is an infection of the genital tract occurring at any time between the rupture of membranes or labor and the 42nd day postpartum, characterized by fever, lower abdominal pain, and foul-smelling lochia. Risk factors include frequent vaginal examinations during prolonged labor, premature rupture of membranes, poor perineal hygiene, and pre-existing reproductive tract infections. Nurses play a crucial role in preventing sepsis through strict adherence to aseptic techniques during labor examinations, proper wound care for episiotomies or cesarean sections, and early administration of prescribed broad-spectrum antibiotics. On the other hand, obstructed labor occurs when the presenting part of the fetus cannot progress through the birth canal due to mechanical blockage, despite strong uterine contractions. Causes include cephalopelvic disproportion, fetal malpresentation, or pelvic tumors. Prolonged obstructed labor can lead to severe maternal complications such as uterine rupture, postpartum hemorrhage, maternal exhaustion, and obstetric fistula—a devastating condition causing continuous urinary or fecal incontinence. Student nurses must monitor labor progress using a partograph, identify early signs of failure to progress, and facilitate timely referral for emergency surgical interventions like cesarean sections.

Indirect Obstetric Causes: Pre-existing and Co-existing Medical Conditions

Indirect obstetric causes of maternal morbidity encompass pre-existing or co-existing medical conditions that are aggravated by the physiological demands of pregnancy. Although these diseases are not initiated by pregnancy itself, the normal hemodynamic, metabolic, and respiratory changes of gestation can severely strain diseased organ systems. Common indirect causes in Nigeria include malaria, anemia, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection, cardiac diseases, and diabetes mellitus. For instance, malaria in pregnancy can cause severe maternal anemia, placental parasitemia, low birth weight, and spontaneous abortion due to systemic hemolysis and fever. Severe anemia reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, making the parturient highly vulnerable to cardiovascular collapse during even minor blood loss at delivery. Furthermore, infectious diseases like HIV require rigorous prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT) protocols alongside antiretroviral therapy to protect the health of both mother and child. Student nurses must conduct thorough health histories during antenatal bookings to identify pre-existing conditions early. Collaborative management involving multidisciplinary teams—including obstetricians, physicians, dietitians, and midwives—is vital to stabilize these medical conditions throughout the antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal periods, thereby safeguarding maternal well-being.

Socioeconomic and Cultural Determinants of Maternal Health in Nigeria

Maternal health outcomes in Nigeria are heavily influenced by a complex interplay of socioeconomic and cultural determinants. Factors such as poverty, low educational attainment, gender inequality, harmful traditional practices, and geographical barriers significantly shape a woman's health-seeking behavior and access to quality care. Poverty restricts families from affording prescribed medications, diagnostic investigations, and user fees at health facilities, often leading to dangerous delays in seeking or receiving care. Low literacy levels among women can hinder their understanding of danger signs in pregnancy and reduce their autonomy in making critical health decisions, which are frequently dominated by male partners or family elders. Culturally, some Nigerian communities rely heavily on traditional birth attendants (TBAs) or herbal concoctions during labor, sometimes bypassing formal medical facilities until catastrophic complications arise. Early marriage and adolescent pregnancies are also prevalent in certain regions, where biological immaturity of the pelvis increases the likelihood of obstructed labor and severe morbidity. Student nurses must develop cultural competence to address these barriers respectfully and effectively. Community health outreach, girl-child education advocacy, and male partner involvement programs are indispensable strategies that nurses can champion to transform community norms and improve maternal health indices across Nigeria.

Health System Challenges and Delays in Seeking Care

The Three Delays Model provides a vital framework for understanding why maternal morbidity and mortality remain disproportionately high in Nigeria. The first delay is the delay in deciding to seek care, which is often rooted in low awareness of danger signs, financial constraints, cultural beliefs, and lack of decision-making power by women. The second delay involves identifying and reaching a medical facility, exacerbated by poor road infrastructure, long distances to health centers, lack of reliable transportation, and high transit costs, especially in rural Nigerian communities. The third delay occurs within the health facility itself, where patients experience delays in receiving adequate, quality care due to stock-outs of essential supplies, shortage of skilled personnel, power outages, and poor attitudes or delayed responses from healthcare providers. Student nurses must critically evaluate these systemic bottlenecks to appreciate their role in mitigating delays. By advocating for efficient triage systems, maintaining emergency supply inventories, displaying compassionate and prompt professional conduct, and participating in referral network coordination, nurses can directly target and minimize facility-based delays, saving countless maternal lives.

Assessment, Early Identification, and Nursing Interventions

Comprehensive assessment, early identification, and prompt nursing interventions are paramount in reducing maternal morbidity and preventing near-miss cases from progressing to mortality. Student nurses must develop sharp clinical observation skills during antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum care. Assessment begins with a thorough history-taking to uncover risk factors, followed by meticulous physical examinations including vital signs monitoring, fundal height measurement, assessment of uterine contractions, and evaluation of lochia or vaginal bleeding. Utilizing standardized tools such as the partograph during labor enables nurses to track cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, and maternal condition, facilitating the early detection of deviations from normal progress. When complications arise, rapid nursing interventions are vital: securing intravenous access with large-bore cannulas, administering prescribed emergency fluids, drawing blood for hemoglobin and cross-matching, monitoring strict fluid balance, and positioning the patient appropriately (e.g., lateral position for shock or cord prolapse). Furthermore, nurses must engage in effective interprofessional communication, escalating concerns promptly to medical officers and coordinating emergency referrals when higher-level care is required. Through vigilant monitoring, swift action, and compassionate patient advocacy, nursing students build the foundation for safe, high-quality maternal healthcare delivery in Nigeria.
