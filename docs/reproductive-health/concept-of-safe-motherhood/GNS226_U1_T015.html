Causes of Maternal Morbidity

Introduction to Maternal Morbidity

Maternal morbidity refers to any short- or long-term health condition that results from being pregnant and giving birth. While maternal mortality measures deaths, morbidity captures the heavy burden of physical and psychological illnesses that women survive. For every woman who dies from pregnancy complications, dozens more suffer severe, life-altering morbidities like chronic pain, uterine prolapse, infertility, or organ damage.

The Global Safe Motherhood Initiative was launched to reduce this burden by ensuring that every pregnant woman has access to high-quality care, skilled birth attendants, and emergency obstetric services. In clinical practice, understanding the causes of maternal morbidity helps nurses spot danger signs early, prevent permanent disability, and advocate for vulnerable mothers.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Hemorrhage and Hypertensive Disorders

Direct obstetric causes stem directly from obstetric complications of pregnancy, labor, or the postpartum period. Two of the biggest killers and disablers are severe bleeding and dangerously high blood pressure.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding following childbirth, most commonly caused by uterine atony, where the muscle fails to contract and clamp down on open blood vessels.
  • Pre-eclampsia and Eclampsia: Hypertensive disorders marked by high blood pressure and protein in the urine, which can progress to life-threatening seizures, stroke, or multi-organ failure if left untreated.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Sepsis and Obstructed Labor

Infection and mechanical birth blocks form the other major group of direct obstetric complications.

  • Puerperal Sepsis: A serious bacterial infection of the reproductive tract occurring anytime from the rupture of membranes or labor up to the 42nd day postpartum. Poor hygiene during delivery and prolonged rupture of membranes greatly increase this risk.
  • Obstructed Labor: Occurs when the baby cannot exit the pelvis during childbirth due to being physically blocked, often caused by cephalopelvic disproportion, malpresentation, or a narrow pelvis. If prolonged, it can cause severe tissue necrosis leading to obstetric fistula.

Indirect Obstetric Causes: Pre-existing Medical Conditions

Indirect obstetric causes are medical conditions that were either present before pregnancy or developed during it, and were made worse by the physiological demands of carrying a child. Pregnancy places a massive load on the heart, kidneys, and lungs.

  • Anemia: Very common in resource-limited settings due to poor nutrition and malaria, reducing the blood's ability to carry oxygen and leaving women vulnerable to heart failure or fatal bleeding.
  • Cardiac Disease: Rheumatic heart disease or congenital heart conditions can decompensate under the increased blood volume of pregnancy.
  • Infectious Diseases: Malaria, HIV, and tuberculosis weaken the mother, increasing risks of miscarriage, premature birth, and maternal death.

Socioeconomic and Cultural Determinants

Medical complications do not happen in a vacuum. A complex web of social, economic, and cultural factors heavily influences whether a pregnant woman stays healthy or suffers severe morbidity.

  • Poverty: Limits a family's ability to pay for transport, prescribed drugs, and hospital bills.
  • Education: Low literacy rates correlate with poor understanding of pregnancy danger signs and delayed healthcare-seeking.
  • Cultural Practices: Reliance on traditional birth attendants for high-risk deliveries, dietary taboos that worsen maternal anemia, and gender inequality that leaves women without decision-making power over their own health.

Health System Challenges and Delays

The Three Delays Model helps explain why women suffer or die from preventable obstetric causes:

  1. First Delay: Deciding to seek care due to lack of money, low perception of risk, or cultural beliefs.
  2. Second Delay: Reaching a medical facility due to poor roads, lack of transport, and long distances.
  3. Third Delay: Receiving adequate, timely treatment at the health facility due to staff shortages, stock-outs of blood or drugs, and poor referral systems.

Healthcare systems must address all three delays to truly lower maternal morbidity rates.

Nursing Assessment and Early Identification

Nurses act as the frontline defense against maternal morbidity through vigilant monitoring and prompt action. Regular antenatal assessments must check for rising blood pressure, urine protein, pallor indicating anemia, and abnormal fetal positioning.

During labor, partograph use is mandatory to track cervical dilation and fetal heart rate so obstructed labor or fetal distress is caught early. Nurses must also educate pregnant women and their families to recognize emergency danger signs—such as severe headache, blurred vision, vaginal bleeding, and fever—and seek immediate help without waiting.
