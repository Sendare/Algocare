Emergency Obstetric Care

Introduction to Emergency Obstetric Care

Emergency Obstetric Care (EmOC) encompasses a set of life-saving interventions designed to manage complications arising during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. In regions with high maternal mortality ratios, such as Nigeria, timely access to these services remains a core strategy for reducing preventable maternal and neonatal deaths. Most maternal complications cannot be reliably predicted during routine antenatal visits, making readiness for rapid identification and management essential at all healthcare tiers. The framework distinguishes between basic and comprehensive care configurations to ensure resources target the most critical interventions efficiently.

Levels of Emergency Obstetric Care Facilities

Healthcare delivery systems categorize EmOC facilities into two distinct tiers based on the specific clinical interventions they are equipped to perform:

  • Basic Emergency Obstetric Care (BEmOC): Facilities capable of performing seven specific signal functions, including administration of parenteral antibiotics, uterotonics, and anticonvulsants, manual removal of the placenta, removal of retained products, assisted vaginal delivery, and neonatal resuscitation.
  • Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric Care (CEmOC): Facilities that provide all BEmOC functions plus two additional services: life-saving major surgery (caesarean delivery) and blood transfusion services.

Major Direct Obstetric Complications

Direct obstetric complications result directly from obstetric interventions, omissions, incorrect treatment, or from a chain of events resulting from any of the above during pregnancy, labor, and the puerperium. Recognizing the clinical presentation of these conditions guides immediate life-saving nursing actions.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Blood loss exceeding 500 mL following vaginal delivery or 1,000 mL following caesarean section, frequently caused by uterine atony.
  • Pre-eclampsia and Eclampsia: Pregnancy-induced hypertension accompanied by significant proteinuria, which may progress to generalized convulsions.
  • Sepsis: Systemic infection arising from the genital tract during pregnancy, labor, or the postpartum period.
  • Obstructed Labor: Failure of the fetus to descend through the birth canal due to mechanical blockage, risking uterine rupture.
  • Complications of Abortion: Severe hemorrhage or sepsis following spontaneous or induced pregnancy termination.

Signal Functions of Emergency Obstetric Care

Signal functions serve as direct indicators of a facility's capacity to manage obstetric emergencies. Monitoring the availability of these nine functions helps healthcare planners evaluate the quality and reach of maternal health networks.

  1. Administer parenteral antibiotics
  2. Administer uterotonic drugs (oxytocin, misoprostol)
  3. Administer parenteral anticonvulsants (magnesium sulfate)
  4. Perform manual removal of the placenta
  5. Perform removal of retained products of conception
  6. Perform assisted vaginal delivery (vacuum extraction or forceps)
  7. Perform neonatal resuscitation with a bag and mask
  8. Perform blood transfusion
  9. Perform surgery (caesarean section)

Initial Nursing Assessment and Triage

Rapid evaluation upon arrival in the labor ward or emergency unit determines the clinical trajectory of a compromised mother. Triage requires immediate assessment of airway, breathing, circulation, and vital sign stability. Nurses must quickly identify red flags such as altered mental status, severe dyspnea, systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg, or active, heavy vaginal bleeding. Concurrent history taking focuses on gestational age, rupture of membranes, and previous obstetric complications. Quick categorization ensures that patients facing imminent mortality bypass administrative delays and receive immediate resuscitation.

Immediate Nursing Management in Obstetric Emergencies

Once an emergency is identified, execution of prompt nursing interventions stabilizes the patient before definitive medical or surgical treatment. For hemorrhage, large-bore intravenous access is secured, crystalloid fluids are infused, and oxytocic medications are administered as ordered. In eclamptic seizures, maintaining a patent airway, placing the patient in a lateral recovery position, and administering magnesium sulfate take precedence. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, fluid balance charting, and clear documentation allow the healthcare team to evaluate treatment efficacy in real-time.

Inter-Professional Collaboration and Emergency Referral

Managing severe obstetric crises requires seamless coordination between midwives, nurses, general practitioners, obstetricians, anesthetists, and laboratory personnel. When a primary facility lacks comprehensive capabilities, initiating an emergency referral becomes a life-saving decision. The referral process involves stabilizing the patient, communicating directly with the receiving facility, organizing transport with a skilled attendant, and compiling an accurate written summary of interventions already administered.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Complication Prevention

Following the stabilization of an acute obstetric emergency, nursing care shifts toward vigilant monitoring to detect secondary complications such as secondary hemorrhage, acute kidney injury, or thromboembolism. Vital sign tracking continues at frequent intervals, and wound sites or perineal repairs are inspected regularly for signs of infection or hematoma formation. Psychological support remains vital, as mothers who experience near-miss events frequently suffer from acute stress or trauma. Preparing the mother and family for discharge involves structured education regarding danger signs and postpartum self-care.
