Emergency Obstetric Care

Introduction to Emergency Obstetric Care

Emergency Obstetric Care, commonly called EmOC, represents a package of life-saving medical services designed to treat pregnancy and childbirth complications that threaten the life of the mother and her newborn. Most maternal deaths occur suddenly during labor, delivery, or the first twenty-four hours postpartum, often without clear prior warning. While routine antenatal care helps screen for risk factors, unpredictable emergencies like severe bleeding or sudden eclamptic seizures can strike any woman, regardless of her health history. Therefore, accessible and rapid emergency care remains the single most impactful strategy to reduce maternal mortality rates globally, particularly in resource-constrained regions.

Levels of Emergency Obstetric Care Facilities

Healthcare delivery systems organize emergency obstetric services into two primary tiers to ensure proper resource allocation and timely patient transfer. Basic Emergency Obstetric Care, known as BEmOC, handles non-surgical emergencies at primary health centers. Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric Care, or CEmOC, provides advanced surgical and blood transfusion services at larger hospitals.

  • Basic Emergency Obstetric Care (BEmOC): Managed primarily by midwives and general clinicians, offering injectable uterotonics, antibiotics, anticonvulsants, manual placenta removal, and newborn resuscitation.
  • Comprehensive Emergency Obstetric Care (CEmOC): Includes everything in BEmOC plus major surgical interventions like cesarean sections and safe blood banking and transfusion services.

Major Direct Obstetric Complications

Direct obstetric complications arise directly from the obstetric process of pregnancy, labor, and the puerperium. Recognizing these conditions early is vital for initiating correct life-saving protocols.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding following childbirth, usually defined as blood loss over 500 mL after vaginal delivery or 1000 mL after cesarean birth.
  • Pre-eclampsia and Eclampsia: Dangerous pregnancy-induced hypertension accompanied by end-organ damage, which can progress to grand mal seizures in eclampsia.
  • Obstruction of Labor: Failure of the fetus to descend through the birth canal due to mechanical blockage or inadequate uterine contractions.
  • Sepsis: Severe systemic infection originating from the genital tract during labor or the postpartum period.
  • Unsafe Abortion: Complications arising from the termination of pregnancy by unskilled providers or in unhygienic environments.

Signal Functions of Emergency Obstetric Care

Signal functions are specific medical interventions used to evaluate the functionality and readiness of health facilities to provide emergency obstetric care. Health planners track these core actions to assess whether a facility truly functions as a BEmOC or CEmOC center.

There are seven signal functions for BEmOC facilities, and two additional functions for CEmOC facilities, bringing the total to nine. The basic functions include administering parenteral antibiotics, oxytocic drugs, and anticonvulsants, manually removing the placenta, removing retained products, performing assisted vaginal delivery, and resuscitating newborns with a bag and mask. CEmOC facilities add surgery and blood transfusions. If a facility lacks even one of these designated functions, it cannot be classified as fully operational for emergency care.

Initial Nursing Assessment and Triage

Rapid triage in the labor ward separates routine patients from those requiring immediate resuscitation. Nurses must use a systematic ABCDE approach or specialized obstetric triage tools to identify life threats within seconds of arrival. Assess the airway, check breathing effort, measure blood pressure and pulse rate, and evaluate the level of consciousness. A common clinical mistake is getting distracted by paperwork or asking detailed obstetric histories before stabilizing an unstable patient. Always prioritize stabilization of vital signs over administrative admission processes.

Immediate Nursing Management in Obstetric Emergencies

Once an emergency is identified, prompt nursing actions can prevent rapid deterioration. For postpartum hemorrhage, call for help, massage the uterus, empty the bladder, and start large-bore intravenous lines for fluid and oxytocin administration. For eclamptic seizures, maintain a patent airway on the side, protect the patient from injury, and administer magnesium sulfate as ordered. Monitor urine output closely using an indwelling catheter, as renal function reflects overall hemodynamic stability. Clear, concise communication with the multidisciplinary team ensures rapid delivery of blood products and surgical instruments.

Inter-Professional Collaboration and Emergency Referral

No single nurse or midwife can manage complex obstetric emergencies alone; seamless teamwork involving midwives, doctors, laboratory scientists, and anesthetists is essential. When a complication exceeds the capacity of a primary clinic, organized emergency referral becomes necessary. The referring nurse must stabilize the patient as much as possible, package her safely with intravenous lines in place, send accompanying medical notes, and arrange rapid transport. Communication with the receiving facility ahead of time ensures that the operating theater and blood bank are prepared before the patient arrives.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Complication Prevention

After surviving an acute obstetric crisis, the patient enters a vulnerable recovery phase requiring vigilant nursing oversight. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, lochia, and urine output helps detect secondary complications like acute kidney injury or secondary hemorrhage. Psychological support is equally important, as traumatic births often leave mothers with anxiety or post-traumatic stress. Nurses must provide compassionate debriefing, explain what happened during the emergency, and encourage maternal-infant bonding as soon as the clinical condition allows.
