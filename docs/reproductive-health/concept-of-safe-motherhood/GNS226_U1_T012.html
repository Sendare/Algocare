Maternal Mortality

Defining Maternal Mortality

Maternal mortality refers to the death of a woman while pregnant or within forty-two days of termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and site of the pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes. Understanding this exact timeline is vital for accurate health record keeping. Direct obstetric deaths result from obstetric complications of the pregnancy state, interventions, omissions, or incorrect treatment. Indirect obstetric deaths result from previously existing disease or disease that developed during pregnancy and was not due to direct obstetric causes, but was aggravated by the physiological effects of pregnancy. A common misconception is that deaths occurring months after delivery due to pregnancy complications count as maternal mortality, but the strict forty-two-day limit applies for international standard reporting.

Epidemiology and the Nigerian Context

Sub-Saharan Africa carries the heaviest burden of maternal deaths globally, with Nigeria alone accounting for a disproportionately high percentage of these tragedies. Most of these deaths occur due to preventable medical conditions compounded by fragile health systems. The maternal mortality ratio measures the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births, serving as a harsh indicator of a nation's overall healthcare quality and women's social status. Rural areas experience significantly higher mortality rates compared to urban centers due to geographic isolation and absolute poverty.

Major Direct and Indirect Causes

  • Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding following childbirth, standing as the leading cause of maternal death globally.
  • Pregnancy-induced hypertension (Pre-eclampsia and Eclampsia): Dangerous spikes in blood pressure leading to seizures, stroke, or organ failure.
  • Sepsis: Severe systemic infections arising from the genital tract during or after labor or abortion.
  • Unsafe abortion: Terminations performed by unskilled persons or in environments lacking minimal medical standards.
  • Indirect causes: Pre-existing medical conditions such as malaria, anemia, cardiac disease, and HIV/AIDS that are worsened by the physiological demands of pregnancy.

The Three Delays Model

The Three Delays framework helps nurses and public health workers understand why women die during childbirth by breaking down the timeline of seeking and receiving care:

  1. First Delay: Delay in deciding to seek care. This is influenced by cultural beliefs, lack of education, financial constraints, and failure to recognize danger signs.
  2. Second Delay: Delay in reaching a medical facility. Poor road networks, lack of transportation, and great physical distances trap women at home.
  3. Third Delay: Delay in receiving adequate treatment. Substandard care at the health facility, stock-outs of essential drugs, lack of blood for transfusion, and untrained personnel contribute here.

Socio-Cultural and Systemic Determinants

Cultural practices, gender inequality, and poverty heavily influence maternal survival. In many communities, women lack decision-making power regarding their own health, needing permission and financial support from male heads of household to visit a clinic. Poverty restricts access to proper nutrition, resulting in high baseline rates of iron-deficiency anemia that make women vulnerable to fatal bleeding. Furthermore, systemic failures such as poorly funded health infrastructure, lack of skilled birth attendants, and erratic electricity supply undermine emergency obstetric care.

Assessment, Monitoring, and High-Risk Identification

Early booking for antenatal care (ANC) allows nurses to screen for risk factors and establish a baseline for maternal and fetal health. Nurses must vigilantly monitor vital signs during labor, using tools like the partograph to track cervical dilation and fetal heart rate, which helps detect obstructed labor early. Recognizing danger signs such as severe headache, blurred vision, vaginal bleeding, and fever can save a life before complications become irreversible.

Nursing Interventions and Safe Motherhood

Nurses play a frontline role in executing the pillars of Safe Motherhood, which include family planning, antenatal care, clean and safe delivery, and essential obstetric care. Intrapartum care involves active management of the third stage of labor with uterotonic drugs like oxytocin to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. Postnatal visits in the first week are equally crucial to catch delayed hemorrhage, infection, or postpartum depression. Patient education empowers women and their families to recognize emergency signs and seek help promptly.

Strategies for Reduction and Policy Implications

Reducing maternal mortality requires multi-sectoral policy changes, including free maternal health services, upgrading rural health clinics, and training community health extension workers. Strengthening referral systems with reliable transportation networks closes the gap during the second delay. Governments must prioritize health budgeting to ensure hospitals have uninterrupted electricity, safe blood supplies, and essential surgical kits. Ultimately, improving female education and empowering women socially remain the most sustainable long-term solutions to saving mothers' lives.
