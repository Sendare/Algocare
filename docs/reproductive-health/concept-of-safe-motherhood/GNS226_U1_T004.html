Strategies of Safe Motherhood

Introduction to Safe Motherhood and Maternal Health in Nigeria

Safe motherhood refers to the strategies, practices, and policies designed to ensure that women experience healthy pregnancies, safe deliveries, and positive postnatal outcomes. In Nigeria, maternal mortality remains a pressing public health concern, driven by a complex web of clinical, economic, and sociocultural challenges. Many pregnant women face severe risks due to delayed decision-making, transportation bottlenecks, and shortages of skilled personnel in rural health facilities. Addressing these systemic gaps requires a multi-pronged approach that goes beyond medical treatment to encompass community engagement, gender equity, and health system strengthening. Nurses serve as the frontline defenders in this fight, providing direct care, health education, and advocacy for vulnerable mothers.

Core Pillars and Objectives of Safe Motherhood Initiatives

The global safe motherhood framework rests on four foundational pillars designed to catch complications before they become life-threatening. These core components work together to build a continuous safety net for the mother and her baby from conception through the early weeks of life.

  • Family Planning: Empowers couples to choose when to conceive, spacing out pregnancies to protect maternal health.
  • Antenatal Care: Provides regular health checks, screening for high-risk conditions, and preventive treatments during pregnancy.
  • Obstetric Care: Ensures skilled attendance during labor and delivery, alongside emergency services for unexpected complications.
  • Postnatal Care: Monitors the mother and newborn during the critical weeks following childbirth to catch early infections or bleeding.

Antenatal Care (ANC) and Routine Maternal Assessment

Antenatal care acts as the early warning system of maternal health. Through scheduled clinic visits, nurses monitor fetal growth, track maternal blood pressure, and screen for infections such as malaria, syphilis, and HIV. Routine iron and folic acid supplementation prevents nutritional anemia, while tetanus toxoid vaccinations protect both mother and newborn from fatal tetanus infections.

A common misconception is that ANC is only necessary when a pregnant woman feels sick; nurses must educate communities that routine visits are preventive. By detecting conditions like gestational hypertension or asymptomatic bacteriuria early, nurses can initiate prompt interventions before severe complications develop.

Skilled Birth Attendance and Intrapartum Care Strategies

The intrapartum period—labor and delivery—carries the highest risk of sudden, life-threatening complications. Skilled birth attendance means that a trained health professional, such as a midwife, nurse, or doctor, is present to manage normal deliveries and recognize the earliest signs of trouble. During this phase, nurses utilize tools like the partograph to track cervical dilation, uterine contractions, and fetal heart rate over time.

Prompt identification of prolonged labor or abnormal fetal heart patterns allows for timely interventions or referrals. Encouraging a clean delivery environment and practicing active management of the third stage of labor significantly reduces the incidence of postpartum hemorrhage, which remains a leading killer of Nigerian mothers.

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC)

Even with optimal antenatal care, unpredictable emergencies happen during labor and delivery. Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care services are categorized into Basic (BEmONC) and Comprehensive (CEmONC) facilities. BEmONC facilities provide parenteral antibiotics, uterotonic drugs, anticonvulsants, manual removal of the placenta, and assisted vaginal delivery. CEmONC facilities add blood transfusion services and the capacity to perform emergency cesarean sections.

When a facility lacks these life-saving capacities, rapid and coordinated referral systems become essential. Nurses working in primary health centers must recognize their facility limits and initiate transfers to secondary or tertiary hospitals without hesitation.

Postnatal Care and the Prevention of Postpartum Complications

The postnatal period begins immediately after the placenta is delivered and extends for six weeks. This window is hazardous because a large proportion of maternal deaths occur within the first forty-eight hours post-delivery due to hemorrhage, sepsis, or eclampsia. Routine postnatal checks involve assessing uterine involution, checking lochia color and volume, monitoring vital signs, and promoting early initiation of breastfeeding.

Nurses also educate new mothers on danger signs such as foul-smelling vaginal discharge, heavy bleeding, severe headaches, and fever. Newborn care runs parallel during this time, focusing on thermal regulation, cord care, and screening for jaundice or feeding difficulties.

Family Planning and Interconception Care

Family planning is not just a population control measure; it is a core life-saving strategy for women and children. Giving a mother's body time to heal between pregnancies reduces the incidence of low birth weight, premature delivery, and maternal depletion syndrome. Interconception care bridges the gap between pregnancies, focusing on optimizing maternal health, nutrition, and chronic disease management before the next conception occurs.

Nurses must provide non-judgmental, comprehensive counseling on various contraceptive methods, allowing clients to make informed choices that fit their lifestyle, health status, and cultural beliefs.

Community Mobilization and Overcoming Barriers to Maternal Healthcare

Medical interventions alone cannot eliminate preventable maternal deaths if cultural and economic barriers keep women away from hospitals. Community mobilization involves working with traditional rulers, religious leaders, and local women's groups to shift health-seeking behaviors and build local support systems, such as emergency transport funds.

Nurses must build trusting relationships with traditional birth attendants, refraining from harsh condemnation while encouraging them to refer high-risk women to formal health facilities. By addressing financial, geographical, and cultural obstacles collaboratively, communities become active partners in safeguarding maternal health.
