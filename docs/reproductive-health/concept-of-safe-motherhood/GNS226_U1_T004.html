Strategies of Safe Motherhood

Introduction to Safe Motherhood and Maternal Health in Nigeria

Safe motherhood encompasses the strategies, interventions, and policies designed to ensure women receive the necessary care throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period. In Nigeria, maternal mortality remains a major public health challenge, driven by delayed care-seeking, poverty, infrastructural deficits, and inadequate facility readiness. The primary goal is not merely survival, but the achievement of optimal physical, mental, and social well-being for both mother and child. Midwives and nurses occupy the front lines of this battle, translating national health policies into compassionate, bedside clinical action. Understanding the local epidemiological landscape allows healthcare providers to anticipate high-risk scenarios and advocate effectively for vulnerable populations.

Core Pillars and Objectives of Safe Motherhood Initiatives

The global framework for safe motherhood rests upon four foundational pillars that guide program planning and resource allocation. - **Family Planning**: Empowers couples to choose when to conceive, spacing pregnancies to reduce maternal depletion syndrome and high-risk births. - **Antenatal Care**: Detects and manages pre-existing medical conditions, screens for gestational complications, and prepares the mother for birth. - **Obstetric Care**: Ensures access to skilled attendants and well-equipped facilities capable of managing emergency complications during labor. - **Postnatal Care**: Monitors recovery, promotes exclusive breastfeeding, and prevents fatal postpartum infections or hemorrhage.

Antenatal Care (ANC) and Routine Maternal Assessment

Modern antenatal care guidelines mandate a minimum of eight contacts throughout gestation to optimize clinical outcomes and foster strong therapeutic relationships. Routine assessments include blood pressure monitoring to catch pre-eclampsia early, urinalysis for protein and glucose, and blood grouping with hemoglobin estimation. Supplementation with iron and folic acid prevents nutritional anemia, while intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy (IPTp) using sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine protects against malaria-related placental damage. Nurses must also use these visits to provide individualized birth preparedness and complication readiness counseling, ensuring the woman knows where, how, and with whom she will deliver.

Skilled Birth Attendance and Intrapartum Care Strategies

The presence of a skilled birth attendant—defined as an accredited health professional such as a midwife, doctor, or nurse—is the single most critical intervention for reducing maternal and neonatal mortality. Intrapartum care centers on the active management of the third stage of labor and the consistent plotting of the partograph. The partograph serves as an early warning system, tracking cervical dilation, descent of the head, and fetal heart rate to catch prolonged labor before uterine rupture occurs. Hygienic delivery practices, including the clean birth practices of clean hands, clean surface, clean cord blade, and clean tie, dramatically drop puerperal sepsis rates.

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC)

When unexpected obstetric complications strike, the availability of Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care determines survival. Facilities are categorized into Basic EmONC (BEmONC) and Comprehensive EmONC (CEmONC) based on the life-saving signal functions they perform. | Level | Signal Functions Offered | | :--- | :--- | | **BEmONC** | Administration of parenteral antibiotics, uterotonics, and anticonvulsants; manual removal of placenta; removal of retained products; assisted vaginal delivery; neonatal resuscitation. | | **CEmONC** | All BEmONC functions PLUS blood transfusion services and performance of caesarean sections. | Nurses working in primary health centers must stabilize emergency patients rapidly and execute timely referrals to CEmONC facilities before irreversible shock sets in.

Postnatal Care and the Prevention of Postpartum Complications

The postnatal period is fraught with danger, as most maternal deaths occur within the first 48 hours following delivery. Essential care schedules dictate contacts at 24 hours, 48 hours, 7 days, and 6 weeks postpartum. Nurses monitor lochial discharge, uterine involution, and perineal healing while screening for silent killers like postpartum hemorrhage, puerperal sepsis, and postpartum depression. Encouraging early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life provides infant nourishment and stimulates oxytocin release, which naturally contracts the myometrium and minimizes bleeding.

Family Planning and Interconception Care

Family planning forms an essential pillar of safe motherhood by preventing unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and closely spaced high-risk births. Interconception care bridges the gap between pregnancies, optimizing maternal health status before the next conception occurs. Nurses provide comprehensive counseling on modern contraceptive methods, ranging from barrier methods and hormonal pills to long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) like implants and intrauterine devices. Dispelling cultural myths and addressing partner resistance through community education remain vital nursing duties to ensure high contraceptive uptake.

Community Mobilization and Overcoming Barriers to Healthcare

Clinical interventions alone cannot eliminate high maternal mortality ratios without active community engagement. Cultural preferences, religious beliefs, financial constraints, and traditional birth attendant practices often delay care-seeking until conditions become catastrophic. Community mobilization involves partnering with local leaders, market associations, and male decision-makers to design culturally sensitive emergency transport schemes and health education programs. Nurses must act as cultural brokers, building bridges of trust between formal biomedical institutions and rural communities to dismantle the three delays that threaten maternal survival.
