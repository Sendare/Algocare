Strategies of Safe Motherhood

Introduction to Safe Motherhood and Maternal Health in Nigeria

Safe motherhood encompasses the strategies, initiatives, and healthcare interventions designed to ensure that all women receive the necessary care throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. In Nigeria, maternal health remains a critical public health concern, characterized by stubbornly high maternal mortality and morbidity ratios. Understanding the scope of safe motherhood is fundamental for nursing students because nurses form the backbone of clinical and community healthcare delivery across the country. Maternal mortality in Nigeria is driven by a complex interplay of clinical, socioeconomic, and cultural determinants, including poverty, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, gender inequality, and delays in seeking or reaching care. Many of these deaths are preventable through timely, evidence-based nursing interventions and proper resource utilization. As future nursing professionals, students must recognize that safe motherhood is not merely about preventing maternal death, but also about promoting the physical, mental, and social well-being of women and their newborns. Nurses in Nigeria play pivotal roles as advocates, educators, and direct care providers who must bridge the gap between vulnerable communities and formal health systems. By addressing the root causes of poor maternal outcomes and championing national safe motherhood protocols, student nurses prepare themselves to contribute effectively to national and global development goals, such as reducing maternal mortality and achieving universal health coverage within the Nigerian healthcare landscape.

Core Pillars and Objectives of Safe Motherhood Initiatives

The Safe Motherhood Initiative, launched globally by the World Health Organization and partner agencies, is built upon core pillars designed to systematically address the multifaceted vulnerabilities of pregnant women. In the Nigerian context, these core pillars include family planning, antenatal care (ANC), skilled attendance at birth, and essential obstetric care. Each pillar functions interdependently to create a continuous safety net for mother and child. The primary objectives of these initiatives are to drastically reduce maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, enhance the quality of life for women of reproductive age, and empower communities to actively participate in health promotion. Family planning prevents unintended pregnancies and high-risk births, while antenatal care allows for the early detection and management of pregnancy-related complications such as pre-eclampsia and gestational diabetes. Skilled attendance ensures that a competent healthcare professional is present at every delivery to manage normal births and swiftly identify deviations from the norm. Essential obstetric care guarantees that when emergencies arise, functional facilities are available to provide life-saving interventions like blood transfusions or caesarean sections. For nursing students, mastering these pillars means understanding that safe motherhood requires a multi-sectoral approach involving clinical excellence, health education, policy implementation, and community engagement to transform maternal health outcomes in Nigeria.

Antenatal Care (ANC) and Routine Maternal Assessment

Antenatal care (ANC) serves as the foundational entry point for pregnant women into the healthcare system, offering a crucial window of opportunity for health promotion, disease prevention, and risk stratification. Current World Health Organization guidelines, adopted across Nigeria, recommend a minimum of eight ANC contacts to ensure comprehensive monitoring and timely interventions. Routine maternal assessment during these visits involves thorough history taking, physical examinations, and targeted laboratory investigations. Nurses must check vital signs, monitor weight gain, assess for pallor indicating anemia, and screen for hypertensive disorders of pregnancy through regular blood pressure monitoring. Laboratory screening typically includes tests for blood group and genotype, hemoglobin levels, HIV serology, syphilis, and urinalysis for protein and glucose. In addition to clinical assessments, ANC provides an ideal platform for administering essential prophylactic interventions, such as intermittent preventive treatment for malaria in pregnancy (IPTp), tetanus toxoid immunization, and iron-folic acid supplementation. Health education is another vital component, covering nutrition, birth preparedness, complication readiness, and recognizing danger signs like vaginal bleeding, severe headaches, or reduced fetal movements. For nursing students, developing proficiency in ANC assessment ensures that potential complications are intercepted long before labor begins, safeguarding both maternal and fetal health.

Skilled Birth Attendance and Intrapartum Care Strategies

Intrapartum care focuses on the management of labor and delivery, a period fraught with high risk for both mother and newborn. Skilled Birth Attendance (SBA) is defined as care provided to a woman during labor, childbirth, and the immediate postpartum period by an accredited health professional—such as a midwife, nurse, or doctor—who has been educated and trained to proficiency in the skills needed to manage normal pregnancies, deliveries, and the initial diagnosis and management of complications. During intrapartum care, nurses utilize the partograph, a graphic representation of labor progress, maternal condition, and fetal well-being. The partograph is an indispensable tool that helps nurses detect prolonged labor, cephalopelvic disproportion, and fetal distress early, thereby preventing obstructed labor and uterine rupture, which are major contributors to maternal mortality and obstetric fistula in Nigeria. Intrapartum strategies also emphasize the active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) to prevent postpartum hemorrhage, which involves the administration of a uterotonic drug (such as oxytocin) immediately after delivery of the baby, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage. Student nurses must master these intrapartum competencies, alongside respectful maternity care principles, to ensure safe, dignified, and clinically sound childbirth experiences.

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC)

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC) is a crucial framework designed to reduce maternal and neonatal mortality by ensuring that life-saving interventions are available, accessible, and utilized when complications arise. Because many obstetric complications are sudden and unpredictable—such as severe postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, sepsis, obstructed labor, and severe newborn asphyxia—the availability of functioning EmONC facilities is non-negotiable. EmONC is categorized into Basic EmONC (BEmONC) and Comprehensive EmONC (CEmONC). BEmONC facilities provide seven signal functions: administration of parenteral antibiotics, uterotonic drugs, and anticonvulsants; manual removal of the placenta; removal of retained products; assisted vaginal delivery; and neonatal resuscitation. CEmONC facilities provide all BEmONC functions plus two additional signal functions: blood transfusion services and surgery (caesarean sections). For Nigerian nursing students, understanding EmONC entails recognizing referral pathways, identifying obstetric emergencies rapidly, and participating in stabilization and resuscitation protocols. Nurses working in rural or primary health centers must be adept at recognizing when a patient exceeds primary capabilities and executing a prompt, well-coordinated emergency referral to a secondary or tertiary CEmONC center, thereby bridging the gap between community-level care and specialized intervention.

Postnatal Care and the Prevention of Postpartum Complications

The postnatal period, particularly the first 42 days following childbirth, is a critical yet often neglected phase of maternal and newborn care. A significant proportion of maternal and neonatal deaths occur during this window, primarily due to complications such as postpartum hemorrhage, postpartum sepsis, thromboembolism, and hypertensive crises. Postnatal care (PNC) strategies dictate that mother and newborn receive regular assessments starting immediately after birth—specifically within the first 24 hours, followed by visits on day 3, between days 7-14, and at six weeks postpartum. During PNC assessments, nurses monitor uterine involution, lochial discharge, vital signs, perineal or abdominal wound healing, and emotional well-being to screen for postpartum blues or depression. Newborn assessments focus on vital signs, cord care, thermal regulation, and feeding support, promoting exclusive breastfeeding as a cornerstone of neonatal health. Education on danger signs is vital; mothers and families must be instructed on when to seek immediate medical help, such as in the case of foul-smelling lochia, heavy vaginal bleeding, fever, or breathing difficulties in the newborn. Student nurses must champion comprehensive PNC to catch secondary complications early and provide holistic support during the physiological and psychological transitions of early motherhood.

Family Planning and Interconception Care

Family planning and interconception care are vital pillars of safe motherhood that directly impact maternal and child survival by optimizing birth intervals and preventing high-risk pregnancies. In Nigeria, short birth intervals are common and contribute significantly to maternal depletion syndrome, premature births, low birth weight, and increased infant mortality. Family planning empowers women and couples to voluntarily decide the number, spacing, and timing of their children through the provision of modern contraceptive methods, ranging from barrier methods and hormonal contraceptives to intrauterine devices and long-acting reversible contraception (LARC). Interconception care extends this by focusing on the health and wellness of women between pregnancies, addressing chronic medical conditions, nutritional deficiencies, and psychological health before a subsequent conception occurs. Nurses have a central role in providing non-judgmental counseling, dispelling cultural myths surrounding contraception, and tailoring family planning advice to individual client needs and lifestyle factors. For nursing students, understanding family planning requires recognizing it not only as a population control measure, but primarily as a life-saving human right and a core clinical strategy for reducing maternal and child mortality.

Community Mobilization and Overcoming Barriers to Maternal Healthcare

Achieving safe motherhood in Nigeria requires more than clinical interventions within hospital walls; it demands active community mobilization and the systematic dismantling of barriers to maternal healthcare. Many maternal deaths occur because pregnant women face profound physical, financial, and sociocultural obstacles that delay care-seeking and transport. Financial barriers, such as out-of-pocket payment systems, and geographical barriers, such as poor road networks and rural isolation, prevent timely access to EmONC facilities. Sociocultural barriers include traditional beliefs, gender norms that restrict women's decision-making autonomy, and reliance on traditional birth attendants (TBAs) for complicated deliveries. Community mobilization strategies involve engaging local leaders, religious figures, men, and women's groups to raise awareness, establish community emergency transport schemes, and foster supportive environments for institutional delivery. Nurses play a crucial role as community health educators and advocates who must collaborate with community stakeholders, respect cultural contexts while challenging harmful practices, and advocate for pro-poor health policies. For nursing students, mastering this topic ensures they can design and implement effective public health campaigns that bridge the gap between healthcare institutions and the grassroots populations they serve.
