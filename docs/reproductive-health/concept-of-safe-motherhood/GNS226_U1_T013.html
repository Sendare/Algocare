Maternal Morbidity

Introduction to Maternal Morbidity and Safe Motherhood

Maternal morbidity refers to any physical or psychological condition resulting from or aggravated by pregnancy and childbirth that has a negative impact on a woman's well-being. Unlike maternal mortality, which marks the tragic end of a life, morbidity captures the vast spectrum of severe illness, chronic disability, and near-death experiences endured by women. The concept of safe motherhood was launched globally to ensure that all women receive the necessary care during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. In resource-constrained settings like Nigeria, the burden of maternal morbidity is disproportionately high, often leaving survivors with permanent organ damage, social stigma, and profound economic hardship.

Severe Acute Maternal Morbidity (SAMM) and Maternal Near Miss

A maternal near miss is defined as a woman who nearly died but survived a complication that occurred during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy. Studying near misses is invaluable because survivors share clinical characteristics with maternal deaths, offering a larger pool of data for healthcare quality improvement.

  • Severe Acute Maternal Morbidity (SAMM): Encompasses life-threatening conditions including organ dysfunction or failure.
  • Near Miss Criteria: Relies on clinical, laboratory, and management-based markers, such as the use of blood products, dialysis, or admission to an intensive care unit.
  • Clinical Utility: Analyzing near-miss cases helps midwives and obstetricians identify systemic delays in care before a fatality occurs.

Major Direct Causes of Maternal Morbidity in Nigeria

Direct obstetric causes stem from interventions, omissions, incorrect treatment, or a chain of events arising from the pregnancy itself. In Nigeria, these factors account for the vast majority of severe maternal morbidity.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding following delivery, often caused by uterine atony or retained placental tissue.
  • Pre-eclampsia and Eclampsia: Hypertensive disorders characterized by high blood pressure, proteinuria, and potential progression to fatal seizures.
  • Obstetric Fistula: Prolonged, obstructed labor that creates an abnormal opening between the bladder and vagina, resulting in continuous urinary leakage.
  • Puerperal Sepsis: Severe systemic infection of the reproductive tract occurring anytime between the rupture of membranes and the 42nd day postpartum.
  • Unsafe Abortion: Complications arising from clandestine pregnancy terminations performed by unskilled providers or in unhygienic environments.

Indirect Causes and Contributing Socioeconomic Factors

Indirect obstetric morbidity results from previous existing disease or a disease that developed during pregnancy and was aggravated by the physiological effects of gestation. Conditions such as severe malaria, anemia, cardiac disease, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection significantly compound pregnancy risks.

Beyond clinical pathology, socioeconomic determinants dictate maternal outcomes. Poverty limits access to nutritious food, exacerbating baseline anemia. Cultural norms and gender inequality often strip women of decision-making power regarding their own health, delaying care-seeking. Furthermore, infrastructural deficits—such as poor roads, lack of electricity in primary health centers, and out-of-pocket payment systems—create monumental barriers that transform manageable obstetric emergencies into near-fatal crises.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of High-Risk Conditions

Early recognition of deterioration is the cornerstone of preventing severe maternal morbidity. Midwives must perform meticulous baseline assessments during antenatal booking and maintain vigilance during intrapartum and postpartum periods.

Vital signs serve as the earliest warning system. A rising pulse rate combined with falling blood pressure often signals occult hemorrhage before massive blood loss is visibly apparent. Monitoring for hyperreflexia, severe frontal headaches, and visual disturbances can catch pre-eclampsia before eclamptic seizures strike. Midwives must utilize risk-scoring tools, review previous obstetric histories for retained placenta or cesarean sections, and listen closely to subjective patient complaints rather than relying solely on numbers.

Nursing Management and Emergency Interventions

When a maternal emergency strikes, immediate, protocol-driven nursing intervention is lifesaving. The midwife acts as the first responder, coordinating resuscitation while calling for multidisciplinary medical backup.

  • Hemorrhage Management: Initiate bimanual uterine compression, administer oxytocics as ordered, establish large-bore intravenous access, and prepare for rapid fluid resuscitation or blood transfusion.
  • Eclamptic Crisis Care: Ensure a patent airway, place the patient in the left lateral position to prevent aspiration, administer magnesium sulfate according to protocol, and protect the patient from physical trauma during convulsions.
  • Shock Management: Elevate the patient's legs, administer high-flow oxygen via a face mask, and continuously monitor urine output via an indwelling urinary catheter to evaluate renal perfusion.

Long-Term Physical and Psychological Complications

Survival does not equal complete health. Many women who endure severe maternal morbidity carry physical and emotional scars for the rest of their lives. Chronic pelvic pain, infertility, dyspareunia, and incontinence resulting from untreated fistulas or perineal trauma destroy marital relationships and social standing.

Psychologically, near-miss survivors experience high rates of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), severe postpartum depression, and anxiety. Mothers may struggle to bond with their newborns due to the trauma surrounding the birth experience. Holistic nursing care must extend beyond the acute hospital stay, incorporating psychological screening, community support group linkages, and long-term pelvic floor rehabilitation.

Prevention Strategies and the Role of the Midwife

Preventing maternal morbidity requires a shift from reactive emergency treatment to proactive, community-based primary care. Midwives occupy the central position in executing these preventative strategies.

  • Antenatal Care (ANC): Encouraging early and regular booking visits to screen for risks, distribute iron-folate supplements, and provide intermittent preventive treatment for malaria.
  • Skilled Birth Attendance: Promoting hospital deliveries managed by trained professionals to ensure timely interventions when complications arise.
  • Family Planning: Providing comprehensive contraceptive counseling to space births adequately, reducing the physiological strain on maternal bodies.
  • Community Advocacy: Educating community leaders and families to recognize danger signs and eliminate cultural barriers that delay emergency referrals.
