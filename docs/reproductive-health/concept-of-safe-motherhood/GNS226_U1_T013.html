Maternal Morbidity

Introduction to Maternal Morbidity and Safe Motherhood

Maternal morbidity refers to any short- or long-term health condition attributed to or aggravated by pregnancy and childbirth, having a negative impact on the woman's well-being. Unlike maternal mortality, which counts women who die, morbidity looks at the heavy burden of illness and disability that many women survive. Safe motherhood initiatives aim to ensure that all women receive the necessary care during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. A key shift in modern nursing practice is focusing not just on keeping the mother alive, but on preventing severe complications that ruin her quality of life long after delivery. In Nigeria, the high rate of maternal ill-health calls for nurses who can spot early warning signs before a simple pregnancy issue turns into a life-threatening crisis.

Severe Acute Maternal Morbidity and Maternal Near Miss

A maternal near miss is a woman who nearly died but survived a complication that occurred during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy. She survives by sheer luck or because she received timely, expert care in a well-equipped health facility. The World Health Organization defines these cases using specific organ dysfunction markers, such as cardiovascular, renal, or neurological failure. Studying near-miss cases is often more useful than studying maternal deaths because the patient is alive to share her experience, and the clinical records are fresh. Nurses must recognize that a near-miss patient is in a fragile state and requires intensive monitoring to prevent sudden relapse.

Major Direct Causes of Maternal Morbidity in Nigeria

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding after delivery, often due to uterine atony, remaining placenta pieces, or tears in the birth canal.
  • Hypertensive Disorders: Conditions like pre-eclampsia and eclampsia that cause dangerously high blood pressure, protein in the urine, and seizures.
  • Pregnancy-Related Infections: Severe sepsis originating from the uterus, surgical wounds after a caesarean section, or untreated urinary tract infections.
  • Unsafe Abortion: Complications arising from illegal or poorly performed pregnancy terminations, leading to severe bleeding and pelvic sepsis.
  • Obstructed Labor: A labor process that stalls completely because the baby cannot fit through the pelvis, risking uterine rupture.

Indirect Causes and Contributing Socioeconomic Factors

Indirect causes of maternal morbidity are pre-existing medical conditions that are worsened by the normal physiological changes of pregnancy. Common examples include severe anemia, malaria, heart disease, and HIV/AIDS. When a pregnant woman's body is already weakened by chronic illness, it struggles to handle the extra load of carrying a baby.

Beyond physical diseases, socioeconomic factors heavily shape maternal health outcomes in Nigeria. Poverty limits a woman's ability to pay for transport to health facilities, buy prescribed drugs, or afford nutritious food. Cultural norms may restrict a woman from seeking care without her husband's permission. Ignorance and poor education delay recognition of danger signs, leading many families to patronize traditional birth attendants before coming to the hospital when it is already too late.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of High-Risk Conditions

Spotting high-risk conditions early is a core duty during antenatal visits and labor admissions. Nurses must master thorough history-taking, checking for previous caesarean sections, multiple pregnancies, or a history of high blood pressure. Routine physical checks include measuring blood pressure, testing urine for protein, checking hemoglobin levels, and monitoring fetal heart tones.

A common clinical mistake is ignoring mild complaints from pregnant women, such as persistent headache, blurred vision, or slight swelling of the face and hands, dismissing them as normal pregnancy discomforts. In reality, these are hallmark warning signs of pre-eclampsia. Regular use of the partograph during labor helps nurses detect prolonged labor early, giving them a tool to intervene before exhaustion and uterine rupture occur.

Nursing Management and Emergency Interventions

When a maternal emergency strikes, fast and organized nursing action saves lives. The immediate priority is stabilizing the ABCs: airway, breathing, and circulation. For a patient experiencing severe bleeding, nurses must quickly set up large-bore intravenous lines, run prescribed replacement fluids and blood products, and massage the uterus. In cases of eclamptic seizures, keeping the airway clear, administering magnesium sulfate, and protecting the patient from physical injury are vital steps. Nurses also coordinate multidisciplinary teams, calling doctors, laboratory scientists, and anesthetists into action. Clear communication during handovers prevents dangerous gaps in patient monitoring.

Long-Term Physical and Psychological Complications

Surviving a severe pregnancy complication does not mean the woman is completely healthy. Many women live with chronic conditions long after discharge. Obstetric fistula, an abnormal opening between the vagina and bladder or rectum resulting from prolonged obstructed labor, causes continuous leaking of urine or stool, leading to severe social isolation. Other long-term physical issues include chronic pelvic pain, infertility, and uterine prolapse.

Psychological trauma is equally devastating. Women who survive near-miss events or lose their babies often suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), severe anxiety, and postpartum depression. They may experience flashbacks, nightmares, and fear of future pregnancies. Nurses must look beyond physical healing, offering empathetic counseling and mental health screening during postnatal visits.

Prevention Strategies and the Role of the Midwife

Preventing maternal morbidity requires proactive community and clinical efforts centered around primary healthcare. Midwives and nurses play a frontline role by providing focused antenatal care, promoting birth preparedness, and encouraging hospital delivery with skilled birth attendants. Educating women on family planning helps space pregnancies, giving the mother's body time to recover and reducing the risks linked with closely spaced births.

Community outreach is also essential. Nurses must work with local leaders to bust harmful myths, advocate against child marriage, and set up reliable transport systems for emergencies. By building trust between communities and health facilities, midwives bridge the gap and ensure that mothers receive safe, dignified care at every stage of their reproductive lives.
