Maternal Morbidity

Introduction to Maternal Morbidity and the Concept of Safe Motherhood

Maternal morbidity refers to any physical or psychological condition that results from or is aggravated by pregnancy and childbirth, having a negative impact on a woman's well-being. Unlike maternal mortality, which represents the tragic loss of a mother's life, maternal morbidity encompasses the spectrum of severe acute conditions, chronic illnesses, and disabilities that women endure during the antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum periods. In the Nigerian context, the burden of maternal morbidity remains disproportionately high, reflecting significant challenges within the healthcare delivery system. The concept of safe motherhood is a global initiative aimed at ensuring all women receive the necessary care for pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period, thereby promoting health and preventing complications. For nursing and midwifery students in Nigeria, understanding maternal morbidity is fundamental to reducing preventable complications. It requires an appreciation of how timely interventions can avert permanent disability or death. Safe motherhood strategies emphasize universal access to quality reproductive health services, skilled attendance at birth, and community education to recognize danger signs early. Nurses and midwives serve as the frontline defenders of safe motherhood, bridging the gap between communities and formal healthcare facilities. By fostering an environment of proactive care, early detection, and compassionate support, nursing professionals can significantly improve maternal health outcomes across Nigeria and contribute to achieving sustainable development goals related to maternal and child health.

Classification: Severe Acute Maternal Morbidity (SAMM) and Maternal Near Miss

In maternal health, Severe Acute Maternal Morbidity (SAMM) and the concept of 'maternal near miss' are vital indicators used to measure the quality of obstetric care. A maternal near miss is defined as a woman who nearly died but survived a complication that occurred during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy. Evaluating near-miss cases provides healthcare institutions with crucial data because survivors of severe complications often share similar clinical profiles with maternal mortality cases, offering a larger pool of information for analysis. SAMM encompasses life-threatening conditions where organ dysfunction occurs, requiring intensive monitoring and immediate medical or surgical intervention. Examples include severe postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, sepsis, and uterine rupture leading to multi-organ failure. In Nigerian clinical settings, utilizing the WHO near-miss criteria helps nurse-midwives and obstetricians audit clinical practices, identify delays in healthcare delivery, and upgrade emergency obstetric care (EmOC) services. Understanding these classifications allows nursing students to recognize the subtle clinical markers that indicate a patient is transitioning from a normal recovery to a life-threatening state. Recognizing organ dysfunction early—such as oliguria, coagulopathy, or altered mental status—enables prompt triage and escalation of care. Ultimately, studying SAMM and near-miss events shifts the focus from solely counting maternal deaths to learning from women who survived severe complications, thereby refining institutional protocols to save future lives.

Major Direct Causes of Maternal Morbidity in Nigeria

The major direct causes of maternal morbidity and mortality in Nigeria are well-documented and remain largely preventable with timely, high-quality clinical interventions. These direct causes stem directly from obstetric complications of the pregnancy, delivery, or postpartum periods. The leading culprits include postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (such as pre-eclampsia and eclampsia), pregnancy-related infections (sepsis), obstructed or prolonged labor, and complications from unsafe abortions. Postpartum hemorrhage, often due to uterine atony, can rapidly progress to hypovolemic shock and acute renal failure if blood loss is not aggressively managed. Hypertensive disorders frequently result in seizures, stroke, or HELLP syndrome, placing both mother and fetus in immediate jeopardy. Puerperal sepsis frequently arises from unsterile delivery practices, prolonged rupture of membranes, or untreated urinary tract infections. Obstructed labor, common among young girls with undeveloped pelves or women lacking access to skilled care, can lead to uterine rupture, vesicovaginal fistulae (VVF), and severe pelvic tissue necrosis. Unsafe abortions contribute heavily to morbidity through severe sepsis, hemorrhage, and chemical toxicity. For nursing and midwifery students in Nigeria, mastering the pathophysiology, early clinical signs, and emergency management protocols for these direct causes is non-negotiable. Midwives must be adept at estimating blood loss, monitoring blood pressure closely, identifying signs of infection, and managing the active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) to mitigate these risks.

Indirect Causes and Contributing Socioeconomic Factors

While direct obstetric causes trigger immediate crises, indirect causes and socioeconomic factors lay the groundwork that increases a woman's vulnerability to maternal morbidity in Nigeria. Indirect causes refer to pre-existing medical conditions that are aggravated by the physiological demands of pregnancy. Common examples include malaria, anemia, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS), cardiovascular diseases, and diabetes. Severe anemia, frequently exacerbated by malaria and nutritional deficiencies, severely compromises a woman's ability to tolerate blood loss during delivery, turning even minor postpartum bleeding into a life-threatening emergency. Beyond medical comorbidities, socioeconomic factors play a profound role. Poverty, lack of formal education, rural geographical isolation, cultural preferences for traditional healers, and gender inequality heavily influence maternal health outcomes. The 'three delays' model—delay in deciding to seek care, delay in reaching a health facility, and delay in receiving adequate treatment—captures how systemic bottlenecks and financial constraints trap Nigerian women in vulnerable situations. Poor road networks and lack of functional emergency transport systems compound these delays. As future nurse-midwives, students must look beyond the hospital bedside to understand these broader social determinants of health. Providing holistic care involves patient advocacy, health education tailored to local cultural contexts, and connecting vulnerable mothers with social support systems to overcome barriers to healthcare utilization.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of High-Risk Conditions

Clinical assessment and the early identification of high-risk conditions are core competencies for every nurse and midwife working in maternal and child health. Routine antenatal care (ANC) serves as the primary screening platform where midwives evaluate pregnant women to detect risk factors before they escalate into emergencies. A thorough clinical assessment begins with a comprehensive history taking, noting past medical and obstetric history, parity, age, and previous complications like cesarean sections or hemorrhage. Physical examination includes baseline vital sign recording, fundal height measurement, assessment of fetal well-being, and screening for edema, pallor, or jaundice. Laboratory investigations are equally critical; screening for hemoglobin levels, blood grouping and cross-matching, urinalysis for proteinuria, and serological tests for HIV, syphilis, and malaria help map out a patient's risk profile. Nurses must be vigilant in recognizing warning signs, such as persistent headaches, blurred vision, sudden facial or hand edema, vaginal bleeding at any gestational age, decreased fetal movements, or foul-smelling lochia. Utilizing validated risk-scoring tools during ANC enables stratification of patients, ensuring that high-risk mothers are referred promptly to secondary or tertiary facilities equipped with comprehensive emergency obstetric care. In labor and delivery, continuous monitoring using the partograph is mandatory to detect prolonged labor patterns early. Effective clinical assessment bridges the gap between early detection and life-saving intervention, requiring nurses to possess sharp observational skills and sound clinical judgment.

Nursing Management and Emergency Interventions

When severe maternal morbidity occurs, rapid, decisive nursing management and emergency interventions are required to stabilize the patient and prevent mortality. Nurse-midwives must be well-versed in executing emergency protocols, often framed around the ABCDE approach (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure) adapted for obstetric emergencies. In cases of severe postpartum hemorrhage, immediate interventions include calling for multidisciplinary backup, performing bimanual uterine compression, administering uterotonic drugs (such as oxytocin, misoprostol, or ergometrine), establishing large-bore intravenous access for rapid fluid resuscitation, and preparing for blood transfusion. For a patient experiencing eclamptic seizures, nursing care prioritizes maintaining a patent airway, administering magnesium sulfate according to strict clinical guidelines, monitoring deep tendon reflexes and respiratory rate to prevent toxicity, and minimizing sensory stimulation. In scenarios of suspected sepsis, obtaining blood cultures, administering broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics promptly, and monitoring fluid balance via an indwelling urinary catheter are vital nursing actions. Effective communication during emergencies using structured tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) ensures seamless teamwork among doctors, nurses, and laboratory personnel. Continuous documentation of vital signs, fluid intake and output, and response to interventions allows the healthcare team to evaluate the efficacy of treatment and make rapid adjustments to care plans.

Long-Term Physical and Psychological Complications

Surviving severe acute maternal morbidity does not always mean a return to full health; many women face profound long-term physical and psychological complications. Physical sequelae can be devastating, including chronic pelvic pain, infertility, urinary or fecal incontinence resulting from obstetric fistulae, chronic renal failure following severe pre-eclampsia or hypovolemic shock, and neurological deficits from eclamptic strokes or hypoxia. Conditions like Sheehan's syndrome—hypopituitarism caused by severe postpartum hemorrhage and ischemic necrosis of the pituitary gland—can cause chronic fatigue, lactation failure, and secondary hypothyroidism. On the psychological front, surviving a near-miss event or losing a child frequently triggers post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), severe postpartum depression, anxiety, and social stigmatization. In many Nigerian communities, women suffering from chronic complications such as fistulae face ostracization, marital breakdown, and severe economic hardship. Nurses and midwives have a critical role to play in identifying these long-term morbidities during postnatal visits and follow-up clinics. Comprehensive care must extend beyond the maternity ward, incorporating empathetic psychological support, referral for specialized surgical repair (such as fistula repair camps), physical rehabilitation, and community reintegration counseling. Recognizing that physical healing must be paired with emotional recovery ensures that holistic, patient-centered nursing is delivered to every maternal survivor.

Prevention Strategies and the Role of the Midwife in Safe Motherhood

Prevention is the cornerstone of reducing maternal morbidity, and midwives occupy a pivotal position in driving safe motherhood initiatives across Nigeria. Preventive strategies operate at multiple levels: primary prevention focuses on promoting overall health, family planning education to prevent high-risk and unwanted pregnancies, adolescent reproductive health programs, and nutritional support. Secondary prevention involves high-quality antenatal care, early screening, prompt identification of complications, and the availability of emergency obstetric care (EmOC). Tertiary prevention encompasses prompt, effective management of acute crises and robust rehabilitation services to prevent long-term disability. The role of the midwife extends from clinical duties in labor rooms to community mobilization and health education. Midwives must educate pregnant women and their families on recognizing pregnancy danger signs, encourage facility-based deliveries attended by skilled personnel, and dispel harmful cultural myths surrounding childbirth. Furthermore, advocating for health system strengthening—such as ensuring a reliable supply of essential drugs like oxytocin and magnesium sulfate, functional blood banks, and efficient referral transport—is essential. By embodying the principles of safe motherhood, practicing evidence-based care, and serving as compassionate educators and advocates, nursing and midwifery students in Nigeria can lead the transformation toward a future where no woman suffers or dies giving life.
