Principles of Safe Motherhood

Introduction and Definition of Safe Motherhood

Safe motherhood is not merely the absence of disease or infirmity during childbearing years, but a dynamic state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being surrounding pregnancy, childbirth, and the puerperium. Launched globally in 1987, the Safe Motherhood Initiative aims to reduce maternal mortality and morbidity, ensuring that every pregnant woman receives the care she needs to survive and thrive. In low-resource settings, particularly across sub-Saharan Africa, maternal mortality remains disproportionately high due to delays in seeking care, reaching health facilities, and receiving adequate treatment upon arrival. Understanding these systemic barriers is foundational for any nursing student practicing in Nigeria, where preventable complications like postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, and obstructed labor continue to threaten family structures and community health.

Core Pillars and Principles of Safe Motherhood

The framework of safe motherhood rests on four primary pillars designed to intercept complications at every stage of the reproductive cycle.

  • Family Planning: Empowers couples to space pregnancies, preventing high-risk and unintended conceptions.
  • Antenatal Care (ANC): Provides early detection, risk stratification, and management of pre-existing or pregnancy-induced conditions.
  • Obstetric Care: Ensures access to skilled attendance during labor and delivery, alongside emergency management for sudden life-threatening complications.
  • Postnatal Care (PNC): Monitors the critical transition period immediately following birth for both mother and neonate.

Underpinning these pillars are core principles of human rights, equity, gender equality, and community participation. Healthcare services must be accessible, culturally acceptable, and free from discrimination, ensuring that socioeconomic status does not dictate survival outcomes during childbirth.

Antenatal Care and Maternal Health Promotion

Antenatal care serves as the primary health contact point for pregnant women, offering an ideal window for health promotion, disease prevention, and risk screening. Current WHO guidelines recommend a minimum of eight contact points to reduce perinatal mortality and provide a positive pregnancy experience. Routine antenatal assessments include measuring blood pressure to screen for gestational hypertension, testing urine for protein and glucose, checking hemoglobin levels for anemia, and monitoring fetal growth and heart tones. Additionally, nurses administer intermittent preventive treatment for malaria in pregnancy (IPTp), tetanus toxoid vaccinations, and routine micronutrient supplementation such as iron and folic acid. Health education sessions during visits focus on nutrition, recognizing danger signs like vaginal bleeding or severe headaches, and creating an individualized birth preparedness and complication readiness plan.

Essential Intrapartum Care and Skilled Attendance

Intrapartum care focuses on managing labor safely, supporting physiological processes, and intervening swiftly when deviations occur. A skilled birth attendant—defined as an accredited health professional such as a midwife, doctor, or nurse—must be present at every birth to monitor labor progress using the partograph, maintain clean delivery practices, and manage normal third-stage labor actively. Active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) involves the administration of a uterotonic drug immediately after fetal delivery, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage, which collectively reduce the incidence of postpartum hemorrhage. Midwives must remain vigilant for obstructed labor, fetal distress, and perineal trauma, ensuring timely referral when complications exceed the capacity of primary health clinics.

Postnatal Care for Mother and Newborn

The postnatal period, or puerperium, is a high-risk phase where the majority of maternal and neonatal deaths occur. Comprehensive postnatal care (PNC) requires scheduled assessments starting within the first 24 hours after birth, followed by additional visits on days three, seven to fourteen, and at six weeks. Nursing assessments for the mother focus on uterine involution, lochial discharge, perineal healing, vital signs, and emotional well-being to detect postpartum depression. Newborn care emphasizes thermal protection through skin-to-skin contact, promoting exclusive breastfeeding, cord hygiene, and screening for neonatal jaundice or signs of infection. Neglecting this phase leaves both mother and infant vulnerable to unmanaged complications arising from the immediate postpartum transition.

Family Planning and Unplanned Pregnancy Prevention

Preventing unintended pregnancies through accessible family planning services is a cornerstone strategy for reducing maternal mortality and unsafe abortions. Providing a diverse method mix—ranging from barrier methods and hormonal contraceptives to long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) and permanent sterilization—allows clients to make informed reproductive choices. Nurses play a crucial role in providing non-judgmental counseling, addressing myths and misconceptions regarding infertility and side effects, and supporting informed consent. In many Nigerian communities, addressing partner involvement and cultural barriers is equally important to ensure women can access and utilize modern contraceptive commodities without social stigma.

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC)

Despite optimal antenatal care, unpredictable obstetric emergencies can arise suddenly, requiring rapid access to Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC) facilities. Facilities are categorized as either Basic EmONC (BEmONC) or Comprehensive EmONC (CEmONC) based on the life-saving signal functions they perform. BEmONC centers handle administration of parenteral uterotonics, anticonvulsants, and antibiotics, manual removal of the placenta, retained products removal, assisted vaginal delivery, and neonatal resuscitation. CEmONC facilities provide all basic functions plus blood transfusion services and surgical interventions like caesarean sections. Ensuring functional referral networks between primary health clinics and higher-tier CEmONC hospitals is vital to prevent preventable maternal deaths during critical emergencies.

The Role of the Nurse and Midwife in Safe Motherhood

Nurses and midwives serve as the frontline guardians of maternal and child health, bridging the gap between communities and formal healthcare systems. Their responsibilities span clinical care, health education, advocacy, and health system management. As educators, they equip women and families with knowledge on nutrition, birth planning, and danger signs. As clinicians, they perform skilled deliveries, manage minor deviations, and initiate emergency protocols without hesitation. Furthermore, nurses advocate for vulnerable women, challenge harmful cultural practices respectfully, and work collaboratively within interdisciplinary teams to ensure compassionate, evidence-based care across every stage of the childbearing continuum.
