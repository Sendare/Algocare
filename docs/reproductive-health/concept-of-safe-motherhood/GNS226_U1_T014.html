Causes of Maternal Mortality

Introduction to Maternal Mortality and Safe Motherhood

Maternal mortality remains a critical global health indicator, disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries. The World Health Organization defines maternal death as the death of a woman while pregnant or within forty-two days of termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and site of the pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes. Safe motherhood initiatives aim to ensure that all women receive the necessary care throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. In Nigeria, the maternal mortality ratio remains unacceptably high, driven by systemic challenges, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, and delayed presentation to medical facilities. Understanding these foundational definitions allows student nurses to contextualize clinical statistics into human experiences, recognizing that most maternal deaths are entirely preventable with timely, high-quality interventions.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Hemorrhage and Hypertension

- **Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH)**: Defined as blood loss exceeding 500 mL following vaginal birth or 1000 mL following cesarean section. Uterine atony remains the leading trigger, requiring prompt uterine massage and uterotonic administration. - **Pre-eclampsia and Eclampsia**: Hypertensive disorders unique to pregnancy characterized by new-onset hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation. Uncontrolled spikes can lead to cerebral hemorrhage, HELLP syndrome, and maternal collapse. Both conditions act as surgical emergencies. Midwives and nurses must monitor vital signs meticulously, maintaining high vigilance during the immediate postpartum hours when hemorrhage can occur silently.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Sepsis and Complications of Abortion

Infection following childbirth or unsafe termination procedures constitutes a major pillar of direct maternal mortality. Puerperal sepsis arises when pathogenic bacteria invade the genital tract post-delivery, often fueled by prolonged rupture of membranes, multiple digital vaginal examinations, or unhygienic birth environments. Unsafe abortion remains a severe public health crisis in regions with restrictive legal frameworks or poor access to safe services. Incomplete evacuations, uterine perforations, and toxic chemical instillations result in catastrophic pelvic infections and septic shock. Nurses encountering these patients must initiate broad-spectrum antibiotics swiftly, monitor fluid balance rigorously, and prepare for surgical intervention such as curettage or exploratory laparotomy.

Indirect Obstetric Causes: Pre-existing Medical Conditions

Indirect causes represent medical conditions that were either pre-existing or developed during pregnancy and were aggravated by the physiological adaptations of gestation. Cardiac disease places immense strain on a pregnant woman because cardiac output increases by up to 50 percent during pregnancy. Women with uncorrected congenital heart lesions or rheumatic heart disease face severe risk of pulmonary edema and heart failure. Other notable indirect contributors include severe anemia, malaria, diabetes mellitus, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection. Malaria in pregnancy causes severe maternal anemia and placental parasitemia, leading to low birth weight and maternal exhaustion. Comprehensive antenatal care must therefore integrate screening and management for these chronic and endemic diseases to protect vulnerable mothers.

The Three Delays Model in Maternal Mortality

The Three Delays Model conceptualizes why women die from obstetric emergencies by breaking down the timeline of seeking and receiving care into distinct phases: 1. **First Delay**: Delay in deciding to seek care due to lack of financial resources, poor problem recognition, or traditional cultural beliefs. 2. **Second Delay**: Delay in reaching a medical facility caused by poor road infrastructure, long distances, and lack of transportation. 3. **Third Delay**: Delay in receiving adequate care at the health facility resulting from staff shortages, stock-outs of essential medicines, or professional incompetence. For nurses, recognizing this model emphasizes that clinical competence inside the ward is insufficient if structural barriers prevent the patient from arriving alive or receiving prompt treatment.

Socio-Cultural, Economic, and Environmental Determinants

Beyond biological mechanisms, maternal mortality is deeply rooted in social determinants of health. Gender inequality often restricts a woman's autonomy, preventing her from making independent decisions regarding her reproductive health or household finances. Poverty restricts access to nutritious foods, routine antenatal check-ups, and user fees charged at healthcare facilities. Cultural practices, such as reliance on traditional birth attendants for complicated deliveries or preference for home births without skilled attendance, significantly contribute to poor outcomes. Environmental factors, including rural isolation, lack of electricity in primary health centers, and erratic water supply, cripple the delivery of safe obstetric care. Addressing these determinants requires multi-sectoral collaboration involving community leaders, policymakers, and public health advocates.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of High-Risk Mothers

Effective risk identification begins at the very first antenatal booking visit through a comprehensive nursing history and physical examination. Nurses must screen for previous obstetric complications, parity extremes, chronic illnesses, and anatomical abnormalities. Routine tracking of weight gain, blood pressure, and urine protein is mandatory at every visit to catch pre-eclampsia early. Clinical intuition combined with objective assessment tools helps spot subtle signs of deterioration. A rising pulse rate or falling blood pressure can be the earliest indicators of internal hemorrhage before overt shock sets in. Nurses must educate clients on red-flag symptoms—such as vaginal bleeding, severe headaches, blurred vision, and reduced fetal movements—ensuring women know when to bypass traditional channels and return immediately to the health facility.

Preventive Strategies and Nursing Interventions in Nigeria

Combating maternal mortality in Nigeria requires a robust framework of preventive strategies anchored in primary healthcare delivery. Active management of the third stage of labor using oxytocins, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage drastically reduces postpartum hemorrhage rates. Routine distribution of insecticide-treated nets and intermittent preventive treatment protects pregnant women against malaria. Community-based midwifery programs and task-shifting initiatives help bridge workforce gaps in rural clinics. Furthermore, nurses play a pivotal role in promoting family planning education, which prevents high-risk pregnancies, unintended high parities, and unsafe abortions. By combining vigilant clinical care with community outreach, nurses stand at the forefront of saving maternal lives across Nigeria.
