Causes of Maternal Mortality

Introduction to Maternal Mortality and Safe Motherhood

Maternal mortality remains a critical public health challenge globally, with a disproportionately high burden resting in low-income regions, particularly sub-Saharan Africa, including Nigeria. Defined by the World Health Organization as the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy, maternal mortality is an indicator of healthcare system quality and equity. Safe motherhood initiatives are foundational frameworks designed to ensure that all women receive the necessary care, support, and medical interventions throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period. For student nurses, understanding the multi-faceted nature of maternal mortality is essential because nurses often serve as the frontline defenders in maternal health. By championing safe motherhood practices, student nurses learn to appreciate that the vast majority of maternal deaths are entirely preventable with timely access to quality antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care. The overarching goal of safe motherhood is to eliminate preventable maternal deaths and severe morbidities, addressing not just the biological causes of death, but also the systemic failures that endanger women. Nurses must recognize that every maternal death is a tragedy that impacts families, communities, and national development, making proactive education, advocacy, and skilled attendance at birth non-negotiable standards of modern nursing practice.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Hemorrhage and Hypertension

Direct obstetric causes are those resulting from obstetric complications of the pregnancy state, labor, and puerperium, as well as from interventions, omissions, incorrect treatment, or a chain of events resulting from any of the above. Among these, postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, notably pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, stand out as leading killers of mothers globally and particularly in Nigeria. Postpartum hemorrhage, defined as excessive bleeding following childbirth, can quickly lead to hypovolemic shock, multi-organ failure, and death if uterine atony, retained placenta, or lacerations are not managed aggressively. Nurses must be highly skilled in estimating blood loss, performing fundal massages, administering uterotonic drugs like oxytocin, and monitoring vital signs. Similarly, hypertensive disorders pose immense danger through sudden spikes in blood pressure, leading to cerebral hemorrhage, HELLP syndrome, or fatal convulsions. Antenatal detection of proteinuria and blood pressure abnormalities, coupled with the administration of magnesium sulfate for severe pre-eclampsia, are critical nursing and medical interventions. Student nurses must master the pathophysiology and emergency protocols for both hemorrhage and hypertension, as prompt recognition and rapid intervention are the difference between life and death for vulnerable parturients.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Sepsis and Complications of Abortion

Puerperal sepsis and complications arising from unsafe abortions constitute major pillars of direct maternal mortality, especially in resource-constrained settings where legal restrictions and poor sanitation prevail. Puerperal sepsis refers to an infection of the genital tract occurring at any time between the rupture of membranes or labor and the 42nd day postpartum, characterized by fever, lower abdominal pain, and foul-smelling lochia. Poor aseptic techniques during delivery, prolonged rupture of membranes, and frequent vaginal examinations in unhygienic environments significantly heighten the risk of severe bacterial invasion, leading to septic shock and death. On the other hand, complications of unsafe abortion occur when pregnancy termination is performed either by persons lacking the necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards. Women facing unwanted pregnancies often resort to clandestine procedures involving toxic substances, unsterile instruments, or physical trauma, resulting in severe uterine perforation, peritonitis, massive hemorrhage, and systemic infection. Student nurses must approach these conditions with high clinical suspicion, strict infection control measures, empathetic non-judgmental attitudes, and prompt administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics and supportive resuscitative care.

Indirect Obstetric Causes: Pre-existing and Co-existing Medical Conditions

Indirect obstetric causes of maternal mortality are those resulting from previous existing disease or disease that developed during pregnancy and which was not due to direct obstetric causes, but which was aggravated by the physiological effects of pregnancy. As maternal age rises and non-communicable diseases become more prevalent, conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, chronic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, severe anemia, chronic renal disease, and infectious illnesses like HIV/AIDS, malaria, and tuberculosis significantly threaten pregnant women. The hemodynamic burden of pregnancy—characterized by increased blood volume, elevated cardiac output, and altered metabolic demands—places immense strain on compromised organ systems. For instance, a woman with underlying rheumatic heart disease may experience life-threatening heart failure during the second or third trimester due to the normal hypervolemic state of pregnancy. Similarly, severe malaria can induce profound maternal anemia, placental parasitemia, and spontaneous abortion or stillbirth. Nursing care for indirect causes requires meticulous multidisciplinary collaboration, comprehensive health histories, regular monitoring of systemic organ functions, and patient education on how chronic conditions interact with the pregnant state. Student nurses must understand that optimizing a woman's health before and during pregnancy is vital to preventing these indirect tragedies.

The Three Delays Model in Maternal Mortality

The Three Delays Model is a crucial conceptual framework used to understand why maternal mortality remains high in developing countries like Nigeria. The model breaks down delays in receiving adequate emergency obstetric care into three distinct phases. The First Delay is the delay in deciding to seek care, which is heavily influenced by factors such as lack of recognition of danger signs, low socioeconomic status, gender inequality, and cultural beliefs or reliance on traditional healers. The Second Delay involves the delay in reaching a medical facility, driven by poor transport infrastructure, long geographic distances, difficult terrain, and prohibitive transportation costs. The Third Delay is the delay in receiving adequate and appropriate care once the patient arrives at a health facility, caused by staff shortages, lack of essential supplies, stock-outs of blood products, power outages, and poor staff attitude or clinical incompetence. Student nurses must analyze this model deeply to appreciate that saving a mother's life involves more than clinical skill inside a hospital ward; it requires community mobilization, infrastructural advocacy, health system strengthening, and efficient triage protocols to bridge these fatal gaps.

Socio-Cultural, Economic, and Environmental Determinants

Maternal mortality is not solely a medical phenomenon; it is deeply rooted in socio-cultural, economic, and environmental determinants that dictate a woman's health status and access to resources. In many Nigerian communities, deep-seated cultural norms grant men primary decision-making power regarding family finances and healthcare utilization, meaning a pregnant woman may require spousal permission or financial backing before seeking emergency care. Low educational attainment among women correlates strongly with reduced health literacy, poor understanding of pregnancy danger signs, and a higher likelihood of utilizing unskilled birth attendants. Economic poverty restricts access to nutritious foods, leading to widespread micronutrient deficiencies and chronic anemia, while also creating insurmountable barriers to paying out-of-pocket medical expenses, transportation fees, and purchasing prescribed drugs. Environmental determinants, such as poor housing, lack of clean potable water, and inadequate sanitation facilities, predispose pregnant women to water-borne illnesses and severe infections. Furthermore, harmful traditional practices, such as female genital mutilation or dietary restrictions during pregnancy, add layers of biological and psychological hazard. Student nurses must cultivate cultural humility and community engagement skills to address these determinants effectively through targeted health education and advocacy.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of High-Risk Mothers

Nursing assessment and the systematic identification of high-risk mothers are cornerstone activities designed to intercept maternal mortality before emergencies strike. During antenatal care (ANC) visits, nurses must perform thorough and holistic assessments, including comprehensive health histories covering past obstetric performance, medical history, family history, and socio-economic challenges. Regular physical examinations must focus on tracking weight gain, serial blood pressure measurements to detect early pre-eclampsia, urinalysis for protein and glucose, and routine screening for anemia, malaria, and HIV. Identifying high-risk factors—such as grand multiparity, previous Cesarean sections, adolescent or advanced maternal age, multiple gestations, or chronic comorbidities—allows nurses to stratify risk and ensure appropriate referral pathways to specialist obstetric care. Effective nursing assessment also involves active listening and emotional support to detect psychological distress or domestic violence, which can adversely affect pregnancy outcomes. Student nurses must learn that accurate charting, vigilant vital sign monitoring, and timely escalation of abnormal findings are vital competencies that empower the multidisciplinary team to intervene proactively, transforming routine ANC into a life-saving screening mechanism.

Preventive Strategies and Nursing Interventions in Nigeria

Implementing robust preventive strategies and targeted nursing interventions is essential for drastically reducing maternal mortality in Nigeria. Preventive care begins at the community level through health education campaigns promoting birth preparedness, complication readiness, family planning, and the utilization of skilled birth attendants (SBAs). Nurses must advocate for and actively participate in the scale-up of active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) to prevent postpartum hemorrhage, which includes the timely administration of uterotonics, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage. Furthermore, ensuring the availability and correct use of emergency obstetric and newborn care (EmONC) supplies, promoting prompt referral systems, and providing empathetic, respectful maternity care are vital professional responsibilities. Student nurses must also champion task-shifting policies where appropriate, engage community leaders to dismantle harmful cultural norms, and provide comprehensive postnatal care to detect late-onset complications like postpartum sepsis or postpartum depression. Through clinical vigilance, compassionate care, and active public health advocacy, Nigerian nursing students play a transformative role in securing the health and survival of mothers across the nation.
