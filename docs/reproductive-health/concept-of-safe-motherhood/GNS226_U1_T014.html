Causes of Maternal Mortality

Understanding Maternal Mortality and Safe Motherhood

Maternal mortality remains a critical public health challenge, particularly in developing nations like Nigeria. The maternal mortality ratio measures the number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births due to any cause related to or aggravated by pregnancy or its management. Safe motherhood initiatives aim to ensure that all women receive the necessary care throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period to prevent these tragic outcomes. Most maternal deaths are entirely preventable with timely access to quality healthcare services.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Hemorrhage and Hypertension

Direct obstetric causes stem from direct complications of the pregnant state, interventions, omissions, or incorrect treatment.

  • Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding following childbirth, often caused by uterine atony, retained placental tissue, or genital tract trauma. It can kill a healthy woman in less than two hours if untreated.
  • Hypertensive disorders: Conditions like preeclampsia and eclampsia involve dangerously high blood pressure during pregnancy, which can lead to stroke, organ failure, and seizures.

Direct Obstetric Causes: Sepsis and Unsafe Abortion

Infection and unsafe termination procedures are major contributors to maternal loss.

  • Puerperal sepsis: An infection of the genital tract occurring anytime between the rupture of membranes or labor and the 42nd day postpartum. Bacteria ascend from the lower reproductive tract, often linked to prolonged rupture of membranes or unsterile vaginal examinations.
  • Unsafe abortion: The termination of a pregnancy by persons lacking the necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards. It frequently leads to severe hemorrhage, pelvic infection, and uterine perforation.

Indirect Obstetric Causes: Co-existing Medical Conditions

Indirect obstetric deaths result from previous existing disease or a disease that developed during pregnancy and was not due to direct obstetric causes, but was aggravated by the physiological effects of pregnancy. Common conditions include malaria, anemia, heart disease, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). For example, pregnancy increases cardiovascular demand, which can push a compromised heart into failure. Severe anemia reduces the body's tolerance to blood loss, making even a small hemorrhage fatal.

The Three Delays Model

The Three Delays model helps explain why maternal deaths happen by breaking down the time-sensitive barriers to receiving emergency care:

  1. First Delay: Delay in deciding to seek care. This is influenced by cultural beliefs, lack of problem recognition, and financial constraints.
  2. Second Delay: Delay in reaching a medical facility. Poor road networks, lack of transport, and geographic distance trap patients far from help.
  3. Third Delay: Delay in receiving adequate care once at the facility. Shortages of blood, trained staff, medications, or equipment cause fatal holdups.

Socio-Cultural, Economic, and Environmental Factors

Broader societal issues heavily influence maternal survival rates. Poverty restricts access to nutritious food, quality healthcare, and transport. Gender inequality often leaves women with little power to make decisions regarding their own health, contraception, or when to seek medical attention. Harmful traditional practices, early marriage, and female genital mutilation also increase physical vulnerability during childbirth. Education plays a protective role; literate women are more likely to attend antenatal clinics and recognize obstetric danger signs early.

Nursing Assessment and High-Risk Identification

Nurses act as the first line of defense in maternal health through comprehensive assessments. During antenatal visits, nurses screen for high-risk factors such as previous cesarean sections, grand multiparity, teenage pregnancy, chronic hypertension, and severe anemia. Nurses must educate expectant mothers and their families on danger signs—including vaginal bleeding, severe headaches, blurred vision, fever, and decreased fetal movement—so they can report to the facility immediately.

Preventive Strategies and Nursing Interventions

Reducing maternal mortality requires multi-layered interventions. Nurses promote the use of family planning to space births and prevent unwanted pregnancies that might lead to unsafe abortions. In the clinical setting, active management of the third stage of labor using uterotonic drugs drastically cuts rates of postpartum hemorrhage. Community outreach programs led by nurses help bridge the gap between rural populations and formal health facilities, ensuring health education reaches even the most vulnerable women.
