High Risk Pregnancy in Labour

Understanding High-Risk Labour

A high-risk labour means the mother, the unborn child, or both face a significantly greater chance of illness or death during childbirth compared to a normal pregnancy. Nurses must quickly identify these cases because small problems can rapidly turn into life-threatening emergencies. High risk does not always mean a poor outcome, but it does demand closer observation, specialized skills, and immediate access to advanced medical care. In many resource-limited settings across Nigeria, delays in recognizing these risks and transferring patients to higher-level facilities remain major contributors to maternal and newborn mortality.

Underlying Risk Factors and Causes

Risk factors can be present before pregnancy begins or develop while the mother is carrying the child. A thorough nursing history uncovers these hidden dangers so the delivery plan can be adjusted accordingly.

  • Pre-existing medical disorders: Chronic conditions like hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, and sickle cell anemia severely strain maternal organs during the physical stress of labour.
  • Obstetric history: Previous cesarean sections, repeated miscarriages, uterine scars, or a history of postpartum hemorrhage elevate the chance of recurring complications.
  • Current pregnancy complications: Conditions that arise unexpectedly, such as gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, placenta previa, multiple gestations, or breech presentation.
  • Demographic and social factors: Extremes of maternal age (very young teenagers or women over thirty-five), poor nutritional status, and lack of antenatal care access.

Intrapartum Monitoring and Partograph Utilization

The partograph is a pictorial overview of labour that serves as a vital early warning system, especially in busy Nigerian maternity wards where staff shortages occur. It plots cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, contractions, and maternal vital signs on a single sheet of paper.

Two critical lines guide nursing decisions on the partograph. The alert line represents the slow-moving average of normal cervical dilation. When dilation crosses to the right of the alert line, it warns the nurse that labour is progressing slower than normal and demands heightened vigilance. The action line sits four hours to the right of the alert line. Crossing the action line means definitive medical intervention—such as oxytocin augmentation or preparation for cesarean delivery—must be taken immediately to prevent ruptured uterus or severe fetal distress.

Essential Nursing Management and Interventions

Managing a high-risk labour requires a calm, organized, and proactive approach. Nurses coordinate care between midwives, obstetricians, and pediatricians while providing direct bedside support.

Positioning plays a major role; placing a mother in the left lateral position relieves pressure on the inferior vena cava, improving blood flow to the placenta and boosting fetal oxygen levels. Hydration and electrolyte balance must be maintained through intravenous lines, particularly if labour is prolonged or oral intake is restricted. Pain management should be tailored to the specific risk condition, avoiding medications that might depress fetal respiration if delivery is imminent. Clear communication with the family helps reduce anxiety, fostering cooperation during stressful moments.

Emergency Obstetric Complications and Crisis Management

Sudden emergencies can strike during any high-risk labour, turning a stable delivery room into a chaotic scene within seconds. Uterine rupture, postpartum hemorrhage, umbilical cord prolapse, and severe eclamptic seizures demand immediate, rehearsed teamwork.

For instance, if a cord prolapses, the nurse must immediately insert a sterile gloved hand into the vagina to lift the presenting fetal part off the cord, preventing fatal asphyxiation, and maintain that position while rushing the patient to the operating theater. In cases of sudden postpartum hemorrhage, calling for help, massaging the fundus, and establishing large-bore intravenous access take absolute priority. Every second counts, and hesitation leads directly to preventable maternal death.

Postpartum Care and Prevention of Long-Term Sequelae

The danger does not end when the baby is born. The immediate postpartum period carries high risks of hemorrhage, infection, and blood clots, especially following a complicated labour. Nurses must closely monitor vital signs, uterine firmness, and lochia flow every fifteen minutes for the first hour, then at regular intervals.

Beyond physical recovery, addressing psychological trauma is essential. Women who experience terrifying emergency deliveries frequently suffer from anxiety or postpartum post-traumatic stress disorder. Providing compassionate debriefing, educating them on warning signs to watch for at home, and ensuring proper follow-up appointments safeguard both physical health and emotional well-being long after hospital discharge.
