High Risk Pregnancy in Labour

Introduction and Definition of High Risk Labour

A high-risk pregnancy in labour refers to any gestation where the mother, the fetus, or both are at an increased risk of morbidity or mortality during the intrapartum period compared to a normal, uncomplicated delivery. In the Nigerian context, where maternal and perinatal mortality rates remain a significant public health concern, identifying these pregnancies early in labour is a critical core competency for nursing students and midwifery practitioners. High-risk labour can stem from pre-existing maternal medical conditions, obstetric complications that arise suddenly, or fetal distress that threatens the wellbeing of the unborn child. Nurses play a frontline role in risk stratification upon admission to the labour ward. By conducting a thorough baseline assessment, reviewing the antenatal card for known risk factors, and maintaining high clinical vigilance, nurses can anticipate adverse outcomes and initiate prompt interdisciplinary interventions. Understanding this definition helps nursing students recognize that intrapartum care extends beyond routine monitoring; it requires dynamic evaluation, critical thinking, and a readiness to transition from normal care pathways to emergency obstetric protocols. Recognizing these risks early prevents prolonged obstructed labour, severe postpartum hemorrhage, and birth asphyxia, which are leading contributors to poor maternal and child health indices in sub-Saharan Africa.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Risk Factors

The pathophysiology of high-risk labour involves complex biological and mechanical disruptions that threaten the homeostasis of the mother-fetus unit. Underlying risk factors are broadly categorized into maternal medical disorders, pre-existing obstetric complications, and acute intrapartum events. Maternal conditions such as chronic hypertension, pre-eclampsia, and gestational diabetes alter vascular integrity and placental perfusion, often resulting in uteroplacental insufficiency. This insufficiency restricts the delivery of oxygen and essential nutrients to the fetus, precipitating intrauterine growth restriction, fetal distress, or hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Mechanical factors, including cephalopelvic disproportion, malpresentations like breech or transverse lies, and uterine scar rupture from previous Cesarean sections, disrupt the normal progression of labour. These mechanical barriers frequently lead to prolonged or obstructed labour, causing excessive uterine fatigue, uterine rupture, maternal exhaustion, and ascending genital tract infections. For nursing students, grasping these pathophysiological mechanisms is essential for connecting clinical signs—such as abnormal fetal heart rates, erratic uterine contraction patterns, and maternal tachycardia—to the root physiological failure. Recognizing how conditions like pre-eclampsia or obstructed labour compromise oxygenation allows nurses to anticipate complications, position patients appropriately, administer prescribed medications logically, and prepare for emergency operative deliveries swiftly and effectively.

Classification and Types of High Risk Conditions During Labour

High-risk conditions during labour manifest in various forms, requiring nurses to classify and prioritize care based on immediate physiological threats. Major categories include hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (such as severe pre-eclampsia and eclampsia), antepartum and intrapartum hemorrhage (placenta previa and placental abruption), malpresentations and malpositions, multiple gestations, and prolonged or obstructed labour. Hypertensive disorders pose severe risks of maternal stroke, HELLP syndrome, and eclamptic seizures, requiring strict blood pressure monitoring and magnesium sulfate administration. Hemorrhagic conditions threaten maternal circulatory volume, quickly leading to hypovolemic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation if blood loss is uncontained. Malpresentations like shoulder dystocia or transverse lie create surgical and emergency obstetric challenges that demand specialized maneuvers and immediate theater readiness. In resource-constrained settings across Nigeria, conditions like prolonged and obstructed labour are unfortunately prevalent, often culminating in uterine rupture, vesicovaginal fistulae, and severe sepsis. By understanding the distinct types of high-risk conditions, nursing students can tailor their assessments, anticipate specific medical and surgical orders, and communicate clinical deterioration effectively to the obstetric team, ensuring timely and lifesaving interventions for both mother and baby.

Maternal and Fetal Assessment Techniques

Maternal and fetal assessment techniques are the cornerstone of safe intrapartum care in high-risk pregnancies. Nurses must utilize both clinical examination skills and technological monitoring tools to evaluate dynamic physiological changes. Maternal assessment includes regular monitoring of vital signs (blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and respiration), assessment of pain, evaluation of uterine contraction frequency, intensity, and duration, and monitoring urine output via catheterization, especially in pre-eclampsia or prolonged labour. Assessment of hydration, emotional status, and signs of impending complications (such as visual disturbances or right upper quadrant pain) are equally crucial. Fetal assessment focuses on determining fetal well-being through intermittent auscultation using a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler ultrasound, as well as continuous electronic fetal monitoring (cardiotocography) when indicated. Nurses must learn to interpret fetal heart rate patterns, identifying baseline rates, accelerations, and concerning decelerations (late, variable, or prolonged) which signal fetal hypoxia or distress. In Nigerian healthcare facilities, where access to continuous electronic monitors may be limited, mastering the art of intermittent auscultation combined with strict timing of contractions is an indispensable clinical skill. Thorough documentation and prompt reporting of abnormal assessment findings enable the healthcare team to intervene before irreversible fetal or maternal harm occurs.

Intrapartum Monitoring and Partograph Utilization

Intrapartum monitoring and the utilization of the partograph are vital practices for preventing prolonged and obstructed labour, particularly in resource-constrained Nigerian hospitals. The partograph is a pictorial overview of labour that tracks maternal and fetal wellbeing alongside the progress of labour on a single sheet of paper. It includes specific plotting for cervical dilation, descent of the fetal head, uterine contractions, maternal vital signs, and fetal heart rate. The partograph features an 'alert line' and an 'action line', separated by four hours. When the cervical dilation curve crosses to the right of the alert line, it serves as a warning that labour is progressing slower than normal, prompting increased vigilance and investigation. If the plotting crosses the action line, immediate management interventions—such as augmentation, transfer to a facility with surgical capabilities, or preparation for Cesarean section—must be implemented. Nurses are primarily responsible for accurately plotting and interpreting the partograph in real time. Proper utilization ensures that complications like cephalopelvic disproportion or uterine inertia are detected early before they cause catastrophic outcomes such as uterine rupture, postpartum hemorrhage, or severe birth asphyxia. Mastery of the partograph is an essential, mandatory skill for every nursing and midwifery student in Nigeria.

Nursing Management and Interventions

Nursing management and interventions for high-risk pregnancies in labour require a systematic, holistic, and calm approach. Once a high-risk condition is identified, the nurse initiates targeted interventions designed to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes. These include establishing intravenous access with large-bore cannulas for fluid resuscitation or emergency medication administration, collecting blood samples for baseline investigations (such as full blood count, blood grouping, and cross-matching), and ensuring strict fluid balance charts. Positioning the mother is another critical nursing intervention; for example, placing a mother with suspected cord prolapse or fetal distress in the left lateral or knee-chest position relieves pressure on the umbilical cord and enhances placental perfusion. Nurses must also administer prescribed medications accurately—such as antihypertensives for severe pre-eclampsia, oxytocin for induction or augmentation (under strict supervision), and magnesium sulfate for seizure prophylaxis. Emotional support and clear communication are equally essential; high-risk labour induces intense fear and anxiety for the mother and her family. By explaining procedures, providing reassurance, and maintaining a professional demeanor, the nurse helps reduce maternal stress, which can positively influence physiological parameters. Collaborative teamwork with attending obstetricians, anesthetists, and pediatricians ensures seamless transitions of care during complex intrapartum crises.

Emergency Obstetric Complications and Crisis Management

Emergency obstetric complications during labour demand rapid, highly coordinated crisis management to save maternal and fetal lives. Conditions such as postpartum hemorrhage, uterine rupture, eclamptic seizures, shoulder dystocia, and severe acute fetal distress require nurses to activate emergency response protocols instantly. Crisis management begins with calling for help, securing airway, breathing, and circulation (the ABCs), and assigning specific roles to the multidisciplinary team. For shoulder dystocia, nurses must recall and assist with maneuvers like McRoberts and suprapubic pressure while avoiding fundal pressure. For postpartum hemorrhage, bimanual uterine compression, administration of uterotonics (oxytocin, misoprostol, or ergometrine), and preparation for massive blood transfusion are paramount. In cases of eclamptic seizures, maintaining a patent airway, preventing injury, and administering intravenous magnesium sulfate are critical steps. Simulation training and clear communication using structured tools (like SBAR) are vital for Nigerian nursing students to master, as chaos during obstetric emergencies significantly worsens patient outcomes. Nurses must remain calm, act decisively, document all interventions and medication times accurately, and prepare patients emotionally and physically for emergency operative interventions when conservative measures fail.

Postpartum Care and Prevention of Long-term Sequelae in Nigeria

Postpartum care and the prevention of long-term sequelae are critical concluding components of high-risk labour management, especially within the Nigerian healthcare system. The immediate and extended postpartum periods carry significant risks of secondary postpartum hemorrhage, postpartum pre-eclampsia, thromboembolism, and puerperal sepsis. Nurses must maintain vigilant monitoring of vital signs, uterine involution, and lochia characteristics for at least the first 24 to 48 hours following delivery. Beyond physical recovery, addressing the psychosocial impact of a traumatic or high-risk labour is essential; mothers frequently experience anxiety, depression, or post-traumatic stress disorder. Prevention of long-term sequelae involves meticulous wound care for perineal tears or Cesarean section incisions, early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis, and promotion of exclusive breastfeeding where appropriate. Furthermore, educating the mother and her family on danger signs—such as heavy vaginal bleeding, severe headaches, fever, or foul-smelling discharge—ensures prompt seeking of medical care if complications arise after discharge. Providing comprehensive contraceptive counseling empowers the mother to space future pregnancies, protecting her reproductive health and reducing cumulative obstetric risks. For nursing students, mastering this transition from intrapartum emergency care to supportive postnatal recovery completes the spectrum of safe, high-quality maternal care.
