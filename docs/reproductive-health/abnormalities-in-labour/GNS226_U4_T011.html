Lacerations

Introduction and Definition of Perineal and Genital Lacerations

A laceration is an accidental tear of the skin and underlying soft tissues, distinct from an episiotomy which is a controlled surgical incision. In obstetric practice, perineal and genital lacerations occur frequently during the second stage of labour as the fetal head distends and crowns against the pelvic outlet. These soft tissue injuries can involve the vaginal mucosa, perineal muscles, and the anal sphincter complex. While minor grazes heal spontaneously without intervention, deeper tears require prompt and accurate anatomical apposition to restore normal function, preserve continence, and prevent long-term morbidity.

Anatomy of the Female Perineum and Pelvic Floor

The perineum is a diamond-shaped muscular and fibromuscular area situated below the pelvic floor, bounded by the pubic symphysis anteriorly, ischial tuberosities laterally, and the coccyx posteriorly. The pelvic floor is primarily formed by the levator ani muscle group—specifically the puborectalis, pubococcygeus, and iliococcygeus muscles—which act as a supportive sling for the pelvic viscera. The perineal body acts as a central tendon of the perineum, serving as an anchor for multiple muscles including the bulbospongiosus, superficial transverse perineal, and external anal sphincter. Understanding these structural relationships is vital for accurate diagnosis and layered repair following childbirth.

Classification and Degrees of Perineal Tears

Perineal trauma is universally classified into four distinct degrees based on anatomical depth:

  • First-degree tear: Injury limited to the vaginal mucosa and perineal skin without underlying muscle involvement.
  • Second-degree tear: Involves the vaginal mucosa, skin, and perineal muscles, but spares the anal sphincter complex.
  • Third-degree tear: Extends through the skin, mucosa, and perineal muscles, and partially or completely disrupts the anal sphincter complex.
  • Fourth-degree tear: Involves all structures of a third-degree tear with an additional breach through the rectal mucosa into the lumen.

Etiology and Risk Factors During Labour

Numerous intrapartum factors contribute to the stretching and eventual tearing of perineal tissues. Rapid descent of the fetal head, persistent occipitoposterior positions, and shoulder dystocia place excessive mechanical strain on the pelvic floor. Primiparity is a major independent risk factor due to the unyielding nature of the virgin perineum. Other contributors include instrumental deliveries (forceps or ventouse), macrosomia, prolonged second stage of labour, and precipitous delivery where the midwife lacks time to control the rate of crowning.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis of Lacerations

Following the delivery of the placenta, a systematic inspection of the lower genital tract is mandatory for every postpartum woman, regardless of whether she had an unassisted vaginal birth or an episiotomy. Adequate lighting, proper patient positioning in the dorsal lithotomy or left lateral position, and gentle exposure using sterile swabs are essential. The midwife must inspect the labia, clitoris, urethra, vagina, and perineum. Digital rectal examination is crucial if a deep second-degree or suspected third- or fourth-degree tear is present to assess the tone and integrity of the anal sphincter.

Immediate Midwifery Management and Repair

Once the extent of the trauma is identified, repair should be performed under adequate local or regional anesthesia using sterile technique. Synthetic absorbable sutures, such as polyglycolic acid, are standard for apposing mucosal, muscular, and dermal layers. The apex of the vaginal tear is closed first, continuing down to the introitus, followed by careful reconstruction of the perineal muscles and the perineal body. For third- and fourth-degree tears, prompt referral to an obstetrician or surgeon is indicated for specialized sphincteroplasty, ensuring the rectal mucosa is repaired separately from the muscular layers.

Postoperative Care and Pain Management

Post-repair management focuses on promoting wound healing, reducing edema, and alleviating discomfort. Regular analgesia using paracetamol and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs forms the cornerstone of pain relief. Cold packs applied to the perineum during the first twenty-four hours help minimize swelling. Midwives should encourage good perineal hygiene, regular handwashing, and frequent pad changes to prevent infection. Adequate fluid intake and a high-fiber diet prevent constipation, reducing the strain on the perineal sutures during defecation.

Potential Complications and Long-term Sequelae

Failure to recognize or adequately repair genital tract lacerations can lead to both short-term and chronic complications. Immediate risks include excessive postpartum hemorrhage, wound hematoma formation, and localized wound infection or dehiscence. Long-term sequelae encompass chronic perineal pain, dyspareunia, urinary incontinence, and fecal or flatal incontinence resulting from unhealed anal sphincter injuries. These complications profoundly affect a woman's physical recovery, psychological well-being, and sexual health.
