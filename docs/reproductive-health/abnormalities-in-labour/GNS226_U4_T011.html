Lacerations

Introduction and Anatomy of Perineal Lacerations

A perineal laceration is a tear of the skin, muscles, and supporting tissues located between the vaginal opening and the anus during childbirth. These tears occur naturally as the baby's head and shoulders pass through the birth canal, differing from an episiotomy, which is a surgical cut made by a healthcare provider. The female perineum consists of a complex network of muscles, fascia, and blood vessels that support the pelvic organs. The perineal body serves as a vital anchor point where multiple pelvic floor muscles meet, including the bulbospongiosus, superficial transverse perineal, and external anal sphincter muscles. When this area stretches during delivery, excessive tension can cause the tissue layers to tear, requiring careful clinical evaluation and sometimes surgical repair to restore normal anatomy and function.

Classification and Degrees of Perineal Tears

  • First-degree tear: Involves only the vaginal mucosa and perineal skin without affecting the underlying muscles.
  • Second-degree tear: Extends deeper to involve the perineal muscles and fascia, but leaves the anal sphincter intact.
  • Third-degree tear: Involves the perineal skin, muscles, and the anal sphincter complex. This is further divided into subcategories (3a, 3b, 3c) based on how much of the sphincter muscle is torn.
  • Fourth-degree tear: The most severe classification, where the tear extends completely through the anal sphincter and the rectal mucosa, exposing the lumen of the rectum.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Several factors increase the likelihood of a woman sustaining a severe perineal laceration during childbirth. Fetal macrosomia, defined as a birth weight above 4,000 grams, creates excessive stretching forces on the vaginal tissues. Occiput posterior positions, where the baby faces the mother's abdomen, increase the diameter of the presenting part. Rapid second-stage labour leaves insufficient time for the perineal tissues to stretch gradually. Instrumental deliveries using forceps or vacuum extractors add mechanical stress and raise tear rates. Nulliparity, meaning a woman is having her first vaginal delivery, is a major risk factor because the virgin tissues are less compliant. Conversely, controlled delivery techniques, warm compresses applied to the perineum during the second stage, and careful perineal massage can help reduce the severity of trauma.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

Prompt and thorough examination following the delivery of the placenta is essential to identify all genital tract injuries. The midwife or doctor must position the patient comfortably in the dorsal lithotomy position with adequate lighting. Gentle retraction of the labia allows clear visualization of the anterior vaginal walls, urethra, clitoris, and cervix. A digital rectal examination is mandatory when a second-degree or deeper tear is suspected, or whenever a repair is planned, to ensure the anal sphincter and rectal mucosa are intact. Missing a third- or fourth-degree tear during this initial assessment can lead to long-term complications like fecal incontinence. Careful documentation of the exact location, depth, and structures involved guides proper surgical repair.

Immediate Management and Repair

Surgical repair of perineal lacerations aims to restore anatomical alignment, achieve hemostasis, and minimize pain and infection risk. Local anesthesia, such as a pudendal block or local infiltration with lidocaine, is administered to numb the area adequately before suturing begins. The repair typically uses absorbable synthetic sutures like polyglactin 910. For first- and second-degree tears, continuous running suture techniques are associated with less postpartum pain compared to interrupted sutures. Third- and fourth-degree tears require specialized surgical repair in an operating theatre, often involving overlapping or end-to-end approximation of the torn anal sphincter ends. Strict aseptic technique is maintained throughout the procedure to prevent wound contamination.

Postoperative Care and Pain Management

Comprehensive postpartum care focuses on promoting wound healing, preventing infection, and managing pain effectively. Cold packs applied to the perineum during the first twenty-four hours help reduce local edema and numb nerve endings. Mild analgesics like paracetamol or ibuprofen are routinely prescribed, while stronger pain relief may be needed following extensive repairs. Encouraging adequate fluid intake and a high-fiber diet prevents constipation, which minimizes strain on the healing perineal tissues. Stool softeners are frequently prescribed for patients with third- or fourth-degree tears. Proper perineal hygiene, including washing with plain water from front to back after using the toilet, keeps the surgical site clean and reduces the risk of infection.

Complications and Long-Term Sequelae

While most perineal lacerations heal without incident, complications can occasionally arise. Wound breakdown, or dehiscence, can occur due to infection, hematoma formation, or excessive tension on the sutures. Perineal hematomas present as severe, disproportionate pelvic pain and swelling, often requiring surgical evacuation and re-suturing. Long-term sequelae of poorly healed or unrecognised deep tears include dyspareunia, chronic pelvic pain, and urinary or fecal incontinence. Providing compassionate psychological support is crucial, as birth trauma and associated physical discomfort can significantly impact maternal mental health, bonding, and sexual well-being.
