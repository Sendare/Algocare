Foetal Distress

Introduction and Definition of Foetal Distress

Foetal distress, clinically referred to as non-reassuring foetal status, describes a state where the foetus is not receiving adequate oxygen during pregnancy or labor. This oxygen deprivation leads to intrauterine hypoxia and subsequent acidosis if uncorrected. When gas exchange across the placenta is compromised, the foetus cannot meet its baseline metabolic demands, triggering compensatory physiological responses that quickly exhaust foetal reserves. In busy maternity units across Nigeria, early recognition of this state is paramount because a prolonged hypoxic episode can rapidly transition from a functional impairment to permanent neurological injury or intrapartum stillbirth. Understanding this baseline definition guides every subsequent intervention, shifting the clinical focus from routine observation to active preservation of foetal well-being.

Pathophysiology and Causes of Intrauterine Hypoxia

Intrauterine hypoxia arises when maternal-foetal oxygen transfer is disrupted at any point along the pathway. The primary causes span maternal, placental, and foetal origins:

  • Maternal factors: Severe hypotension, maternal hypoxia from respiratory disease, and uterine hyperstimulation caused by excessive oxytocin infusion.
  • Placental factors: Placental abruption, placenta praevia, and post-term pregnancies leading to placental insufficiency.
  • Foetal factors: True knots of the umbilical cord, nuchal cord compression, foetal anemia, and congenital anomalies.

When oxygen tension drops, the foetus initiates a central redistribution of cardiac output. Blood flow is preferentially shunted away from the periphery, kidneys, and gastrointestinal tract to protect vital organs like the brain, heart, and adrenal glands. While adaptive in the short term, this redistribution causes oliguria and reduces gastrointestinal motility, often resulting in the passage of meconium into the amniotic fluid.

Clinical Signs and Symptoms in the Mother and Foetus

Detecting compromised foetal well-being relies on observing specific clinical indicators. Foetal heart rate abnormalities serve as the most prominent warning sign, detectable through intermittent auscultation or electronic foetal monitoring.

  • Bradycardia: A baseline foetal heart rate persistently below 110 beats per minute.
  • Tachycardia: A baseline rate exceeding 160 beats per minute, often an early sign of hypoxia or maternal infection.
  • Late decelerations: Heart rate drops that begin after the peak of a uterine contraction and return to baseline slowly.
  • Meconium staining: Thick, green or yellowish staining of the amniotic fluid after membrane rupture, signaling foetal stress.

On the maternal side, signs are often indirect. A mother may report a sudden decrease or cessation of foetal movements, or present with vaginal bleeding if the cause is placental abruption or praevia.

Intrapartum Monitoring and Diagnostic Techniques

Clinical evaluation of the foetus during labor utilizes both low-tech and advanced technological modalities. Intermittent auscultation using a Pinard stethoscope or a Doppler ultrasound device remains a standard baseline screening tool, particularly in resource-constrained settings across primary healthcare facilities in Nigeria. For high-risk labors, Cardiotocography (CTG) provides continuous electronic recording of the foetal heart rate alongside uterine contractions. CTG assessment evaluates baseline rate, variability, accelerations, and decelerations. When CTG tracings are ambiguous or non-reassuring, confirmatory tests such as foetal scalp blood sampling for pH analysis may be employed where available to directly measure metabolic acidosis, though its use is increasingly limited by logistical constraints.

Immediate Nursing Interventions and Resuscitation Measures

Upon identifying signs of a non-reassuring foetal status, immediate intrauterine resuscitation must be initiated to improve foetal oxygenation before permanent damage occurs.

  1. Change maternal position: Turn the patient onto her left lateral side to relieve pressure from the gravid uterus on the inferior vena cava, thereby enhancing venous return and cardiac output.
  2. Discontinue oxytocin: Stop any oxytocin infusion immediately if uterine hyperstimulation is suspected or confirmed.
  3. Administer oxygen: Provide high-flow oxygen via a tight-fitting face mask to optimize maternal saturation gradient.
  4. Correct hypotension: Administer rapid intravenous fluid boluses, such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate, if blood pressure is low.
  5. Perform vaginal examination: Check for cord prolapse or assess the rapid progress of labor.

Documenting the exact timeline of these interventions and the foetal response is a critical legal and clinical duty for the attending nurse.

Medical and Surgical Management of Foetal Distress

When conservative intrauterine resuscitation measures fail to restore a reassuring foetal status, prompt escalation to medical or surgical delivery is mandatory. Obstetricians evaluate the stage of labor to determine the safest route. If the cervix is fully dilated and the foetus is at a low station, operative vaginal delivery using forceps or a ventouse vacuum extractor may be performed for rapid extraction. Conversely, if delivery is not imminent and foetal acidosis deepens, an emergency caesarean section is immediately scheduled. The multidisciplinary team—comprising the obstetrician, anaesthetist, scrub nurse, and neonatal team—must work with extreme urgency. Preoperative preparation must be streamlined to minimize the decision-to-delivery interval, ensuring the newborn receives prompt pediatric resuscitation at birth.

Potential Complications and Outcomes

The long-term and short-term consequences of intrauterine hypoxia vary based on the severity and duration of the oxygen deprivation. Immediate neonatal complications include birth asphyxia, hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE), meconium aspiration syndrome, and neonatal shock. Survivors of severe prolonged asphyxia may face chronic neurodevelopmental challenges, such as cerebral palsy, developmental delay, learning disabilities, and seizure disorders. Beyond physical outcomes, the sudden emergency surrounding an operative delivery often leaves parents grappling with psychological trauma, anxiety, or grief. Comprehensive neonatal resuscitation and supportive postnatal care remain critical in mitigating these adverse outcomes.
