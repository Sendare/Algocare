Foetal Distress

Understanding Foetal Distress

Foetal distress is a term used by clinical teams when an unborn baby does not receive enough oxygen during pregnancy or labour. This lack of oxygen, medically known as intrauterine hypoxia, threatens the well-being of the foetus and requires urgent attention from the midwifery and nursing staff. When oxygen supply drops, the baby's body responds by shifting blood flow away from less vital organs, like the skin and gut, to protect the brain, heart, and adrenal glands. If this state continues without relief, it can lead to permanent injury or even stillbirth. Nurses working in labour wards must always be vigilant, watching for subtle shifts in the mother and baby's condition that signal distress before a true emergency unfolds.

Causes and Pathophysiology of Intrauterine Hypoxia

Intrauterine hypoxia does not happen in a vacuum; it stems from distinct disruptions in the chain of oxygen transfer from the mother to the foetus. This chain involves maternal oxygenation, placental function, and umbilical cord blood flow. Problems anywhere along this path can trigger foetal compromise.

  • Maternal factors: Severe maternal hypotension, chronic hypertension, or respiratory illness that reduces the amount of oxygen in the mother's blood.
  • Placental factors: Placental abruption or premature aging of the placenta, which cuts off the transfer of nutrients and gases.
  • Umbilical cord issues: Cord prolapse, tight nuchal cords, or cord compression during strong uterine contractions.
  • Uterine hyperstimulation: Excessive contractions caused by medications like oxytocin, which prevent the placenta from refilling with oxygenated maternal blood between contractions.

Clinical Signs and Symptoms

Detecting foetal distress relies on observing specific warning signs during antenatal visits and active labour. The most common indicator is an abnormal foetal heart rate (FHR) pattern on a cardiotocograph (CTG). Late decelerations, severe variable decelerations, and persistent bradycardia all point toward oxygen deprivation.

Another critical sign is the passage of meconium into the amniotic fluid before birth. When a foetus experiences hypoxia, the anal sphincter relaxes, and the bowel releases its first stool into the waters, turning the fluid greenish or brownish. Nurses must note the consistency of meconium, as thick, pea-soup meconium indicates a higher risk of aspiration by the baby at birth. In addition, a sudden drop or absence of foetal movements reported by the mother serves as an important warning sign requiring immediate physical assessment.

Monitoring and Diagnostic Techniques

Clinical assessment goes beyond listening to the baby's heartbeat with a simple Pinard stethoscope, though intermittent auscultation remains vital in low-risk settings. Modern labour wards rely heavily on electronic foetal monitoring, also known as CTG, to continuously trace the foetal heart rate alongside uterine contractions.

When a CTG reading looks suspicious or non-reassuring, doctors and senior midwives may use secondary tests to confirm if the baby is truly in danger. Foetal scalp blood sampling measures the pH level of the baby's blood directly, identifying acidosis if the pH falls below normal thresholds. Ultrasound scans are also employed to check amniotic fluid volume and assess placental blood flow using Doppler technology. Understanding these diagnostic tools allows nurses to prepare equipment and assist the medical team without delay.

Immediate Nursing Interventions and Resuscitation

When signs of foetal distress appear, the nurse must act swiftly using intrauterine resuscitation techniques to improve the baby's oxygen supply before resorting to surgery.

  1. Change maternal position: Turn the mother onto her left or right lateral side to relieve pressure from the heavy uterus on the inferior vena cava and aorta.
  2. Administer oxygen: Provide supplemental oxygen via a tight face mask to boost the oxygen saturation in maternal blood.
  3. Stop oxytocin infusion: If the mother is receiving oxytocin to speed up contractions, stop the infusion immediately to let the uterus rest.
  4. Correct maternal hypotension: Administer intravenous fluids as prescribed to raise maternal blood pressure and improve placental perfusion.
  5. Perform vaginal examination: Check for a cord prolapse or assess the speed of cervical dilation.

Medical and Surgical Management

If intrauterine resuscitation measures fail to restore a normal foetal heart rate within a short window, emergency delivery becomes the primary goal. The obstetrician decides the route of delivery based on how far labour has progressed and the station of the foetal head.

If the cervix is fully dilated and the baby's head is low in the pelvis, an assisted vaginal delivery using forceps or a vacuum extractor may be performed for a speedy birth. If the cervix is not fully dilated, or if resuscitation fails in the first stage of labour, an emergency caesarean section is carried out without delay. Operating room staff, anaesthetists, and neonatal nurses must be mobilized rapidly to receive a potentially compromised newborn who may need active resuscitation in the delivery suite.

Complications and Long-Term Outcomes

Prolonged intrauterine hypoxia leaves lasting impacts on the newborn, depending on how severe and how long the oxygen deprivation lasted. Immediate complications include birth asphyxia, meconium aspiration syndrome, and neonatal seizures. Babies who survive severe oxygen deprivation may develop hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE), a type of brain injury caused by lack of blood and oxygen.

Over the long term, children affected by severe birth hypoxia can face neurological challenges such as cerebral palsy, developmental delays, and learning disabilities. Prompt action by nursing and medical teams during labour reduces the incidence of these devastating outcomes. Postnatal care requires close monitoring in a neonatal intensive care unit, where therapies like therapeutic hypothermia can sometimes protect the newborn brain from further damage.
