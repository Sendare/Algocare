Anaemia in Labour

Introduction and Definition of Anaemia in Labour

Anaemia during labor is a critical condition defined by a low hemoglobin level when a woman is admitted to the delivery ward. According to the World Health Organization, a parturient woman is considered anaemic if her hemoglobin concentration falls below 11.0 g/dL. In low-resource settings like Nigeria, this threshold is often tested upon arrival in labor because many women present with unrecognized or poorly managed nutritional deficiencies. During childbirth, the body undergoes significant blood loss, meaning a compromised baseline leaves very little physiological safety margin. Recognizing anaemia early in the first stage of labor helps the healthcare team prepare for potential complications such as postpartum hemorrhage or poor uterine muscle contraction.

Physiological Changes and Oxygen Delivery in Pregnancy

Normal pregnancy triggers massive adaptations in the maternal circulatory system to support the growing fetus. Plasma volume expands by nearly 50%, while red blood cell mass increases by only about 25 to 30%. This mismatch creates physiological anaemia of pregnancy, a state of hemodilution where blood is effectively thinned out. However, true pathological anaemia goes beyond this normal dilution. Because red blood cells carry oxygen via hemoglobin, a severe deficit reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood. During the intense physical exertion of labor contractions, the myometrium demands high oxygen flow. When supply drops due to low hemoglobin, fetal distress and maternal exhaustion develop rapidly.

Classification and Severity Grading

Clinicians grade anaemia severity using specific hemoglobin thresholds to guide immediate intrapartum interventions. Mild anaemia ranges from 10.0 to 10.9 g/dL, moderate spans 7.0 to 9.9 g/dL, and severe anaemia is diagnosed when hemoglobin drops below 7.0 g/dL. In very severe cases, levels fall under 4.0 g/dL, representing an extreme medical emergency requiring immediate blood transfusion. Recognizing these grades helps nurses anticipate whether oral replacement, intravenous therapy, or emergency blood products are necessary before delivery progresses further.

Etiology and Risk Factors in Nigeria

  • Nutritional Deficiencies: Inadequate dietary intake of iron, folate, and vitamin B12 remains the leading cause, often worsened by poverty and traditional diets.
  • Infectious Diseases: High endemic rates of malaria destroy red blood cells, while hookworm infestation causes chronic gastrointestinal blood loss.
  • Genetic Hemoglobinopathies: Sickle cell disease and trait variations complicate red blood cell survival and oxygen transport during pregnancy.
  • Frequent Multiparity: Closely spaced pregnancies deplete maternal nutrient stores before the body can recover.

Maternal and Fetal Complications During Labour

Anaemia during labor threatens both mother and baby. For the mother, reduced oxygen-carrying capacity increases the risk of cardiac strain, heart failure, and poor tolerance to normal blood loss. Postpartum hemorrhage becomes much more dangerous because a woman starting with low hemoglobin cannot tolerate even a standard volume of blood loss without going into hypovolemic shock. For the fetus, chronic intrauterine hypoxia can cause fetal distress, low birth weight, preterm delivery, and low Apgar scores at birth.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Nurses must perform thorough physical and laboratory assessments upon admitting any laboring woman. Clinical signs of anaemia include pallor of the conjunctiva, nail beds, and palms, alongside tachycardia and tachypnea as the body tries to compensate for low oxygen. However, physical signs can be subtle in mild cases. The definitive diagnostic test is checking hemoglobin or hematocrit levels via laboratory analysis or a reliable point-of-care hemoglobinometer. Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring is also essential to detect early signs of fetal oxygen compromise.

Intrapartum Nursing Care and Management

Managing a laboring woman with anaemia requires a proactive multidisciplinary approach. The nurse ensures large-bore intravenous access is secured early in case rapid fluid or blood replacement becomes necessary. Vital signs and uterine contractions are monitored closely. Oxygen therapy via nasal cannula may be administered if oxygen saturation drops. During the third stage of labor, active management is critical: administering uterotonic drugs promptly helps prevent excessive bleeding, which can be fatal for an anaemic patient.

Emergency Interventions and Postpartum Prevention

When a woman presents with severe anaemia in labor or experiences severe bleeding afterward, emergency steps must be taken without delay. Blood typing and crossmatching must be expedited to secure compatible blood for transfusion. Resuscitation protocols focus on stabilizing maternal blood pressure and oxygenation. Following delivery, nurses play a key role in secondary prevention by providing postpartum iron and folate supplementation, encouraging nutrient-rich diets, and offering family planning counseling to ensure adequate spacing before future pregnancies.
