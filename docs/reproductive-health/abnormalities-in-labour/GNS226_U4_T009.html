Anaemia in Labour

Introduction and Definition of Anaemia in Labour

Anaemia in labour is a critical obstetric condition that poses significant risks to both the parturient woman and her developing fetus. Globally and within the Nigerian healthcare context, anaemia is formally defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a haemoglobin (Hb) concentration of less than 11.0 g/dL during pregnancy, and specifically in the postpartum or late third trimester period, an Hb level below 10.0 g/dL is frequently used locally to denote significant anaemia that requires urgent intervention. During labour, the demands on the cardiovascular and respiratory systems peak due to the physical exertion of uterine contractions and the physiological blood loss inherent in delivery. When a woman enters labour with depleted iron stores and a low baseline haemoglobin level, her body's ability to compensate for this acute blood loss is severely compromised. As nursing students preparing for clinical practice in Nigeria, recognizing anaemia early is paramount because obstetric populations in West Africa experience a high prevalence of nutritional deficiencies, malaria, and hemoglobinopathies. The presence of anaemia reduces oxygen-carrying capacity, translating directly into a diminished physiological reserve. This makes the parturient highly susceptible to intrapartum complications such as uterine inertia, prolonged labour, postpartum haemorrhage, and shock. Understanding the baseline definition allows midwives and nurses to immediately identify at-risk admissions upon arrival in the labour ward, facilitating prompt multidisciplinary management, timely investigations, and preventative therapies to safeguard maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Pathophysiology and Physiological Changes in Pregnancy

To effectively care for an anaemic woman in labour, a nurse must understand the normal physiological adaptations of pregnancy, particularly regarding hematology. Throughout normal gestation, maternal blood volume increases significantly—by approximately 30 to 50 percent—to meet the metabolic demands of the uteroplacental unit and the growing fetus. However, plasma volume expands to a much greater extent than the increase in red blood cell mass, resulting in a condition known as physiological anaemia of pregnancy or hemodilution. This state lowers the baseline haemoglobin concentration, yet it serves a protective purpose by decreasing blood viscosity and safeguarding against the acute blood loss of delivery. Pathologically, true anaemia occurs when the red blood cell mass falls short of functional requirements due to nutritional depletion, excessive destruction, or impaired production. During the active stages of labour, every uterine contraction temporarily shifts 300 to 500 mL of blood from the intervillous space back into the systemic circulation, causing intermittent increases in maternal cardiac output. If the parturient has underlying pathological anaemia, her myocardium must work exponentially harder to maintain tissue oxygenation against reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. This can precipitate maternal tachycardia, tachypnoea, and eventually cardiac decompensation or congestive heart failure. Nurses must monitor vital signs closely, recognizing that normal compensatory mechanisms in pregnancy can mask early signs of hypovolaemic shock until a catastrophic deterioration occurs.

Classification and Severity Grading of Anaemia

Anaemia in obstetric patients is formally classified based on haemoglobin (Hb) levels to determine the severity and guide emergency or routine interventions. According to standard clinical guidelines, anaemia is generally graded into mild, moderate, and severe categories. Mild anaemia is typically characterized by an Hb concentration between 10.0 and 10.9 g/dL. Moderate anaemia falls between 7.0 and 9.9 g/dL, a range where many women begin to display noticeable symptoms such as generalized fatigue, pallor, and exertional dyspnoea. Severe anaemia is defined as an Hb concentration of less than 7.0 g/dL, representing a critical medical and obstetric emergency that requires immediate intervention, such as blood transfusion, to prevent life-threatening maternal morbidity and mortality. In busy clinical settings across Nigeria, nurses often encounter pregnant women presenting in labour with moderate to severe anaemia due to late booking or unmanaged antenatal deficiencies. Recognizing these grades is essential for triaging patients accurately. Mild cases may be managed with oral supplementation and close observation, whereas severe cases demand immediate intravenous access, cross-matching of blood products, and vigilant hemodynamic monitoring. Grading also helps healthcare teams communicate effectively during handovers, ensuring that the urgency of the patient's condition is universally understood by midwives, obstetricians, and anaesthetists alike.

Etiology and Risk Factors in Pregnant Women in Nigeria

The etiology of anaemia among pregnant women in Nigeria is multifactorial, stemming from a combination of nutritional, infectious, socioeconomic, and genetic factors. Iron deficiency remains the single most common cause, exacerbated by poor dietary intake of bioavailable iron, high parity, and short birth intervals that do not allow maternal iron stores to replenish. Folate and vitamin B12 deficiencies also contribute significantly, impairing erythropoiesis. Infectious diseases are another major driver in the sub-Saharan region; malaria caused by Plasmodium falciparum leads to the destruction of red blood cells and suppresses bone marrow function. Similarly, hookworm infestation and chronic urinary tract infections cause ongoing, low-grade blood loss and inflammation, which traps iron in reticuloendothelial stores (anaemia of chronic disease). Furthermore, inherited hemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell disease (HbSS and HbSC) and the trait variants add layers of complexity, predisposing women to severe haemolytic crises during the metabolic stress of labour. Socioeconomic challenges, including poverty, limited access to formal education, and delayed antenatal care booking, mean that many women reach the point of delivery without having received routine prophylactic iron and folic acid supplements or intermittent preventive treatment for malaria (IPTp). As student nurses, understanding these root causes empowers you to provide holistic care, emphasizing both acute management in labour and vital health education for future pregnancies.

Maternal and Fetal Complications of Anaemia During Labour

Anaemia during labour places a heavy burden on both the mother and the fetus, drastically increasing the risk of adverse intrapartum outcomes. For the mother, the primary danger is her inability to tolerate the normal blood loss associated with the third stage of labour. Even a moderate postpartum haemorrhage (PPH) that a healthy woman might tolerate well can quickly trigger hypovolaemic shock, renal failure, and cardiac arrest in an anaemic parturient. Anaemia is also strongly associated with uterine inertia and poor muscle contractility due to cellular hypoxia, which leads to prolonged or obstructed labour and a higher rate of operative interventions such as emergency Caesarean sections or instrumental deliveries. Furthermore, immune compromise secondary to severe nutritional depletion increases the risk of puerperal sepsis and wound infections. For the fetus and newborn, maternal anaemia impairs oxygen transfer across the placenta, resulting in chronic or acute intrauterine hypoxia. This manifests as non-reassuring fetal heart rate patterns, meconium staining of the amniotic fluid, low Apgar scores at birth, intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), and prematurity. Newborns of severely anaemic mothers may also be born with depleted iron stores, predisposing them to developmental and haematological problems in early infancy. Comprehensive intrapartum nursing care must therefore focus on vigilant surveillance of both maternal hemodynamic stability and fetal well-being throughout all stages of labour.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Clinical assessment and diagnostic evaluation are vital steps in managing a labouring woman with suspected or confirmed anaemia. During the admission routine, the nurse must perform a thorough history-taking and physical examination, specifically looking for classic clinical signs of anaemia. These include generalized pallor of the conjunctivae, nail beds, and palmar creases, as well as angular stomatitis, koilonychia (spoon-shaped nails), glossitis, tachycardia, tachypnoea, and functional systolic heart murmurs caused by hyperdynamic circulation. Diagnostic confirmation relies heavily on laboratory investigations, most notably a serial haemoglobin or packed cell volume (PCV) estimation. Upon admission to the labour ward, blood should be promptly collected for an immediate PCV test, blood grouping, and cross-matching to ensure that blood products are readily available if needed. Additional diagnostic tests may include a peripheral blood film to morphology-type the anaemia (e.g., microcytic hypochromic for iron deficiency or macrocytic for folate deficiency), a malaria parasite test (rapid diagnostic test or microscopy), and urinalysis to rule out urinary tract infections. In resource-constrained Nigerian settings where point-of-care testing is vital, the use of a Haemoglobinometer or a reliable PCV centrifuge can expedite decision-making. Nurses must interpret these clinical and laboratory findings promptly and communicate them effectively to the attending obstetrician to establish a safe, individualized intrapartum care plan.

Intrapartum Nursing Care and Management

Intrapartum nursing care and management for an anaemic parturient require a proactive, highly organized, and multidisciplinary approach across all stages of labour. In the first stage, the nurse should ensure the patient is placed in a comfortable position, optimize her oxygenation by administering supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula or face mask if her oxygen saturation drops, and establish reliable intravenous access using large-bore cannulas (e.g., 16G or 18G) in anticipation of fluid or blood administration. Continuous or frequent intermittent electronic fetal monitoring is essential because the compromised fetus cannot tolerate the stress of uterine contractions well. Vital signs, including maternal blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and temperature, must be monitored and charted partographically every 30 to 60 minutes. Throughout the second stage of labour, the nurse should guide maternal pushing efforts carefully, avoiding prolonged or Valsalva maneuvers that deplete maternal oxygen reserves and stress the heart, and consider performing an assisted (instrumental) delivery if indicated to shorten the second stage and minimize maternal exhaustion. In the third stage, active management of the third stage of labour (AMTSL) is mandatory: administering a uterotonic agent (such as oxytocin) immediately after delivery of the baby, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage. AMTSL is the single most effective nursing intervention to minimize blood loss and prevent postpartum haemorrhage in a woman whose blood volume is already compromised by anaemia.

Emergency Interventions for Severe Anaemia and Postpartum Haemorrhage Prevention

When managing a parturient with severe anaemia or anticipating a high risk of postpartum haemorrhage (PPH), emergency interventions must be executed swiftly and systematically by the healthcare team. If a patient presents with severe anaemia (Hb < 7 g/dL) in active labour, the obstetrician and anaesthetist must be notified immediately to evaluate the need for an emergency blood transfusion, utilizing type-specific or O-negative blood if cross-matched blood is not yet ready. The nurse must maintain strict fluid balance charts, monitor urine output via an indwelling urinary catheter (aiming for a minimum of 0.5 mL/kg/h to assess renal perfusion), and watch for early signs of circulatory overload or heart failure, such as basal crackles and elevated jugular venous pressure. If PPH occurs despite active management of the third stage, protocol-driven emergency measures must be initiated without delay: call for multidisciplinary backup, administer secondary uterotonics (such as ergometrine, misoprostol, or carboprost), perform bimanual uterine compression or aortic compression, and prepare for surgical interventions if medical management fails. Post-delivery, the mother requires close monitoring in a high-dependency or intensive care unit setting for at least 24 to 48 hours. Comprehensive nursing documentation, patient reassurance, and discharge planning—including iron supplementation, nutritional counseling, and family planning advice—are essential components to ensure long-term recovery and prevent recurrence in subsequent pregnancies.
