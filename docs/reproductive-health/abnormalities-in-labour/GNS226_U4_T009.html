Anaemia in Labour

Introduction and Definition of Anaemia in Labour

Anaemia in labour is a prevalent global health concern with profound implications for obstetric outcomes. According to the World Health Organization, a parturient woman is diagnosed with anaemia when her hemoglobin concentration falls below 11.0 g/dL. During the intrapartum period, this condition compromises the body's ability to tolerate normal blood loss associated with delivery. The physiological demands of pregnancy heavily tax maternal iron stores, and when reserves deplete completely, haemoglobin synthesis plummets. In resource-constrained settings across sub-Saharan Africa, late booking for antenatal care often means pre-existing nutritional deficiencies go uncorrected, culminating in women entering active labour with severely compromised oxygen-carrying capacity. Consequently, even standard obstetrical blood loss can precipitate cardiovascular collapse in these vulnerable patients.

Pathophysiology and Physiological Changes in Pregnancy

Normal pregnancy induces profound alterations in maternal hematology. Plasma volume increases by roughly 40 to 50 percent, while red cell mass expands by only 20 to 30 percent. This discrepancy creates a condition known as physiological anaemia of pregnancy or hemodilution, where the blood appears diluted. While hemodilution serves to reduce blood viscosity and enhance placental perfusion, true pathological anaemia occurs when hemoglobin drops past normal gestational dilution limits. During labour, uterine contractions periodically restrict intervillous blood flow, requiring adequate maternal baseline oxygen stores to prevent fetal hypoxia during the stress of delivery.

Classification and Severity Grading

  • Mild Anaemia: Hemoglobin levels ranging from 10.0 to 10.9 g/dL.
  • Moderate Anaemia: Hemoglobin levels ranging from 7.0 to 9.9 g/dL, requiring active intervention to prevent decline.
  • Severe Anaemia: Hemoglobin levels falling below 7.0 g/dL, constituting an obstetric emergency.
  • Very Severe Anaemia: Hemoglobin levels dropping under 4.0 g/dL, carrying imminent risk of heart failure and maternal mortality.

Etiology and Risk Factors in Nigeria

Anaemia in Nigerian pregnant women is multifactorial, rooted in a complex interplay of nutritional deficits, infectious burdens, and socioeconomic constraints.

  • Nutritional Deficiencies: Inadequate dietary intake of bioavailable iron, folate, and vitamin B12 remains the leading cause, often worsened by cultural food taboos or poverty.
  • Malaria in Pregnancy: Plasmodium falciparum infection causes hemolysis and suppresses erythropoiesis, acting as a primary driver of falling packed cell volumes in endemic zones.
  • Helminthic Infestations: Hookworm infections, particularly in rural agrarian communities, cause chronic gastrointestinal blood loss.
  • Genoglobinopathies: High prevalence of sickle cell trait (HbAS) and disease (HbSS, HbSC) contributes significantly to baseline hemolytic states.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

The consequences of intrapartum anaemia ripple across both maternal and neonatal outcomes. The maternal heart must work excessively to circulate poorly oxygenated blood, predisposing the woman to high-output cardiac failure, especially when combined with the exertion of labour. Furthermore, anaemic myometrial cells contract poorly, increasing the incidence of uterine atony and subsequent postpartum haemorrhage. Fetal complications stem directly from chronic uteroplacental insufficiency, manifesting as intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, premature birth, and intrapartum asphyxia due to a severely depleted margin of safety during uterine contractions.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Detecting anaemia during labour requires a combination of vigilant physical examination and rapid laboratory screening. Nurses must inspect the conjunctivae, nail beds, and palmar creases for pallor, though physical signs can be notoriously unreliable in mild cases. Tachycardia, tachypnoea, and functional systolic murmurs point toward hemodynamic compensation. The definitive diagnostic test in the labour ward is the rapid estimation of hemoglobin or packed cell volume using a hemoglobinometer or a centrifuge. A baseline blood sample should be sent immediately for full blood count, blood grouping, and cross-matching to ensure cross-matched units are readily available should haemorrhage occur.

Intrapartum Nursing Care and Management

Intrapartum care for an anaemic woman centers on conserving energy, optimizing oxygen delivery, and monitoring closely for decompensation. Administer supplemental oxygen via face mask to maximize maternal and fetal oxygen saturation. Establish reliable intravenous access early using large-bore cannulas. Continuous electronic fetal monitoring is vital, as the fetus has little tolerance for intrapartum hypoxia. Avoid prolonged second-stage labour by considering active instrumental delivery if maternal exhaustion sets in. Maintain strict fluid balance charts to avoid fluid overload, which can precipitate pulmonary edema in a compromised myocardium.

Emergency Interventions and Postpartum Haemorrhage Prevention

Preventing and managing postpartum haemorrhage in an anaemic parturient requires aggressive, protocol-driven care. Active management of the third stage of labour is mandatory: administer prophylactic uterotonics such as oxytocin immediately upon delivery of the anterior shoulder or infant. Ensure controlled cord traction and prompt uterine massage. If severe anaemia is confirmed and acute blood loss occurs, activate the massive obstetric hemorrhage protocol and transfuse packed red blood cells promptly. Close postpartum monitoring in a high-dependency unit is critical for the first twenty-four hours to catch delayed atony or hemodynamic collapse early.
