Sickle Cell Disease in Pregnancy

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Sickle Cell Disease

Sickle cell disease is a group of inherited red blood disorders characterized by the presence of hemoglobin S. Under conditions of low oxygen tension, dehydration, or acidosis, hemoglobin S polymerizes, causing red blood cells to distort into a rigid, crescent shape. These abnormal cells cause vaso-occlusion in the microvasculature, leading to ischemia, tissue infarction, and severe pain. Chronic hemolytic anemia is also a hallmark of the condition, as damaged erythrocytes are prematurely destroyed in the spleen. In Nigeria, the homozygous HbSS genotype is most prevalent, presenting significant clinical challenges when complicated by the unique hemodynamic demands of gestation.

Physiological Changes in Pregnancy and Their Impact on Sickle Cell Disease

Normal pregnancy induces profound physiological adjustments that directly challenge a compromised hematological system. Blood volume expands significantly, by up to 50%, which disproportionately increases plasma volume compared to red cell mass, resulting in physiological anemia of pregnancy. This dilutional effect compounds the baseline chronic hemolytic anemia seen in sickle cell disease. Furthermore, hypercoagulability increases the baseline thromboembolic risk inherent to vaso-occlusion. Increased oxygen consumption by the uteroplacental unit creates a state of relative tissue hypoxia, which readily triggers sickling episodes if hydration and oxygenation are not meticulously maintained.

Maternal and Fetal Complications Associated with Sickle Cell Disease

Maternal and perinatal outcomes are markedly compromised when pregnancy coincides with sickle cell disease. Pregnant women face heightened frequencies of acute chest syndrome, vaso-occlusive painful crises, urinary tract infections, and pre-eclampsia. Fetal complications stem primarily from chronic uteroplacental insufficiency caused by microvascular infarction in the placental bed. These vascular disruptions lead directly to intrauterine growth restriction, preterm birth, low birth weight, and an increased rate of perinatal mortality.

Antenatal Assessment, Monitoring, and Multidisciplinary Care

Optimal management requires early booking at the antenatal clinic and coordinated care involving obstetricians, hematologists, and specialized midwives. Routine visits must incorporate close surveillance for maternal infection, blood pressure abnormalities, and signs of impending crisis.

  • Fetal Surveillance: Serial ultrasound scans monitor fetal growth velocity and amniotic fluid volume starting in the second trimester.
  • Laboratory Monitoring: Regular complete blood counts, urinalysis, and renal function tests track baseline anemia and screen for silent bacteriuria.
  • Nutritional Support: Aggressive supplementation with folic acid is mandatory to support accelerated erythropoiesis.

Pharmacological Management and Preventive Interventions in Pregnancy

Medication choices during gestation require careful balancing of maternal benefit against teratogenic risks. Hydroxyurea, a mainstay of non-pregnant sickle cell management, is generally discontinued prior to or upon confirmation of pregnancy due to teratogenicity concerns. Prophylactic penicillin may be continued if the patient is asplenic. Pain management during outpatient crises relies on safe analgesics such as paracetamol and carefully monitored opioids. Malaria chemoprophylaxis and intermittent preventive treatment are also vital components in malaria-endemic regions like Nigeria to prevent parasite-induced hemolysis.

Intrapartum Care and Management of Sickle Cell Crises in Labour

Labour and delivery represent periods of intense physiological stress that can easily provoke an acute sickle cell crisis. Continuous fetal heart rate monitoring is standard practice due to the high risk of intrapartum fetal distress. Care teams must rigorously maintain hydration via intravenous fluids, provide supplemental oxygen to prevent hypoxia, and ensure adequate pain relief, as pain and anxiety trigger catecholamine release and subsequent sickling. Epidural analgesia is often recommended to blunt the stress response of labour. If an acute painful crisis or acute chest syndrome develops during delivery, immediate resuscitation, targeted pain management, and possible blood transfusion are initiated.

Postpartum Care, Contraception, and Breastfeeding Considerations

The postpartum period carries a peak risk for maternal complications, including thromboembolism, severe postpartum hemorrhage, and delayed-onset sickle cell crises triggered by the sudden fluid shifts of delivery. Early ambulation, pneumatic compression devices, and prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin are utilized to prevent venous thromboembolism. Contraceptive counseling must be initiated early before hospital discharge; progestin-only pills, depot medroxyprogesterone acetate, or long-acting reversible contraceptives are generally safe and effective choices that avoid the thrombotic risks associated with estrogen-containing formulations. Breastfeeding is strongly encouraged, provided maternal nutrition and hydration are carefully maintained.
