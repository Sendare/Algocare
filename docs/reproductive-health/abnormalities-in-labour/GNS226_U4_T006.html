Diabetes Mellitus in Pregnancy

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Diabetes in Pregnancy

Pregnancy is naturally a diabetogenic state. As gestation progresses, placental hormones—most notably human placental lactogen (hPL), progesterone, and cortisol—induce progressive maternal insulin resistance. This physiological shift ensures a continuous supply of glucose to the developing fetus. In a healthy pancreas, maternal insulin secretion increases threefold to compensate for this resistance. When maternal pancreatic beta-cells fail to meet this heightened insulin demand, gestational hyperglycemia ensues.

Classification: Pregestational Versus Gestational Diabetes Mellitus

Distinguishing between pregestational and gestational diabetes is vital for accurate risk stratification and clinical management.

  • Pregestational Diabetes Mellitus (PGDM): Refers to women who had established Type 1 or Type 2 diabetes prior to conception. These pregnancies carry a high risk of teratogenicity, including major congenital malformations and early pregnancy loss, because chronic hyperglycemia exists during the critical period of organogenesis in the first trimester.
  • Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM): Defined as carbohydrate intolerance of variable severity with onset or first recognition during pregnancy. GDM typically manifests in the second or third trimester as placental hormone levels peak, and it generally resolves following delivery, though it elevates the mother's long-term risk for developing overt Type 2 diabetes.

Maternal and Fetal Risks Associated with Diabetes

Uncontrolled maternal hyperglycemia wreaks havoc on both maternal physiology and fetal development. High circulating glucose levels cross the placenta freely, prompting the fetal pancreas to secrete excessive insulin. Because insulin acts as a powerful growth hormone in the fetus, this hyperinsulinemia drives accelerated somatic growth, culminating in macrosomia (fetal birth weight above 4.0 kg or the 90th percentile). Macrosomia dramatically increases the likelihood of shoulder dystocia, perineal lacerations, and operative deliveries.

Screening, Diagnosis, and Antenatal Assessment

Early detection forms the bedrock of safe obstetric care for diabetic mothers. In busy antenatal clinics across Nigeria, routine screening begins at the initial booking visit to identify unrecognized pregestational diabetes using fasting blood glucose or random blood glucose tests. For gestational diabetes, universal or risk-based screening is universally performed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation using the Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT).

Antenatal surveillance must also track fetal well-being. Serial ultrasound scans monitor fetal growth patterns, amniotic fluid volume, and estimated fetal weight. When glycemic control is suboptimal, biophysical profiles and cardiotocography are deployed in the third trimester to detect chronic fetal hypoxia before it results in stillbirth.

Glycemic Control and Medical Management

Achieving strict normoglycemia minimizes fetal and maternal morbidity. Management begins with targeted medical nutrition therapy and moderate physical activity tailored to the client's physical capacity and cultural context. When lifestyle interventions fail to maintain fasting capillary blood glucose below 5.3 mmol/L and 1-hour postprandial levels below 7.8 mmol/L, pharmacological therapy is immediately introduced.

Insulin remains the gold standard pharmacological treatment because it does not cross the placenta in clinically significant amounts. While oral hypoglycemic agents like metformin and glyburide are increasingly utilized due to patient preference and ease of administration, nurses must monitor clients closely for compliance and efficacy.

Intrapartum Care and Management of Labour

Labour and delivery require meticulous glycemic management because the physical exertion of contractions alters maternal energy demands and insulin requirements. Women with well-controlled GDM on diet alone often require only intermittent blood glucose monitoring during labour. Conversely, those on insulin regimens typically receive a continuous insulin infusion paired with an intravenous dextrose solution to maintain blood glucose strictly between 4.0 and 7.0 mmol/L, preventing both maternal ketoacidosis and neonatal hypoglycemia at birth.

Elective induction of labour or planned Caesarean section is frequently scheduled around 38 to 39 weeks for well-controlled diabetes to minimize the risk of stillbirth and macrosomia-related birth trauma.

Postpartum Care and Neonatal Complications

Delivery of the placenta triggers an immediate and dramatic drop in anti-insulin placental hormones, causing maternal insulin requirements to plummet instantly. Women with GDM generally discontinue insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents immediately postpartum. However, neonates born to diabetic mothers require vigilant monitoring in the nursery.

  • Neonatal Hypoglycemia: Occurs because the newborn's hyperactive pancreas continues to secrete high insulin levels in the absence of maternal glucose supply. Blood glucose must be checked within the first few hours of life.
  • Respiratory Distress Syndrome (RDS): High fetal insulin levels can delay pulmonary surfactant production, making newborn lungs stiff and prone to collapse.
  • Hypocalcemia and Polycythemia: Metabolic disturbances that require close pediatric observation and early initiation of feeding.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care bridges the gap between complex clinical protocols and everyday patient understanding. Nurses teach self-monitoring of blood glucose, proper insulin injection techniques, and carbohydrate counting. Education must also focus on recognizing warning signs of hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia, empowering clients to take prompt corrective action.

Furthermore, nurses coordinate postpartum follow-up care, ensuring that women with a history of gestational diabetes undergo a 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test at 6 weeks postpartum to reclassify their glucose tolerance status and facilitate early intervention for future metabolic health.
