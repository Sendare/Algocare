Postpartum Haemorrhage

Definition and Classification of Postpartum Haemorrhage

Postpartum haemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal mortality across Sub-Saharan Africa. Traditionally, it is defined as blood loss exceeding 500 mL following a vaginal delivery, or greater than 1000 mL after a caesarean section, within the first twenty-four hours postpartum. When bleeding occurs after the first twenty-four hours up to six weeks postpartum, it is classified as secondary or late postpartum haemorrhage.

Clinicians further categorize the condition by volume and timing:

  • Primary PPH: Excessive bleeding occurring within the initial 24 hours after birth, typically driven by acute uterine atony or genital tract trauma.
  • Secondary PPH: Bleeding manifesting between 24 hours and 12 weeks post-delivery, commonly linked to retained placental tissue or subinvolution of the placental bed.
  • Minor PPH: Total blood loss ranging between 500 mL and 1000 mL without clinical signs of shock.
  • Major PPH: Blood loss exceeding 1000 mL, or any volume accompanied by hemodynamic instability. Major PPH is sub-classified into moderate (1000–2000 mL) and severe (greater than 2000 mL).

Underestimating blood loss is a frequent clinical error in busy Nigerian maternity wards. Visual estimation often underreports actual volume by up to 50 percent. Gravimetric methods, such as weighing soaked surgical pads or utilizing calibrated collection bags (drapes), provide a much more accurate assessment and prompt earlier escalation.

The 4 Ts of Postpartum Haemorrhage Etiology

Understanding the root causes of excessive bleeding after childbirth is streamlined through the mnemonic known as the 4 Ts: Tone, Tissue, Trauma, and Thrombin.

  • Tone: Uterine atony accounts for approximately 70 to 80 percent of all primary postpartum haemorrhage cases. The myometrium fails to contract effectively, leaving open maternal spiral arterioles bleeding freely into the uterine cavity.
  • Tissue: Retained placental fragments, membranes, or blood clots prevent the myometrium from contracting tightly. This can stem from a succenturiate placental lobe, manual removal complications, or morbidly adherent placenta.
  • Trauma: Lacerations of the cervix, vagina, or perineum, along with uterine rupture or inversion, account for roughly 20 percent of bleeding episodes. These often occur despite an otherwise well-contracted uterus.
  • Thrombin: Inherited or acquired bleeding disorders, such as von Willebrand disease, hemophilia carriers, disseminated intravascular coagulation, or severe preeclampsia-induced coagulopathies, impair the blood's ability to clot.

A thorough clinical evaluation requires systematically checking each of these four categories when bleeding fails to stop after initial interventions.

Risk Factors and Identification

While postpartum haemorrhage can strike unexpectedly in low-risk women, specific antepartum and intrapartum risk factors heighten vulnerability. Recognizing these factors during antenatal booking visits allows healthcare teams to prepare emergency medications and blood products in advance.

Grand multiparity is particularly prevalent in many West African populations, increasing the risk of myometrial fatigue and subsequent atony. Similarly, prolonged obstructed labor, often resulting from delayed referrals from rural health posts, severely compromises uterine muscle tone before delivery even occurs.

Prevention and Active Management of the Third Stage of Labour

The active management of the third stage of labour represents the single most effective intervention for reducing postpartum haemorrhage rates. This evidence-based package consists of three core components administered immediately following the birth of the baby:

  1. Administration of a uterotonic: A prophylactic dose of oxytocin (typically 10 international units given intramuscularly or slowly intravenously) is the gold standard. It stimulates rhythmic myometrial contractions.
  2. Controlled cord traction: Gentle tension is applied to the umbilical cord combined with counter-pressure (a Brandt-Andrews maneuver) on the suprapubic area to deliver the placenta safely.
  3. Uterine massage: Palpating the fundus periodically after placental delivery ensures the uterus remains firm and encourages ongoing contraction.

When oxytocin is unavailable or heat-sensitive storage has degraded its potency, alternative uterotonics such as misoprostol tablets or ergometrine can be utilized, keeping specific contraindications like hypertension in mind.

Clinical Assessment and Early Recognition

Early detection of abnormal blood loss prevents progression to severe hypovolemic shock. Midwives and nurses must monitor vital signs closely, remembering that healthy young women can compensate for significant blood loss before exhibiting a drop in blood pressure.

A rising pulse rate (tachycardia) and subtle tachypnea are often the earliest warning signs of acute hypovolemia. Pallor, dizziness, restlessness, and cold, clammy skin point toward moderate blood loss and impending shock.

Utilizing the Shock Index—calculated by dividing the heart rate by the systolic blood pressure—offers an objective tool for assessment. A normal shock index typically ranges from 0.5 to 0.7. Values exceeding 0.9 indicate significant blood volume depletion, signaling the urgent need for aggressive fluid resuscitation and senior medical intervention.

Emergency Medical and Surgical Interventions

When bleeding persists despite active management and initial uterotonics, structured emergency protocols must be initiated rapidly. Multidisciplinary teamwork involving midwives, obstetricians, anesthetists, and laboratory staff is critical.

Medical escalation involves administering second-line uterotonics such as fixed-dose oxytocin-ergometrine combinations, carboprost (prostaglandin F2-alpha), or sublingual misoprostol. Tranexamic acid should be administered intravenously within three hours of birth to inhibit fibrinolysis and reduce bleeding-related mortality.

If medical measures fail to control uterine atony, mechanical and surgical interventions are implemented sequentially:

  • Bimanual compression: Compressing the uterus between a fist inside the vagina and a hand on the abdominal wall.
  • Uterine balloon tamponade: Inserting a hydrostatic balloon (such as a Bakri balloon) to exert direct pressure against the bleeding myometrium.
  • Compressive suture: Surgical techniques like the B-Lynch suture applied directly to the uterus.
  • Devascularization or Hysterectomy: Ligation of uterine or ovarian arteries, or subtotal/total hysterectomy as a life-saving last resort.

Nursing Resuscitation Protocols and Contextual Challenges

Managing a severe postpartum hemorrhage in resource-constrained Nigerian clinical settings requires swift nursing improvisation alongside adherence to protocols. Establishing large-bore intravenous access (16-gauge or 18-gauge cannulae in both arms) is a primary nursing priority for rapid fluid and blood replacement.

Resuscitation requires rapid infusion of warm isotonic crystalloids, followed by whole blood or packed red cells as soon as cross-matched blood is available. In emergency scenarios where typed blood is delayed, emergency uncrossed O-negative or type-specific blood may be necessary. Meticulous fluid balance charting, recording urinary output via an indwelling catheter with a urometer, is vital to monitor renal perfusion and prevent acute tubular necrosis.

Systemic challenges, such as erratic electricity supply, delayed blood bank responses, and out-of-pocket expenses for consumables, place immense pressure on nursing staff. Maintaining a well-stocked emergency drug tray ('PPH box') in every labor ward is an essential mitigation strategy.

Complications and Long-Term Prognosis

Severe postpartum haemorrhage carries significant short- and long-term consequences that extend far beyond the immediate bleeding episode. Acute complications include hemorrhagic shock, dilutional coagulopathy, multiorgan failure, and acute kidney injury resulting from prolonged renal hypoperfusion. Sheehan syndrome—hypopituitarism caused by ischemic necrosis of the anterior pituitary gland due to severe blood loss—can manifest later as failure to lactate, amenorrhea, and fatigue.

Psychological sequelae are frequently overlooked by healthcare providers. Mothers who experience near-miss mortality events often suffer from postpartum post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, and depression, which can impair bonding with the newborn. Comprehensive post-discharge care must include anemia management, nutritional support, psychological counseling, and structured follow-up appointments.
