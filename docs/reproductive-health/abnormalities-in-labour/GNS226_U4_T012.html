Postpartum Haemorrhage

Definition and Classification of Postpartum Haemorrhage

Postpartum haemorrhage is traditionally defined as blood loss exceeding 500 mL following a vaginal delivery, or more than 1,000 mL following a caesarean section, within the first twenty-four hours after birth. When excessive bleeding occurs after the first twenty-four hours and up to six weeks postpartum, it is classified as secondary postpartum haemorrhage. Clinically, estimating blood loss visually is notoriously inaccurate; healthcare providers often underestimate the actual volume lost, which delays critical interventions.

  • Primary Postpartum Haemorrhage: Occurs within 24 hours of birth, most commonly caused by uterine atony.
  • Secondary Postpartum Haemorrhage: Occurs from 24 hours up to 12 weeks postpartum, frequently linked to retained products of conception or subinvolution of the placental site.
  • Minor Postpartum Haemorrhage: Blood loss ranging between 500 mL and 1,000 mL with no signs of clinical shock.
  • Major Postpartum Haemorrhage: Blood loss exceeding 1,000 mL, or any blood loss accompanied by hemodynamic instability.

The 4 Ts of Postpartum Haemorrhage

Mnemonic devices are valuable clinical tools in emergency obstetric care. The causes of postpartum haemorrhage are categorized into four major groups known as the 4 Ts: Tone, Tissue, Trauma, and Thrombin. Uterine atony accounts for the vast majority of cases and falls under tone. Retained placental fragments or membranes represent tissue causes. Lacerations of the birth canal, uterine rupture, or inversion are grouped under trauma. Finally, coagulation disorders that prevent proper blood clotting fall under thrombin. Recognizing which category is driving the haemorrhage dictates the immediate medical and nursing response.

Risk Factors and Clinical Assessment

Identifying women at high risk for excessive bleeding allows the maternity team to prepare before delivery occurs. Major risk factors include a previous history of postpartum haemorrhage, prolonged labour, polyhydramnios, multiple gestations, and high parity. Assessment requires continuous vigilance because physiological adaptation in pregnancy masks early signs of shock. A pregnant woman can lose up to thirty percent of her blood volume before showing a significant drop in blood pressure. Therefore, nurses must track rising pulse rates and subtle changes in consciousness rather than waiting for hypotension to appear.

Active Management of the Third Stage of Labour

Active management of the third stage of labour is the single most effective intervention for preventing primary postpartum haemorrhage. It involves three core components performed immediately after the baby is born: administering a uterotonic drug, performing controlled cord traction, and massaging the uterus.

  1. Uterotonic Administration: Oxytocin is the drug of choice, given intramuscularly or intravenously to stimulate sustained uterine contractions.
  2. Controlled Cord Traction: The attendant applies gentle tension to the umbilical cord while counter-pressure is applied suprapubically to safely deliver the placenta.
  3. Uterine Massage: Palpating and massaging the uterine fundus periodically ensures the muscle remains firmly contracted.

Emergency Medical and Surgical Management

When bleeding persists despite initial uterotonics and uterine massage, escalation to advanced medical and surgical therapies is necessary. Second-line medications include ergometrine, synthetic prostaglandins like misoprostol, and carboprost. Bimanual compression of the uterus provides temporary mechanical control by squeezing the organ between a hand inside the vagina and another on the abdominal wall. If medical management fails, surgical interventions such as uterine balloon tamponade, B-Lynch compression sutures, uterine artery ligation, or subtotal hysterectomy may be performed to save the patient's life.

Nursing Resuscitation and Clinical Protocols

Managing acute postpartum haemorrhage requires a coordinated multidisciplinary team effort. Nurses must initiate a structured emergency protocol immediately when blood loss becomes excessive. This includes calling for help, securing large-bore intravenous access, drawing blood for hemoglobin, cross-matching, and coagulation studies, and starting rapid fluid resuscitation with warmed crystalloids. Monitoring vital signs every five to fifteen minutes helps evaluate the effectiveness of interventions. Accurate measurement of blood loss using calibrated drapes or weighing pads prevents delayed recognition of ongoing hemorrhage.

Complications and Long-Term Prognosis

Severe postpartum haemorrhage carries significant physical and psychological consequences for the mother. Acute complications include hypovolemic shock, dilutional coagulopathy, renal failure from prolonged hypoperfusion, and Sheehan syndrome resulting from ischemic necrosis of the pituitary gland. Anemia is widespread among survivors, leading to chronic fatigue, impaired bonding with the newborn, and increased risk of postpartum depression. Long-term care must encompass nutritional support with iron supplementation, psychological counseling, and education on future reproductive health.
