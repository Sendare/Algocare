Dysmenorrhoea

Introduction and Definition of Dysmenorrhoea

Dysmenorrhoea refers to painful menstrual cramps of uterine origin that occur during menstruation. It is one of the most common gynaecological complaints among adolescent girls and premenopausal women globally. In Nigerian clinical practice, many young women normalise this pain, delaying valuable medical evaluation until symptoms severely disrupt academic attendance or daily productivity. Understanding that dysmenorrhoea is a medical condition rather than an inevitable part of the female cycle is the first step toward effective nursing intervention.

Pathophysiology and Hormonal Mechanisms

The underlying mechanism of primary dysmenorrhoea centres on excessive production and release of uterine **prostaglandins**, particularly prostaglandin F2-alpha (PGF2α). Following progesterone withdrawal at the end of the luteal phase, endometrial cell breakdown releases these inflammatory mediators. PGF2α triggers strong, uncoordinated myometrial contractions and vasoconstriction of uterine blood vessels. This localized ischemia leads to severe, crampy lower abdominal pain. Vasopressin also plays an amplifying role by increasing myometrial sensitivity and reducing uterine blood flow.

Classification: Primary versus Secondary Dysmenorrhoea

Dysmenorrhoea is clinically divided into two distinct categories that guide diagnostic approaches and therapeutic interventions. - **Primary Dysmenorrhoea**: Painful menstruation in the absence of identifiable pelvic pathology, typically starting within six to twelve months of menarche when ovulatory cycles become established. - **Secondary Dysmenorrhoea**: Painful menstruation linked to an underlying structural or pathological pelvic condition, commonly appearing later in life after years of painless cycles.

Etiology and Common Risk Factors

While primary dysmenorrhoea stems from normal yet exaggerated biochemical pathways, secondary dysmenorrhoea arises from distinct anatomical abnormalities. Common structural causes include **endometriosis**, **uterine fibroids (leiomyomas)**, **adenomyosis**, and pelvic inflammatory disease. Recognised risk factors that increase the likelihood of experiencing severe menstrual pain include: - Age under thirty years - Early menarche (before age twelve) - Heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding - Family history of dysmenorrhoea - Smoking and high stress levels

Clinical Presentation and Assessment

The clinical picture of dysmenorrhoea extends beyond lower abdominal cramping. Pain often radiates to the lower back and thighs, starting shortly before or precisely at the onset of menstruation and lasting for 48 to 72 hours. Accompanying systemic symptoms include nausea, vomiting, fatigue, diarrhoea, headaches, and dizziness due to systemic prostaglandin spillover. A thorough nursing assessment requires capturing a detailed menstrual history: age at menarche, cycle length, pain severity scoring using a visual analogue scale, and the impact of symptoms on daily activities and school or work attendance.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Evaluating menstrual pain begins with a comprehensive medical history and physical examination. For adolescents with typical primary dysmenorrhoea and no red flags, extensive pelvic imaging is rarely necessary. However, when secondary causes are suspected, or when initial medical therapy fails, further diagnostic measures are warranted. Pelvic ultrasonography serves as the primary imaging modality to identify fibroids, ovarian cysts, or adenomyosis. Diagnostic laparoscopy remains the gold standard for visualising and confirming conditions like endometriosis.

Medical and Non-Pharmacological Management

Managing dysmenorrhoea effectively combines pharmacological treatments with supportive lifestyle modifications. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen are first-line pharmacological agents because they directly inhibit cyclooxygenase enzymes, thereby reducing uterine prostaglandin synthesis. Hormonal contraceptives, including combined oral contraceptive pills, suppress ovulation and thin the endometrium, effectively lowering intrauterine prostaglandin levels. Non-pharmacological approaches provide valuable adjunct relief and can be used independently or alongside medications: - Applying local heat therapy (such as a warm compress or heating pad) to the lower abdomen - Engaging in regular aerobic exercise to improve pelvic blood circulation - Practising relaxation techniques and stress reduction - Dietary adjustments emphasizing hydration and anti-inflammatory nutrients

Nursing Care Plan and Health Education

Nursing care for patients with dysmenorrhoea focuses on comfort promotion, symptom tracking, and dispelling cultural myths surrounding menstruation. Nurses should educate patients to initiate NSAIDs at the very onset of bleeding or slightly before pain peaks, rather than waiting for discomfort to become severe. Lifestyle coaching should highlight the benefits of heat application and regular physical activity. Furthermore, nurses must screen for red-flag symptoms such as sudden heavy bleeding, intermenstrual spotting, or pain that resists standard analgesia, ensuring prompt referral to a gynaecologist for specialised investigation.
