Amenorrhoea

Introduction and Definition of Amenorrhoea

Amenorrhoea is the absence of menstruation. It is a clinical sign rather than a disease in itself, signaling an underlying disruption in the intricate hormonal feedback loops governing the female reproductive system. While physiologic amenorrhoea is entirely normal during pregnancy, lactation, and the post-menopausal phase, pathological amenorrhoea requires systematic investigation.

Clinicians categorize the condition based on whether a menstrual cycle has ever been established. Recognizing this distinction guides early differential diagnosis and prevents unnecessary, invasive testing.

Physiology of Normal Menstruation and the HPO Axis

Normal menstruation relies on the coordinated functioning of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis and an intact anatomical outflow tract.

  • Hypothalamus: Secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone in a pulsatile manner.
  • Anterior Pituitary: Responds to GnRH by releasing luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone.
  • Ovaries: Produce estrogen and progesterone in response to gonadotropins, driving endometrial proliferation and shedding.
  • Outflow Tract: Includes the uterus, cervix, and vagina, which must be patent for menstrual blood to escape.

Classification: Primary vs. Secondary Amenorrhoea

Distinguishing between primary and secondary forms provides a structural framework for nursing history-taking and diagnostic workups.

  • Primary Amenorrhoea: Absence of menarche by age 13 in the absence of secondary sexual development, or by age 15 despite normal secondary sexual development.
  • Secondary Amenorrhoea: Cessation of menstruation for three consecutive cycles in a woman with previously established, regular menses, or for six months in women with oligomenorrhoea.

Aetiology and Risk Factors

Pathological causes of amenorrhoea are traditionally grouped by anatomical and functional level:

  1. Hypothalamic-Pituitary Disorders: Stress, extreme weight loss, excessive endurance exercise, and prolactinomas.
  2. Ovarian Disorders: Premature ovarian insufficiency, polycystic ovary syndrome, and gonadal dysgenesis like Turner syndrome.
  3. Outflow Tract Anomalies: Congenital imperforate hymen, transverse vaginal septum, or Asherman syndrome resulting from intrauterine scarring.

In busy Nigerian clinical settings, healthcare workers must also keep chronic systemic illnesses, severe anemia, and tuberculosis in mind as contributors to secondary cycle cessation.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough nursing history establishes timeline patterns, athletic habits, nutritional status, and signs of hyperandrogenism such as hirsutism or acne. Physical examination includes a general systemic review, body mass index calculation, and pelvic assessment when appropriate.

Initial laboratory screening typically features a serum pregnancy test, thyroid-stimulating hormone measurement, and serum prolactin levels. Further evaluation may involve pelvic ultrasonography to check for anatomical anomalies or ovarian morphology, alongside progestin challenge tests to assess endogenous estrogen status and outflow tract patency.

Medical and Nursing Management

Therapeutic strategies target the underlying pathology rather than the symptom of absent menses alone. Medical management may involve dopamine agonists for hyperprolactinaemia, hormone replacement therapy for ovarian failure, or lifestyle counseling for weight-related hypothalamic amenorrhoea.

Nurses play a vital role by monitoring treatment adherence, assessing for medication side effects, and supporting patients through complex diagnostic routines. Clear communication helps alleviate anxiety during pelvic examinations and imaging procedures.

Potential Complications and Long-Term Implications

Prolonged hypo-oestrogenaemia carries significant long-term health risks. Women with untreated chronic amenorrhoea face premature bone mineral density loss, increasing their lifetime vulnerability to osteopenia and osteoporosis. Furthermore, conditions like polycystic ovary syndrome cause chronic, unopposed estrogen exposure on the endometrium, elevating the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and malignancy.

Fertility impairment is another major concern, requiring early identification and timely referral to specialist reproductive endocrinologists.
