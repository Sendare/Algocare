Management of Abortion

Classification and Clinical Types of Abortion

An abortion is the termination of pregnancy before 20 weeks of gestation, or the expulsion of a fetus weighing less than 500 grams. Understanding the specific clinical classification guides nursing care and medical treatment. Miscarriages present in several distinct forms, each requiring different clinical interventions.

  • Threatened abortion: Vaginal bleeding occurs with or without mild cramping, but the cervical os remains closed, and the products of conception are intact.
  • Inevitable abortion: Bleeding and cramping worsen, and the internal cervical os is dilated, making continuation of the pregnancy impossible.
  • Incomplete abortion: Some products of conception have been expelled, but part of the placenta or fetus remains inside the uterus, causing continuous bleeding and pain.
  • Complete abortion: All products of conception have been completely expelled from the uterus, and the cervix has closed down.
  • Missed abortion: The embryo or fetus has died intrauterine, but the products of conception are retained within the closed cervix for weeks without bleeding.
  • Septic abortion: Any form of abortion complicated by intrauterine infection, commonly seen in unsafe or illegal terminations.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostics

A thorough assessment begins with a detailed menstrual and obstetric history to estimate gestational age and identify potential risk factors. Nurses must take a careful history regarding the volume of bleeding, severity of pain, and presence of any expelled tissue.

Physical examination focuses on hemodynamic stability. Vital signs reveal early signs of hypovolemic shock if hemorrhage is heavy. A sterile speculum examination helps visualize the cervix to check for dilation, active bleeding, or visible tissue fragments. Bimanual examination should be performed with caution or avoided if severe infection is suspected.

Diagnostic tests are vital for confirming the diagnosis and planning care. A quantitative serum or urine human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) test confirms pregnancy status. A pelvic ultrasound is the gold standard for visualizing uterine contents and determining if the abortion is complete, incomplete, or missed. Baseline laboratory investigations must include a full blood count to check hemoglobin levels, blood typing, and Rhesus factor determination to see if anti-D immunoglobulin is required.

Immediate Resuscitation and Stabilization

When a patient presents with heavy bleeding or signs of septic shock, resuscitation takes absolute precedence over diagnostic procedures. The primary nursing goal is to restore effective circulating blood volume and maintain tissue perfusion.

Secure large-bore intravenous access immediately using a 16-gauge or 18-gauge cannula. Infuse isotonic crystalloids such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate rapidly while awaiting blood products. Monitor vital signs continuously, watching for tachycardia, tachypnea, and dropping blood pressure, which signal worsening hemorrhage or shock.

Cross-match blood urgently if signs of severe blood loss are present. Administer supplemental oxygen via face mask to ensure adequate cellular oxygenation. Keep an accurate fluid balance chart, inserting an indwelling urinary catheter to monitor hourly urine output closely as a direct indicator of renal perfusion.

Medical Management of Abortion

Medical management offers a non-invasive alternative to surgery for early pregnancy loss, incomplete abortion, or missed abortion, typically up to 12 weeks of gestation. It relies on specific pharmacological agents to stimulate uterine contractions and expel tissue.

  • Mifepristone: An antiprogestogen that breaks down the decidua and softens the cervix, usually given first in medical abortion regimens.
  • Misoprostol: A prostaglandin analogue that binds to myometrial receptors, causing strong uterine contractions and cervical dilation. It can be administered sublingually, buccally, or vaginally.

Nurses must prepare patients for what to expect, as cramping and heavy bleeding are normal parts of the process, often worse than a normal heavy period. Provide effective pain relief using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or opioids as prescribed. Monitor for excessive bleeding, fever, or allergic reactions to the medications.

Surgical Management and Evacuation Techniques

Surgical evacuation is indicated when medical management fails, when there is excessive ongoing bleeding, hemodynamic instability, or suspected uterine infection.

Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) is the preferred surgical technique for early pregnancy loss up to 12 to 14 weeks. It uses a specialized plastic syringe to create a vacuum that gently empties the uterine contents. Compared to sharp curettage, MVA is safer, requires less anesthesia, and has a shorter recovery time.

Dilatation and Curettage (D&C) or electric vacuum aspiration may be used for more advanced gestations or when MVA equipment is unavailable. Nurses play a crucial role during the procedure by monitoring vital signs, offering psychological reassurance, and managing pain control. Post-procedure, nurses must check the evacuated tissue to ensure completeness and monitor for complications such as uterine perforation or excessive bleeding.

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) and Counseling

Post-Abortion Care is a comprehensive approach designed to treat complications while addressing the emotional and reproductive health needs of the patient. PAC saves lives by combining emergency treatment with compassionate counseling.

Reproductive counseling should be offered before discharge. Fertility returns very quickly after an abortion—often within two weeks—making immediate contraception counseling essential. Discuss various contraceptive methods openly and provide the chosen method before the patient leaves the health facility.

Emotional support is equally critical. Grief, guilt, anxiety, and relief are all normal emotional responses. Nurses should provide a non-judgmental, empathetic environment, encouraging patients to express their feelings without fear of social stigma or condemnation.

Prevention and Management of Complications

Complications following abortion can be severe, particularly when care is delayed or when septic abortion occurs due to unsafe procedures. Nurses must remain vigilant for warning signs during the recovery period.

  • Hemorrhage: Caused by incomplete evacuation, uterine atony, or cervical lacerations. Managed with uterotonics, bimanual compression, or surgical re-evacuation.
  • Infection (Sepsis): Results from retained tissue or unsterile instrumentation. Presents with foul-smelling vaginal discharge, pelvic pain, and fever. Requires broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and prompt evacuation.
  • Uterine Perforation: A mechanical injury caused by surgical instruments. Manifests as severe abdominal pain and signs of internal bleeding or peritonitis.
  • Asherman's Syndrome: Intrauterine adhesions forming after aggressive curettage, leading to secondary amenorrhea and infertility.

Patient education is vital. Instruct patients to report heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, foul-smelling discharge, or high fever immediately.

Legal, Ethical, and Nursing Responsibilities in Nigeria

Abortion laws in Nigeria are among the most restrictive in the world, governed primarily by the Criminal Code in the Southern states and the Penal Code in the Northern states. Under Nigerian law, abortion is illegal except when performed strictly to save the life of the pregnant woman. Performing or seeking an illegal abortion carries severe legal penalties for both the practitioner and the patient.

These restrictive laws create a major public health crisis, driving many women to seek clandestine, unsafe abortions performed by unskilled providers, leading to high rates of maternal morbidity and mortality.

Ethically and professionally, Nigerian nurses face difficult dilemmas. The Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) code of ethics emphasizes the preservation of human life. However, nurses are ethically bound to provide life-saving emergency care to any patient presenting with complications of abortion, regardless of the circumstances surrounding the procedure, without judgment or discrimination.
