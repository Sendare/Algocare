Management of Abortion

Introduction and Classification of Abortion

Abortion is defined as the termination of pregnancy before 20 to 28 weeks of gestation, or before the fetus is viable outside the womb. In the context of nursing and midwifery care in Nigeria, understanding the various classifications of abortion is critical for prompt clinical decision-making and appropriate emergency care. Abortions are broadly categorized into spontaneous and induced types. Spontaneous abortion, commonly referred to as a miscarriage, occurs naturally and includes several sub-classifications such as threatened, inevitable, incomplete, complete, missed, and septic abortions. A threatened abortion involves vaginal bleeding with a closed cervix and a viable fetus, whereas an inevitable abortion presents with bleeding, lower abdominal pain, and a dilated cervix indicating that pregnancy loss cannot be halted. In an incomplete abortion, some products of conception are expelled while others remain within the uterine cavity, posing a severe risk of hemorrhage and infection. A complete abortion means all products of conception have been fully expelled, and the cervix closes spontaneously. A missed abortion occurs when the fetus has died in utero, but no tissue has been passed, which can lead to clotting disorders if prolonged. Finally, a septic abortion involves any form of abortion complicated by intrauterine infection, frequently seen in unsafe or clandestine terminations. Induced abortions are those that are deliberately initiated, which may be therapeutic to save the mother's life or elective based on personal choice. As nursing students preparing for clinical practice in Nigeria, recognizing these distinct categories allows us to anticipate complications, prioritize interventions, and provide compassionate, evidence-based care to women presenting with pregnancy complications in emergency departments and maternity wards across the country.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

Clinical assessment and diagnosis of a patient presenting with suspected abortion require a systematic, empathetic, and thorough approach by the nursing and midwifery team. When a patient arrives at a health facility in Nigeria with complaints of vaginal bleeding, lower abdominal pain, or amenorrhea during early pregnancy, immediate history-taking is essential. Nurses must ascertain the exact gestational age, the volume and character of bleeding, the nature of pain, and any history of interference or trauma. A comprehensive physical and systemic examination follows, focusing heavily on vital signs to detect early indicators of hypovolemic shock or systemic infection. Tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnea, and pyrexia are critical red flags that demand immediate escalation. Abdominal examination assesses for tenderness, guarding, and uterine size, while a sterile speculum examination is performed to evaluate the source of bleeding, degree of cervical dilation, and presence of retained tissue or foul-smelling discharge. Bimanual examination should be performed with caution or deferred if heavy bleeding or placenta previa is suspected. Diagnostic investigations are pivotal; a baseline hemoglobin or packed cell volume (PCV) is vital to assess blood loss anemia, and a quantitative or qualitative serum/urine human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) confirms pregnancy status. Pelvic ultrasonography serves as the gold standard diagnostic tool to visualize the uterine contents, determine fetal cardiac activity, identify retained products of conception, and rule out ectopic pregnancy. Blood grouping and rhesus typing must be ordered promptly to determine the need for anti-D immunoglobulin administration in Rhesus-negative mothers. Documenting these findings accurately ensures continuity of care and guides the multidisciplinary team in formulating an appropriate medical or surgical management plan.

Immediate Resuscitation and Stabilization

Immediate resuscitation and stabilization take absolute precedence when managing patients with complicated abortions, particularly those presenting with heavy hemorrhage, severe sepsis, or hypovolemic shock. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where delayed presentation is common due to geographic or financial barriers, nurses must act swiftly using the ABCDE approach (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure). Circulation is typically the most threatened system due to acute blood loss from incomplete or septic abortions. Nurses must immediately secure large-bore intravenous access—ideally two lines with 16G or 18G cannulas—and commence rapid fluid resuscitation with isotonic crystalloids such as normal saline or Ringer's lactate. Blood samples must be drawn concurrently for a full blood count, cross-matching, and coagulation profile. If the patient exhibits signs of severe hemorrhage or profound anemia, urgent blood transfusion should be requested and initiated. Oxygen therapy via a face mask or nasal cannula should be administered to maintain adequate tissue oxygenation, especially if the patient is tachypneic or pale. In cases of septic abortion, immediate broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics must be commenced to cover polymicrobial infections before surgical evacuation is undertaken. Vital signs, including blood pressure, pulse rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation, must be monitored continuously or at least every fifteen minutes until the patient is stable. Urinary catheterization with an indwelling catheter connected to a urometer is crucial to monitor accurate hourly urine output, serving as a reliable indicator of renal perfusion and fluid resuscitation adequacy. Effective stabilization prevents multi-organ failure and prepares the patient safely for subsequent medical or surgical evacuation.

Medical Management of Abortion

Medical management of abortion has revolutionized modern obstetric care, offering a safe, non-invasive alternative to surgical evacuation for specific types of pregnancy loss, such as incomplete, missed, or inevitable abortions up to a certain gestational age. The primary pharmacological agents utilized are mifepristone, an antiprogestin that softens the cervix and causes decidual necrosis, and misoprostol, a prostaglandin analogue that stimulates effective uterine contractions to expel products of conception. In incomplete or missed abortions, misoprostol can be administered via sublingual, buccal, vaginal, or oral routes, with vaginal and sublingual routes generally demonstrating high efficacy and fewer systemic side effects. As nursing students, it is vital to understand the correct dosage, administration schedules, and expected clinical responses when caring for patients undergoing medical management. Nurses must prepare the patient by explaining the expected side effects, which commonly include moderate to severe lower abdominal cramping, vaginal bleeding heavier than a normal menstrual period, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and transient low-grade fever. Pain management is an integral component of nursing care; non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen are usually administered proactively to control uterine cramp pain. Nurses must monitor the passage of products of conception, inspect passed tissue when possible, and assess cumulative blood loss to ensure the patient is not hemorrhaging. If bleeding is excessively heavy, continuous, or if the medical management fails to completely empty the uterus, the nurse must notify the physician promptly, as backup surgical evacuation may be required. Emotional support throughout the process is equally crucial, as witnessing the passage of tissue can be psychologically distressing for the patient.

Surgical Management and Evacuation Techniques

Surgical management and evacuation techniques are indicated when medical management fails, when there is heavy or ongoing hemorrhage, or when signs of sepsis and retained tissue demand immediate intervention. The two primary surgical methods utilized in modern practice are Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) and Dilatation and Curettage (D&C), although MVA is widely preferred in contemporary nursing and midwifery practice due to its high safety profile, efficiency, and lower requirement for general anesthesia. MVA involves gentle cervical dilation followed by the insertion of a specialized plastic cannula connected to a vacuum syringe to evacuate the uterine contents safely. It can be performed outpatient under local anesthesia, such as a paracervical block, combined with non-pharmacological comfort measures and analgesics. Dilatation and Curettage (D&C), traditionally performed in an operating theater under general anesthesia, involves the mechanical dilation of the cervix using Hegar dilators followed by the use of sharp metal curettes to scrape the uterine walls. While D&C remains common, it carries a slightly higher risk of uterine perforation, cervical trauma, and intrauterine adhesion formation (Asherman's syndrome) compared to MVA. Nursing responsibilities during surgical evacuation are extensive and begin with pre-operative preparation: verifying informed consent, ensuring the bladder is emptied, administering ordered premedications or antibiotics, and offering emotional reassurance. Intra-operatively, the nurse assists the clinician, monitors the patient's vital signs, observes for vasovagal reactions, and ensures sterile technique is strictly maintained. Post-operatively, nurses must monitor vaginal bleeding, check uterine tone, assess pain levels, and watch for immediate complications such as hemorrhage, uterine perforation, or anesthesia-related adverse events.

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) and Counseling

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) and comprehensive counseling form a critical pillar of holistic nursing and midwifery practice, addressing not only the physical recovery of the patient but also her emotional, psychological, and reproductive health needs. PAC services are designed to treat complications of incomplete or unsafe abortions, provide contraceptive counseling to prevent unintended repeat pregnancies, and link women to broader reproductive health services. As nurses in Nigeria, providing non-judgmental, empathetic, and confidential care is paramount, especially given the socio-cultural stigma surrounding abortion and pregnancy loss. Emotional counseling helps women and their partners process grief, anxiety, or guilt associated with the loss of a pregnancy. Furthermore, fertility returns very rapidly following an abortion—often within two weeks—making immediate post-abortion contraceptive counseling and provision essential before the patient leaves the health facility. Nurses must educate patients on all available family planning methods, including short-acting hormonal methods, barrier methods, injectables, implants, and intrauterine devices (IUDs), allowing the woman to make an informed, voluntary choice. Discharge education must be clear, written in simple language, and include warning signs that require immediate medical attention, such as foul-smelling vaginal discharge, severe or worsening abdominal pain, heavy bleeding soaking more than two large pads per hour for two consecutive hours, or persistent fever. Nutritional advice to combat blood loss anemia, instructions on resuming physical activities and sexual intercourse, and scheduling a follow-up appointment are vital components of a successful PAC discharge plan.

Prevention and Management of Complications

The prevention and management of complications associated with abortion are core competencies for nurses and midwives practicing in emergency and obstetric units. Complications can be immediate, such as severe hemorrhage, uterine perforation, cervical lacerations, and anesthetic mishaps, or delayed, such as pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), chronic pelvic pain, secondary infertility, and psychological trauma. Septic abortion remains a major cause of maternal morbidity and mortality in Nigeria, often resulting from unsafe or unsterile termination attempts. Management of a septic abortion requires an aggressive multidisciplinary approach: prompt resuscitation, blood cultures, aggressive broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics targeting gram-negative bacilli, anaerobes, and staphylococci, and timely surgical evacuation of any remaining infected necrotic tissue once antibiotic therapy has been initiated. Nurses must monitor for signs of septic shock, such as hypothermia or hyperthermia, hypotension, altered mental status, and oliguria, reporting any deterioration instantly. Hemorrhage management involves monitoring pad counts, administering uterotonics like oxytocin or ergometrine if the uterus is atonic, and preparing for blood transfusion. Uterine perforation, which may occur during instrumental evacuation, is suspected when a surgical instrument passes further than expected or when unusual abdominal pain and signs of internal hemorrhage arise; nurses must monitor for peritoneal signs and prepare the patient for potential exploratory laparoscopy or laparotomy. Preventive strategies focus heavily on community health education regarding safe sex, expanding access to effective modern contraceptives, post-abortion counseling to prevent recurrent unwanted pregnancies, and advocating for safe legal frameworks and high-standard clinical care.

Legal, Ethical, and Nursing Responsibilities in Nigeria

The management of abortion operates within a complex web of legal, ethical, and professional responsibilities, particularly within the Nigerian legal and cultural landscape. Under Nigerian criminal and penal codes (Sections 228, 229, and 230 of the Criminal Code and Section 232 of the Penal Code), induced abortion is strictly illegal unless performed explicitly to save the life of the pregnant woman. Engaging in unlawful abortion carries severe legal consequences, including lengthy prison sentences for both the practitioner and the woman involved. This restrictive legal environment contributes significantly to the high prevalence of clandestine, unsafe abortions performed by unqualified individuals, leading to severe morbidity and mortality across the nation. For nursing and midwifery students, navigating this reality requires balancing strict adherence to the law with unwavering professional ethical duties. The Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) Code of Ethics mandates that nurses must prioritize patient life and health above all else. Therefore, when a patient presents with complications from an abortion—regardless of whether it was spontaneous or induced—nurses have an absolute ethical and professional obligation to provide emergency life-saving treatment, stabilization, and compassionate care without judgment, moralizing, or discrimination. Breaching patient confidentiality or withholding emergency care due to personal moral beliefs constitutes professional misconduct. Nurses also play a vital role in patient advocacy, public health education on contraception, and understanding the boundaries of legal termination to protect both their patients and their nursing licenses.
