Problems of Unsafe Abortion

Introduction to Unsafe Abortion in Nigeria

Unsafe abortion remains a major public health crisis in Nigeria, contributing significantly to maternal morbidity and mortality. According to the World Health Organization, an unsafe abortion is a procedure for terminating an unintended pregnancy carried out either by persons lacking the necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards, or both. In settings with restrictive laws and poor access to modern contraception, desperate women and girls often resort to dangerous methods performed by unqualified providers. These methods range from the ingestion of toxic concoctions, herbal preparations, and high doses of hormonal medications to the mechanical insertion of foreign objects like knitting needles, cassava stems, or catheter tubes into the uterus.

The burden of unsafe abortion falls heavily on adolescents and young women who lack financial resources, comprehensive sex education, and autonomous healthcare access. Many arrive at hospital emergency wards only when complications have become life-threatening, presenting with severe hemorrhage, profound septic shock, or acute peritonitis. Understanding the epidemiology and reality of unsafe abortion in our communities helps nurses move past moral judgment and focus strictly on life-saving clinical interventions.

Legal, Social, and Cultural Contexts

The legal framework surrounding abortion in Nigeria is notoriously restrictive, derived from English criminal law provisions inherited during the colonial era. Under the Nigerian Criminal Code applicable in the southern states, and the Penal Code in the northern states, abortion is illegal unless performed strictly to save the pregnant woman's life. This criminalization creates a profound culture of secrecy, forcing abortion seekers underground. When providers and patients face potential prison sentences, complications are hidden, and care is delayed until the physical situation is catastrophic.

Socially and culturally, strong religious beliefs and patriarchal norms heavily stigmatize premarital sex and unwanted pregnancies. A woman seeking pregnancy termination often faces severe community ostracism, rejection by her family, and economic abandonment by her partner. This intense social pressure overrides concerns about personal safety, driving women toward secretive, cheap, and ultimately dangerous procedures rather than seeking professional help early.

Immediate Physical Complications and Emergencies

  • Hemorrhage: Severe bleeding resulting from incomplete evacuation, uterine atony, or traumatic lacerations of the cervix and vaginal wall.
  • Sepsis: Life-threatening systemic infection caused by the introduction of unsterile instruments, unhygienic substances, or retained necrotic tissue.
  • Uterine Perforation: Puncture of the uterine wall by sharp objects, sounds, or curettes, which can subsequently damage adjacent bowel, bladder, or major pelvic blood vessels.
  • Chemical Toxicity: Systemic poisoning resulting from the oral ingestion or intravaginal application of caustic herbs, detergents, or high doses of unregulated substances.

Long-Term Reproductive Health Consequences

Survivors of acute unsafe abortion complications frequently face chronic, life-altering sequelae that impact their future well-being and fertility. Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) often develops from ascending infections, leading to chronic pelvic pain and permanent tubal scarring. This tubal damage significantly increases the risk of subsequent ectopic pregnancies or absolute secondary infertility. Furthermore, traumatic cervical dilation or aggressive surgical curettage can cause cervical incompetence, wherein the cervix cannot remain closed during subsequent pregnancies, resulting in recurrent mid-trimester miscarriages or preterm births. Chronic pelvic pain and dyspareunia also place substantial emotional strains on interpersonal and marital relationships.

Psychological and Social Impacts

The emotional aftermath of an unsafe abortion is heavily compounded by societal stigma, guilt, and fear of legal consequences. Many patients grapple with profound psychological distress, including acute anxiety, depression, and symptoms consistent with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Because they cannot openly discuss their physical suffering or the nature of their trauma for fear of judgment or arrest, they often endure their mental anguish in complete isolation. Nurses must recognize that physical recovery does not equate to complete healing; holistic care requires careful screening for psychological trauma, offering a non-judgmental listening ear, and providing sensitive mental health referrals.

Nursing Assessment and Risk Identification

Effective nursing care begins with a thorough, confidential, and empathetic history and physical assessment. Because patients may lie about the cause of their bleeding due to fear, nurses must establish a safe, non-judgmental rapport. Assessment must focus on vital sign instability, quantity and character of vaginal bleeding, presence of abdominal rigidity or rebound tenderness, and signs of pelvic sepsis. Nurses should skillfully differentiate between spontaneous miscarriages and induced terminations without adopting an accusatory tone. Early identification of clinical red flags—such as a rigid abdomen indicating peritonitis or tachycardia signaling hypovolemic shock—can prevent rapid clinical deterioration.

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) and Medical Management

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) is a critical global health strategy designed to save lives and manage complications arising from spontaneous or induced abortions. The clinical pillars of PAC include emergency medical treatment for complications, comprehensive family planning counseling, and linkage to broader reproductive healthcare services.

Medical management often involves the administration of uterotonics like oxytocin or misoprostol to promote complete uterine evacuation, alongside broad-spread antibiotic therapy to treat or prevent infection. Where incomplete abortion is confirmed without severe sepsis or hemorrhage, manual vacuum aspiration (MVA) is the preferred safe surgical intervention. Throughout this process, compassionate nursing care centers on pain management, vital sign monitoring, and preparing the patient for discharge with adequate contraceptive supplies to prevent repeat unintended pregnancies.

Prevention Strategies and the Role of the Nurse

Preventing unsafe abortion requires proactive community engagement, education, and accessible reproductive health services. Nurses play a frontline role in advocacy, demystifying modern contraception, and dispelling cultural myths that surround family planning methods among adolescents and rural populations. By providing youth-friendly reproductive health clinics, nurses offer safe spaces where young people can obtain accurate information and barrier methods without facing social hostility or moral scolding. Furthermore, nurses must advocate for policy reforms that protect women's health, improve access to safe healthcare services, and shift the societal focus from punishment and stigma to education, empowerment, and comprehensive care.
