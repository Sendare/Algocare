Unsafe Abortion in Nigeria

Definition and Scope of Unsafe Abortion in Nigeria

An unsafe abortion is defined by the World Health Organization as a procedure for terminating an unintended pregnancy carried out either by persons lacking the necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards, or both. In Nigeria, the burden of unsafe abortion is remarkably high, contributing heavily to the country's maternal mortality ratio. Millions of pregnancies occur unintended each year due to unmet needs for modern contraception, financial constraints, and educational gaps. Many affected young women and adolescents resort to clandestine procedures performed by unqualified providers in unsterile settings out of fear of social stigma and judgment. Consequently, Nigeria records significant numbers of emergency hospital admissions daily for complications arising from these unsafe procedures, making it a major public health concern for nursing professionals and healthcare planners alike.

Legal and Socio-Religious Context in Nigeria

The legal framework governing abortion in Nigeria is deeply restrictive, rooted in the Penal Code applied in the Northern states and the Criminal Code applied in the Southern states. Under these laws, abortion is strictly illegal unless it is performed to save the pregnant woman's life. Anyone who performs an abortion, as well as the woman herself, can face severe prison sentences. This restrictive legal environment drives the practice underground, preventing open dialogue and safe clinical access. Furthermore, Nigerian society is deeply religious, with pervasive Islamic and Christian moral values that largely view termination as a sin. This cultural and religious condemnation creates a powerful social stigma. Victims of botched procedures often suffer in silence, delaying crucial medical treatment because they fear police arrest, community gossip, or religious judgment.

Etiology and Contributing Factors

  • Unmet Contraceptive Needs: Limited access to reliable family planning methods and widespread myths about modern contraceptives.
  • Poverty and Economic Hardship: Inability to care for an additional child, coupled with the high cost of raising children in current economic realities.
  • Gender Inequality and Sexual Violence: High rates of rape, incest, and coerced sex where victims lack power to negotiate safe sex.
  • Educational Gaps: Lack of comprehensive sexuality education in schools, leaving adolescents ignorant about reproductive biology.
  • Peer Pressure and Stigma: Fear of dropping out of school or being ostracized by family and community after an unplanned pregnancy.

Common Methods Used and Associated Pathophysiology

Desperate women use various crude methods to terminate unwanted pregnancies, each causing distinct pathological damage:

  1. Insertion of Sharp Objects: Using knitting needles, bicycle spokes, or cassava stems directly into the cervix to puncture the amniotic sac. This frequently causes severe cervical lacerations, uterine perforation, and hemorrhage.
  2. Toxic Chemical Douching: Using caustic substances like potassium permanganate, bleach, or concentrated detergent solutions vaginally. This causes chemical burns of the vagina, severe tissue necrosis, and systemic poisoning.
  3. Ingestion of Concoctions: Drinking heavy herbal mixtures, animal blood, or overdoses of unprescribed medications. This leads to acute gastritis, liver damage, heavy systemic bleeding, and incomplete expulsion of products of conception.
  4. Abdominal Trauma: Traditional massage practitioners applying excessive external pressure on the abdomen to crush the fetus, resulting in internal organ rupture and internal bleeding.

Clinical Presentation and Complications

Patients presenting to emergency wards with complications from unsafe abortions typically exhibit signs of hypovolemic shock, sepsis, or acute abdominal pain.

A common misconception is that patients will always confess to having an induced abortion; however, many present denying interference out of fear, claiming spontaneous miscarriage instead. Nurses must maintain high clinical suspicion.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis

Comprehensive nursing assessment begins with stabilizing the patient's airway, breathing, and circulation (the ABCs). Nurses must rapidly evaluate vital signs to detect early signs of hemorrhagic or septic shock. A focused obstetric history should be gathered gently, without moralizing language, determining the last menstrual period, estimated gestational age, and details of any substances or instruments used. Physical assessment includes abdominal palpation for tenderness or guarding, and a sterile speculum examination, performed only by skilled clinicians, to inspect the cervix and remove any visible products of conception in the vaginal vault.

Relevant nursing diagnoses often include:

  • Risk for Fluid Volume Deficit related to excessive uterine bleeding.
  • Acute Pain related to uterine cramping, trauma, or infection.
  • Risk for Infection related to unsterile instrumentation and retained tissue.
  • Anxiety related to fear of legal consequences, social stigma, and physical health threats.

Emergency Medical and Surgical Management

Management of unsafe abortion complications requires immediate medical stabilization followed by surgical evacuation of the uterus when products of conception are retained. Intravenous access must be secured immediately with large-bore cannulas for fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion if hemorrhage is severe. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics are administered promptly to treat or prevent ascending pelvic infections and sepsis.

Surgical evacuation is primarily performed using Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) or Dilatation and Curettage (D&C), though MVA is preferred in modern practice due to lower complication rates and less trauma to the uterine lining. For cases involving uterine perforation, bowel injury, or peritonitis, emergency exploratory laparotomy is mandatory. Nurses play a crucial role in monitoring vital signs, maintaining strict fluid balance charts, administering blood products safely, and preparing the patient and theatre environment for surgical intervention.

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) and Counseling

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) is a vital strategy designed to reduce maternal death and injury resulting from unsafe abortions. PAC consists of five core elements: emergency treatment of complications, contraception counseling and provision, post-abortion counseling, community empowerment, and linkage to other reproductive health services.

Counseling must be empathetic, confidential, and tailored to the client's emotional and physical state. Many patients experience profound guilt, grief, or anxiety following the ordeal. Nurses provide a safe space to address these emotions without condemnation. Crucially, fertility returns rapidly after an abortion; ovulation can occur within two weeks. Therefore, providing contraceptive counseling and initiating the patient on a chosen modern method before hospital discharge is essential to prevent repeat unintended pregnancies.

Preventive Strategies and the Role of the Nurse

Preventing unsafe abortion in Nigeria requires a multi-sectoral approach where nurses occupy the frontline. Nurses must actively participate in community health education, dispelling myths about modern contraceptives and advocating for youth-friendly reproductive health services in schools and local clinics. By offering non-judgmental counseling, nurses build trust, encouraging adolescents and women to seek safe advice rather than dangerous alternatives.

Furthermore, nurses advocate for policy reforms that expand access to safe reproductive healthcare and protect women's health. Collaboration with community leaders, religious bodies, and traditional birth attendants is essential to bridge the gap between cultural beliefs and safe medical practices. Through education, advocacy, and compassionate clinical care, nurses remain instrumental in reducing the tragic toll of unsafe abortion across Nigerian communities.
