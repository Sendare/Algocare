Establishing PAC Services

Introduction and the Five Elements of Post-Abortion Care

Post-Abortion Care, commonly known as PAC, is a critical global health strategy designed to reduce maternal death and injury resulting from incomplete and unsafe abortions. Establishing a PAC service requires a comprehensive approach rather than just treating a medical emergency in the ward. The global model rests on five interconnected pillars:

  1. Community and service provider partnership: Building bridges with local communities to ensure women know where to seek timely help and reducing stigma.
  2. Counseling: Providing confidential, non-judgmental support to address emotional, physical, and psychological needs.
  3. Treatment of complications: Managing incomplete abortions, sepsis, hemorrhage, or trauma using evidence-based methods like Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) or medical treatment.
  4. Contraceptive and family planning services: Offering immediate access to a chosen method before the client leaves the facility to prevent repeat unintended pregnancies.
  5. Reproductive and other health services: Connecting clients to broader primary healthcare services, STI screening, or referrals when needed.

A common misconception is that PAC is only about clearing the uterus during an emergency. In reality, failing to provide family planning counseling before discharge means the service is incomplete.

Assessing Community Needs and Facility Readiness

Before opening or expanding a PAC clinic, nurse managers must evaluate both the community environment and the health facility's capacity. Needs assessment involves gathering local data on maternal morbidity, cultural barriers, and transportation hurdles that delay women from reaching care. If women in a rural district take hours to reach a clinic, mobile outreach or primary health center task-shifting becomes necessary.

Simultaneously, facility readiness ensures the physical plant can handle emergency obstetric care 24 hours a day, 7 days a week. Clinics must possess reliable water sources, electricity or backup generators, secure waste management systems, and a dedicated room ensuring patient privacy and confidentiality. Without these basic structural foundations, even the best-trained staff will struggle to deliver safe care.

Essential Equipment, Supplies, and Infection Prevention

Clinical safety in PAC depends heavily on maintaining an uninterrupted stock of essential commodities. Nurses play a frontline role in inventory management, ensuring that items never stock-out during an emergency.

  • Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) kits: Reusable, plastic syringes and cannulae used for uterine evacuation.
  • Uterotonics: Medications like oxytocin or misoprostol used to manage or prevent postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Antibiotics: Broad-spectrum drugs to treat or prevent pelvic infection.
  • Contraceptive commodities: Oral pills, injectables, implants, and intrauterine devices (IUDs).
  • Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Gloves, aprons, eye protection, and heavy utility gloves for decontamination.

Strict adherence to infection prevention protocols—such as proper decontamination, thorough washing, and high-level disinfection or sterilization of MVA instruments—protects both clients and healthcare workers from bloodborne pathogens.

Provider Training and Competency-Based Capacity Building

Establishing sustainable PAC services requires a shift from traditional didactic lectures to competency-based training. Healthcare providers—including midwives, nurses, and general medical officers—must demonstrate actual clinical proficiency before performing procedures independently on patients.

Training programs must cover empathetic communication, values clarification exercises to address personal biases, pain management techniques, MVA operation, and management of complications like septic shock or uterine perforation. Mentorship programs after formal workshops ensure that newly trained staff retain their skills and gain confidence in low-resource settings.

Clinical Management of Complications and Referral Pathways

When a woman presents with complications from an incomplete or unsafe abortion, rapid clinical triage is lifesaving. Nurses must quickly assess vital signs to identify signs of shock, heavy bleeding, or pelvic infection.

Treatment depends on gestational age and clinical status. For incomplete abortion with minimal bleeding and a soft, non-infected uterus in early gestation, medical management with misoprostol or surgical evacuation via MVA is performed. If severe complications arise—such as suspected uterine perforation, bowel injury, or severe septic shock—the patient requires immediate intravenous fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, blood transfusion, and urgent surgical intervention by a gynecologist.

Establishing clear, functional referral pathways with higher-level hospitals ensures that lower-tier primary clinics can safely transfer unstable patients without bureaucratic delays.

Integrating Family Planning and Counseling Services

PAC counseling is rooted in client-centered communication. Nurses must use empathetic listening, ensure absolute privacy, and avoid imposing personal opinions. The counseling session provides emotional reassurance and prepares the client for physical recovery.

Crucially, contraceptive counseling must be offered before the client leaves the facility. Because ovulation can resume as early as two weeks after an abortion, women are at immediate risk of a new pregnancy if contraception is delayed. Offering a full range of methods—including barrier methods, pills, injectables, implants, and IUDs—allows the client to choose what best fits her reproductive goals. Providing these commodities directly on-site eliminates the barrier of an extra trip to a pharmacy.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and Quality Improvement

To ensure PAC services remain safe, effective, and accessible over time, facilities must implement continuous monitoring and evaluation (M&E) frameworks. Clinic managers track key indicators such as the number of PAC clients treated, complication rates, turnaround times, and uptake rates of modern contraception.

Regular audits and confidential mortality or morbidity reviews help teams identify gaps—such as recurring stock-outs of MVA kits or delays in emergency transfers—without assigning blame. A strong quality improvement culture encourages nursing staff to suggest workflow changes, update clinical protocols, and maintain high standards of compassionate, patient-centered care.
