Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA)

Introduction and Definition of Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA)

Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) is a safe, highly effective surgical technique used to empty the uterine cavity using a specially designed vacuum syringe. Unlike electric vacuum aspiration systems that rely on motorized pumps, MVA uses a handheld, reusable plastic syringe that creates a vacuum of up to 600 to 660 mmHg. This procedure is primarily performed for the management of early pregnancy loss, incomplete miscarriages, and induced abortions up to the 12th to 14th week of gestation. In many Nigerian healthcare facilities, MVA has largely replaced traditional sharp metallic curettage due to its lower complication rates, reduced blood loss, and the fact that it can be performed outpatient under local anesthesia without requiring a main operating theatre.

Indications and Contraindications for MVA

Clinical decision-making for MVA depends on accurate patient assessment. Knowing when the procedure is appropriate—and when it is dangerous—prevents severe morbidity.

  • Incomplete abortion: Removal of retained products of conception when the os is open and bleeding is persistent.
  • Missed abortion or blighted ovum: Evacuation of non-viable intrauterine contents up to 14 weeks from the last menstrual period.
  • Induced abortion: Safe elective termination of early pregnancies.
  • Endometrial biopsy: Obtaining tissue samples for diagnostic histology.
  • Acute pelvic inflammatory disease: Active infection must be treated with antibiotics before instrumentation to prevent spreading pathogens.
  • Uterine gestation beyond 14 weeks: Exceeds the capacity and safe limits of standard MVA cannulae.
  • Known or suspected bleeding disorders: Requires hematological stabilization and specialized management before any surgical intervention.

Components of the MVA Instrument and Assembly

The MVA kit consists of durable, autoclavable parts designed to work as a closed vacuum system.

  • The Aspirator Syringe: A 60ml medical-grade plastic cylinder with a plunger, a locking mechanism (arms), and a valve. The plunger creates and maintains the negative pressure.
  • The Valve: Controls the flow between the cannula and the syringe barrel, stopping or releasing suction instantly via pinch-valves or push-buttons.
  • The Cannulae (Karman catheters): Flexible, semi-rigid plastic tubes ranging from 4mm to 12mm in diameter. They feature side-openings near the tip to prevent tissue grabbing and reduce perforation risks.
  • Adapter Rings and O-rings: Ensure an airtight seal within the syringe barrel and connect different cannula sizes securely to the valve base.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Patient Counseling

Thorough preparation reduces procedural anxiety and identifies risks. Empathetic counseling builds trust, especially in sensitive contexts like post-abortion care or pregnancy loss.

  1. History and Physical Examination: Confirm gestational age, check vital signs, and assess for anemia or signs of infection.
  2. Emotional Support: Validate the patient's feelings, explain the steps clearly using simple terms, and address any fears regarding pain or future fertility.
  3. Pain Management: Administer non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or a paracervical block using 1% lidocaine to minimize uterine cramping.
  4. Informed Consent: Ensure the patient understands the procedure, potential risks, and post-procedure expectations, then obtain written consent.
  5. Bladder Evacuation: Instruct the patient to empty her bladder immediately before the procedure to displace the uterus forward and reduce discomfort.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Performing MVA

Executing an MVA requires sterile technique, gentle handling, and precise coordination between the clinician and the assisting nurse.

  1. Preparation and Setup: Assemble the pre-tested MVA syringe (ensuring the vacuum is locked) and lay out sterile speculums, tenacula, and cannulae.
  2. Pelvic Examination: Perform a bimanual exam to determine uterine size, position (anteverted or retroverted), and consistency.
  3. Visualization and Antisepsis: Insert a speculum, visualize the cervix, and cleanse the ectocervix with an antiseptic solution.
  4. Cervical Stabilization: Apply a single-tooth tenaculum to the anterior lip of the cervix and gently straighten the uterocervical axis.
  5. Sounding: Pass a sterile uterine sound to measure depth and direction.
  6. Cannulation and Evacuation: Select the appropriate cannula size, insert it gently through the internal os, connect the prepared vacuum syringe, release the pinch-valve, and rotate the syringe 180 degrees in both directions while moving it gently in and out until the cavity is empty.

Post-Procedure Care and Monitoring

Following the evacuation, immediate and structured post-procedure care ensures patient safety and detects early complications.

  • Immediate Recovery: Monitor vital signs (blood pressure, pulse rate) and assess vaginal bleeding intensity every 15 minutes for the first hour.
  • Tissue Inspection: Empty the syringe contents into a strainer or glass dish to inspect the tissue, verifying products of conception and ensuring completeness.
  • Uterine Tone Assessment: Palpate the abdomen to ensure the uterus is firm and well-contracted; administer uterotonics like oxytocin or misoprostol if atonic bleeding occurs.
  • Discharge Counseling: Instruct the patient on warning signs (heavy bleeding, severe pelvic pain, foul-smelling discharge, fever), advise pelvic rest for two weeks, and discuss future contraceptive options before she leaves the clinic.

Recognition and Management of Complications

Although MVA is exceptionally safe, nurses must remain vigilant for potential adverse events. Recognizing early signs prevents life-threatening deterioration.

  • Uterine Perforation: Suspected if the sound or cannula goes deeper than measured or passes without resistance. Stop the procedure immediately, monitor vitals, and observe for internal bleeding or organ injury.
  • Hemorrhage: Excessive bleeding caused by uterine atony or retained tissue. Treat with bimanual massage, uterotonics, and re-aspiration if tissue remains.
  • Infection (Endometritis): Presents with fever, lower abdominal pain, and purulent discharge days after the procedure. Manage promptly with broad-spectrum antibiotics.
  • Incomplete Evacuation: Persistent cramping and bleeding with positive pregnancy tests. Requires a repeat evacuation procedure.

Instrument Processing, Cleaning, and Sterilization

Proper reprocessing of MVA equipment prevents healthcare-associated infections and extends the lifespan of reusable instruments.

  1. Decontamination: Immediately submerge used syringes and cannulae in a 0.5% chlorine solution for 10 minutes to kill bloodborne pathogens.
  2. Cleaning: Wash all parts thoroughly in soapy water using a soft brush, flushing warm water through cannulae and syringe valves to remove organic matter.
  3. Rinsing and Drying: Rinse with clean water and allow components to air-dry completely.
  4. High-Level Disinfection or Sterilization: Process items using high-level disinfection (boiling for 20 minutes or chemical soaking) or autoclaving according to manufacturer guidelines, ensuring silicone O-rings are lubricated with medical-grade silicone oil to maintain the vacuum seal.
