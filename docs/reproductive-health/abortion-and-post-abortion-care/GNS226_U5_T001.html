Maternal Morbidity from Unsafe Abortion

Maternal Morbidity and Unsafe Abortion in Nigeria

Unsafe abortion remains a major driver of preventable maternal mortality and morbidity across Nigeria. Defined by the World Health Organization as a procedure for terminating an unintended pregnancy carried out either by persons lacking the necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards, unsafe procedures contribute significantly to the burden on local healthcare facilities. Many women present to emergency wards with severe hemorrhage, sepsis, and organ failure following clandestine interventions. Cultural stigma, restrictive legal frameworks, and socioeconomic barriers often delay care-seeking, worsening clinical outcomes. Midwives and nurses are frequently the first point of contact, making early recognition of complications critical for survival.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms of Abortion Injury

Physical trauma during termination attempts typically stems from the blind insertion of foreign instruments into the uterine cavity. Perforation of the uterine wall can injure adjacent pelvic structures, including the bowel, bladder, and major blood vessels. Chemical agents introduced into the vagina or uterus cause extensive local necrosis, chemical peritonitis, and rapid systemic absorption leading to multi-organ dysfunction. When retained products of conception remain inside the uterus, the myometrium fails to contract effectively, preventing vessel closure and resulting in continuous, heavy postpartum-like bleeding.

Classification and Types of Complications

  • Immediate Complications: Severe hemorrhage, uterine perforation, lacerations of the cervix, and acute trauma to adjacent pelvic organs.
  • Delayed Complications: Pelvic inflammatory disease, secondary infertility, chronic pelvic pain, and Asherman's syndrome resulting from intrauterine scarring.
  • Systemic Complications: Septic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), acute kidney injury from prolonged hypoperfusion, and electrolyte imbalances.

Etiology and Sociodemographic Risk Factors

Vulnerability to unsafe abortion is heavily influenced by systemic inequalities. Young age, lack of formal education, low socioeconomic status, and rural residence strongly correlate with higher rates of morbidity. Unintended pregnancies frequently occur due to unmet contraceptive needs, misinformation, or gender power imbalances that limit a woman's ability to negotiate safe sexual practices. In resource-constrained settings, the absence of youth-friendly reproductive health services pushes vulnerable populations toward clandestine practitioners.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A systematic clinical evaluation begins with a rapid assessment of hemodynamic stability, checking vital signs for signs of shock. Nurses must take a focused reproductive history while maintaining strict confidentiality, noting the estimated gestational age and the nature of any intervention performed. Physical examination includes abdominal palpation for tenderness and a sterile speculum examination to inspect active bleeding, cervical dilation, and tissue presentation. Bedside point-of-care testing or laboratory investigations typically include packed cell volume, blood typing and crossmatching, and serum electrolytes to guide resuscitation.

Emergency Nursing Management and Stabilization

Emergency stabilization requires immediate vascular access using large-bore cannulae for aggressive fluid resuscitation and blood transfusion. If septic shock is suspected, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics must be administered promptly without waiting for culture results. Uterotonic agents such as oxytocin or misoprostol are administered to encourage uterine contraction and minimize ongoing hemorrhage. Nurses prepare the patient for surgical evacuation if products of conception are retained, monitoring input and output closely via indwelling catheterization to protect renal function.

Post-Abortion Care and Holistic Rehabilitation

Post-abortion care encompasses medical treatment, psychological support, and contraceptive counseling delivered in a non-judgmental environment. Physical recovery involves monitoring for persistent bleeding, infection, and anemia resolution. Psychological support is equally vital, as patients frequently experience guilt, anxiety, or trauma associated with stigma and near-miss mortality events. Comprehensive discharge planning includes providing chosen contraceptive methods before the patient leaves the facility, as fertility can return within two weeks of pregnancy termination.

Preventative Strategies, Community Education, and Policy

Reducing abortion-related morbidity requires multi-level interventions ranging from grassroots community education to healthcare policy reform. Nurses play an active role in dispelling reproductive myths, expanding access to modern contraceptives, and advocating for youth-friendly clinics. Interventions must engage traditional and religious leaders to shift community attitudes toward reproductive health. Strengthening healthcare infrastructure ensures that when complications do arise, treatment is rapid, equitable, and free from discrimination.
