Maternal Morbidity from Unsafe Abortion

Introduction to Maternal Morbidity and Unsafe Abortion in Nigeria

Maternal morbidity and mortality stemming from unsafe abortion remain critical public health crises in Nigeria, significantly impacting the healthcare system and maternal well-being. An unsafe abortion is defined by the World Health Organization as a procedure for terminating an unintended pregnancy carried out either by persons lacking the necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards. In Nigeria, restrictive legal frameworks, societal stigma, poverty, and limited access to modern contraceptive services drive desperate women and girls toward clandestine procedures. These often involve non-medical personnel, traditional concoctions, sharp objects, and unsterile instruments. The resulting morbidities are extensive, ranging from severe hemorrhage and pelvic infections to chronic pelvic pain, secondary infertility, and psychological trauma. For nursing students, understanding this reality is vital because nurses form the frontline of emergency obstetric care. Recognizing the profound socio-cultural context of unsafe abortion enables nurses to approach affected patients with empathy, free from judgment, while delivering life-saving interventions. Addressing this issue requires not only clinical competence in recognizing early signs of shock and sepsis but also active participation in community education and advocacy for safe reproductive healthcare policies across Nigeria.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms of Abortion-Related Injury

The pathophysiology of abortion-related injury involves acute mechanical trauma, chemical toxicity, and severe infections that can rapidly precipitate multisystem organ failure if left untreated. When unsterile instruments or catheters are forcefully introduced into the uterine cavity, mechanical perforation of the myometrium, cervix, or adjacent pelvic organs such as the bowel and bladder frequently occurs. This perforation leads to internal hemorrhage and peritonitis. Furthermore, incomplete evacuation of products of conception prevents the uterus from contracting effectively, resulting in uterine atony and profound postpartum-like hemorrhage, which quickly depletes clotting factors and causes hypovolemic shock. Chemical agents and toxic traditional concoctions introduced into the vagina or uterus cause direct cellular necrosis, sloughing of the endometrial lining, and chemical peritonitis or systemic toxicity. Infectious pathogens—most commonly ascending polymicrobial flora from the lower genital tract—rapidly colonize necrotic tissue within the uterine cavity. This triggers endometritis, parametritis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and bacteremia, which can progress to severe sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and septic shock. Nurses must understand these underlying mechanisms to anticipate rapid clinical deterioration, monitor vital signs meticulously, and administer prompt, targeted interventions like fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and blood component therapy.

Classification and Types of Complications from Unsafe Abortion

Complications arising from unsafe abortion are broadly classified into short-term (acute) and long-term (chronic) categories, each presenting distinct clinical challenges for nurses and healthcare teams. Acute complications manifest within days of the procedure and represent medical emergencies. These include severe hemorrhage, hypovolemic shock, pelvic sepsis, septic shock, acute renal failure secondary to prolonged hypotension or nephrotoxic chemical agents, and visceral injuries such as bowel perforation and peritonitis. Immediate surgical or medical intervention is required to save the patient's life. Chronic or long-term complications develop weeks to years later, significantly diminishing the woman's quality of life and reproductive health. Chronic pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia, and tubal occlusion are frequent outcomes of post-abortion infections, directly leading to secondary infertility or an increased risk of future ectopic pregnancies. Additionally, cervical incompetence resulting from traumatic mechanical dilatation can cause recurrent mid-trimester miscarriages in subsequent pregnancies. Psychological morbidity, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), severe anxiety, and depression, is also prevalent. Comprehensive nursing care requires vigilance in identifying both acute life threats during initial admission and subtle chronic reproductive or psychological sequelae during follow-up visits.

Etiology and Sociodemographic Risk Factors

The etiology of unsafe abortion is multifaceted, driven by a complex interplay of sociodemographic, economic, cultural, and systemic risk factors. In Nigeria, young women, adolescents, and unmarried individuals are disproportionately affected due to pervasive cultural stigma surrounding premarital sex, lack of comprehensive sexuality education, and restricted access to youth-friendly reproductive health services. Low socioeconomic status, poverty, and rural residency further exacerbate the problem; women lacking financial resources cannot afford safe, legal medical termination services or travel to specialized centers, forcing them to resort to clandestine, unqualified practitioners. Gender inequality, intimate partner violence, and sexual assault are also significant underlying etiologies, leaving victims with unwanted pregnancies and no agency or support system. Furthermore, systemic barriers within the healthcare infrastructure—such as provider bias, conscientious objection without proper referral systems, and Stock-outs of essential contraceptive supplies—create an environment where safe care is inaccessible. Understanding these root causes is crucial for nursing students, as effective nursing care extends beyond the hospital bedside into community advocacy, targeted sex education, and addressing the social determinants of health that perpetuate maternal morbidity.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Clinical assessment and diagnostic evaluation of a patient presenting with complications from an unsafe abortion require a rapid, systematic, and compassionate approach. Nurses and midwives often perform the initial triage, assessing airway, breathing, and circulation (the ABCs) to identify signs of hemorrhagic or septic shock. A focused history must be gathered discreetly and confidentially, noting the last menstrual period, estimated gestational age, methods used to induce the abortion, and the time elapsed since the procedure, while remaining non-judgmental to build trust. Physical examination includes vital signs monitoring, abdominal palpation for tenderness, guarding, or rigidity (suggestive of peritonitis or uterine perforation), and a sterile speculum or bimanual pelvic examination to assess cervical dilation, uterine size, active bleeding, and foul-smelling discharge. Diagnostic investigations are essential to guide medical and surgical management. Baseline laboratory tests typically include a complete blood count (CBC) to check for anemia or leukocytosis, blood typing and crossmatching for potential transfusions, coagulation profile, serum electrolytes, urea, and creatinine to evaluate renal function, and a pregnancy test. Imaging studies, particularly pelvic ultrasonography, are invaluable in confirming retained products of conception, assessing endometrial thickness, and detecting free fluid in the peritoneal cavity indicative of visceral perforation or internal hemorrhage.

Emergency Nursing Management and Stabilization

Emergency nursing management and stabilization of a patient suffering from unsafe abortion complications focus on restoring hemodynamic stability, halting hemorrhage, controlling infection, and preparing the patient for urgent surgical evacuation if necessary. Nurses must immediately secure large-bore intravenous (IV) access (16-gauge or 18-gauge) and initiate aggressive fluid resuscitation with isotonic crystalloids like normal saline or Ringer's lactate. If the patient exhibits signs of severe hemorrhage, crossmatched blood products must be ordered and transfused promptly. Oxygen therapy should be administered via nasal cannula or face mask to maintain adequate tissue oxygenation. For patients presenting with sepsis, blood cultures must be drawn, and broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics (such as a combination of ampicillin, gentamicin, and metronidazole) should be initiated within the first hour of admission to cover polymicrobial infections. Uterotonic agents, such as oxytocin or misoprostol, may be administered to stimulate uterine contractions and reduce bleeding if products of conception are still partially retained. The nurse prepares the patient for emergency uterine evacuation—typically using Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) or surgical curettage—to remove retained tissue stopping the bleeding. Throughout this critical phase, meticulous intake and output monitoring via an indwelling urinary catheter is essential to evaluate renal perfusion and response to fluid therapy.

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) and Holistic Rehabilitation

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) is a comprehensive public health and clinical model designed to treat abortion complications, provide post-abortion contraception, and link women to broader reproductive and mental health services. Once the acute emergency has been stabilized, the holistic rehabilitation phase begins. PAC places strong emphasis on empathetic, non-judgmental counseling, recognizing that patients often experience intense fear, guilt, stigma, or psychological trauma following an unsafe procedure. Nurses play a pivotal role in providing emotional support and facilitating mental health referrals when post-traumatic stress or depression is suspected. Another cornerstone of PAC is contraceptive counseling and provision. Because a woman can return to fertility within weeks of an abortion, offering a wide range of modern contraceptive methods—including oral pills, injectables, implants, and intrauterine devices (IUDs)—before discharge is crucial to preventing repeat unintended pregnancies. Furthermore, holistic rehabilitation includes nutritional support for recovering anemic patients, screening and management for chronic reproductive complications such as pelvic inflammatory disease, and education on warning signs requiring immediate medical re-evaluation. By delivering compassionate PAC, nurses help bridge the gap between emergency medical intervention and long-term wellness.

Preventative Strategies, Counseling, and Policy in Nursing Practice

Preventative strategies, comprehensive counseling, and healthcare policy reform are powerful tools in nursing practice to reduce the burden of maternal morbidity from unsafe abortion in Nigeria. Nurses and midwives are uniquely positioned to advocate for and deliver preventative interventions at both the grassroots and institutional levels. This includes participating in community-based sex education programs, promoting adolescent reproductive health, and destigmatizing the use of modern contraceptives. Clinical nurses must provide accessible, confidential, and youth-friendly family planning services to minimize rates of unintended pregnancies. Furthermore, nursing professionals must engage in policy advocacy, supporting legislative reforms that expand access to safe, legal abortion care where permitted, and strengthening healthcare systems to ensure healthcare providers are trained in safe uterine evacuation techniques like Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA). Nurses also play a role in challenging provider bias, ensuring that all women receive respectful, non-discriminatory care regardless of the circumstances surrounding their pregnancy. Through active community engagement, rigorous education, and evidence-based policy advocacy, Nigerian nurses can help drive down maternal morbidity rates and champion safe reproductive health for all women.
