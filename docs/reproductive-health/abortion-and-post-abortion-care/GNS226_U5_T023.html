Termination of Unwanted Pregnancy

Introduction and Legal Framework of Pregnancy Termination in Nigeria

Termination of pregnancy is a critical public health and clinical issue in Nigeria, heavily governed by restrictive federal laws. Under the Nigerian Criminal Code (applicable in the Southern states) and the Penal Code (applicable in the Northern states), abortion is strictly illegal unless it is performed strictly to save the pregnant woman's life. The law criminalizes both the person undergoing the procedure and the practitioner performing it, carrying severe prison sentences. This restrictive legal framework creates an environment of fear and secrecy, forcing many women and girls with unwanted pregnancies to resort to clandestine, illegal procedures. As a nursing student in Nigeria, it is essential to understand this legal landscape not only to pass licensure examinations but also to recognize how criminalization impacts maternal morbidity and mortality. Despite the laws, unsafe abortions remain a leading cause of maternal death and hemorrhage across Nigerian hospitals. Nurses must navigate these ethical and legal boundaries by providing compassionate, non-judgmental emergency care to women suffering from complications of termination, while strictly adhering to professional codes of conduct and the limits of the law. Understanding these statutes helps clinicians advocate for safe maternal health practices and recognize the socio-legal barriers that prevent women from accessing legitimate reproductive healthcare services in the country.

Terminology, Classifications, and Safe vs. Unsafe Abortion

Understanding the terminology surrounding the termination of pregnancy is vital for accurate clinical documentation and professional communication. Abortion refers to the premature expulsion or extraction of a conceptus from the uterus prior to the period of viability, generally considered before 28 weeks of gestation in Nigeria. Abortions are broadly classified as spontaneous (commonly known as a miscarriage, occurring naturally due to chromosomal or anatomical abnormalities) or induced (deliberately brought about by medical or surgical means). Induced abortions are further categorized into safe and unsafe procedures. A safe abortion is defined by the World Health Organization as one performed using methods recommended by the WHO, appropriate to the gestational age, and carried out by trained healthcare personnel in a sanitary environment. Conversely, an unsafe abortion is a procedure for terminating an unintended pregnancy carried out either by persons lacking the necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards, or both. In Nigeria, unsafe abortions are frequently performed by traditional practitioners, unqualified chemists, or self-administered toxic concoctions, leading to severe physical complications such as incomplete expulsion, severe hemorrhage, sepsis, and uterine perforation. Nurses play an essential role in differentiating these classifications during patient intake to ensure appropriate, stigma-free emergency and supportive clinical management.

Etiology and Contributing Factors to Unwanted Pregnancies

Unwanted and unintended pregnancies are the root cause of both safe and unsafe induced abortions. Identifying the etiology and contributing factors is critical for community health nurses and midwives working to lower the incidence of unwanted pregnancies in Nigeria. Key factors include lack of or limited access to modern contraceptive methods, cultural and religious barriers to family planning education, poverty, and gender inequality, which often leaves women and young girls unable to negotiate safe sex. Additionally, inadequate comprehensive sexuality education in schools and communities contributes to widespread myths and misconceptions about fertility and contraception. Sexual violence, rape, and incest are also significant contributors to unwanted pregnancies, particularly in vulnerable populations and conflict-affected zones. Adolescents and young adults are disproportionately affected due to peer pressure, experimentation, and structural barriers that prevent them from accessing youth-friendly reproductive health services. As healthcare providers, nurses must recognize these socio-economic and cultural determinants to offer empathetic care and target educational interventions effectively. Addressing these underlying causes requires a multi-sectoral approach involving community leaders, policy makers, and healthcare professionals working together to empower women and girls with reproductive autonomy and resources.

Clinical Assessment and Pre-Termination Counseling

Clinical assessment and pre-termination counseling are vital components of comprehensive reproductive healthcare where legal and safe services are available. The primary goal of a clinical assessment is to accurately determine the gestational age of the pregnancy through a thorough menstrual history, bimanual pelvic examination, and obstetric ultrasonography. Nurses must also screen for ectopic pregnancy, pelvic inflammatory disease, bleeding disorders, and medical comorbidities that could contraindicate certain termination methods. Laboratory investigations, including a complete blood count, blood grouping and rhesus factor testing, and hemoglobin levels, are essential baseline requirements. Pre-termination counseling must be non-directive, confidential, and empathetic, ensuring that the patient is making a voluntary and informed choice. The nurse or counselor should discuss the available methods, expected physical sensations, potential complications, and post-procedure care. In the context of Nigeria, where legal restrictions are tight, nurses must also assess patients presenting with post-abortion complications with absolute neutrality and confidentiality, prioritizing life-saving emergency management over moral judgment. Compassionate communication builds trust, reduces patient anxiety, and ensures that women receive the holistic psychological and medical support they need during a vulnerable time.

Medical and Surgical Methods of Pregnancy Termination

Medical and surgical methods are the two primary approaches used globally for the safe termination of pregnancy, depending on gestational age and clinical guidelines. Medical termination involves the sequential administration of pharmacological agents, most commonly a combination of mifepristone (an antiprogestin) followed by misoprostol (a prostaglandin analogue), or misoprostol alone where mifepristone is unavailable. This method induces uterine contractions and cervical softening to expel the products of conception, mimicking a natural miscarriage. It is typically effective in early pregnancies (up to 10 to 12 weeks gestation) and allows the patient to avoid surgical intervention. Surgical methods include Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) and Dilatation and Evacuation (D&E). MVA is a safe, highly effective outpatient procedure that uses a handheld plastic vacuum syringe to empty the uterine contents, particularly recommended for first-trimester terminations. Dilatation and Evacuation involves mechanical cervical dilation followed by the use of surgical instruments and suction curettage for pregnancies further along in the second trimester. Nurses play a critical role in preparing the patient, monitoring vital signs and pain levels during and after the procedure, administering prescribed medications such as analgesics and uterotonics, and providing clear discharge instructions regarding warning signs and follow-up care.

Complications of Unsafe Termination and Early Recognition

Unsafe terminations—frequently occurring in clandestine settings across Nigeria—carry a high risk of severe, life-threatening complications. Nurses must be highly vigilant in recognizing these complications early to institute rapid, lifesaving interventions. The most common immediate complication is severe hemorrhage, resulting from incomplete abortion, uterine atony, or traumatic cervical and uterine lacerations caused by unsterile instruments like knitting needles, sticks, or catheter tubes. Sepsis is another critical danger, arising from the introduction of unsterile foreign bodies and bacteria into the uterine cavity, which can rapidly progress to pelvic peritonitis, septic shock, and multi-organ failure. Uterine perforation is a severe mechanical injury that can damage adjacent abdominal organs such as the bowel and bladder, leading to acute abdomen and internal bleeding. Long-term complications include chronic pelvic pain, secondary infertility due to intrauterine adhesions (Asherman's syndrome), and psychological trauma such as depression and post-traumatic stress disorder. When assessing a patient presenting with acute lower abdominal pain, abnormal vaginal bleeding, fever, and foul-smelling discharge following a suspected termination, nurses must immediately check vital signs, establish intravenous access, send blood samples for cross-matching, and prepare the patient for potential emergency surgical evacuation or exploratory laparotomy.

Comprehensive Post-Abortion Care (PAC) Nursing Interventions

Comprehensive Post-Abortion Care (PAC) is a vital global and national strategy designed to reduce the high rates of maternal death and disability resulting from incomplete and unsafe abortions. PAC consists of three core pillars: emergency treatment for abortion complications, contraceptive and family planning counseling and services, and linkage to other reproductive health services. As a nurse, providing PAC requires a compassionate, respectful, and non-judgmental attitude, ensuring that every woman receives high-quality care regardless of the circumstances surrounding her pregnancy. Emergency nursing interventions include stabilizing vital signs, managing acute hemorrhage with uterotonics (such as oxytocin or misoprostol), initiating broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics if sepsis is suspected, and assisting with uterine evacuation (such as MVA) to clear retained products of conception. Emotional and psychological support is equally important, as patients often experience fear, guilt, or trauma. Furthermore, PAC provides an ideal window of opportunity for contraceptive counseling, allowing the nurse to help the patient choose and initiate an effective method of birth control before discharge. Administering RhoGAM to Rhesus-negative women is also a critical preventative step to prevent isoimmunization. Effective PAC not only saves lives but also empowers women with the knowledge and tools to prevent future unintended pregnancies.

Contraceptive Counseling and Preventing Future Unwanted Pregnancies

Contraceptive counseling and the prevention of future unwanted pregnancies are the ultimate goals of comprehensive reproductive health nursing. After an abortion or miscarriage, a woman's body recovers fertility rapidly, with ovulation potentially occurring within two weeks. Therefore, initiating effective contraception before discharge from the healthcare facility is crucial. Nurses play a frontline role in educating patients about the full range of modern contraceptive options available in Nigeria, including short-acting methods (male and female condoms, oral contraceptive pills, injectables like Depo-Provera) and long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs such as intrauterine devices and subdermal implants), as well as permanent methods like tubal ligation. Counseling must be tailored to the individual's lifestyle, medical history, reproductive goals, and personal preferences. For example, emergency contraception pills should be discussed as a backup method for unprotected intercourse or contraceptive failure, rather than as a primary routine method. Nurses must also actively address myths, misconceptions, and cultural fears associated with modern family planning methods in Nigerian communities. By empowering women and couples with accurate knowledge and accessible contraceptive supplies, nurses directly contribute to reducing the incidence of unwanted pregnancies, decreasing reliance on unsafe abortions, and improving overall maternal and child health outcomes.
