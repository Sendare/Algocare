Termination of Unwanted Pregnancy

Legal Framework and Status of Abortion in Nigeria

Nigeria operates under restrictive laws regarding the termination of pregnancy, primarily governed by the Criminal Code Act (applicable in the southern states) and the Penal Code Act (applicable in the northern states). Under these laws, procuring or attempting to procure a miscarriage is a criminal offense punishable by lengthy prison sentences for both the pregnant individual and the person performing the procedure, unless it is done strictly to save the life of the pregnant woman. This legal restriction creates a profound barrier to safe clinical care, driving many desperate individuals toward clandestine, illegal providers. Understanding these statutes is vital for nursing practice, as clinicians must navigate the delicate boundary between upholding the law, protecting patient confidentiality, and delivering compassionate, life-saving emergency care when complications from unsafe procedures inevitably present in hospital wards.

Definitions: Safe Versus Unsafe Abortion

The World Health Organization explicitly distinguishes between safe and unsafe abortion practices based on the provider's training, the method utilized, and the clinical environment. - **Safe Abortion**: A procedure performed using WHO-recommended methods appropriate to the gestational age, carried out by a skilled healthcare provider in a hygienic, regulated setting. - **Unsafe Abortion**: A procedure for terminating an unwanted pregnancy carried out either by persons lacking necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards, or both.

Etiology of Unwanted Pregnancies

Unwanted or unintended pregnancies arise from a complex interplay of social, economic, and behavioral determinants. A primary driver is the unmet need for modern contraception, often caused by stock-outs at public clinics, fear of hormonal side effects, or cultural and religious opposition to birth control methods. Gender inequality, power imbalances in sexual negotiations, and partner refusal to use barrier methods severely restrict a woman's reproductive autonomy. Additionally, systemic failures in comprehensive sexuality education leave adolescents and young adults unequipped with accurate knowledge regarding human reproduction, fertility cycles, and dual-protection strategies against both infections and unintended conception.

Clinical Assessment and Pre-Termination Counseling

Prior to any intervention, a thorough clinical evaluation is mandatory. This includes confirming the pregnancy via quantitative or qualitative hCG testing, establishing exact gestational age through bimanual examination and obstetric ultrasonography, and screening for sexually transmitted infections or anemia. Blood grouping and rhesus factor determination are essential to identify candidates requiring anti-D immunoglobulin. Concurrently, non-judgmental pre-termination counseling must be initiated. The nurse explores the patient's voluntary decision-making process, ensures she is free from coercion, assesses emotional stability, and provides accurate information regarding what the procedure entails, expected recovery, and available support systems.

Medical and Surgical Methods of Termination

Modern clinical practice utilizes two primary approaches for terminating pregnancy safely, depending primarily on gestational age and medical contraindications. Medical termination typically involves the sequential administration of mifepristone followed by misoprostol, inducing uterine contractions and expulsion of the gestational sac, which is highly effective in early first-trimester pregnancies. Surgical termination employs manual vacuum aspiration (MVA) or electric vacuum aspiration (EVA), which are rapid, highly effective outpatient procedures suitable for early gestations. Dilatation and evacuation (D&E) may be required for more advanced pregnancies. Throughout these procedures, vigilant monitoring for vital sign instability, hemorrhage, and uterine perforation is required.

Complications of Unsafe Termination and Early Recognition

Unsafe termination procedures present severe clinical emergencies in emergency wards across Nigeria. The most frequent immediate complication is acute hemorrhage, resulting from incomplete expulsion, uterine atony, or cervical lacerations. Pelvic sepsis and generalized peritonitis often develop days later due to unsterile instrumentation or retained products of conception, marked by high fever, foul-smelling vaginal discharge, and severe lower abdominal tenderness. Uterine perforation can lead to intra-abdominal visceral injury and severe internal bleeding. Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion, rapidly assessing hemodynamic stability, monitoring for signs of septic shock, and preparing patients for urgent surgical intervention or blood transfusion.

Comprehensive Post-Abortion Care (PAC) Nursing Interventions

Post-Abortion Care is an emergency intervention model designed to treat abortion complications, provide emotional support, and link individuals to necessary reproductive health services. PAC nursing care centers on stabilizing the patient, managing pain, administering prescribed antibiotics and uterotonics, and monitoring lochial flow and vital signs. Beyond physical recovery, empathetic psychological support is critical, as patients often grapple with stigma, guilt, or trauma—especially when managing complications from illegal procedures. Nurses must provide a safe, confidential environment, ensuring the patient is treated with dignity, free from moral judgment or punitive attitudes.

Contraceptive Counseling and Preventing Future Unwanted Pregnancies

The PAC continuum is incomplete without immediate contraceptive counseling and provision. Return of fertility occurs rapidly following the termination of a pregnancy—ovulation can resume as early as two weeks post-procedure. Therefore, birth control discussions should occur before discharge, allowing the patient to choose a method that aligns with her lifestyle and reproductive goals. Options ranging from barrier methods and oral contraceptive pills to long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) like intrauterine devices or implants should be discussed and made available on-site. Empowering patients with effective contraception breaks the cycle of recurrent unintended pregnancies and safeguards long-term reproductive health.
