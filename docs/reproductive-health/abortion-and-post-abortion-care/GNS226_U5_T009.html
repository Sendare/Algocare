Complications of Abortion

Classification and Clinical Types of Abortion

Abortion refers to the termination of pregnancy before the fetus is viable outside the womb, typically defined as before 24 weeks of gestation in Nigeria. Clinical classification helps determine the appropriate medical or surgical intervention and anticipates specific complications.

  • Spontaneous Abortion: Occurs naturally without external interference, commonly known as a miscarriage, frequently triggered by chromosomal abnormalities.
  • Induced Abortion: Deliberately initiated through medical or surgical means, which can be safely performed in legal settings or unsafely in clandestine environments.
  • Incomplete Abortion: Some, but not all, products of conception have expelled from the uterine cavity, leaving retained tissue that causes persistent bleeding.
  • Septic Abortion: Any type of abortion complicated by intrauterine infection, a major contributor to maternal morbidity and mortality across West Africa.

Immediate Complications: Hemorrhage and Hypovolemic Shock

Hemorrhage is the leading acute complication of both incomplete spontaneous miscarriages and unsafe induced abortions. Excessive blood loss stems from uterine atony, retained placental fragments, or direct vascular lacerations during instrumentation.

When heavy bleeding goes unchecked, circulating blood volume plummets, triggering hypovolemic shock. Clinical indicators include tachycardia, tachypnea, falling blood pressure, pallor, cold clammy skin, and altered mental status. In busy Nigerian emergency wards, rapid identification of these signs is lifesaving. Initial management centers on establishing large-bore intravenous access, aggressive fluid resuscitation with crystalloids, administering uterotonics like oxytocin or misoprostol to promote uterine contraction, and preparing the client for emergency surgical evacuation of the uterus.

Infectious Complications and Septic Abortion

Infections arise when pathogens ascend from the lower genital tract into the uterine cavity, frequently occurring after unsterile clandestine procedures using non-sterile instruments or foreign bodies. The resulting spectrum ranges from localized endometritis to severe pelvic inflammatory disease and generalized septic shock.

  • Endometritis: Inflammation of the inner uterine lining, marked by foul-smelling vaginal discharge and lower abdominal tenderness.
  • Parametritis: Spread of infection into the parametrial tissue, causing severe pelvic pain and fixation of the uterus.
  • Pelvic Peritonitis: Extension of infection through the fallopian tubes or via direct uterine wall extension into the peritoneal cavity.

Management requires aggressive broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, careful monitoring of vital signs, fluid resuscitation, and prompt surgical evacuation of infected retained products once antibiotic coverage is established.

Traumatic Complications: Uterine Perforation and Organ Injury

Surgical evacuations and clandestine attempts using rigid probes, curettes, or suction catheters carry a distinct risk of mechanical trauma. The most frequent structural injury is uterine perforation, where an instrument breaches the myometrium. The fundus and the posterior wall are common sites of injury, especially when the uterus is soft, boggy, or retroverted.

When the uterine wall is breached, adjacent intra-abdominal structures—such as loops of the bowel, omentum, or the urinary bladder—risk laceration or thermal injury if electrocautery is used. Clients may present with sudden severe abdominal pain, internal hemorrhage, or signs of an acute abdomen. Small perforations limited to the myometrium with a blunt sound may be managed conservatively with close observation and uterotonics. However, suspected bowel injury or persistent bleeding mandates immediate exploratory laparotomy to inspect internal organs and repair damaged tissues.

Delayed and Long-Term Sequelae

The physical and psychological impact of complicated abortions often extends far beyond the acute recovery window. Chronic pelvic pain resulting from adhesions, secondary infertility from tubal occlusion, and menstrual irregularities frequently trouble survivors of septic or traumatic procedures.

  • Asherman's Syndrome: Intrauterine adhesions forming after aggressive or traumatic curettage, leading to secondary amenorrhea and recurrent pregnancy loss.
  • Tubal Factor Infertility: Chronic inflammation and scarring of the fallopian tubes from untreated pelvic inflammatory disease.
  • Psychological Sequelae: Anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder, compounded in regions where abortion carries severe legal and societal stigma.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Evaluation

Systematic nursing assessment is vital for detecting subtle clinical deterioration in post-abortion clients. The evaluation begins with a thorough history focusing on the LMP, estimated gestational age, bleeding characteristics, and any history of instrument insertion. Vital signs must be monitored serially to catch early trends of hemorrhage or sepsis.

Abdominal palpation checks for uterine tenderness, guarding, or rebound pain suggestive of perforation or peritonitis. Inspection of the perineum evaluates the volume and odor of vaginal bleeding. Laboratory investigations requested by the care team typically include a full blood count for hemoglobin levels, blood typing and cross-matching, clotting profiles, and high vaginal swabs for microscopy and culture. Nurses must maintain a non-judgmental, empathetic demeanor, as fear of social stigma often causes clients to conceal details of unsafe procedures.

Emergency Management and Post-Abortion Care

Comprehensive emergency management bridges acute stabilization with holistic recovery. Stabilization focuses on restoring hemodynamic stability through intravenous access, fluid resuscitation, blood transfusion when indicated, and prompt surgical evacuation of retained tissue. Antibiotic therapy is initiated immediately if signs of infection are present.

Post-abortion care (PAC) extends beyond physical treatment to include compassionate counseling, emotional support, and family planning education. Since fertility returns rapidly after an abortion, discussing and providing contraceptive options before discharge prevents rapid repeat unintended pregnancies. Nurses also screen for emotional distress and link clients to supportive community or psychological resources.
