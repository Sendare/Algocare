Complications of Abortion

Classification and Overview of Abortion Complications

An abortion is the termination of a pregnancy before the fetus can survive outside the womb, typically before 20 to 28 weeks of gestation depending on regional medical guidelines. Abortions are broadly categorized into spontaneous (commonly called a miscarriage, occurring naturally due to chromosomal or anatomical factors) and induced (deliberately brought about medically or surgically). Complications can arise from either type, though unsafe induced abortions carry a significantly higher burden of morbidity and mortality, particularly in settings with restricted legal access.

Clinically, healthcare providers divide these complications based on how soon they appear after the procedure or event:

  • Immediate complications: Occur during the abortion or within 24 hours, primarily involving heavy bleeding or physical trauma.
  • Delayed complications: Develop days to weeks later, most notably infections and retained products of conception.
  • Long-term complications: Manifest months or years later, affecting future reproductive health and psychological well-being.

Immediate Complications: Hemorrhage and Shock

Post-abortion hemorrhage is one of the most dangerous immediate emergencies. It is defined as excessive bleeding that leads to signs of hemodynamic instability. The most common cause is incomplete abortion, where fragments of the placenta or fetus remain inside the uterus, preventing the myometrium from contracting effectively to seal off open blood vessels. Uterine atony, cervical lacerations, and underlying blood clotting disorders can also trigger severe bleeding.

When blood loss is rapid and significant, the patient quickly enters hypovolemic shock. Nurses must monitor vital signs vigilantly for classic warning signs:

  • Rapid, weak pulse (tachycardia)
  • Falling blood pressure (hypotension)
  • Pale, cold, or clammy skin
  • Restlessness, confusion, or dizziness

Immediate medical intervention requires bimanual uterine massage, administration of uterotonic drugs like oxytocin or misoprostol to force uterine contraction, and rapid fluid resuscitation through large-bore intravenous lines.

Traumatic Complications: Uterine Perforation and Organ Damage

Surgical termination carries a mechanical risk of physical injury to the reproductive organs. Uterine perforation occurs when instruments such as a sound, dilator, or curette puncture the muscular wall of the uterus. The risk increases significantly if the uterus is retroverted, if the provider has limited experience, or if the procedure is performed in later gestational weeks.

Perforation can be silent, involving only the uterine wall, or it can be complicated by damage to adjacent structures:

  • Bowel or bladder injury: Sharp instruments can lacerate the intestines or urinary bladder, leading to peritonitis or internal bleeding.
  • Broad ligament hematoma: Blood vessels within the supporting pelvic ligaments can be torn, causing hidden internal bleeding.

Patients with a perforated uterus often experience severe, persistent lower abdominal pain that differs from normal cramping, along with abnormal abdominal tenderness and signs of internal bleeding.

Infectious Complications: Sepsis and Pelvic Inflammatory Disease

Infection is a frequent and serious complication, especially when abortions are performed under unsterile conditions or when tissue is left inside the uterus. Pathogens ascend from the lower genital tract through the open cervix, multiplying rapidly in the warm, blood-rich environment of the uterine cavity.

Common infectious presentations include:

  • Endometritis: Infection of the inner lining of the uterus, marked by foul-smelling vaginal discharge, uterine tenderness, and fever.
  • Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID): Spread of infection to the fallopian tubes and ovaries, which can cause chronic pelvic pain and future tubal factor infertility.
  • Septic Shock: A life-threatening systemic reaction where bacteria and their toxins enter the bloodstream, causing a drastic drop in blood pressure and multiple organ failure.

Nursing care focuses on early recognition of fever, chills, and purulent discharge, followed by prompt administration of broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and supportive care.

Delayed and Long-Term Complications

Not all complications present immediately. Weeks, months, or even years after an abortion, patients may face chronic physical and psychological challenges. One notable delayed physical issue is Asherman's syndrome ( intrauterine adhesions), which occurs when aggressive curettage damages the basal layer of the endometrium, causing the opposing walls of the uterus to scar and stick together. This leads to amenorrhea (absence of menstruation) and secondary infertility.

Long-term reproductive consequences also include an increased risk of premature birth or low birth weight in subsequent pregnancies, often linked to cervical incompetence caused by forced or traumatic mechanical dilation.

On the psychological front, while many women feel relief, some experience prolonged grief, anxiety, or post-traumatic stress symptoms. Comprehensive nursing care must validate these emotional responses without judgment and provide accessible counseling referrals.

Nursing Assessment, Clinical Evaluation, and Care

A thorough nursing assessment is vital for any patient presenting with post-abortion complications. The nurse begins with a detailed health history, asking about the date and type of abortion, the estimated gestational age, the amount and color of vaginal bleeding, and the severity and location of any pain.

Physical and diagnostic evaluations include:

  • Vital signs monitoring: Checking for fever (indicating infection) or tachycardia and hypotension (indicating hemorrhage).
  • Abdominal and pelvic examination: Assessing for direct or rebound tenderness, uterine size, cervical motion tenderness, and abnormal discharge.
  • Laboratory and imaging tests: Reviewing complete blood counts for anemia or leukocytosis, testing human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) levels, and performing a pelvic ultrasound to check for retained products of conception or free fluid in the peritoneal cavity.
