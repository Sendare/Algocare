Collection of Specimens for Microbiological Investigation

Introduction to Microbiological Specimen Collection

Microbiological specimen collection is the process of gathering clinical samples from a patient to identify the exact microorganisms causing an infection. A laboratory cannot treat a guess; it requires a pure sample to isolate bacteria, fungi, parasites, or viruses. When a nurse collects a specimen properly, the healthcare team can choose the right antimicrobial therapy quickly. Conversely, poor collection habits yield misleading results, exposing patients to ineffective drugs and prolonged suffering.

  • Diagnostic Confirmation: Proves the presence of a specific pathogen rather than assuming based on symptoms.
  • Antimicrobial Stewardship: Guides targeted drug therapy instead of broad-spectrum guesswork.
  • Outbreak Monitoring: Helps infection control teams track contagious organisms in hospital wards.

General Principles and Universal Precautions

Every specimen collected from the human body must be treated as potentially infectious. Universal precautions protect both the nurse and the surrounding environment from bloodborne and body fluid pathogens. Hand hygiene must occur before and after every procedure, paired with the appropriate use of clean or sterile gloves. Nurses must minimize the time instruments are exposed to the open air to prevent environmental contamination.

Timing is another critical principle. Collect specimens before antimicrobial therapy begins whenever possible, because starting drugs first can kill the bacteria locally and result in a false-negative culture. Also, gather samples from the actual site of infection, not from surrounding colonized skin or drainage that has pooled elsewhere.

Equipment, Reagents, and Transport Media

Having the correct tools ready before approaching the patient reduces errors and patient anxiety. Different tests require specific swabs, collection containers, and liquid additives.

  • Swabs: Dacron or rayon tips with plastic shafts are preferred; wooden shafts and cotton tips can contain toxins that kill fragile bacteria.
  • Transport Media: Special gels or liquids inside collection tubes that keep microorganisms alive without allowing them to multiply during transit.
  • Sterile Containers: Used for urine, sputum, and tissue biopsies to prevent outside environmental bacteria from entering the sample.

Collection Techniques for Blood and Urine

Blood cultures require strict aseptic technique because the skin is full of normal bacteria that can easily contaminate the bottle. Clean the skin thoroughly with an antiseptic like chlorhexidine and let it dry completely before inserting the needle. Draw blood from two different venipuncture sites to help distinguish true bloodstream infections from skin contaminants.

For urine cultures, the midstream clean-catch method is standard. Instruct the patient to clean the urethral opening, pass a small amount of urine into the toilet to flush out surface microbes, and then collect the midstream portion in a sterile cup. For patients with indwelling catheters, urine must be aspirated from the sampling port using a sterile syringe, never from the drainage bag.

Collection Techniques for Respiratory and Wound Specimens

Respiratory and wound samples require careful execution to ensure the material tested actually comes from the site of the infection.

Sputum specimens must come from deep within the lungs, not just saliva from the mouth. Ask the patient to take deep breaths, cough forcefully, and spit the deep secretions into a sterile container, preferably first thing in the morning.

Wound specimens should never be collected from superficial pus or crusts, as these reflect surface contamination rather than the deep tissue infection. Clean the wound surface gently with sterile saline to remove surface debris, then swab the cleanest area of active pus or use needle aspiration for closed abscesses.

Specimen Labeling, Documentation, and Request Forms

A perfectly collected specimen is useless if the laboratory cannot identify whose body it came from. Label every specimen container immediately at the bedside in the presence of the patient. Never pre-label tubes before the collection occurs.

The label must include the patient's full legal name, hospital identification number, date and time of collection, specific anatomical site, and the initials of the nurse who collected it. This information must match the laboratory request form exactly. Discrepancies between the container label and the request form will cause the laboratory to reject the sample, delaying vital patient care.

Storage, Preservation, and Transportation

Microorganisms are living entities that change behavior outside the human body. If a specimen sits too long at room temperature, fragile bacteria may die while resilient contaminants multiply, giving false results.

Most routine specimens should be transported to the laboratory within two hours of collection. If transport is delayed, place the sample in a refrigerator or appropriate incubator depending on the specific protocol for that organism. Cerebrospinal fluid and other critical body fluids must never be refrigerated, because delicate pathogens like Neisseria meningitidis will die quickly in the cold. Always use biohazard leak-proof bags for transport to protect couriers and laboratory staff.

Preventing Contamination and Ensuring Patient Safety

Contamination ruins laboratory work and leads to inappropriate antibiotic prescribing. Nurses act as the final defense against contamination by maintaining strict sterile technique during collection and handling.

Avoid touching the sterile tips of swabs, the insides of collection containers, or needle insertion points. If any sterile equipment touches a non-sterile surface like bed sheets or the patient's skin outside the prep zone, discard it immediately and get a new one. Patient safety also involves explaining the procedure clearly to reduce anxiety and prevent accidental spills or needle injuries.
