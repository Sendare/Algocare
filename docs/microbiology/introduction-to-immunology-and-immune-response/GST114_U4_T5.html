Specific Body Defences

Introduction to Specific Body Defences

Specific body defences, also known as the adaptive or acquired immune system, represent the third line of defence against pathogens. Unlike innate immunity which is non-specific and acts rapidly, the adaptive immune response is highly specific to a particular foreign agent, or antigen. It possesses the unique properties of heterogeneity, diversity, self-tolerance, and immunological memory, allowing the body to mount a targeted and robust attack against specific microbial invaders. When non-specific physical barriers and inflammatory responses are breached, the adaptive immune system is activated, involving complex interactions between specialized white blood cells known as lymphocytes. Nursing students in Nigeria must understand that while innate immunity provides immediate general protection, specific immunity is crucial for long-term protection and recovery from infectious diseases commonly encountered in clinical practice, such as malaria, typhoid, and tuberculosis. The adaptive system is broadly categorized into two major branches: humoral immunity, mediated by antibodies produced by B cells, and cell-mediated immunity, orchestrated by T cells. Both arms work synergistically to identify, neutralize, and eliminate pathogens while preserving the host's own healthy tissues through strict regulatory mechanisms.

Antigens and Immunogens

An antigen is any substance that is capable of triggering an immune response, specifically by interacting with components of the adaptive immune system such as T-cell receptors or antibodies. The term 'immunogen' is often used interchangeably, though strictly speaking, an immunogen is a specific type of antigen that can successfully induce a complete humoral or cell-mediated immune response on its own. For a molecule to be a strong immunogen, it typically needs to be foreign to the host, have a high molecular weight, and possess chemical complexity. Proteins and complex polysaccharides are usually potent immunogens, whereas small molecules called haptens are non-immunogenic by themselves. Haptens can only elicit an immune response when they bind to a larger carrier protein, a clinical concept important in understanding certain drug allergies. Specific regions on the antigen molecule that bind to antibodies or T-cell receptors are called epitopes, or antigenic determinants. Pathogens like bacteria and viruses carry numerous unique epitopes on their surfaces, allowing the immune system to recognize them through specific molecular binding. Understanding antigens and immunogens helps nursing students comprehend how diagnostic tests work, such as antigen-detecting rapid diagnostic tests for malaria.

Role of Lymphoid Organs and Cells

The structures responsible for housing, developing, and deploying immune cells are known as lymphoid organs, which are categorized into primary and secondary organs. Primary lymphoid organs include the bone marrow and the thymus gland, where lymphocytes originate and mature. B cells mature in the bone marrow, while T cells migrate to and mature within the thymus. Secondary lymphoid organs include the lymph nodes, spleen, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), and gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), such as Peyer's patches. These secondary sites are where mature lymphocytes encounter trapped antigens and initiate adaptive immune responses. Lymphocytes are the primary cellular components of specific immunity, consisting principally of B cells, T cells, and natural killer cells. Helper T cells (CD4+) and cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) play pivotal regulatory and effector roles respectively. Antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and B cells, play a critical bridge role by engulfing pathogens, processing them, and presenting their epitopes on major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules to T cells. In clinical practice, swollen and tender lymph nodes in the neck or groin often indicate that secondary lymphoid organs are actively mounting an immune response against an infection.

Humoral Immunity and B-Cell Activation

Humoral immunity is an arm of the adaptive immune system mediated by antibodies present in bodily fluids, principally driven by B lymphocytes. B-cell activation begins when naive B cells encounter their specific extracellular antigen via membrane-bound antibodies. In most cases, full B-cell activation requires a second signal from a helper T cell (CD4+). Once stimulated, the B cell undergoes clonal expansion, dividing rapidly to form a large army of identical cells. Most of these clone cells differentiate into plasma cells, which are specialized factories dedicated to synthesizing and secreting massive quantities of soluble antibodies specific to the invading antigen. A smaller portion of the activated B cells differentiates into memory B cells, which persist in the body for years or decades, providing rapid and robust protection if the same pathogen is encountered again in the future. Nurses should recognize that vaccines often work by stimulating this humoral pathway to generate protective plasma and memory B cells without causing disease. The secreted antibodies circulate in the blood and lymph, where they neutralize toxins, opsonize microbes for phagocytosis, and activate the complement cascade to clear extracellular bacteria and viruses.

Cell-Mediated Immunity and T-Cell Responses

Cell-mediated immunity (CMI) is the arm of the adaptive immune response that does not involve antibodies; instead, it relies primarily on the direct action of T lymphocytes to eliminate intracellular pathogens, cancer cells, and transplanted tissues. T cells are produced in the bone marrow but mature in the thymus, where they develop specific T-cell receptors (TCRs) and undergo rigorous selection for self-tolerance. There are two primary functional subsets of mature T cells: helper T cells, which express CD4 markers, and cytotoxic T cells, which express CD8 markers. Helper T (CD4+) cells recognize antigens presented on MHC class II molecules by antigen-presenting cells and secrete cytokines that stimulate and coordinate both B cells and cytotoxic T cells. Cytotoxic T (CD8+) cells recognize antigens presented on MHC class I molecules, which are found on virtually all nucleated cells in the body. When a cytotoxic T cell detects a virus-infected cell or a tumor cell displaying a foreign peptide on its MHC class I, it binds and releases perforins and granzymes, inducing apoptosis (programmed cell death) in the target cell. Cell-mediated immunity is essential for defending the body against intracellular microbes such as viruses, fungi, and certain bacteria like Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which is a major pathogen of public health concern in Nigeria.

Antibody Structure and Function

Antibodies, also known as immunoglobulins (Igs), are Y-shaped proteins produced by plasma cells that play a central role in humoral immunity. Each antibody molecule consists of four polypeptide chains: two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains, held together by disulfide bonds. The structure features two distinct regions: the Fab (fragment antigen-binding) region at the tips of the 'Y', which contains the variable amino acid sequences responsible for binding specifically to a particular antigen epitope, and the Fc (fragment crystallizable) region at the base, which interacts with immune cell receptors and complement proteins. There are five major classes of immunoglobulins in humans, each with unique biological properties and clinical significance: IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE, and IgD. IgG is the most abundant antibody in serum, crosses the placenta to confer passive immunity to the fetus, and protects against bacteria and viruses. IgA is predominantly found in mucosal secretions like saliva, tears, and breast milk, providing local defense on mucosal surfaces. IgM is the first antibody produced during a primary immune response and is an efficient agglutinator. IgE plays a major role in allergic reactions and defense against parasitic infections, while IgD functions mainly as a B-cell receptor. Nurses must understand these antibody classes when interpreting immunological lab results and administering passive immunizations.

Active and Passive Immunity

Immunity can be acquired through various pathways, broadly classified into active and passive immunity, each of which can be further subdivided into natural and artificial types. Active immunity occurs when the host's own immune system is stimulated to produce antibodies and T-cell responses after encountering an antigen. Natural active immunity develops following recovery from a natural infection, such as contracting measles and subsequently becoming immune. Artificial active immunity is achieved through vaccination, where weakened or killed pathogens, or their toxins, are introduced to stimulate the immune system without causing severe disease. In contrast, passive immunity involves the transfer of pre-formed antibodies from another person or animal, meaning the recipient's own immune system does not actively participate. Natural passive immunity occurs when maternal IgG antibodies cross the placenta or IgA is passed through breast milk to an infant, protecting the newborn during early life. Artificial passive immunity involves the administration of exogenous antibodies, such as tetanus antitoxin or rabies immunoglobulins, for immediate post-exposure prophylaxis. Nurses frequently educate patients about these concepts, emphasizing that while active immunity provides long-lasting protection due to memory cells, passive immunity offers immediate but temporary protection because no memory cells are generated.

Immunological Memory and Vaccination

Immunological memory is one of the defining features of the adaptive immune system, allowing the body to remember past infections and respond much faster and more vigorously upon subsequent exposures. When a naive lymphocyte first encounters its specific antigen during a primary response, it undergoes activation, proliferation, and differentiation into effector cells and long-lived memory cells. During a secondary response, these memory B and T cells recognize the same antigen much more rapidly, bypassing the lengthy lag phase of the primary response. Consequently, antibody titers rise sharply, reaching much higher levels with greater affinity for the antigen, usually clearing the pathogen before symptoms can develop. Vaccination is a monumental public health application of immunological memory, designed to safely prime the adaptive immune system against dangerous pathogens like polio, tetanus, and yellow fever. Vaccines introduce immunogens in a controlled manner to stimulate primary responses and generate memory cells without causing the full-scale pathology of the natural disease. Booster doses are often administered in immunization schedules to reinforce this memory response and maintain protective antibody thresholds within populations, which is vital for community-wide disease prevention and eradication efforts in Nigeria.
