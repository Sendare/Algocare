Specific Body Defences

Introduction to Specific Body Defences

Specific body defences, known collectively as the adaptive immune system, target distinct pathogens with high precision. Unlike innate barriers that act broadly against any invader, adaptive immunity recognizes specific molecular signatures called antigens. This branch of immunity develops over time through actual exposure or immunization and features a remarkable memory component that ensures a faster, stronger response upon subsequent encounters with the same pathogen. The primary players in this system are specialized white blood cells called lymphocytes, which originate in the bone marrow and mature in primary lymphoid organs. Recognizing self from non-self is the fundamental mechanism preventing the immune system from attacking healthy human tissues, a failure of which leads to autoimmune disorders.

Antigens and Immunogens

An antigen is any substance that triggers an immune response, specifically by binding to components of the adaptive immune system like T-cell receptors or antibodies. While all immunogens are antigens capable of provoking a robust immune response on their own, some small molecules called haptens can only stimulate immunity when bound to a larger carrier protein. Antigens typically feature specific surface regions known as **epitopes** or antigenic determinants, which are the exact sites recognized by immune receptors. Pathogens carry numerous distinct epitopes, allowing multiple clones of lymphocytes to attack a single invading microbe from different angles.

Role of Lymphoid Organs and Cells

The organs of the immune system are categorized into primary and secondary structures. - **Primary lymphoid organs**: Bone marrow and the thymus, where lymphocytes are formed and mature. - **Secondary lymphoid organs**: Lymph nodes, spleen, and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), where mature lymphocytes encounter active pathogens and initiate immune responses. Lymphocytes consist mainly of **B-cells** and **T-cells**, alongside **natural killer cells** which bridge innate and adaptive responses. Macrophages and dendritic cells act as **antigen-presenting cells (APCs)**, capturing foreign invaders and displaying their fragments to T-cells in secondary lymphoid organs to kickstart adaptive immunity.

Humoral Immunity and B-Cell Activation

Humoral immunity centers on the production of circulating antibodies by specialized B-cells. When a naive B-cell encounters its matching extracellular antigen, it binds to it and internalizes it. With help from activated T-helper cells, the B-cell undergoes clonal expansion, multiplying rapidly into two main populations: **plasma cells** and **memory B-cells**. Plasma cells act as dedicated antibody factories, releasing millions of proteins into the blood and lymph. These antibodies neutralize toxins, coat pathogens for phagocytosis, and activate the complement cascade. Meanwhile, memory B-cells remain dormant for years, ready to mount a rapid response if the same antigen appears again.

Cell-Mediated Immunity and T-Cell Responses

Cell-mediated immunity defends against intracellular pathogens like viruses, fungi, and certain bacteria that hide inside host cells. This arm relies on T-lymphocytes, which mature in the thymus. - **Cytotoxic T-cells (CD8+)**: Directly destroy infected, cancerous, or foreign host cells by releasing perforins and granzymes that induce apoptosis. - **Helper T-cells (CD4+)**: Coordinate the entire adaptive immune response by releasing cytokines that stimulate both B-cells and cytotoxic T-cells. T-cells cannot recognize free-floating antigens; the antigen must be processed and presented on the surface of a cell via major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules.

Antibody Structure and Function

Antibodies, also called **immunoglobulins (Igs)**, are Y-shaped proteins composed of two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains. The tips of the Y-shape form the **variable region**, which contains unique antigen-binding sites tailored to fit specific epitopes. The stem of the Y-shape is the **constant region**, which determines the antibody class and how it interacts with other immune components. | Antibody Class | Primary Function and Location | | :--- | :--- | | **IgG** | Most abundant in blood; crosses placenta to protect newborns | | **IgM** | First antibody produced during primary infection; powerful agglutinating agent | | **IgA** | Found in mucosal secretions, tears, saliva, and breast milk | | **IgE** | Involved in allergic reactions and defense against parasitic worms | | **IgD** | Acts primarily as an antigen receptor on the surface of naive B-cells |

Active and Passive Immunity

Immunity is acquired through active or passive pathways, each occurring naturally or artificially. **Active immunity** happens when an individual's own immune system encounters an antigen and manufactures its own T-cells and antibodies. This occurs naturally through contracting an infection or artificially via vaccination, resulting in long-lasting protection. **Passive immunity** involves receiving pre-formed antibodies from an external source. Natural passive immunity occurs when maternal antibodies pass through the placenta or breast milk, while artificial passive immunity involves injecting immune globulins extracted from a donor. Passive immunity provides immediate defense but fades quickly because the recipient's body does not produce memory cells.

Immunological Memory and Vaccination

Immunological memory relies on long-lived B and T memory cells generated during an initial exposure to an antigen. The **primary response** is slow and modest as naive lymphocytes discover the antigen, proliferate, and produce antibodies. The **secondary response** occurs when the same antigen appears later; memory cells recognize it instantly, triggering a massive, rapid flood of antibodies that clear the pathogen before symptoms develop. Vaccines exploit this exact mechanism by introducing harmless forms, fragments, or inactivated toxins of pathogens to safely train the adaptive immune system without causing severe disease.
