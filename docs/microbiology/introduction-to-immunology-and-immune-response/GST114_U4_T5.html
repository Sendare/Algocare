Specific Body Defences

Introduction to Specific Body Defences

Specific body defences, known as the adaptive immune system, act as our body's targeted security force. Unlike non-specific barriers like skin or stomach acid, adaptive immunity targets exact invaders. It mounts a tailored attack against specific bacteria, viruses, or foreign substances.

Key features of this system include:

  • Specificity: Ability to recognize a unique molecular signature on a single pathogen.
  • Versatility: Capability to react to millions of different foreign invaders.
  • Memory: Ability to remember past infections and mount a faster response upon re-exposure.
  • Tolerance: Ability to recognize the body's own healthy cells and leave them unharmed.

Understanding Antigens and Immunogens

An antigen is any substance that the immune system identifies as foreign and worthy of an attack. Most antigens are large proteins or polysaccharides located on the outer surfaces of invading microbes, pollen grains, or transplanted tissues.

An immunogen is a specific type of antigen that actually triggers an immune response. While all immunogens are antigens, not all antigens provoke a strong response on their own. The exact spot on the antigen that our immune receptors bind to is called an epitope. Think of an epitope as the specific lock that a white blood cell's key fits into. If an antigen lacks a recognizable epitope, the immune system may ignore it entirely.

Role of Lymphoid Organs and Lymphocytes

Lymphoid organs are the training grounds and stations where immune cells live and work. They are divided into primary and secondary organs.

  1. Primary Lymphoid Organs: The bone marrow and thymus. This is where white blood cells, called lymphocytes, are born and learn how to do their jobs. B-cells mature in the bone marrow, while T-cells travel to the thymus to mature.
  2. Secondary Lymphoid Organs: Lymph nodes, the spleen, tonsils, and mucosal-associated lymphoid tissue. This is where mature lymphocytes gather to trap and fight incoming pathogens.

Lymphocytes consist mainly of B-cells and T-cells, which circulate constantly through the blood and lymphatic vessels looking for trouble.

Humoral Immunity and B-Cell Activation

Humoral immunity relies on antibodies circulating freely in blood plasma and lymph. This branch is managed primarily by B-cells. When a naive B-cell encounters its matching antigen in a lymph node, it gets activated.

Assisted by helper T-cells, the activated B-cell multiplies rapidly to form a large clone army. Most of these cells turn into plasma cells, which are biological factories pumping out millions of matching antibodies into the body fluids. A small portion of these cells become memory B-cells, which stay behind for years, ready to jump into action if the same infection returns.

Cell-Mediated Immunity and T-Cell Responses

While humoral immunity targets invaders floating in body fluids, cell-mediated immunity deals with cells that are already infected, cancerous, or foreign. This system is driven by T-cells.

  • Cytotoxic T-cells (CD8+): These cells seek out and destroy abnormal body cells, such as those infected by viruses or tumor cells, by releasing chemicals that punch holes in their membranes.
  • Helper T-cells (CD4+): These do not kill directly. Instead, they release chemical signals called cytokines that coordinate the entire immune response, waking up both B-cells and cytotoxic T-cells.

Without helper T-cells, the entire adaptive immune response collapses—which is precisely what happens when HIV attacks CD4+ cells.

Antibody Structure and Function

Antibodies, also known as immunoglobulins (Igs), are Y-shaped protein molecules. Each antibody has two distinct parts:

  • Variable Region: The tips of the Y arms. This area is uniquely shaped to bind to one specific antigen epitope.
  • Constant Region: The stem of the Y. This part determines how the antibody will handle the invader once it is caught.

Active and Passive Immunity

Immunity can be acquired through different pathways depending on whether the body makes its own antibodies or receives them pre-made.

  • Active Immunity: Occurs when your own immune system encounters an antigen and makes its own T-cells and antibodies. This happens naturally when you catch an infection, or artificially through vaccination. It takes time to develop but provides long-lasting protection.
  • Passive Immunity: Occurs when you receive pre-made antibodies from another person or animal. This happens naturally when maternal antibodies cross the placenta to a fetus, or artificially through an injection of immune globulins after exposure to a toxin like rabies. It works immediately, but fades after a few weeks or months because the body made no memory cells.

Immunological Memory and Vaccination

Vaccination is one of modern medicine's greatest achievements, relying entirely on the principle of immunological memory. A vaccine introduces a weakened, killed, or partial piece of a pathogen into the body. This is an immunogen, but it is too weak to cause the actual disease.

The primary immune response handles this harmless imposter, creating plasma cells and memory cells. If the person encounters the real, wild pathogen months or years later, their secondary response is lightning-fast and massive. Antibody levels surge before the pathogen can multiply enough to cause severe symptoms. This protects both the individual and the wider community through herd immunity.
