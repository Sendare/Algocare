Anaphylaxis

Understanding Anaphylaxis and Immune System Overreaction

Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic, and life-threatening allergic reaction that requires immediate medical intervention. When a sensitized person encounters a specific trigger, the immune system mounts a massive, rapid response. This reaction is mediated primarily by immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies. During a prior exposure, the body mistakenly identifies a harmless substance as a dangerous invader and produces specific IgE against it. These antibodies attach themselves to mast cells in tissues and basophils circulating in the blood.

Upon re-exposure, the trigger binds directly to the IgE antibodies on these cells. This binding triggers degranulation, a process where mast cells and basophils rapidly release stored inflammatory chemicals into the bloodstream. The most prominent of these chemical mediators is histamine, alongside tryptase, leukotrienes, and prostaglandins. These substances cause widespread blood vessel dilation, increased vascular permeability, and smooth muscle contraction, particularly in the airways. Because this process affects multiple organ systems simultaneously, symptoms escalate within minutes from mild itching to complete cardiovascular collapse.

Triggers and Common Etiologies in Local Practice

Anaphylactic triggers vary widely, but they generally fall into a few major categories: foods, insect stings, medications, and latex. In clinical practice across West Africa, nurses frequently encounter specific local allergens that prompt acute admissions.

  • Foods: Peanuts, tree nuts, shellfish, and certain local seed extracts are potent triggers.
  • Medications: Beta-lactam antibiotics, particularly penicillins, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are common culprits in hospital settings.
  • Stings: Wasps, bees, ants, and hornets inject venom that can induce a systemic reaction.
  • Traditional and Herbal Extracts: Unregulated local herbal concoctions or topical preparations can sometimes contain unlisted botanical allergens that provoke unexpected immune responses.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms and Clinical Manifestations

The clinical presentation of anaphylaxis reflects the simultaneous failure of multiple organ systems driven by massive systemic vasodilation and fluid shift. Nurses must recognize these signs promptly because symptoms can progress from localized discomfort to fatal airway obstruction in less than ten minutes.

Rapid Nursing Assessment and Emergency Triage

When a patient arrives at the emergency unit with suspected anaphylaxis, seconds matter. Nurses must utilize the Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure (ABCDE) framework instantly while simultaneously calling for medical help. Do not wait for laboratory results or diagnostic confirmation before acting.

Assess the airway immediately for hoarseness, swelling, or stridor, which indicate impending closure. Check breathing effort, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation. Evaluate circulation by checking peripheral pulses, capillary refill time, and blood pressure—hypotension is a hallmark of distributive shock. Quick identification of skin changes such as generalized urticaria or facial swelling provides crucial supporting evidence, though it is worth noting that a small percentage of patients experience severe shock with zero skin manifestations.

Emergency Pharmacological Management

The absolute first-line treatment for anaphylaxis is epinephrine, administered intramuscularly without delay. Hesitating to give epinephrine is a common and dangerous clinical error. Epinephrine reverses both respiratory distress and cardiovascular collapse by acting on adrenergic receptors: it causes vasoconstriction to raise blood pressure, relaxes bronchial smooth muscle to open airways, and stops further mediator release from mast cells.

The standard adult dose of 1:1,000 epinephrine is 0.3 to 0.5 milligrams injected into the mid-outer thigh. Intravenous epinephrine is reserved strictly for cardiac arrest or profound shock unresponsive to fluid resuscitation and IM doses, as IV administration carries a high risk of dangerous heart arrhythmias.

Following epinephrine, supportive therapies are administered. Antihistamines like diphenhydramine help control itching and hives. Corticosteroids such as hydrocortisone are given to prevent late-phase or biphasic reactions, though they do nothing to treat acute symptoms. Supplemental oxygen must be delivered via face mask to maintain adequate tissue perfusion.

Monitoring, Complications, and Biphasic Reactions

Stabilizing a patient after the initial epinephrine dose does not mean the emergency is over. Nurses must maintain continuous monitoring of vital signs, cardiac rhythm, and oxygen saturation for several hours. A major clinical concern is the biphasic reaction, a secondary wave of anaphylactic symptoms that occurs without any re-exposure to the original trigger.

Biphasic reactions typically appear anywhere from one to twenty-four hours after the initial episode resolves, though most occur within eight hours. Because of this unpredictable risk, patients who experience anaphylaxis must remain under observation in a healthcare facility. Additional complications include severe hypoperfusion leading to renal failure, myocardial ischemia triggered by low blood pressure combined with high-dose epinephrine, and prolonged mechanical ventilation if laryngeal edema was severe.

Patient Education and Long-Term Prevention

Comprehensive patient education is the cornerstone of preventing future life-threatening episodes. Once a patient stabilizes, the nurse teaches them how to identify and strictly avoid their specific allergen. When complete avoidance is impossible, patients must be equipped with an auto-injector device containing epinephrine.

Practical teaching points include demonstrating the correct technique for using the auto-injector, explaining that it must be pressed firmly against the outer thigh through clothing if necessary, and checking expiration dates regularly. Nurses should also instruct patients to wear a medical alert bracelet or necklace. Finally, patients and their family members must understand that if an auto-injector is used, they must immediately call emergency services or travel to the nearest hospital because a single dose may not prevent a biphasic reaction.
