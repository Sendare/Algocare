Anaphylaxis

Introduction to Anaphylaxis and Immune System Response

Anaphylaxis is a severe, systemic, multi-organ allergic reaction that has a rapid onset and can cause death. At its core, the condition represents a profound hypersensitivity reaction driven primarily by the immune system. When a susceptible person encounters a specific trigger, their body mounts an exaggerated response mediated by Immunoglobulin E (IgE) antibodies.

During the initial exposure, sensitization occurs without outward signs of trouble. The body produces specific IgE antibodies that bind to the surface of mast cells and basophils located abundantly in the skin, lungs, and gastrointestinal tract. Upon re-exposure, the offending substance cross-links these surface-bound IgE molecules. This triggers immediate degranulation, releasing a flood of preformed and newly synthesized inflammatory mediators into the bloodstream, most notably histamine, tryptase, and leukotrienes.

Common Etiologies and Environmental Triggers

Triggers for anaphylaxis vary widely, though certain categories dominate clinical practice globally and within West Africa. Food allergens, insect stings, and medications are the most prevalent culprits.

  • Food Allergies: Groundnuts (peanuts), tree nuts, shellfish, and eggs are notorious triggers. In many Nigerian households, shared cooking utensils or hidden groundnut paste in soups can cause accidental, severe ingestion.
  • Medications: Beta-lactam antibiotics like penicillin, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and intravenous contrast media frequently provoke severe reactions in hospital settings.
  • Insect Stings: Wasps, bees, and fire ants inject venom that can incite a rapid systemic response in sensitized individuals.
  • Latex: Rubber gloves and medical equipment present a known hazard to both healthcare workers and frequent surgical patients.

Pathophysiology and Systemic Effects

Once inflammatory mediators flood the circulation, they unleash widespread vascular and smooth muscle changes. Vasodilation causes a catastrophic drop in systemic vascular resistance, leading to profound hypotension. Simultaneously, increased capillary permeability allows fluid to leak rapidly from the intravascular space into interstitial tissues, causing generalized edema and a depleted effective circulating blood volume.

In the respiratory tract, inflammatory mediators cause intense bronchospasm and mucosal edema. This narrows the airways dramatically, trapping air and increasing the work of breathing. The combined effects of hypovolemia and airway obstruction rapidly compromise tissue oxygenation, putting vital organs like the brain and myocardium at immediate risk of ischemic injury.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Recognition

Anaphylaxis is clinically characterized by the involvement of two or more organ systems following exposure to a potential allergen. Skin findings are often the first visible indicator, though their absence does not rule out the diagnosis.

  • Skin and Mucosal Tissue: Generalized urticaria (hives), flushing, pruritus, and angioedema affecting the lips, tongue, or uvula.
  • Respiratory System: Dysphonia, throat tightness, persistent dry cough, wheezing, and stridor indicating upper or lower airway compromise.
  • Cardiovascular System: Dizziness, syncope, tachycardia, and profound hypotension resulting in diaphoresis and confusion.
  • Gastrointestinal Tract: Crampy abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and urgent diarrhea triggered by visceral smooth muscle contraction.

Emergency Medical Management and Pharmacological Intervention

Prompt recognition must be immediately followed by decisive intervention. Time lost is life lost. Epinephrine is the absolute first-line treatment and must be administered without delay; there are no absolute contraindications to its use in anaphylaxis.

  • Adrenaline Administration: Inject 0.5 mg (0.5 mL of 1:1000 solution) intramuscularly into the anterolateral thigh for adults. Repeat every 5 to 15 minutes if symptoms persist.
  • Patient Positioning: Place the patient flat with elevated lower extremities to maximize venous return, unless they are vomiting or experiencing severe respiratory distress, in which case a comfortable sitting position may be allowed.
  • Airway and Oxygen: Administer high-flow oxygen via a non-rebreather mask and prepare for advanced airway management if upper airway edema progresses.
  • Fluid Resuscitation: Infuse rapid boluses of isotonic crystalloids (normal saline) to counteract the massive third-spacing and vasodilation.

Differential Diagnosis and Potential Complications

While severe allergic reactions present distinctly, clinicians must differentiate anaphylaxis from other acute medical emergencies that mimic its signs. Conditions such as acute severe asthma, vasovagal syncope, hereditary angioedema, and panic attacks can share overlapping features like dyspnea, anxiety, or dizziness.

Unlike panic attacks or vasovagal spells, true anaphylaxis features objective cutaneous changes, bronchospasm, and hypotension without bradycardia. Complications arise primarily from prolonged tissue hypoperfusion and hypoxia. Cardiac arrest, cerebral ischemia, hypoxic encephalopathy, and multi-organ dysfunction syndrome can develop rapidly if the airway remains obstructed or shock goes uncorrected.

Patient Education and Prevention Strategies

Long-term management centers on strict avoidance of identified triggers and preparedness for accidental re-exposure. Following stabilization, patients and their families require thorough counseling before discharge from the healthcare facility.

Patients should learn how to read food product labels carefully, recognize early prodromal symptoms, and wear a medical alert bracelet. For individuals with documented severe reactions, prescription and instruction on the use of an auto-injector are mandatory. Nursing staff must demonstrate the correct technique—pressing firmly against the outer thigh until a click is heard—and ensure the patient or caregiver can perform a return demonstration before leaving the clinical environment.
