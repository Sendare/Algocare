Antibodies

Introduction to Antibodies and Immunoglobulins

Antibodies, also known as immunoglobulins (Igs), are large, Y-shaped proteins produced mainly by plasma cells. These specialized proteins play a central role in the body's adaptive immune system by recognizing and neutralizing foreign invaders such as pathogenic bacteria, viruses, and fungi. When a pathogen enters the body, B-lymphocytes encounter its unique surface markers. Upon activation, these lymphocytes transform into plasma cells dedicated to mass-producing antibodies tailored specifically to bind that exact foreign marker, known as an antigen.

Basic Structure of an Antibody Molecule

  • Heavy Chains: Two identical, larger polypeptide chains that form the core base and lower stem of the Y-shaped molecule.
  • Light Chains: Two identical, smaller polypeptide chains paired with the upper portions of the heavy chains.
  • Fab Region: The arms of the Y-shaped structure containing the variable regions that bind directly to antigens.
  • Fc Region: The constant stem of the Y-shaped structure that interacts with immune cells and complement proteins.

Classes and Isotypes of Antibodies

Human antibodies are divided into five distinct classes, or isotypes, based on differences in the amino acid sequence of their heavy chain constant regions. Each isotype serves a unique physiological function and location in the body.

IgGBlood and extracellular fluidLong-term immunity, crosses placenta
IgAMucosal secretions, breast milkProtects body surfaces and mucosal linings

A common misconception is that all antibodies perform the exact same task; however, their heavy chain variations dictate entirely different protective roles across various body compartments.

Mechanisms of Antibody Action

Antibodies do not typically destroy pathogens directly; instead, they mark them for elimination through several distinct mechanisms.

  1. Neutralization: Antibodies cover critical surface sites on viruses or toxins, preventing them from attaching to or entering host cells.
  2. Opsonization: Coating a pathogen with antibodies makes it much easier for phagocytic cells like macrophages to recognize and engulf it.
  3. Complement Activation: The binding of antibodies triggers a cascade of plasma proteins that punch holes in the cell walls of pathogens, causing lysis.

Primary and Secondary Immune Responses

When the immune system encounters an antigen for the very first time, it mounts a primary immune response. This process is relatively slow because naive B-cells take days to multiply, differentiate, and begin producing detectable levels of IgM followed by IgG.

Subsequent exposure to the exact same antigen triggers a secondary immune response. Thanks to long-lived memory B-cells created during the initial encounter, the reaction is much faster, stronger, and produces significantly higher levels of IgG antibodies. This rapid response clears the pathogen before clinical symptoms have a chance to develop.

Clinical Significance and Diagnostic Applications

Medical laboratories exploit the specificity of antibody-antigen binding for numerous diagnostic tests, including ELISA and blood typing. Detecting specific antibody isotypes in a patient's serum helps clinicians diagnose recent versus past infections. For instance, high levels of specific IgM point toward an active, recent infection, whereas elevated IgG indicates past exposure or successful vaccination. Furthermore, manufactured monoclonal antibodies are widely utilized in modern targeted therapies for cancers and autoimmune diseases.
