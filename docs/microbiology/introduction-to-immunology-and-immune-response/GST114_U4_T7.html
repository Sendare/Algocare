Abnormal Immune Responses

Introduction to Immune System Dysregulation

The human immune system relies on precise recognition mechanisms to distinguish between self and non-self antigens. When this system fails to regulate its responses, pathology occurs. Dysregulation generally falls into two extremes: overactivity, where the body mounts aggressive attacks against harmless environmental substances or its own healthy tissues, and underactivity, where the immune response fails to protect against infections or malignancy. Understanding these shifts provides the foundation for managing allergies, autoimmune conditions, and immunodeficiency disorders encountered in everyday clinical practice across Nigerian healthcare facilities.

Hypersensitivity Reactions: Types I through IV

Gell and Coombs classified hypersensitivity reactions into four distinct types based on the underlying immunological mechanism and speed of onset. - **Type I (Immediate)**: IgE-mediated reaction involving mast cell degranulation, leading to rapid manifestations like anaphylaxis, urticaria, and allergic rhinitis. - **Type II (Cytotoxic)**: IgG or IgM antibodies bind to cellular antigens, triggering complement activation and cell lysis, seen in blood transfusion incompatibilities. - **Type III (Immune Complex)**: Deposition of antigen-antibody complexes in tissues activates the complement cascade, causing localized inflammation and tissue damage, such as in systemic lupus erythematosus. - **Type IV (Delayed-type)**: T-cell mediated response taking 48 to 72 hours to develop, typical of contact dermatitis from latex or tuberculin skin testing.

Autoimmune Diseases: Mechanisms and Pathophysiology

Autoimmunity arises when tolerance to self-antigens breaks down, leading to immune effector cells attacking normal body structures. Genetic predisposition combined with environmental triggers, such as viral infections or hormonal influences, often initiates this pathological process. Chronic inflammation leads to progressive tissue destruction and loss of organ function. Common clinical entities include rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes mellitus, and systemic lupus erythematosus. Diagnosis frequently relies on detecting circulating autoantibodies, though clinical presentation guides the ultimate treatment plan.

Primary and Secondary Immunodeficiencies

Immunodeficiency states leave individuals vulnerable to recurrent, severe, and opportunistic infections. - **Primary Immunodeficiencies**: Inherited genetic defects affecting lymphocyte development or function, often manifesting early in infancy with persistent thrush, recurrent pneumonia, or failure to thrive. - **Secondary (Acquired) Immunodeficiencies**: Result from external factors such as viral infections (e.g., HIV), malnutrition, immunosuppressive medications, or malignancies. In West Africa, protein-energy malnutrition and HIV remain major drivers of secondary immune suppression, demanding vigilant infection control in clinical wards.

Transplantation Immunology and Graft Rejection

When organs or tissues are transplanted between non-identical individuals, the recipient's immune system recognizes major histocompatibility complex (MHC) or human leukocyte antigens (HLA) on the donor tissue as foreign. Rejection types include hyperacute, occurring within minutes due to pre-existing antibodies; acute, happening weeks to months later via T-cell mediated cellular responses; and chronic, developing slowly over months or years through fibrosis and vessel occlusion. Lifelong immunosuppressive pharmacotherapy remains necessary to prevent graft destruction, though this places patients at high risk for opportunistic infections.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Testing

Evaluating immune dysregulation requires a thorough clinical history, physical examination, and targeted laboratory investigations. Clinicians assess patterns of infection, allergic triggers, family history, and signs of chronic inflammation. Diagnostic tests commonly include complete blood counts with differential to evaluate white blood cell populations, serum immunoglobulin levels, specific autoantibody panels such as antinuclear antibodies (ANA), and skin prick tests for allergies. Interpreting these results helps categorize the immune dysfunction and direct appropriate therapeutic interventions.

Nursing Management and Patient Care Strategies

Nursing care for patients with immune system disorders centers on infection prevention, symptom relief, medication administration, and comprehensive health education. For immunosuppressed clients, strict hand hygiene, protective isolation principles, and meticulous wound care are vital. When caring for individuals with hypersensitivity or autoimmune diseases, nurses monitor for adverse medication reactions, manage chronic pain, and support emotional well-being. Educating patients on avoiding known triggers and recognizing early signs of infection ensures safer transitions from hospital care back to the community.
