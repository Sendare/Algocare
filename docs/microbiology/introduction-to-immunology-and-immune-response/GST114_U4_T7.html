Abnormal Immune Responses

Introduction to Immune System Dysregulation

The human immune system is designed to protect the body from invading pathogens, foreign cells, and malignant transformations. However, when this intricate defense mechanism fails to function correctly, it leads to immune system dysregulation. Dysregulation can manifest in various ways, primarily categorized into overactivity, such as hypersensitivity and autoimmunity, or underactivity, known as immunodeficiency. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice and licensure examinations in Nigeria, understanding these broad categories is fundamental. Immune dysregulation often disrupts homeostasis, leading to chronic inflammation, tissue destruction, or increased vulnerability to opportunistic infections. Environmental factors, genetic predispositions, and infectious triggers frequently contribute to the breakdown of immune tolerance. Self-tolerance is the immune system's ability to recognize self-antigens and refrain from attacking the body's own tissues. When central or peripheral tolerance mechanisms fail, autoreactive lymphocytes escape suppression and initiate pathogenic cascades. Furthermore, socioeconomic factors and regional health challenges, such as the high burden of infectious diseases in sub-Saharan Africa, heavily influence secondary immunodeficiencies. Nurses must recognize that immune system dysfunction is rarely isolated; it often affects multiple organ systems and requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach to assessment, diagnosis, and long-term management.

Hypersensitivity Reactions: Types I through IV

Hypersensitivity reactions represent inappropriate or exaggerated immune responses to harmless environmental antigens, leading to tissue injury and disease. The Gell and Coombs classification divides these reactions into four distinct types. Type I, or immediate hypersensitivity, is mediated by IgE antibodies, which bind to mast cells and basophils, triggering the release of histamine and inflammatory mediators, as seen in anaphylaxis and allergic rhinitis. Type II hypersensitivity involves cytotoxic reactions where IgG or IgM antibodies bind to cellular antigens, leading to complement activation and cell lysis, exemplified by blood transfusion reactions and hemolytic disease of the newborn. Type III reactions are immune complex-mediated; circulating antigen-antibody complexes deposit in tissues, particularly in blood vessels and glomeruli, causing acute inflammation and neutrophil infiltration, as observed in systemic lupus erythematosus and serum sickness. Type IV is delayed-type hypersensitivity, which is cell-mediated rather than antibody-mediated. T-lymphocytes and macrophages drive this reaction, taking 24 to 48 hours to manifest, with classic examples including the tuberculin skin test (Mantoux) and contact dermatitis from poison ivy or latex. Nursing students must be vigilant in identifying the specific type of hypersensitivity a patient is experiencing, as emergency interventions for an acute Type I anaphylactic reaction differ vastly from the chronic management of Type III or the avoidance strategies required for Type IV contact dermatitis.

Autoimmune Diseases: Mechanisms and Pathophysiology

Autoimmune diseases occur when the immune system loses its ability to distinguish between self-antigens and non-self-antigens, mounting a destructive adaptive immune response against the host's own organs and tissues. The pathophysiology involves a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility, such as specific human leukocyte antigen (HLA) alleles, and environmental triggers like viral infections, ultraviolet radiation, or chemical exposures. Molecular mimicry is a prominent mechanism where an infectious agent shares structural similarities with self-peptides, tricking the immune system into attacking both the pathogen and the healthy host tissue. Alternatively, sequestered antigens that are normally hidden from the immune system—such as those within the eye or testes—can be released following trauma, inciting an autoimmune response. Autoimmune conditions can be organ-specific, like Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus where pancreatic beta cells are destroyed, or systemic, such as Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) where widespread immune complexes affect multiple organ systems including the skin, joints, and kidneys. Nursing care for patients with autoimmune diseases centers around understanding exacerbations and remissions, managing immunosuppressive therapies, monitoring for signs of infection or organ failure, and providing holistic support to improve the patient's quality of life.

Primary and Secondary Immunodeficiencies

Immunodeficiencies represent states of impaired immune function that leave individuals highly susceptible to recurrent, persistent, or unusual infections. They are classified into primary and secondary categories. Primary immunodeficiencies (PIDs) are congenital or inherited genetic defects affecting various components of the immune system, such as B-lymphocytes, T-lymphocytes, phagocytes, or the complement system. Examples include X-linked agammaglobulinemia and Severe Combined Immunodeficiency (SCID), often requiring specialized care like bone marrow transplantation or immunoglobulin replacement therapy. Conversely, secondary (acquired) immunodeficiencies develop later in life due to external factors such as malnutrition, severe burns, immunosuppressive medications, malignancies, or viral infections like the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV). HIV specifically targets CD4+ T-helper cells, gradually eroding cellular immunity and progressing to Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) if left untreated. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding secondary immunodeficiency is critically important due to the regional prevalence of HIV/AIDS, protein-energy malnutrition, and infectious morbidities. Nurses must implement stringent infection control practices, educate patients on nutritional support, and monitor laboratory values such as absolute lymphocyte counts and CD4+ cell counts to evaluate immune competence.

Transplantation Immunology and Graft Rejection

Transplantation immunology explores how the recipient's immune system responds to introduced cells, tissues, or organs from a donor. The central challenge in transplantation is major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, known in humans as human leukocyte antigens (HLAs). Because HLAs are highly polymorphic, the recipient's immune system almost universally recognizes non-self donor antigens as foreign, initiating graft rejection. Graft rejection is categorized into three types: hyperacute, acute, and chronic. Hyperacute rejection occurs minutes to hours post-transplant due to pre-existing antibodies in the recipient's blood against donor antigens, causing immediate thrombosis and tissue necrosis. Acute rejection occurs days to months later, driven by T-cell mediated and antibody-mediated responses against mismatched HLA antigens, leading to cellular infiltration and graft dysfunction. Chronic rejection develops over months to years, characterized by smooth muscle proliferation, fibrosis, and gradual vessel occlusion within the graft. To prevent these outcomes, transplant recipients require lifelong immunosuppressive therapy, such as calcineurin inhibitors, corticosteroids, and antiproliferative agents. Nurses play a crucial role in managing these patients by monitoring for signs of rejection, enforcing strict medication adherence, preventing infections, and providing psychological support throughout the transplantation journey.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Testing

Clinical assessment and diagnostic testing are vital for identifying immune system disorders, staging their severity, and monitoring therapeutic responses. A comprehensive nursing assessment begins with a detailed health history, noting recurrent infections, family history of autoimmune diseases, medication use, occupational exposures, and lifestyle factors. Physical examination should focus on signs of chronic inflammation, lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, skin rashes, joint swelling, and oral lesions. Laboratory diagnostics are indispensable tools in immunology. Complete blood counts (CBC) with differentials help identify leukopenia, lymphopenia, or eosinophilia, which provide clues to underlying immune abnormalities. Specific assays include measurement of immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE) to detect deficiencies or hyper-reactivity. Autoantibody testing, such as Antinuclear Antibody (ANA) titers, Rheumatoid Factor (RF), and anti-double-stranded DNA, helps confirm autoimmune disorders like lupus and rheumatoid arthritis. Flow cytometry is utilized to quantify lymphocyte subsets, particularly measuring CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell counts in HIV-positive patients. Furthermore, allergy testing—including skin prick tests and radioallergosorbent tests (RAST)—identifies specific Type I hypersensitivity triggers. Nurses must prepare patients for these tests, ensure accurate specimen collection and labeling, and interpret results within the context of the patient's overall clinical presentation.

Nursing Management and Patient Care Strategies

Nursing management of patients with abnormal immune responses requires a multifaceted, patient-centered approach focused on safety, education, and symptom control. Because patients with immunodeficiencies or those on immunosuppressive therapy are at extreme risk for opportunistic infections, rigorous implementation of standard precautions and hand hygiene is mandatory. Nurses must educate patients on recognizing early signs of infection, such as low-grade fever, chills, cough, or localized pain, and instruct them to report these symptoms immediately. For patients experiencing hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis, nurses must maintain a patent airway, administer emergency medications like epinephrine and antihistamines promptly, and monitor vital signs continuously. In cases of autoimmune diseases, nursing care involves assisting with pain management, promoting joint mobility through physical therapy, ensuring adequate nutritional intake, and offering emotional support to cope with chronic illness. Medication compliance is a critical educational priority; abrupt cessation of immunosuppressants can cause graft rejection or autoimmune flare-ups, while missed doses of antiretroviral therapy can lead to drug resistance. Additionally, nurses should review vaccination guidelines, emphasizing that live vaccines are generally contraindicated in immunocompromised individuals. Through empathetic communication, vigilant monitoring, and expert clinical judgment, nurses empower patients to manage their conditions and achieve an optimal quality of life.
