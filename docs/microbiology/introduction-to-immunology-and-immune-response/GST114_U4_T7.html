Abnormal Immune Responses

Introduction to Immune System Dysregulation

The immune system is designed to protect the body from harmful invaders like bacteria, viruses, and parasites. However, when this defense mechanism fails to function correctly, immune system dysregulation occurs. This malfunction generally falls into three main categories: overactivity through hypersensitivity, mistaken identity via autoimmunity, or underactivity through immunodeficiency.

Understanding these shifts helps nurses recognize why a patient's body might attack its own tissues or fail to fight off a simple infection. Dysregulation is rarely random; it often involves a complex mix of genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and hormonal influences.

Hypersensitivity Reactions: Types I through IV

Hypersensitivity reactions represent an overzealous or inappropriate immune response to harmless substances. They are classified into four distinct types based on the speed of the reaction and the underlying immune mechanism:

  • Type I (Immediate): IgE-mediated reactions, such as allergic rhinitis, asthma, and anaphylaxis, occurring minutes after exposure.
  • Type II (Cytotoxic): IgM or IgG antibodies bind to cell surface antigens, leading to cell lysis, seen in blood transfusion reactions.
  • Type III (Immune Complex): Antigen-antibody complexes deposit in tissues, triggering inflammation and tissue damage, as in systemic lupus erythematosus.
  • Type IV (Delayed): Cell-mediated reactions driven by T-lymphocytes, taking 48 to 72 hours to appear, such as a positive tuberculin skin test or contact dermatitis from poison ivy.

Autoimmune Diseases and Loss of Self-Tolerance

Self-tolerance is the immune system's ability to recognize the body's own cells as friendly and leave them alone. When this tolerance breaks down, the immune system launches an attack against normal, healthy tissues, resulting in an autoimmune disease.

Common examples include rheumatoid arthritis, where the synovium of joints is targeted, and type 1 diabetes mellitus, where insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreas are destroyed. A common misconception is that autoimmune diseases burn out completely on their own; instead, they typically follow a chronic course marked by unpredictable flare-ups and periods of remission.

Primary and Secondary Immunodeficiencies

Immunodeficiency occurs when the immune system is missing components or fails to work adequately, leaving the person vulnerable to frequent infections.

  1. Primary Immunodeficiencies: These are rare, inherited genetic defects present at birth, such as severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).
  2. Secondary Immunodeficiencies: These are acquired later in life due to external factors, malnutrition, medical treatments like chemotherapy, or viral infections such as HIV.

Patients with immunodeficiencies often present with unusual, persistent, or hard-to-treat infections that typical individuals easily fight off.

Transplantation Immunology and Graft Rejection

When a patient receives an organ transplant, the recipient's immune system inspects the donor organ for foreign proteins known as human leukocyte antigens (HLAs). If the antigens do not match closely, the immune system treats the organ like a dangerous invader and attempts to destroy it.

  • Hyperacute Rejection: Occurs within minutes to hours due to pre-existing antibodies against the graft.
  • Acute Rejection: Occurs weeks to months later, driven primarily by T-cell cellular responses.
  • Chronic Rejection: Develops slowly over months or years, causing gradual organ scarring and vessel blockage.

To prevent rejection, transplant recipients must take lifelong immunosuppressive medications, which unfortunately increase their risk for severe infections and cancers.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Testing

Evaluating a patient with suspected immune dysregulation requires a thorough health history and targeted laboratory tests. Nurses must ask about family history, frequent infections, environmental exposures, and current medications.

Diagnostic tools commonly used include:

  • Complete Blood Count (CBC) with differential: To check white blood cell counts and proportions.
  • Antinuclear Antibody (ANA) test: Often elevated in autoimmune connective tissue disorders.
  • Immunoglobulin levels (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE): To assess overall antibody production capacity.
  • Skin allergy testing: To pinpoint specific Type I hypersensitivity triggers.

Nursing Management and Patient Care Strategies

Managing patients with abnormal immune responses centers on preventing infection, reducing inflammation, and supporting quality of life. For immunodeficient clients, strict hand hygiene, protective isolation when necessary, and avoiding live vaccines are top priorities.

For patients with overactive immune conditions like allergies or autoimmune flares, nurses monitor for adverse medication effects, teach avoidance of known triggers, and support comfort during painful episodes. Careful education empowers patients to recognize early signs of infection or disease exacerbation so they can seek prompt medical attention.
