Acquired Immune Deficiencies

Understanding Acquired Immune Deficiencies

Unlike primary immune deficiencies which stem from genetic mutations present at birth, acquired immune deficiencies develop later in life due to external factors, infections, medical treatments, or environmental pressures. The common thread among these conditions is the progressive impairment of the body's immune defense, leaving the affected person vulnerable to opportunistic infections and malignancies that a healthy immune system easily repels. In clinical practice, recognizing that a deficiency is acquired rather than hereditary directs the nurse to investigate environmental exposures, medication histories, nutritional status, and systemic illnesses.

Pathophysiology and Mechanisms of Immune Decline

The steady breakdown of the immune system typically targets specific cellular components, most notably the CD4+ T helper lymphocytes. These cells act as master coordinators of the immune response, signaling other cells like B-lymphocytes and cytotoxic T-cells to action. When the population of CD4+ cells plummets, the entire immune network loses its coordination. Consequently, the body cannot mount an effective defense against pathogens, allowing even harmless environmental microbes to cause severe, life-threatening illness.

HIV Infection and Progression to AIDS

The Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is the most prominent cause of acquired immune deficiency worldwide. The infection advances through distinct stages, starting with a brief acute flu-like illness, moving through a long asymptomatic chronic phase, and eventually culminating in Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) if untreated. A patient is officially classified as having AIDS when their CD4+ count drops below 200 cells per microliter or when they develop a defining opportunistic infection such as Pneumocystis pneumonia or Kaposi's sarcoma.

Secondary Deficiencies from Malnutrition and Infections

  • Protein-Energy Malnutrition: Severe nutrient deficiencies impair the body's ability to produce new immune cells and antibodies.
  • Chronic Systemic Infections: Persistent infections like tuberculosis or malaria exhaust the bone marrow and alter immune cell function.
  • Synergistic Cycle: Malnutrition weakens immunity, making infections more severe; infections in turn worsen nutritional status by increasing metabolic demands and reducing appetite.

Iatrogenic Causes of Acquired Immune Deficiency

Iatrogenic immunodeficiencies are unintended consequences of medical therapies. They occur most frequently when patients receive treatments designed to suppress the immune system deliberately, such as chemotherapy for cancer, corticosteroids for inflammatory disorders, or immunosuppressive drugs following organ transplantation. While these therapies are lifesaving for their intended purposes, they strip away the body's normal infection-fighting capabilities, turning routine environmental bacteria and fungi into major health hazards.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosing acquired immune deficiencies requires a combination of thorough clinical history-taking and specific laboratory investigations. Nurses must look for red flags such as recurrent, stubborn infections, unexpected weight loss, and prolonged fevers. Key diagnostic tests include CD4+ T-cell counts to measure immune system strength, viral load testing for infectious agents like HIV, and complete blood counts to check for severe leukopenia or anemia.

Nursing Management and Patient Care Strategies

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with compromised immunity focuses heavily on infection prevention, nutritional support, and emotional encouragement. Nurses must practice strict aseptic techniques during all procedures, monitor vital signs for early signs of sepsis, and educate patients on hand hygiene and safe food handling. Additionally, supporting medication adherence for chronic therapies like antiretrovirals is crucial to prevent drug resistance and further immune decline.

Public Health and Treatment Considerations in Nigeria

In Nigeria, acquired immune deficiencies—particularly HIV and malnutrition-related deficiencies—present significant public health challenges. Effective management relies on expanding access to free or subsidized Antiretroviral Therapy (ART), promoting community education to reduce stigma, and integrating nutritional rehabilitation into primary healthcare centers. Nurses play a frontline role in public health by offering voluntary counseling and testing, promoting maternal-child health programs to prevent mother-to-child transmission, and advocating for vulnerable populations.
