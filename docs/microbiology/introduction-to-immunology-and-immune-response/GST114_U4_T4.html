Non-specific Body Defences

Introduction to Non-Specific Body Defences

Non-specific body defences, often called innate immunity, represent the human body's first and most immediate line of resistance against harmful invaders. Unlike adaptive immunity, which targets specific pathogens with tailored antibodies, innate defences act the same way regardless of the invader's identity. They are present from birth and function continuously without requiring prior exposure to a microbe.

  • Broad spectrum: Attacks bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites indiscriminately.
  • Rapid response: Reacts within minutes or hours of tissue invasion.
  • No memory: Does not improve with repeated exposure to the same pathogen.

First Line of Defence: Barriers and Secretions

The first line of defence consists of physical and chemical barriers that prevent pathogens from entering the body tissues in the first place. The skin serves as the largest mechanical barrier, featuring a dry, keratinized outer layer that sloughs off along with attached microbes.

Mucous membranes line the respiratory, digestive, and urogenital tracts, producing sticky mucus that traps foreign particles. Specialized cells within these tracts feature cilia, microscopic hair-like structures that beat rhythmically to sweep trapped debris upward toward the throat for expulsion. Chemical secretions like sweat, tears, and saliva contain lysozyme, an enzyme that destroys bacterial cell walls.

Second Line of Defence: Cellular Components and Phagocytosis

When pathogens breach surface barriers, they encounter the second line of defence, which includes specialized white blood cells and biochemical processes. Phagocytes are the primary cellular combatants, engulfing and digesting foreign invaders.

  • Neutrophils: The most abundant white blood cells; they arrive first at the site of infection and actively engulf bacteria.
  • Macrophage: Large cells that develop from monocytes; they patrol tissues and clean up cellular debris as well as microbes.
  • Natural Killer cells: Lymphocytes that destroy virus-infected body cells and tumor cells by releasing chemical toxins.

The Inflammatory Response

Inflammation is a localized tissue reaction to injury, irritation, or infection. Damaged cells release chemical alarms, notably histamine, which causes local blood vessels to dilate and become more permeable. This increased blood flow brings vital immune cells and clotting factors directly to the affected area.

The classic signs of inflammation include redness and warmth from increased blood flow, swelling from fluid leaking into tissues, and pain caused by pressure on nerve endings. While uncomfortable, this process isolates the injury and promotes rapid tissue repair.

Fever as a Systemic Defence Mechanism

A fever is a systemic elevation of core body temperature regulated by the hypothalamus. When immune cells encounter pathogens, they release signalling molecules called pyrogens. These pyrogens reset the body's internal thermostat to a higher temperature.

Elevated body temperatures create a hostile environment for many pathogens by reducing their ability to reproduce. At the same time, fever accelerates metabolic rates, speeding up tissue repair and enhancing the activity of white blood cells.

Antimicrobial Proteins: Complement and Interferons

Antimicrobial proteins provide powerful chemical backup in non-specific defence. The complement system consists of a group of plasma proteins that circulate in an inactive state. When triggered by infection, they activate in a cascade that punches holes in bacterial cell membranes, leading to cell lysis.

Interferons are signaling proteins released by virus-infected cells. They bind to neighboring healthy cells, warning them to produce protective compounds that block viral replication, effectively halting the spread of the infection.

Clinical Implications and Nursing Care

Understanding non-specific defences helps nurses protect vulnerable patients in clinical settings. Breaks in the skin from intravenous lines, surgical incisions, or urinary catheters bypass the first line of defence, greatly increasing infection risks. Strict aseptic technique during dressing changes and catheter care is vital to prevent hospital-acquired infections.

Nurses must also recognize that dampening an inflammatory response too aggressively, or reducing every mild fever with antipyretics, can sometimes interfere with the body's natural healing process. Patient assessments should focus on monitoring for signs of localized infection versus systemic deterioration.
