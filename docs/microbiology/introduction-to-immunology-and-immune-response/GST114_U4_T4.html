Non-specific Body Defences

Introduction to Non-Specific Body Defences

Non-specific body defences, also known as innate immunity, represent the body's primary, generalized response system against any invading pathogen. Unlike specific or adaptive immunity, which tailors its response to a particular foreign antigen and develops immunological memory, innate immunity is present from birth and acts rapidly without prior exposure to the pathogen. Nursing students in Nigeria must understand that these defence mechanisms are broadly categorized into two tiers: the first line of defence, which includes external physical and chemical barriers that prevent pathogens from entering the body tissues, and the second line of defence, which involves internal processes such as phagocytic cells, inflammation, fever, and specialized antimicrobial proteins that combat invaders once they breach the surface. These systems do not target specific strains of bacteria or viruses; instead, they recognize broad molecular patterns common to many pathogens. This makes innate defences our frontline shield in everyday life, protecting against the countless environmental microorganisms encountered in hospitals, communities, and tropical climates. When these non-specific barriers are compromised—such as through burns, surgical incisions, or chronic illnesses like diabetes—patients become exceptionally vulnerable to opportunistic infections. Recognizing the limitations and functions of innate immunity allows nurses to implement effective preventative care, maintain skin and mucosal integrity, and identify early signs of localized or systemic infection in diverse clinical settings.

First Line of Defence: Anatomical and Physiological Barriers

The first line of defence comprises anatomical and physiological barriers that physically and chemically block pathogens from entering the internal environment of the body. The skin is the largest anatomical barrier, featuring multiple layers of keratinized epithelial cells that create a tough, nearly impenetrable shield against microbial entry. Furthermore, the skin's surface is kept slightly dry and acidic by sebum and sweat, which inhibits the growth of many pathogenic bacteria and fungi. Mucous membranes line the respiratory, digestive, urinary, and reproductive tracts, providing a moist surface that traps foreign particles. In the respiratory tract, the mucociliary escalator—composed of beating cilia and mucus-producing goblet cells—constantly sweeps trapped dust and pathogens upward toward the pharynx, where they can be swallowed and destroyed by stomach acid or expelled through coughing. Physiological barriers also include natural flushing mechanisms such as tears washing the eyes, saliva cleansing the oral cavity, urine flushing the urinary tract, and peristalsis moving substances through the gastrointestinal tract. For nursing practice, preserving these barriers is paramount. Invasive procedures like catheterization, endotracheal intubation, or intravenous cannulation bypass these natural defenses, directly increasing the risk of healthcare-associated infections. Nursing interventions that minimize the duration of indwelling devices, promote proper hygiene, and protect skin integrity are therefore direct applications of supporting the patient's first line of defence.

Chemical and Biochemical Defences

In addition to physical structures, chemical and biochemical defenses provide an inhospitable environment for potential pathogens at body surfaces. Body secretions contain specialized antimicrobial chemicals that destroy or inhibit microbial proliferation. For example, tears, saliva, and nasal secretions contain lysozyme, an enzyme that hydrolyzes the peptidoglycan cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria, leading to their lysis and death. The stomach produces hydrochloric acid and digestive enzymes that create a strongly acidic environment, destroying most ingested microorganisms before they can reach the intestines. Sweat and sebum secreted by skin glands maintain an acidic pH mantle (typically between 3.5 and 5.5), which deters the colonization of transient pathogenic bacteria. Additionally, antimicrobial peptides known as defensins are produced by epithelial cells and white blood cells; these small molecules disrupt the cell membranes of bacteria, fungi, and enveloped viruses. In the female reproductive tract, the acidic environment maintained by normal vaginal flora (Lactobacilli) prevents the overgrowth of harmful microbes like Candida albicans. Nurses must be aware of how interventions can disrupt these biochemical defenses. For instance, frequent or inappropriate douching can wash away protective vaginal flora and alter pH, leading to secondary infections. Similarly, long-term use of broad-spectrum antibiotics can eradicate normal protective bacterial flora across various body sites, allowing resistant pathogens to thrive and cause superinfections like antibiotic-associated colitis.

Second Line of Defence: Cellular Components and Phagocytosis

When pathogens successfully breach the body's physical and chemical barriers, they encounter the second line of defence, which is heavily reliant on cellular components and the process of phagocytosis. The primary cellular defenders are leukocytes (white blood cells), specifically phagocytes such as neutrophils and macrophages, along with natural killer (NK) cells. Neutrophils are typically the first responders to an infection site; they are abundant, highly mobile, and rapidly engulf and destroy bacteria through enzymatic action. Monocytes migrate from the blood into tissues where they differentiate into macrophages, which are larger, long-lived phagocytic cells that clean up cellular debris, dead neutrophils, and pathogens. Phagocytosis is a multi-step process: first, the phagocyte adheres to the pathogen using surface receptors; second, it extends pseudopodia to engulf the microbe, enclosing it in a vesicle called a phagosome; third, the phagosome fuses with a lysosome to form a phagolysosome; and finally, digestive enzymes and toxic oxygen radicals break down and destroy the pathogen. Natural killer (NK) cells constitute another vital group; instead of phagocytizing microbes directly, they patrol the body and monitor cellular abnormalities, destroying virus-infected body cells and tumor cells by releasing perforins and granzymes that induce apoptosis. In clinical practice, monitoring white blood cell differentials—such as a high neutrophil count (neutrophilia)—gives nurses crucial diagnostic insight into acute bacterial infections, guiding effective patient care and antibiotic administration.

The Inflammatory Response

The inflammatory response is a localized, protective tissue reaction to injury, irritation, or infection. Its primary goals are to confine the extent of injury, neutralize the offending agent, and set the stage for tissue repair and healing. Cardinal signs of acute inflammation include redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor), and loss of function (functio laesa). These signs are driven by vascular and cellular changes. When tissue is injured, resident mast cells release inflammatory mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes. Histamine causes local blood vessels to dilate (vasodilation), increasing blood flow to the area, which accounts for the redness and heat. Concurrently, vasodilation and increased capillary permeability allow fluid, proteins, and white blood cells to leak out of the bloodstream into the interstitial tissue spaces, causing localized swelling (edema). The accumulation of fluid and pressure on nerve endings generates pain. As blood flow slows down in dilated vessels, neutrophils and later monocytes marginate and emigrate through vessel walls into the tissue via diapedesis to perform phagocytosis. Fibrinogen leaking into the area forms a meshwork that walls off the injured site, preventing the spread of pathogens. Purulent exudate, commonly known as pus, often accumulates, consisting of dead neutrophils, tissue debris, and fluid. For nursing students, understanding inflammation helps interpret common clinical symptoms and aids in managing wounds, reducing swelling through cold or warm compresses, and administering anti-inflammatory medications safely.

Fever as a Systemic Defence Mechanism

Fever, or pyrexia, is a systemic response to infection and tissue damage, orchestrated by the central nervous system. When phagocytic white blood cells encounter pathogens or tissue injury, they release chemical signals called endogenous pyrogens, including interleukins and tumor necrosis factor. These pyrogens travel through the bloodstream to the hypothalamus, the thermoregulatory center of the brain, and trigger the synthesis of prostaglandins (specifically PGE2), which reset the body's internal thermostat to a higher temperature set-point. To reach this new, elevated set-point, the body initiates heat-promoting mechanisms such as peripheral vasoconstriction (reducing heat loss through the skin), shivering (increasing skeletal muscle metabolic heat production), and behavioral changes like putting on warm clothing. Patients often experience chills and feel cold as their core temperature rises toward the new set-point. Once the pyrogen threat subsides, the thermostat resets to normal, triggering heat-loss mechanisms like vasodilation and sweating (crisis or defervescence). Moderate fever serves several beneficial defensive purposes: it inhibits the reproduction of many temperature-sensitive pathogens, enhances the motility and proliferation of lymphocytes, accelerates metabolic reactions, and speeds up tissue repair. However, extremely high fevers can be dangerous, causing cellular damage, denaturation of proteins, febrile seizures in children, and delirium in adults. Nursing responsibilities include monitoring vital signs, administering antipyretic medications when indicated, encouraging adequate oral hydration to offset fluid loss from sweating, and providing comfort measures.

Antimicrobial Proteins: The Complement System and Interferons

Antimicrobial proteins, notably the complement system and interferons, provide powerful chemical defenses as part of the innate immune response. The complement system consists of a group of over thirty plasma proteins synthesized mainly by the liver, circulating in an inactive state. When activated through pathways such as the classical, lectin, or alternative pathways, these proteins trigger a cascade of enzymatic reactions. This cascade enhances the immune response in several ways: it promotes inflammation by releasing anaphylatoxins that stimulate mast cells, coats pathogens with molecules like C3b to enhance phagocytosis (a process known as opsonization), and forms membrane attack complexes (MACs) that punch holes in the cell membranes of invading bacteria, leading to osmotic lysis and cell death. Interferons (IFNs) are signaling proteins produced and released by virus-infected host cells. When neighboring, uninfected cells receive interferon signals, they synthesize antiviral proteins that inhibit viral replication, effectively halting the spread of the infection through the tissue. Interferons also stimulate the activity of natural killer cells and macrophages. For nursing students, understanding these proteins highlights how the body naturally combats viral and bacterial infections at a molecular level, reinforcing why supporting organ function and nutrition is vital for immune competence. Deficiencies in complement proteins, for instance, lead to recurrent severe bacterial infections, requiring specialized clinical vigilance and prophylactic interventions.

Clinical Implications and Nursing Relevance in Patient Care

Comprehending non-specific body defences is crucial for clinical practice and patient care across all healthcare settings in Nigeria. Nurses frequently care for patients whose natural defenses are compromised due to age, malnutrition, immunosuppressive medications, chemotherapy, surgery, or chronic conditions like HIV/AIDS and diabetes mellitus. Recognizing that skin breakdown, mucosal damage, and indwelling medical devices compromise the first line of defence enables nurses to prioritize meticulous aseptic techniques, timely dressing changes, and early removal of urinary catheters. Furthermore, understanding inflammatory and systemic responses allows nurses to correctly interpret clinical observations—such as differentiating between normal post-operative inflammation and spreading infection, or evaluating vital signs like temperature and white blood cell counts. Nurses also educate patients and their families on maintaining robust innate immunity through proper nutrition, adequate hydration, rest, and personal hygiene. In tropical environments, where vector-borne diseases and water-borne pathogens are prevalent, protecting physical barriers (e.g., using bed nets, wearing protective footwear) and supporting physiological defenses can prevent severe illnesses. Ultimately, integrating knowledge of non-specific defences into daily nursing assessments empowers clinicians to detect early signs of sepsis, advocate for prompt diagnostic testing, and deliver holistic, evidence-based care that promotes rapid healing and recovery.
