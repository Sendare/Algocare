Non-specific Body Defences

Introduction to Non-Specific Body Defences

Non-specific body defences, scientifically known as **innate immunity**, represent the body's immediate, generalized response to any foreign invader. Unlike adaptive immunity, which targets specific pathogens and requires time to mount a tailored attack, innate defences are present from birth and act within minutes of a pathogen breaching the body. These mechanisms do not improve with repeated exposure, meaning the body reacts to a second encounter with the same organism with the exact same speed and intensity as the first. In Nigerian clinical practice, where patients frequently present with endemic infections like malaria or typhoid, understanding this baseline defense helps clinicians appreciate how quickly the body attempts to contain localized insults before systemic overwhelming occurs.

First Line of Defence: Anatomical and Physiological Barriers

The first line of defence consists of physical and chemical barriers that prevent pathogens from entering the internal environment. These structural boundaries form the initial wall against environmental microbes. - **Skin**: The stratified squamous epithelium of the epidermis provides a tough, dry, keratinized outer layer that microorganisms struggle to penetrate unless broken by wounds or injections. - **Mucous Membranes**: Lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary tracts, these membranes produce sticky mucus that traps inhaled or ingested dust, dirt, and pathogens. - **Cilia**: Microscopic hair-like projections in the respiratory tract beat in unison to sweep trapped mucus and debris upward toward the throat, where it is swallowed or expelled. - **Physiological Flushing**: Tears, saliva, and urine flush surfaces continuously, physically washing away transient microbes before they can establish colonization.

Chemical and Biochemical Defences

Beyond physical structures, body fluids secrete chemical substances that actively inhibit or destroy invading microorganisms. These biochemical agents create a hostile microenvironment. - **Gastric Acid**: Hydrochloric acid secreted by the stomach creates a highly acidic pH (1.5 to 3.5) that destroys most swallowed pathogens. - **Lysozyme**: An enzyme found in tears, sweat, and saliva that breaks down the peptidoglycan cell walls of Gram-positive bacteria. - **Sebum and Sweat**: Sebum produced by sebaceous glands creates a low pH environment on the skin surface, while sweat contains salt and antimicrobial peptides. - **Normal Microbiota**: Harmless resident bacteria on the skin and gut mucosa outcompete pathogenic microorganisms for space and nutrients.

Second Line of Defence: Cellular Components and Phagocytosis

When a pathogen manages to breach the physical and chemical barriers, it encounters the second line of defence, which features specialized internal cellular components. The primary actors here are **leukocytes** (white blood cells), particularly phagocytes such as neutrophils and macrophages. Phagocytosis is a multi-step cellular process where a cell engulfs a solid particle or microbe. 1. **Chemotaxis**: Phagocytes are attracted to the site of infection by chemical signals released by damaged cells or pathogens. 2. **Adherence**: The phagocyte attaches to the surface of the target microorganism. 3. **Ingestion**: Extensions of the cell membrane, called pseudopods, wrap around the microbe and engulf it into a vesicle known as a phagosome. 4. **Digestion**: The phagosome fuses with a lysosome to form a phagolysosome, where digestive enzymes and reactive oxygen species destroy the ingested pathogen.

The Inflammatory Response

Inflammation is a localized tissue reaction triggered by physical injury, burns, or microbial infection. Its primary purpose is to confine the damage, destroy the agent of injury, and set the stage for tissue repair. Damaged tissue cells and mast cells release chemical mediators such as **histamine**, prostaglandins, and leukotrienes. These substances cause local vasodilation, increasing blood flow to the area, which results in the classic signs of inflammation: redness (rubor) and heat (calor). Concurrently, capillary permeability increases, allowing plasma to leak into the interstitial space, producing localized swelling (tumor) and pain (dolor) due to nerve ending compression. The elevated blood flow and altered vessel permeability recruit neutrophils and monocytes to migrate into the tissue via diapedesis to clear out debris and pathogens.

Fever as a Systemic Defence Mechanism

Fever, or pyrexia, is an abnormally high systemic body temperature that serves as a valuable secondary defence mechanism. When phagocytes ingest gram-negative bacteria or other pathogens, they release proteins called **pyrogens**, particularly endogenous pyrogens like interleukin-1. These molecules travel through the bloodstream to the hypothalamus, the brain's thermoregulatory center, and reset the body's thermostat to a higher temperature setting. An elevated temperature creates an unfavorable internal climate for many pathogens, directly inhibiting their growth and replication rates. Furthermore, fever enhances the metabolic rate of body cells, which accelerates tissue repair processes and speeds up the maturation and activity of immune cells. While a moderate fever is beneficial, extremely high temperatures require clinical intervention to prevent cellular damage.

Antimicrobial Proteins: The Complement System and Interferons

Antimicrobial proteins provide chemical reinforcement to cellular defences by directly attacking microorganisms or hindering their spread. | Protein Group | Primary Mechanism of Action | | --- | --- | | **Complement System** | A group of blood plasma proteins that cascade in activation, leading to cell lysis, opsonization of pathogens, and enhancement of inflammation. | | **Interferons** | Signaling proteins released by virus-infected cells that bind to neighboring uninfected cells, inducing them to synthesize defensive proteins that block viral replication. | Both systems act rapidly without prior sensitization, representing critical components of the innate immune network.

Clinical Implications and Nursing Relevance in Patient Care

Nurses play a critical role in supporting and monitoring a patient's non-specific body defences during clinical care. Recognizing when these barriers fail prevents hospital-acquired infections. For example, breaking the skin barrier with an intravenous cannula or urinary catheter provides a direct route for pathogens to bypass the first line of defence, making strict aseptic technique vital during insertion and maintenance. Furthermore, nurses must look beyond simply suppressing symptoms like fever, understanding that mild elevations in temperature are part of the body's defense strategy. Clinical assessments should focus on inspecting surgical wounds for signs of localized inflammation versus abnormal infection, tracking vital signs, and ensuring adequate hydration and nutrition to support metabolic and immune function.
