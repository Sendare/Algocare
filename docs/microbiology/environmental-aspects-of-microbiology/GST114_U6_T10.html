Classification of Health Care Waste

Introduction to Health Care Waste

Health care waste encompasses all the waste generated during medical diagnosis, treatment, immunization, or research involving humans and animals. Roughly 75% to 90% of this material is completely non-hazardous, originating from administrative areas, kitchens, and routine patient care. The remaining 10% to 25% is categorized as hazardous and poses various environmental and public health risks. Nurses stand at the forefront of generation and management, making awareness of these categories a daily safety requirement.

General Health Care Waste (Non-Hazardous)

General waste is comparable to ordinary municipal household refuse and does not pose any biological, chemical, or radioactive hazards. Examples include cardboard boxes, food leftovers from non-infectious patients, plastic water bottles, and office paper. This stream does not require any special treatment methods beyond standard municipal collection and disposal, provided it has not been contaminated by infectious fluids.

Infectious and Pathological Waste

Infectious waste contains pathogens like bacteria, viruses, parasites, or fungi in sufficient concentration to cause disease in susceptible hosts. This group includes laboratory cultures, swabs, bandages carrying blood, and disposable medical devices soiled with body fluids. Pathological waste is a subset consisting of human tissues, organs, body parts, and recognizable fetuses. Both streams demand stringent handling and pre-treatment, usually by autoclaving or incineration, to destroy viable microorganisms before final disposal.

Sharps Waste and Injury Prevention

Sharps include any objects that can puncture or cut skin, such as hypodermic needles, scalpels, infusion sets with needles, and broken glass ampoules. These items carry a severe double threat: direct physical trauma combined with the transmission of blood-borne pathogens like Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV.

  • Immediate Disposal: Never recap needles after use; drop them directly into a rigid container.
  • Overfilling Risk: Seal and replace safety boxes once they reach the three-quarters full mark.
  • Placement: Keep puncture-resistant containers at eye level and within arm's reach wherever injections occur.

Pharmaceutical and Cytotoxic Waste

Pharmaceutical waste comprises expired, unused, spilled, and contaminated drugs, vaccines, and sera that are no longer required. Cytotoxic waste is a highly dangerous subset consisting of medications with teratogenic, mutagenic, or carcinogenic properties, commonly used in cancer chemotherapy. Cytotoxic drugs require extreme caution from preparation to disposal, often needing high-temperature incineration above 1,000°C to completely destroy active chemical compounds.

Chemical and Radioactive Waste

Chemical waste consists of discarded substances used in diagnostic and experimental work, cleaning procedures, and housekeeping, such as laboratory reagents, solvents, disinfectants, and heavy metals like mercury from broken thermometers. Radioactive waste includes solid, liquid, and gaseous materials contaminated with radionuclides originating from radiotherapy or clinical laboratories. Radioactive materials must be stored in shielded containers until their activity decays to safe background levels before final handling.

Color-Coding and Containerization Standards in Nigeria

Standardized color-coding systems prevent confusion and ensure that every category of waste reaches the correct treatment facility. In Nigeria, national guidelines align with international best practices to streamline sorting at the bedside:

  • Black or Brown Bin: General, non-hazardous municipal waste.
  • Yellow Bin with Biohazard Symbol: Infectious and pathological waste requiring autoclaving or incineration.
  • Yellow Puncture-Proof Box: Sharps waste exclusively.
  • Red or Cytotoxic-Labeled Bin: Cytotoxic and heavy chemical pharmaceutical waste.
