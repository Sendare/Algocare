Sewage Disposal

Introduction to Sewage and Public Health

Sewage refers to liquid waste containing human excreta, household slops, and industrial effluents. In public health, the primary objective of safe sewage management is to break the epidemiological chain of the **fecal-oral route**. When wastewater accumulates near human habitation, it creates ideal breeding grounds for vectors like mosquitoes and contaminates local drinking water sources, particularly groundwater tables during the rainy season in regions with high water tables. Effective sanitation acts as an absolute barrier against waterborne scourges, safeguarding entire communities from preventable morbidity.

Composition and Microbiology of Sewage

Raw sewage is deceptively mundane in appearance: roughly 99.9% of its mass is pure water, while the remaining 0.1% consists of suspended and dissolved organic and inorganic solids. Despite the tiny fraction of solid matter, this mixture teems with billions of microorganisms. Bacteria, viruses, protozoan cysts, and helminth ova thrive within the nutrient-rich stream. Most of these microbes are harmless saprophytes breaking down organic matter, but a significant minority represent pathogenic agents shed by infected individuals in the community.

Pathogens Associated with Improper Sewage Disposal

Flushed waste carries a dangerous cross-section of community infections. The spectrum of disease agents spans several major biological categories: - **Bacterial Pathogens**: *Vibrio cholerae* and *Salmonella typhi*, responsible for severe diarrheal disease and enteric fever respectively. - **Viral Agents**: Hepatitis A and poliovirus, which spread rapidly through contaminated water systems. - **Protozoan Parasites**: *Entamoeba histolytica* and *Giardia lamblia*, whose resistant cysts withstand standard environmental stressors. - **Helminths**: Ascaris lumbricoides and hookworms, whose eggs persist in damp soil long after raw sewage has been used indiscriminately for crop irrigation.

On-Site Sewage Disposal Systems

Where municipal sewer lines are absent, individual properties rely on on-site containment and treatment. The most common unit is the **septicsystem**, comprising a watertight underground settling tank connected to a perforated distribution network within a drain field. Solid matter settles to the bottom forming sludge, oils float as scum, and partially clarified liquid flows into the soil for biological filtration. In many urban and peri-urban areas across Nigeria, ventilated improved pit (VIP) latrines and standard soakaway pits remain the prevailing methods, requiring periodic mechanical exhauster services to prevent overflow and environmental contamination.

Off-Site Municipal Sewage Systems

Urban centers manage massive volumes of wastewater through extensive networks of underground gravity pipes, manholes, and lift pumping stations. This infrastructure transports household and industrial runoff away from populated zones toward centralized treatment facilities. Maintaining these networks requires constant vigilance against root intrusion, structural collapse, and illicit solid waste dumping that blocks transit channels. When municipal pipes rupture near broken water supply mains, cross-contamination leads directly to widespread gastrointestinal outbreaks.

Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Wastewater Treatment

Centralized treatment facilities employ a tiered sequence of purification stages to render effluent safe for environmental release: 1. **Primary Treatment**: Physical screening and sedimentation remove large debris and settle out heavy suspended solids. 2. **Secondary Treatment**: Biological processes use aerobic microorganisms in trickling filters or activated sludge basins to dissolve and consume remaining organic compounds. 3. **Tertiary Treatment**: Advanced chemical or physical polishing removes nutrients like nitrates and phosphates, alongside final disinfection using ultraviolet radiation or chlorination.

Environmental and Community Impact of Sewage Pollution

Discharging raw or inadequately treated sewage into natural water bodies triggers catastrophic ecological degradation. Organic enrichment forces a surge in microbial activity, which rapidly consumes dissolved oxygen in the water column. This process, measured as biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), suffocates fish and aquatic flora, creating dead zones. Furthermore, bioaccumulation of chemical pollutants and heavy metals in aquatic food chains poses long-term toxicological threats to human populations relying on local fisheries for sustenance.

The Role of Nurses in Sanitation and Disease Prevention

Nurses occupy a vital frontline position in mitigating the health consequences of poor sanitation. Beyond clinical treatment of gastroenteritis and parasitic infestations, community health nurses conduct targeted health education campaigns on hand hygiene, safe water storage, and proper excreta disposal. In acute outbreak settings, such as cholera epidemics, nursing personnel coordinate rapid rehydration therapy, enforce strict barrier nursing principles, and collaborate with environmental health officers to trace contamination sources back to faulty community sanitation infrastructure.
