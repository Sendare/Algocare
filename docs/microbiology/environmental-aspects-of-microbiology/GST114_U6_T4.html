Sewage Disposal

Introduction to Sewage and Public Health

Sewage refers to the liquid waste collected from domestic, commercial, and industrial establishments, combined with ground water, surface water, and storm water. In public health nursing, proper management of this waste is foundational to preventing disease transmission. When wastewater accumulates without adequate drainage or treatment, it creates breeding grounds for vectors like mosquitoes and pollutes surrounding water sources used for drinking and bathing.

Waterborne illnesses remain a major challenge in resource-limited settings across Nigeria. Pathogens from human waste easily contaminate local streams and boreholes if infrastructure fails. Community health nurses witness firsthand how poor environmental sanitation triggers outbreaks of preventable infections, making sewage control a primary line of defense in healthcare.

Composition and Microbiology of Sewage

Wastewater is largely water, typically containing less than 0.1% dissolved and suspended solid impurities. These impurities include human excreta, food scraps, soaps, oils, and chemical residues.

  • Organic matter: Proteins, carbohydrates, and fats that serve as nutrients for microorganisms.
  • Inorganic matter: Sand, grit, grit, salts, and heavy metals from industrial runoff.
  • Microorganisms: Billions of bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa, including both harmless decomposers and dangerous pathogens.

Aerobic and anaerobic bacteria break down the organic load in sewage. As they consume waste, they consume dissolved oxygen in the water, which can suffocate aquatic life if untreated effluent enters natural water bodies.

Pathogens Associated with Improper Sewage Disposal

Improperly managed wastewater is a vehicle for a wide variety of infectious agents. Transmission typically occurs through the fecal-oral route, either by drinking contaminated water or eating food washed with it.

  • Bacteria: *Vibrio cholerae* causes severe watery diarrhea and rapid dehydration; *Salmonella typhi* causes systemic typhoid fever.
  • Viruses: Hepatitis A and poliovirus spread through contaminated fluids, attacking the liver and central nervous system respectively.
  • Protozoa: *Entamoeba histolytica* and *Giardia lamblia* cause persistent dysentery and malabsorption syndromes.
  • Helminths: Ascaris and hookworm ova persist in soil fertilized by raw sewage, leading to chronic anemia and malnutrition in children.

On-Site Sewage Disposal Systems

Where municipal sewer lines are absent, individual properties rely on on-site sanitation facilities to contain and partially treat waste.

  • Pit Latrines: Basic pits dug into the ground where excreta accumulates and degrades slowly through anaerobic digestion.
  • Septic Tanks: Underground concrete or plastic chambers that separate solid sludge from liquid effluent. Anaerobic bacteria break down solids, while liquids flow out into a soakaway pit or drain field for soil filtration.
  • Ventilated Improved Pit (VIP) Latrines: An upgraded pit design featuring a vertical vent pipe with a fly screen to reduce odors and control insect breeding.

Proper siting is essential for all on-site systems to prevent raw effluent from leaching into shallow groundwater wells used for drinking.

Municipal Sewage Systems and Treatment Stages

Urban areas utilize centralized networks of underground pipes, pumping stations, and treatment plants to manage large volumes of community wastewater. This infrastructure moves waste away from populated zones toward treatment facilities before safe discharge or reuse.

  • Primary Treatment: A physical process where wastewater passes through screens to remove large debris, followed by sedimentation tanks where heavy solids settle out as sludge and oils float to the top.
  • Secondary Treatment: A biological process where microorganisms are introduced to aerated wastewater to consume dissolved and suspended organic compounds.
  • Tertiary Treatment: Advanced chemical or physical filtration steps to remove remaining nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus, along with disinfection using chlorine or ultraviolet light to eliminate residual pathogens.

Environmental Impact and Community Health Consequences

When sewage management systems fail, the consequences ripple across both the ecosystem and human populations. Uncontrolled discharge into rivers and lagoons causes severe environmental degradation. The surplus nutrients from organic waste trigger massive algal blooms, which consume oxygen and result in fish kills.

From a nursing perspective, communities experiencing sewage overflows face high burdens of cholera, typhoid, and skin infections. Children playing in stagnant, contaminated floodwaters are especially vulnerable. Beyond infectious diseases, decaying waste emits foul gases like hydrogen sulfide, causing chronic respiratory irritation and lowering the overall quality of life in affected neighborhoods.

The Role of Nurses in Sanitation and Disease Prevention

Nurses play a vital role beyond hospital walls by acting as educators and advocates for environmental health. In primary healthcare clinics and community outposts, nurses identify sanitation gaps and lead public health campaigns.

  • Education: Teaching families proper handwashing techniques, safe water storage, and hygienic food preparation.
  • Advocacy: Collaborating with local leaders and environmental health officers to demand functional drainage and waste management infrastructure.
  • Surveillance: Tracking and reporting clusters of waterborne illnesses like diarrhea or cholera to prompt rapid public health interventions.
