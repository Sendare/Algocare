Sewage Disposal

Introduction to Sewage and Public Health

Sewage disposal is a critical component of public health and environmental sanitation, particularly in developing nations like Nigeria where rapid urbanization often outpaces infrastructural development. Sewage refers to wastewater generated from domestic, industrial, commercial, or agricultural activities that contains human excreta, dissolved minerals, and various chemical wastes. Proper management of this waste is vital to prevent the spread of waterborne diseases, protect aquatic ecosystems, and ensure the overall well-being of communities. When sewage is inadequately collected or treated, it accumulates in residential areas or discharges directly into local water bodies such as rivers, lagoons, and streams used for drinking, bathing, and irrigation. This creates a breeding ground for vectors and exposes populations to severe health hazards. For student nurses, understanding sewage disposal goes beyond theoretical biology; it is foundational to community health nursing and epidemiological control. By grasping how improper waste management facilitates the transmission of pathogens, nurses can better educate the public on hygienic practices, advocate for improved community sanitation infrastructure, and participate actively in disease outbreak prevention strategies. Effective sanitation infrastructure serves as the primary barrier preventing fecal-oral transmission cycles in vulnerable populations, thereby drastically reducing morbidity and mortality rates associated with infectious diseases.

Composition and Microbiology of Sewage

To effectively manage wastewater, nurses and public health practitioners must understand the composition and microbiology of sewage. Sewage is predominantly water, typically around 99.9%, with the remaining 0.1% consisting of suspended and dissolved organic and inorganic solids. The organic fraction includes human feces, urine, food scraps, soaps, and paper products, while inorganic components include grit, salts, and heavy metals. Microbiologically, sewage is a dense ecosystem teeming with microorganisms. This includes normal enteric flora, harmless saprophytes, and potentially pathogenic bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoan cysts. Key bacterial groups frequently isolated from sewage include Escherichia coli, Salmonella species, Shigella, and Vibrio cholerae. Viruses such as rotaviruses, enteroviruses, and the hepatitis A virus are also routinely present, shed through the feces of infected individuals. Furthermore, parasites like Giardia lamblia and Entamoeba histolytica find their way into sewage systems in the form of hardy cysts or ova. The presence of these microorganisms makes raw sewage biologically hazardous. Understanding this complex microbiology highlights why personal protective equipment is essential when dealing with wastewater and why strict hygiene practices must be observed by healthcare personnel and sanitation workers alike.

Pathogens Associated with Improper Sewage Disposal

Improper sewage disposal directly exposes communities to a wide array of pathogenic microorganisms, leading to preventable outbreaks of infectious diseases. The transmission routes are primarily waterborne or foodborne, often via the fecal-oral route where contaminated water or food is ingested. Classic waterborne bacterial diseases such as cholera, caused by Vibrio cholerae, and typhoid fever, caused by Salmonella typhi, frequently emerge in areas with compromised sewage infrastructure. These conditions can cause severe dehydration and systemic illness, posing life-threatening risks particularly to children and the elderly. Viral pathogens like hepatitis A and poliovirus are also readily spread through sewage-contaminated environments, infecting new hosts through ingestion or poor hand hygiene. Additionally, parasitic infections such as amoebiasis and giardiasis cause prolonged gastrointestinal distress, malabsorption, and chronic weakness. In regions with stagnant, untreated sewage pools, vector-borne diseases can also indirectly thrive, as these sites may attract vectors or worsen general environmental degradation. For nursing students, recognizing these specific pathogens and their associated clinical presentations is crucial for accurate assessment, prompt reporting to public health authorities, and implementing effective isolation and rehydration protocols in clinical and community settings.

On-Site Sewage Disposal Systems

In many suburban and rural areas, as well as in poorly serviced urban communities where central municipal sewerage is unavailable, on-site sewage disposal systems are utilized. These systems collect, treat, and discharge wastewater on the same property where it is generated. The most common on-site system is the septic tank, a buried, watertight container typically made of concrete or fiberglass. Wastewater from household plumbing flows into the septic tank, where a natural sedimentation and anaerobic digestion process occurs. Heavy solids settle to the bottom to form sludge, while grease and lighter materials float to the top as scum. The partially clarified liquid, known as effluent, then flows out of the tank into a drain field or subsurface absorption system, where it filters through soil layers that act as a natural biological filter. Another common on-site method is the Ventilated Improved Pit (VIP) latrine or traditional pit latrine, which isolates human excreta safely away from human contact. However, improper maintenance or high groundwater tables can cause on-site systems to fail, leading to surface pooling or the contamination of nearby shallow wells used for drinking water. Nurses must understand these systems to effectively educate rural and peri-urban communities on safe construction distances between pit latrines and water wells.

Off-Site Municipal Sewage Systems

Off-site municipal sewage systems represent a centralized approach to managing wastewater for densely populated urban areas. These comprehensive networks consist of house connections, lateral sewers, main trunk sewers, and large pumping stations that transport vast quantities of domestic and industrial wastewater away from residential zones. Gravity largely drives the flow of sewage through sloped underground pipes, though lift stations are employed when the topography requires wastewater to be pumped uphill. Once collected, the entire volume is conveyed to a central wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) before being discharged into a receiving water body like a river or ocean. While centralized systems are highly efficient and keep human waste out of immediate living spaces, they require massive capital investment, reliable electrical power, and rigorous engineering maintenance. In many Nigerian cities, aging infrastructure, rapid population growth, and blocked or broken pipelines frequently lead to sewer overflows, untreated discharges, and environmental degradation. Understanding the mechanics and vulnerabilities of municipal systems allows public health nurses to advocate for infrastructure repairs and recognize when municipal water sources are compromised by sewage line ruptures.

Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Wastewater Treatment

Wastewater treatment plants utilize multi-stage processes—primary, secondary, and tertiary treatment—to progressively clean sewage before it is released back into the environment. Primary treatment is a physical process that involves passing raw sewage through screens to remove large debris, followed by sedimentation tanks where heavy solids settle out as primary sludge and floating oils are skimmed off. Secondary treatment is a biological process where aerobic microorganisms are introduced to break down dissolved and suspended organic matter. Aeration tanks or trickling filters encourage bacteria to consume the organic pollutants, converting them into carbon dioxide, water, and biomass, which is then settled out in secondary clarifiers. Tertiary treatment is an advanced, specialized polishing stage designed to remove remaining nutrients like nitrogen and phosphorus, persistent chemicals, and microscopic pathogens through filtration, chemical precipitation, or disinfection methods like UV irradiation or chlorination. Each stage reduces the biological oxygen demand (BOD) of the water, preventing severe oxygen depletion in receiving aquatic ecosystems. Nurses should appreciate these purification stages as they determine whether discharged water is safe for agricultural reuse or recreational contact.

Environmental and Community Impact of Sewage Pollution

When sewage is improperly treated or disposed of directly into the environment, it exerts profound negative impacts on ecosystems and local communities. A primary consequence is organic pollution, which dramatically increases the biological oxygen demand (BOD) in water bodies as microorganisms consume dissolved oxygen to break down waste. This leads to hypoxia or anoxia—severe oxygen depletion—causing massive fish kills, destruction of aquatic flora, and the transformation of vibrant rivers into stagnant, foul-smelling sewers. Furthermore, raw sewage contains high levels of nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus. When these enter water bodies, they trigger eutrophication, a process characterized by explosive algal blooms that block sunlight and suffocate aquatic life. Beyond ecological damage, sewage pollution directly impacts community health and socioeconomic stability. Communities relying on polluted rivers for drinking water or fishing face chronic disease burdens, economic losses in agriculture and aquaculture, and diminished quality of life. For nurses, understanding these environmental ripple effects emphasizes that human health is inextricably linked to environmental health, reinforcing the 'One Health' paradigm where animal, human, and environmental health are managed in harmony.

The Role of Nurses in Sanitation and Disease Prevention

Nurses play a multi-faceted, indispensable role in sanitation advocacy, health education, and disease prevention related to sewage disposal. In community health settings, nurses act as educators by teaching households about safe water storage, proper handwashing techniques after using the toilet, and the construction of sanitary latrines away from water sources. They are also on the frontlines of epidemiological surveillance, tasked with identifying clusters of acute watery diarrhea, typhoid, or cholera that may indicate sewage contamination of a municipal water supply. Prompt reporting of these index cases to public health authorities can avert a full-scale epidemic. Furthermore, nurses participate in community mobilization, collaborating with local leaders and environmental health officers to organize clean-up campaigns, demand better municipal waste infrastructure, and promote sustainable sanitation policies. In clinical settings, nurses manage the care of patients suffering from waterborne illnesses, emphasizing rigorous infection prevention and control (IPC) standards to prevent nosocomial transmission. By bridging clinical care and public health advocacy, nurses safeguard community resilience and actively contribute to achieving global sustainable development goals related to clean water and sanitation.
