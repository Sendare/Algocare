Sewage Disposal

Introduction to Sewage and Public Health

Sewage refers to the liquid waste generated by residential, commercial, and industrial activities, containing human excreta, wash water, and various chemical pollutants. Safe management of this waste is one of the most critical pillars of public health nursing and environmental sanitation. When waste water accumulates or leaks into living spaces and drinking water sources, it creates an immediate breeding ground for disease-causing organisms. Historically, major urban cholera and typhoid outbreaks were traced directly back to contaminated water systems before modern sanitation engineering was introduced. Nurses must understand these foundational concepts because community health is directly tied to how cleanly a population disposes of its liquid waste. Proper disposal breaks the epidemiological chain of infection, keeping water sources pure and protecting vulnerable populations from preventable waterborne illnesses.

Composition and Microbiology of Sewage

Raw sewage is a complex mixture consisting of roughly 99.9% water and only 0.1% solid matter. Despite the tiny fraction of solids, that fraction packs billions of microorganisms, both harmless and pathogenic.

  • Suspended solids: Organic and inorganic particles floating in the liquid, including feces, food scraps, and paper fibers.
  • Dissolved matter: Soluble chemical compounds, urine byproducts, soaps, and detergents.
  • Microbial flora: Coliform bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoan cysts originating from the human gastrointestinal tract.

While many resident bacteria help break down organic material through biodegradation, the presence of enteric pathogens turns raw sewage into a biological hazard. Nurses evaluating sanitation risks should remember that even clear-looking effluent can carry high loads of invisible microbial life.

Pathogens Associated with Improper Sewage Disposal

Improperly managed wastewater exposes communities to a wide spectrum of infectious agents. Bacteria such as *Vibrio cholerae* and *Salmonella typhi* cause severe diarrheal illnesses and systemic infections through contaminated water supplies. Viral pathogens, notably Hepatitis A and norovirus, spread rapidly when runoff contaminates shellfish beds or drinking wells. Parasitic protozoa like *Giardia lamblia* and *Entamoeba histolytica* produce hardy cysts that survive harsh environmental conditions and standard chlorine disinfection processes, leading to prolonged gastrointestinal distress. A common clinical misconception is that boiling water is unnecessary if it looks clear; however, invisible microbial pathogens remain active until properly treated or thermally destroyed. Nurses caring for patients with persistent, watery diarrhea should always investigate potential water and sanitation breaches in the patient's immediate living environment.

On-Site Sewage Disposal Systems

In rural settings or areas lacking municipal infrastructure, households rely on on-site sanitation systems. The septic tank is the most common residential system, serving as an underground chamber where solid waste settles to the bottom as sludge, fats float to the top as scum, and partially clarified liquid flows out into a subsurface absorption field.

  • Pit latrines: Simple dug pits for human waste collection, suitable only for low-density rural areas with deep water tables.
  • Septic tanks: Watertight underground containers providing primary anaerobic treatment before soil absorption.
  • Drain fields: Perforated pipes resting in gravel trenches that distribute effluent safely into the surrounding soil for natural biological filtration.

Off-Site Municipal Sewage Systems

Urban areas require coordinated off-site networks to move large volumes of wastewater away from populated dwellings. Gravity-fed sewer mains collect waste from homes and businesses, routing it through underground trunk lines toward central treatment plants. When gravity is insufficient due to rolling terrain, lift stations and pumping units push the sewage upward. Two primary engineering designs dominate municipal collection: combined sewers, which carry both storm runoff and raw sewage in a single pipe, and separate sanitary sewers, which dedicate pipes strictly to human waste while storm water runs elsewhere. During heavy rainfall, combined systems frequently overflow, dumping untreated sewage directly into local rivers and lakes. Nurses advocating for environmental health should understand local municipal infrastructure designs, as combined sewer overflows present major biological contamination risks.

Wastewater Treatment Stages

Municipal treatment facilities process sewage through sequential stages to render the water safe for environmental discharge or reuse.

Treatment StagePrimary MechanismTarget Contaminants
PrimaryPhysical screening and sedimentationLarge debris, floating grease, suspended solids
SecondaryBiological degradation by microorganismsDissolved organic matter, biodegradable wastes
TertiaryAdvanced chemical and physical filtrationNutrients, heavy metals, residual pathogens

Primary treatment removes heavy objects and grit through physical barriers and settling basins. Secondary treatment introduces aerobic bacteria in aeration tanks to consume dissolved organic compounds. Tertiary treatment polishes the effluent using sand filters, membrane technology, and chemical disinfection like chlorination or ultraviolet light before releasing the clean water back into nature.

Environmental and Community Impact of Sewage Pollution

When raw or poorly treated sewage enters aquatic ecosystems, it triggers severe environmental degradation. High concentrations of organic matter stimulate rapid microbial growth, which rapidly consumes dissolved oxygen in the water. This phenomenon, known as biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), suffocates fish and aquatic plants, leading to massive fish kills and dead zones. Additionally, excessive nitrogen and phosphorus from sewage promote harmful algal blooms that block sunlight and release toxins into recreational waters. Communities living near polluted water bodies face chronic health hazards, including skin infections, eye irritations, and gastrointestinal illnesses from recreational contact or contaminated seafood. Nurses must recognize these ecological disruptions because environmental health directly influences the disease patterns seen in community clinics.

The Role of Nurses in Sanitation and Disease Prevention

Nurses play a multifaceted role in preventing sanitation-related diseases through clinical care, community advocacy, and health education. In clinical settings, nurses trace the origins of infectious outbreaks, teaching families about safe water storage, hand hygiene, and proper food preparation. At the community level, public health nurses advocate for improved municipal infrastructure, inspect local sanitation standards, and participate in emergency disaster responses where sanitation facilities have collapsed. By bridging the gap between clinical medicine and environmental hygiene, nurses empower individuals to protect themselves against waterborne diseases. Effective nursing practice goes beyond treating active infections to addressing the root environmental conditions that allow illnesses to spread.
