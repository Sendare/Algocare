Risks and Hazards of Health Care Waste

Introduction and Classification of Health Care Waste

Health care waste encompasses all the waste generated during medical diagnosis, treatment, immunization, or research involving humans and animals. About 75 to 90 percent of this waste is general, non-hazardous refuse that resembles domestic waste, originating from administrative, kitchen, and housekeeping functions. The remaining 10 to 25 percent is considered hazardous and poses various physical, chemical, or biological risks to healthcare workers, patients, and the community.

To manage this material safely, facilities divide waste into distinct groups:

  • General waste: Non-hazardous items like paper, cardboard, and food remnants that require no special handling.
  • Infectious waste: Materials contaminated with blood, body fluids, or cultures from laboratory work.
  • Pathological waste: Human tissues, organs, body parts, and recognizable fetuses.
  • Sharps: Objects capable of puncturing or cutting skin, including needles, scalpels, and broken glass.
  • Chemical and pharmaceutical waste: Expired drugs, solvents, disinfectants, and heavy metals like mercury.
  • Radioactive waste: Unused liquids from radiotherapy or research, and contaminated glassware.

Microbiological Hazards and Pathogen Transmission

Infectious waste carries pathogenic microorganisms that can multiply and cause disease if they enter the human body. Nurses and other health workers face exposure through skin puncture, mucosal contact, inhalation, or ingestion. Bloodborne pathogens such as Hepatitis B virus (HBV), Hepatitis C virus (HCV), and Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) are prime concerns due to their persistence and severity.

Transmission occurs most frequently when waste handlers come into accidental contact with soiled dressings, discarded linen, or contaminated laboratory cultures. Proper containment stops these pathogens from spreading into the wider hospital environment or reaching municipal dump sites where scavengers might sift through discarded items.

Chemical, Genotoxic, and Radioactive Hazards

Beyond biological threats, healthcare activities generate hazardous substances that can damage organs, alter DNA, or emit harmful radiation. Chemical waste includes toxic cleaning agents, disinfectants, and heavy metals such as mercury from broken sphygmomanometers. Exposure can cause skin burns, respiratory distress, or systemic toxicity.

Genotoxic waste contains substances with mutagenic, teratogenic, or carcinogenic properties, most notably cytotoxic drugs used in cancer treatment. These agents can damage cellular DNA, posing severe risks to personnel who prepare, administer, or dispose of them without appropriate safety barriers. Radioactive waste, generated in nuclear medicine departments, emits ionizing radiation that destroys living tissue and requires strict decay storage before disposal.

Sharps Injuries and Associated Occupational Risks

Sharps injuries represent one of the most immediate and dangerous occupational hazards in clinical practice. Needles, scalpels, infusion sets, and broken ampoules can easily penetrate skin if handled carelessly or disposed of improperly. A single contaminated needle stick can transmit over twenty different bloodborne pathogens, with Hepatitis B carrying the highest transmission risk per exposure incident.

Risk escalates during unsafe recapping of needles, overfilling of safety boxes, or failure to place puncture-proof containers close to the point of use. Institutional protocols strongly discourage recapping used needles; instead, workers must drop them immediately into designated safety containers.

Environmental Impact and Public Health Risks

Poor healthcare waste management creates severe environmental degradation and public health crises, particularly in resource-constrained settings like Nigeria. When infectious and chemical wastes are dumped alongside municipal refuse, they contaminate local soil and groundwater sources used by surrounding communities.

Open-air burning or primitive incineration of medical waste releases toxic pollutants, including dioxins and furans, into the atmosphere. Furthermore, exposed municipal dumpsites attract scavengers, stray animals, and disease vectors such as rats and flies, which carry pathogens back into residential areas and amplify disease outbreaks.

Standard Precautions and Safe Waste Management Practices

Safe healthcare waste management relies on consistent adherence to standard precautions. Every healthcare worker must treat all patient blood and body fluids as potentially infectious. Segregation must happen at the exact point of generation, requiring color-coded bins and puncture-resistant safety boxes positioned within arm's reach of every procedure area.

  • Yellow containers: Reserved for infectious and pathological waste destined for incineration or autoclaving.
  • Brown or black containers: Used for general, non-hazardous domestic waste.
  • Red or yellow puncture-proof boxes: Strictly designated for sharps.

Personal protective equipment, proper hand hygiene, and never overfilling containers past the three-quarters mark are fundamental daily habits that protect the entire healthcare team.

Regulatory Framework and Disposal Guidelines

Effective waste management requires strict adherence to national and international regulatory standards. In Nigeria, agencies like the Federal Ministry of Environment and the National Agency for Food and Drug Administration and Control (NAFDAC), alongside state environmental protection bodies, provide legal guidelines for medical waste handling.

Nurses hold a professional and legal duty to follow these protocols, ensuring that waste transfer, treatment, and final disposal comply with environmental laws. Proper documentation, training of support staff, and active participation in hospital waste committees help maintain accountability and protect public health.
