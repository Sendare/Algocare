Decontamination

Introduction and Core Terminology of Decontamination

Decontamination is the foundation of hospital infection control, designed to make medical devices, patient care equipment, and physical surfaces safe for handling or use. Without proper decontamination, healthcare facilities become transmission hubs for hospital-acquired infections. Understanding the exact definitions prevents dangerous clinical errors, such as using a disinfected instrument where a sterile one is mandatory.

  • Cleaning: The physical removal of organic matter, blood, tissue, and visible dirt using water, detergents, and mechanical action. This step is mandatory because dried protein protects microorganisms from subsequent chemical agents.
  • Disinfection: A process that eliminates many or all pathogenic microorganisms on inanimate objects, except bacterial spores, using thermal or chemical means.
  • Sterilization: A validated process used to render an object free from all forms of viable microorganisms, including bacterial endospores.

The Spaulding Classification System for Medical Devices

Dr. Earle Spaulding devised a rational approach to disinfection and sterilization based on the degree of risk of infection involved in the use of the items. This system classifies medical devices into three distinct categories to guide nurses on how to reprocess equipment safely.

CategoryDefinitionLevel of Processing RequiredExample
CriticalEnters sterile tissue or the vascular systemSterilizationSurgical instruments, needles
Semi-criticalTouches mucous membranes or non-intact skinHigh-level disinfectionEndoscopes, respiratory therapy equipment
Non-criticalTouches only intact skinLow-level disinfectionStethoscopes, blood pressure cuffs, bedpans

Misclassifying an item poses immediate clinical danger. For instance, treating a semi-critical endoscope as a non-critical item and wiping it with a basic disinfectant can transmit resilient pathogens directly to a patient's GI tract.

Methods of Physical Decontamination

Physical methods rely on heat, radiation, or mechanical action to destroy microorganisms without relying primarily on chemical toxicity.

  • Thermal Disinfection and Pasteurization: Uses hot water (typically 71 to 77 degrees Celsius for specific times) to reduce microbial load on items like dishware or certain respiratory parts.
  • Autoclaving (Steam Sterilization): The most dependable and widely used method in healthcare facilities. It utilizes moist heat under pressure to achieve temperatures high enough (usually 121 or 134 degrees Celsius) to destroy all microbial life, including tough endospores.
  • Radiation: Gamma radiation or electron beams used commercially for heat-sensitive disposable supplies like syringes and sutures.

Chemical Disinfectants and Antiseptics Used in Practice

Chemical agents are selected based on the required level of disinfection, material compatibility, and safety profile. Disinfectants are applied to inanimate objects, whereas antiseptics are formulated for living tissue.

  • Glutaraldehyde and Ortho-phthalaldehyde (OPA): High-level disinfectants frequently used for heat-sensitive endoscopes. They require careful rinsing because residual chemical can cause severe tissue irritation.
  • Chlorine Compounds (Sodium Hypochlorite): Household bleach solutions are highly effective against a broad spectrum of pathogens, including bloodborne viruses and spores when mixed at correct dilutions.
  • Alcohols (Ethanol and Isopropanol): Mid-level agents used for skin preparation and non-critical surface wiping. They evaporate quickly and lack residual action.
  • Quaternary Ammonium Compounds (Quats): Low-level disinfectants ideal for routine environmental cleaning of floors, beds, and bedside tables.

Step-by-Step Reprocessing Workflow for Instruments

Instrument reprocessing follows a strict linear workflow from the point of use to sterile storage to prevent cross-contamination between dirty and clean zones.

  1. Point of Use Care: Wipe away gross soil immediately after a procedure and keep instruments moist to prevent drying.
  2. Transport: Move contaminated instruments in leak-proof, puncture-resistant containers to the central processing department.
  3. Decontamination (Cleaning): Wash manually or mechanically using automated washer-disinfectors with enzymatic detergents.
  4. Inspection and Packaging: Check instruments for cleanliness, function, and wear, then package them with appropriate chemical indicators.
  5. Sterilization: Expose packaged sets to the validated sterilization cycle (steam, ethylene oxide, or hydrogen peroxide gas plasma).
  6. Storage: Store in clean, dry, dust-free shelving with controlled temperature and humidity.

Safety Measures and Occupational Health Hazards

Staff handling decontamination duties face significant chemical, biological, and physical hazards. Standard safety protocols protect personnel from injury and infection.

  • Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Heavy-duty utility gloves, waterproof aprons, face shields, and masks protect against splashes of blood and corrosive chemical agents.
  • Chemical Exposure Prevention: Adequate ventilation, eye-wash stations, and adherence to manufacturer dilution guidelines prevent respiratory distress and chemical burns.
  • Sharps Injury Protocols: Never recap needles manually. Use puncture-resistant bins situated at eye level near point-of-use areas to prevent needlestick injuries during cleanup.

Quality Assurance, Monitoring, and Auditing

Quality assurance ensures that decontamination and sterilization processes work consistently and effectively every single time. Facilities use three main types of monitoring indicators:

  • Mechanical Indicators: Gauges, digital displays, and printouts on sterilizers that measure time, temperature, and pressure during a cycle.
  • Chemical Indicators: Tapes, strips, and cards containing heat- or chemical-sensitive dyes that change color when exposed to specific processing conditions, confirming that a package was exposed to the process.
  • Biological Indicators (BIs): Highly resistant bacterial spores (such as Geobacillus stearothermophilus for steam) used in test vials to verify that conditions inside the sterilizer successfully killed all living spores.
