Handling and Disposal of Sharps

Introduction to Sharps Safety in Healthcare

Sharps safety is a core responsibility for every healthcare worker in Nigeria. Needles, scalpel blades, and glass ampoules are daily tools used for patient care, but they pose a constant physical threat if handled carelessly. Every accidental injury from a sharp tool carries the risk of transmitting dangerous bloodborne infections. Establishing a strict culture of safety protects nurses, doctors, cleaners, and patients from preventable harm. Safety is not just about avoiding injury; it is about respecting the tools of our profession and maintaining a secure clinical environment.

Classification and Examples of Medical Sharps

Medical sharps include any device with points or edges that can puncture or cut skin. Recognizing these items correctly ensures they go into the right waste stream.

  • Hypodermic needles: Hollow needles used for injections or drawing blood.
  • Surgical blades: Detachable scalpels used during procedures and incisions.
  • Lancets: Small pointed instruments used for capillary blood finger-prick tests.
  • Glass ampoules and broken glass: Medication containers and slides that can cause deep lacerations.

Risks of Needle-Stick Injuries and Bloodborne Pathogens

A needle-stick injury happens when a sharp object punctures the skin, inadvertently exposing the worker to a patient's blood or body fluids. The most critical pathogens transmitted through these injuries are the Hepatitis B virus, Hepatitis C virus, and the Human Immunodeficiency Virus. Hepatitis B carries the highest risk of transmission after a single needle exposure, though vaccination offers strong protection. Even a tiny drop of blood inside a hollow needle can carry enough viral load to cause a lifelong chronic infection.

Standard Precautions for Safe Handling

Safe handling habits prevent injuries before they ever happen. A common misconception is that recapping needles protects others; in reality, trying to put a cap back on a used needle is the leading cause of accidental stabs. Never recap used needles unless absolutely required by a specific clinical procedure using a one-handed scoop technique. Always activate safety devices immediately after use. Keep your eyes on what you are doing, never point a sharp toward any part of your body, and dispose of the item instantly.

Proper Use and Placement of Sharps Containers

Sharps containers must be puncture-resistant, leak-proof, and clearly marked with a biohazard symbol. Place these containers as close as practical to the point of use, such as on medication carts or treatment room walls, so staff never have to walk across a busy ward carrying an exposed needle. Never shake or force items into a full container. When the contents reach the fill line—usually about three-quarters full—it is time to close and replace the container.

Procedures for Safe Disposal and Sealing

Disposing of a full sharps container requires care to protect environmental services staff. Lock the temporary closure mechanism on the lid securely once the fill line is reached. Never push down on the contents with your hands to make more room. Store the sealed container in a designated, secure utility room awaiting final incineration. Always wear utility gloves when handling full waste boxes to add an extra layer of protection against unexpected punctures.

Immediate Actions Following a Needle-Stick Injury

If a needle-stick injury occurs, act quickly without panicking. First, encourage the wound to bleed gently by washing the area thoroughly with soap and running water; do not scrub harshly or use harsh chemicals like bleach. Report the incident immediately to your supervisor or the hospital's infection control officer. Seek medical evaluation promptly to assess the source patient's infection status and start post-exposure prophylaxis when indicated, especially for HIV and Hepatitis B.

Regulatory Guidelines and Institutional Policies

Healthcare institutions in Nigeria operate under guidelines set by health ministries and infection control committees to manage medical waste. These policies mandate the provision of safety-engineered devices, proper color-coded bins—usually yellow for infectious waste—and regular staff training. Compliance with these protocols is a professional and legal duty. Hospitals must maintain an environment where safety supplies are always available so workers can follow standard precautions without cutting corners.
