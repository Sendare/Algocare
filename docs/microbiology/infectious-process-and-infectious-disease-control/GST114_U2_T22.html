Functions of the Infection Control Unit of the Hospital

Introduction to Hospital Infection Control

Hospital infection control is a dedicated system put in place to protect patients, healthcare workers, and visitors from picking up preventable illnesses while inside a medical facility. Nosocomial infections, more commonly referred to today as healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), pose a massive challenge to patient safety across all clinical settings. These are infections that a patient catches while receiving medical care for another condition, which were not present or incubating at the time of admission.

The primary goal of the infection control unit is risk reduction. By actively tracking how germs spread and putting strict safety barriers in place, the unit lowers the chance of disease transmission. Without these measures, hospitals can quickly turn into breeding grounds for drug-resistant bacteria, putting vulnerable people at grave risk.

Structure and Composition of the Team

An effective infection control unit requires a multidisciplinary team because hospital safety involves every single department. Nurses, doctors, microbiologists, and sanitation experts all bring unique skills to the table.

  • Infection Control Nurses (ICNs): The core frontline workers who daily track patient charts, round on wards, and mentor staff on hygiene practices.
  • Hospital Epidemiologist: Usually a physician specializing in infectious diseases who interprets microbiology data and guides clinical policy.
  • Microbiology Laboratory Staff: Essential partners who identify specific pathogens and spot emerging antibiotic resistance patterns early.
  • Environmental Services Liaison: Manages cleaning schedules, waste disposal protocols, and ensures facility standards meet health codes.

Surveillance and Monitoring of Infections

Surveillance is the active, systematic collection and analysis of health data to guide infection control interventions. The unit cannot fix a problem if it does not know where and when infections are happening.

There are two main approaches used in hospitals:

  1. Passive Surveillance: Relying on busy ward staff to voluntarily report when a patient develops an infection.
  2. Active Surveillance: Infection control staff proactively reviewing laboratory reports, antibiotic prescriptions, and patient charts to catch missed cases.

Targeted surveillance often focuses on high-risk areas like intensive care units (ICUs) or surgical wards, tracking specific problems like catheter-associated urinary tract infections or surgical site infections.

Developing Policies and Protocols

Creating rules is not enough; policies must be grounded in current scientific evidence and tailored to the specific resources of the hospital. The unit drafts guidelines covering everything from hand hygiene techniques to proper device insertion procedures.

A common pitfall is writing policies that are too long and complicated for busy nurses to read. Effective infection control manuals must be concise, practical, and readily accessible at the bedside. Once written, these protocols undergo regular review to reflect changes in microbial resistance patterns or updates from global health authorities.

Staff Training and Standard Precautions

Having the best rules on paper does nothing if healthcare workers do not practice them. Staff education is an ongoing duty, starting during orientation and continuing through annual refresher courses.

Training focuses heavily on standard precautions, which treat all patient blood, body fluids, non-intact skin, and mucous membranes as potentially infectious. Key pillars include proper hand hygiene, safe injection practices, cough etiquette, and the correct use of personal protective equipment (PPE) like gloves, gowns, and masks. The unit also monitors compliance, gently correcting bad habits before they cause an outbreak.

Outbreak Investigation and Management

When the number of infections in a ward rises above the normal baseline, the unit swings into emergency mode. An outbreak investigation follows a structured path to find the source and stop transmission immediately.

First, the team verifies the diagnosis and defines a case. Next, they map out where the affected patients stayed and who cared for them to find a common link. Once the culprit is found—whether it is a contaminated medical supply, poor handwashing technique, or an unsterilized tool—targeted control measures are deployed. This may include isolating sick patients, closing a ward for deep cleaning, or changing a specific clinical practice.

Environmental Hygiene and Waste Management

Germs can survive on hospital surfaces for days or even weeks. Because of this, the infection control unit works closely with housekeeping to oversee environmental cleaning standards. This includes choosing effective disinfectants, checking that cleaning schedules are followed, and testing surfaces for lingering pathogens.

Proper waste management is equally vital. Medical waste must be sorted at the point of generation into color-coded bins or bags. Sharps like needles go into puncture-proof safety boxes to prevent accidental needle-stick injuries, while infectious waste requires safe incineration or autoclaving to protect the surrounding community.

Public Health Collaboration

A hospital does not exist in a vacuum. Diseases discovered inside the wards often originate from the wider community, and conversely, hospital-acquired pathogens can sometimes spill back out into the general population.

The infection control unit acts as a bridge between the clinical facility and local or national public health agencies. When dangerous or reportable diseases like cholera, Lassa fever, or tuberculosis are identified, the unit is legally required to notify health authorities. This collaboration helps track disease trends across the country, coordinates wider vaccination efforts, and protects public health on a national scale.
