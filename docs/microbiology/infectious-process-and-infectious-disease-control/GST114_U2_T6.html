Hand Washing

Introduction and Definition of Hand Washing in Healthcare

Hand washing in the healthcare setting is formally defined as the vigorous, brief rubbing together of all surfaces of lathered hands, followed by rinsing under a stream of running water. For nursing students in Nigeria, mastering this foundational skill is critical, as it forms the bedrock of patient safety and infection prevention and control (IPC). In clinical environments, healthcare-associated infections (HCAIs) present a significant morbidity and mortality risk to vulnerable patients. HCAIs prolong hospital stays, increase financial burdens, and can lead to permanent disability or death. Hand washing is universally acknowledged by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) as the single most effective, simplest, and most cost-effective intervention to prevent the cross-transmission of infectious pathogens between patients, healthcare workers, and the surrounding environment. Whenever hands are visibly soiled, contaminated with proteinaceous material, or after caring for patients with spore-forming organisms like Clostridioides difficile, mechanical friction combined with soap and water is mandatory. It is important for student nurses to view hand washing not merely as a routine chore, but as a deliberate clinical intervention that safeguards public health. Diligent adherence to hand hygiene protocols directly translates to improved patient outcomes, reduced antibiotic resistance rates, and a safer working environment for the entire multidisciplinary healthcare team.

Microbiological Rationale: Resident versus Transient Flora

To understand why hand hygiene is so effective, nursing students must differentiate between the two main categories of microorganisms found on human skin: resident flora and transient flora. Resident flora, also known as commensal microorganisms, consists of bacteria that permanently colonize the deeper layers of the skin, such as Staphylococcus epidermidis and diphtheroids. These organisms are generally non-pathogenic under normal circumstances and actually help protect the skin by preventing pathogenic colonization. They are difficult to remove completely through mechanical washing alone, but soap and water can significantly reduce their numbers. Conversely, transient flora microorganisms are picked up by healthcare workers through direct contact with patients or contaminated environmental surfaces. Examples include Staphylococcus aureus, gram-negative bacilli like Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and various viruses. Transient flora colonize the superficial layers of the skin, survive for a shorter duration, and are easily transmitted during patient care activities if proper hand hygiene is omitted. Because transient microbes are loosely attached to the skin surface, they are readily removed or inactivated by routine hand washing and alcohol-based hand rubs. Understanding this distinction highlights why healthcare workers must perform hand hygiene frequently: while resident flora protects the skin, transient flora acts as the primary vehicle for healthcare-associated infections.

The Chain of Infection and How Hand Hygiene Breaks It

The spread of infection follows a continuous sequence known as the chain of infection, which comprises six interconnected links: the infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, and susceptible host. In a clinical environment, the hands of the nurse frequently serve as the vehicle for the mode of transmission, specifically acting as the primary vector bridging the reservoir (an infected patient or contaminated equipment) to the portal of entry of a new, susceptible host. When a nurse fails to perform hand hygiene, transient pathogens picked up during patient care are readily transferred to the next patient or surface touched. Hand hygiene acts as a critical barrier that directly disrupts this chain of infection at the mode of transmission link. By mechanically removing or chemically destroying microorganisms on the skin, hand washing halts the journey of the pathogen, rendering it incapable of reaching a susceptible host. Recognizing this mechanism empowers nursing students to appreciate that every instance of hand hygiene is an active disruption of the infection cycle. Breaking this chain not only protects individual patients from acquiring secondary infections, but it also curtails community-wide outbreaks and the emergence of multi-drug resistant organisms within Nigerian healthcare facilities.

Types of Hand Hygiene: Social, Hygienic, and Surgical

Hand hygiene practices in nursing are categorized based on their purpose, depth of microbial removal, and the agents utilized: social, hygienic, and surgical. Social hand washing (or routine hand washing) uses plain soap and water to mechanically remove visible dirt, organic material, and transient microorganisms acquired during routine social or non-invasive clinical interactions. It typically lasts between 40 to 60 seconds. Hygienic hand washing or hand rubbing involves the use of antimicrobial soap or an alcohol-based rub to significantly reduce the number of transient flora, and is indicated before aseptic procedures and after potential body fluid exposure. Surgical hand antisepsis is the most rigorous level, performed before participating in invasive surgical procedures. It requires an antimicrobial soap or alcohol-based surgical scrub product with persistent activity to eliminate transient flora and profoundly reduce resident flora for the duration of the procedure. Surgical hand scrubbing extends from the hands up to the elbows and requires meticulous technique and timed durations, often 2 to 5 minutes, ensuring that hands are held higher than the elbows to prevent recontamination. Each type of hand hygiene serves a distinct clinical purpose, and nursing students must select the appropriate level based on the invasiveness of the care they are providing.

WHO Five Moments for Hand Hygiene

To standardize and guide clinical practice, the World Health Organization introduced the 'Five Moments for Hand Hygiene,' a conceptual framework that dictates exactly when healthcare workers must clean their hands. These moments are: (1) Before touching a patient, which protects the patient against harmful germs carried on the healthcare worker's hands; (2) Before clean/aseptic procedures, such as administering intravenous medications or inserting urinary catheters, which protects the patient from acquiring harmful pathogens, including the patient's own germs, entering their body; (3) After body fluid exposure risk, such as handling blood, urine, wound drainage, or removing gloves, which protects both the healthcare worker and the healthcare environment from colonization; (4) After touching a patient, which protects the healthcare worker and the environment from patient flora; and (5) After touching patient surroundings, including bed rails, infusion pumps, or bedside tables, which similarly protects the worker and environment from transferable pathogens. For nursing students on clinical rotation in Nigerian hospitals, committing these five moments to memory is non-negotiable. Integrating them into daily ward routines ensures that hand hygiene is proactive rather than reactive, drastically curtailing the transmission of healthcare-associated infections across diverse clinical units.

Standard Procedure for Clinical Hand Washing with Soap and Water

Performing clinical hand washing with soap and water requires a precise, step-by-step technique to ensure all skin surfaces are effectively decontaminated. The entire process should take between 40 to 60 seconds. First, wet hands thoroughly with clean running water (preferably warm, as it is more comfortable and less drying to the skin). Apply enough soap to cover all hand surfaces. Rub hands palm to palm to create a lather. Next, place the right palm over the left dorsum with interlaced fingers, and vice versa. Interlace fingers palm to palm to clean between the web spaces. Clean the backs of fingers to opposing palms with fingers interlocked. Grasp the right thumb with the left palm and rotational rub, then switch to the other thumb. Perform rotational rubbing backwards and forwards with clasped fingers of the right hand in the left palm, and vice versa. Rinse hands thoroughly under running water, ensuring all soap residue is removed, as residual soap can cause skin irritation. Finally, dry hands completely using a single-use paper towel or a clean, dry personal towel. Use the paper towel to turn off the water faucet to avoid recontaminating clean hands by touching handles that were manipulated with dirty hands. Proper technique is just as important as the duration of the wash.

Proper Technique for Alcohol-Based Hand Rubbing

Alcohol-based hand rubbing is the preferred method for routine hand antisepsis in clinical care when hands are not visibly soiled. It is faster, more effective at killing most transient microorganisms, and generally causes less skin irritation than soap and water when formulated with emollients. The procedure takes between 20 to 30 seconds. To perform alcohol-based hand rubbing correctly, apply a palmful of the product in a cupped hand, covering all surfaces. Rub hands palm to palm, then proceed with the same rotational and interlacings steps used in hand washing: right palm over left dorsum with interlaced fingers and vice versa, palm to palm with fingers interlaced, backs of fingers to opposing palms, rotational rubbing of thumbs, and rotational rubbing with clasped fingers in the opposite palm. Continue rubbing until the hands are completely dry. The friction generated helps evaporate the alcohol while ensuring complete coverage of the palms, dorsum, web spaces, and fingertips. Nursing students must remember that alcohol hand rubs are ineffective against spore-forming bacteria such as Clostridioides difficile, and if hands are visibly dirty or contaminated with organic matter, hand washing with soap and water must always be chosen instead of hand rub.

Challenges, Compliance, and Skin Care in Nursing Practice

Despite the proven efficacy of hand hygiene, compliance among healthcare workers remains a persistent global challenge, often influenced by factors such as high patient-to-nurse ratios, heavy workloads, forgetfulness, lack of accessible supplies, and skin irritation. In many resource-constrained clinical settings across Nigeria, intermittent shortages of running water, soap, and alcohol-based sanitizers further impede optimal practice. Frequent hand washing and exposure to harsh chemical agents can strip the skin of its natural lipid barrier, leading to irritant contact dermatitis, dryness, cracking, and scaling. Damaged skin not only causes physical discomfort to the nurse but also harbors higher concentrations of pathogenic microorganisms, increasing infection transmission risks. To mitigate skin damage, healthcare institutions must provide high-quality hand hygiene products containing skin emollients and moisturizers. Furthermore, nurses should apply hand cream or lotion regularly, typically at the end of shifts or during off-duty hours, to maintain skin integrity. Educational campaigns, institutional audits, and making hand hygiene supplies readily accessible at the point of care are vital strategies to foster a strong safety culture and improve compliance among nursing students and professional staff alike.
