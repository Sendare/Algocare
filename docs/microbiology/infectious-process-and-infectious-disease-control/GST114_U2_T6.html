Hand Washing

Introduction and Definition of Hand Washing in Healthcare

Hand washing is the vigorous, brief rubbing together of all surfaces of lathered hands, followed by rinsing under a stream of running water. In the clinical setting, it serves as the foundational barrier against healthcare-associated infections. Nurses perform hand hygiene constantly between patient contacts, medication preparations, and clean utility tasks. The main goal is mechanical removal of dirt, organic material, and micro-organisms acquired through direct contact with patients or contaminated environmental surfaces.

Microbiological Rationale: Resident versus Transient Flora

Skin microbiology dictates how nurses approach hand hygiene.

  • Resident flora: Micro-organisms permanently living under the skin strata, such as *Staphylococcus epidermidis*. They are generally low virulence and protect the skin from pathogenic colonization.
  • Transient flora: Contaminants picked up during patient care, such as *Staphylococcus aureus* or enteric gram-negative bacilli. They reside on outer skin layers, survive briefly, and are easily removed by proper hand hygiene.

The Chain of Infection and How Hand Hygiene Breaks It

Infections spread through a continuous chain: infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, and susceptible host. Hand hygiene specifically interrupts the mode of transmission. When a nurse touches a contaminated wound or bed rail, hands become the vehicle. Performing hand hygiene destroys or removes the pathogen before it reaches a new portal of entry on another patient.

Types of Hand Hygiene: Social, Hygienic, and Surgical

Clinical practice requires different levels of hand cleaning depending on the procedure risks.

TypePurposeAgents Used
Social Hand WashRemove visible dirt and casual contaminantsPlain soap and water
Hygienic Hand Rub/WashDestroy transient flora before clean proceduresAlcohol-based rub or antimicrobial soap
Surgical Hand ScrubEliminate transient flora and reduce resident flora long-termAntimicrobial scrub with prolonged friction

Selecting the correct tier prevents unnecessary skin damage while ensuring adequate microbial reduction.

WHO Five Moments for Hand Hygiene

  1. Before touching a patient: Protects the patient against harmful germs carried on the nurse's hands.
  2. Before clean/aseptic procedure: Protects the patient against harmful germs, including the patient's own, entering their body.
  3. After body fluid exposure risk: Protects the nurse and the healthcare environment from harmful patient germs.
  4. After touching a patient: Protects the nurse and the healthcare environment from patient germs.
  5. After touching patient surroundings: Protects the nurse and healthcare environment from germs on nearby objects.

Standard Procedure for Clinical Hand Washing with Soap and Water

Effective hand washing requires a systematic approach lasting 40 to 60 seconds. Wet hands with water, apply enough soap to cover all surfaces, and rub hands palm to palm. Interlace fingers to clean webbing. Clean the backs of fingers against opposing palms with fingers interlocked. Rotate thumbs clasped in opposite palms, and rub fingertips circularly in palms. Rinse thoroughly with water, dry completely with a single-use towel, and use the towel to turn off the tap.

Proper Technique for Alcohol-Based Hand Rubbing

Alcohol-based hand rubs are the gold standard for routine ward care when hands are not visibly soiled. Apply a palmful of product and cover all hand surfaces using the same multi-step friction movements used in hand washing: palms, backs of hands, interdigital spaces, thumbs, and fingertips. Rub continuously for 20 to 30 seconds until the hands are completely dry. Do not wipe excess gel away with a towel, as evaporation is required for antimicrobial action.

Challenges, Compliance, and Skin Care in Nursing Practice

Low hand hygiene compliance remains a major hurdle in busy clinical environments. Common barriers include high workload, skin irritation, lack of accessible sinks or dispensers, and forgetfulness. Frequent washing strips natural skin lipids, leading to contact dermatitis, dryness, and micro-cracks where pathogens thrive. Nurses must use hospital-approved hand lotions regularly, maintain short natural fingernails without artificial extensions, and avoid wrist jewelry to protect both their skin and their patients.
