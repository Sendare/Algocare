Standard Precautionary Measures

Introduction and the Chain of Infection

Standard precautions are the foundational infection control practices applied to all patient care in any healthcare setting, regardless of suspected or confirmed infection status. They protect both healthcare workers and patients from the spread of harmful microorganisms. These measures are built upon the chain of infection, which describes how a disease spreads from a source to a susceptible host. The six links in this chain are the infectious agent, reservoir, portal of exit, mode of transmission, portal of entry, and susceptible host. Standard precautions interrupt this chain primarily at the mode of transmission and portal of entry by creating physical and chemical barriers against pathogens.

Hand Hygiene Protocols and Techniques

Hand hygiene is widely recognized as the single most effective intervention for preventing healthcare-associated infections. Nurses must perform hand hygiene using either an alcohol-based hand rub or soap and water depending on specific clinical situations.

  • Alcohol-based hand rub: Preferred for routine clinical work when hands are not visibly soiled. It takes about 20 to 30 seconds and destroys most transient pathogens effectively.
  • Soap and water: Mandatory when hands are visibly dirty, visibly soiled with blood or other body fluids, after using the restroom, and after caring for patients with spore-forming organisms like *Clostridioides difficile*.
  • The Five Moments: Hand hygiene must occur before touching a patient, before clean or aseptic procedures, after body fluid exposure risk, after touching a patient, and after touching patient surroundings.

Selection and Proper Use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Personal protective equipment acts as a barrier to shield the skin, mucous membranes, and clothing from contact with infectious agents. The selection of PPE depends on the anticipated type of exposure, such as splashes, sprays, or direct touch.

  • Gloves: Wear when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin. Remove them promptly after care and perform hand hygiene.
  • Gowns: Wear to protect skin and clothing during procedures that are likely to generate splashes of blood or body fluids.
  • Masks and Eye Protection: Wear goggles, face shields, or surgical masks whenever procedural splashes to the face, eyes, nose, or mouth are anticipated.

Putting on PPE follows a specific sequence (gown, mask, goggles, gloves), and removal follows a precise order to avoid contaminating your skin or uniform (gloves, goggles, gown, mask).

Safe Injection Practices and Sharps Management

Injections and sharps handling carry a high risk of bloodborne pathogen transmission if protocols are ignored. Safe injection practices protect both nurses and patients from infections like hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV. A core rule is that needles and syringes are single-use devices; they must never be reused for another patient, even if the needle is changed. Medication vials should be treated as single-dose whenever possible to prevent multi-patient contamination. Sharps containers must be placed close to the area of use, kept within arm's reach, and never filled beyond the designated fill line. Nurses must never recap used needles using both hands, and safety devices should be activated immediately after use.

Respiratory Hygiene and Cough Etiquette

Respiratory hygiene and cough etiquette target the containment of respiratory secretions to prevent droplet-transmitted infections such as influenza, pertussis, and tuberculosis. This strategy starts at the point of initial encounter in healthcare settings, such as triage or reception desks. Patients, visitors, and staff should cover their mouth and nose with a tissue when coughing or sneezing, and promptly dispose of used tissues in the nearest waste receptacle. If tissues are unavailable, coughing or sneezing into the inner elbow rather than bare hands prevents droplet spread onto environmental surfaces. Facilities should provide readily accessible stations containing tissues, no-touch waste bins, and alcohol-based hand rub near entrances.

Environmental Cleaning and Safe Waste Handling

Healthcare environments harbor microorganisms on frequently touched surfaces like bed rails, call buttons, and IV pumps. Regular cleaning removes organic matter and dirt, while disinfection inactivates specific pathogens using chemical agents. Hospital grade disinfectants must be used according to manufacturer instructions, respecting required contact times to ensure microbial destruction. Furthermore, safe waste management is essential for public health and safety. Healthcare waste is segregated at the point of generation into color-coded bins or bags: general domestic waste goes into black bags, infectious clinical waste goes into yellow biohazard bags or bins, and sharps go into rigid puncture-resistant containers. Proper segregation protects waste handlers and prevents environmental contamination.

Management of Occupational Exposure and Needle-Stick Injuries

Despite strict precautions, accidental needle-stick injuries and mucosal exposures to blood or body fluids still occur in clinical practice. Immediate post-exposure management can significantly reduce the risk of transmission for bloodborne pathogens like HIV and hepatitis B. First, immediately wash needle-stick injuries and cuts with soap and water, or flush splashes to the eyes or mucous membranes with copious amounts of clean water or saline. Do not scrub or squeeze the wound, as this can force pathogens deeper into tissues. Report the incident immediately to the designated supervisor or occupational health service, document the details of the exposure, and promptly seek medical evaluation for post-exposure prophylaxis.
