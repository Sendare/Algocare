Disposal of Infected Materials and Specimens

Principles and Classification of Infected Waste

Infection control begins with recognizing that all biological specimens and contaminated materials carry potential pathogens. Proper waste management breaks the chain of infection, protecting healthcare workers, patients, and the community. Medical waste is broadly separated into general municipal waste and hazardous infectious waste. Infectious waste includes items soaked in blood, laboratory cultures, pathological specimens, and sharp instruments. Failing to classify waste at the point of generation contaminates safe waste streams, turning a manageable amount of refuse into a massive biological hazard.

Color-Coding and Segregation Guidelines

Standardized color-coding ensures that healthcare workers identify waste categories instantly without reading detailed labels. In Nigerian health facilities, segregation occurs strictly at the point of generation.

  • Black Bins: Non-hazardous domestic waste, food remnants, and office paper.
  • Yellow Bins: Infectious waste that requires incineration, including cotton swabs, dressings, and disposable gowns.
  • Brown Bins: Pharmaceutical and chemical waste such as expired drugs and cytotoxic agents.
  • Yellow Safety Boxes: Puncturable sharps containers for needles, blades, and lancets.

Handling and Transporting Laboratory Specimens

Laboratory specimens require strict containment to prevent leaks and environmental contamination during transit. Nurses and ward assistants must use the triple packaging system for transport.

  1. Primary Recepticle: The leak-proof primary vial or tube holding the actual specimen.
  2. Secondary Packaging: A durable, leak-proof secondary container enclosing the primary vial along with enough absorbent material to soak up the entire liquid volume if breakage occurs.
  3. Outer Packaging: A rigid transit box labeled with the international biohazard symbol and clear destination details.

Methods of Decontamination and Treatment

Decontamination reduces microbial load to a safe level before ultimate disposal or recycling. Autoclaving uses pressurized steam to destroy all forms of microbial life, serving as the gold standard for laboratory cultures and reusable surgical tools. Chemical disinfection using sodium hypochlorite solutions is common for surfaces and non-autoclavable items, though dilution ratios must be monitored closely to maintain efficacy. Incineration destroys organic matter entirely through high-temperature combustion, rendering anatomical parts and heavily soiled dressings unrecognizable.

Management of Accidental Spills and Exposures

Accidents happen despite strict protocols. When an infected specimen spills or a needle stick occurs, immediate action minimizes harm. For skin exposures, wash the site instantly with soap and clean running water without scrubbing aggressively. For floor spills, cordon off the area, put on heavy-duty gloves, cover the spill with absorbent paper towels, and apply a 0.5% chlorine solution starting from the outer edges inward. Report the incident immediately to the infection control officer to evaluate the need for post-exposure prophylaxis.
