Disposal of Infected Materials and Specimens

Principles of Infection Control and Waste Segregation

Infection control begins at the exact moment a contaminated item or biological specimen is generated. Nurses and healthcare workers manage waste to break the chain of infection, protecting patients, hospital staff, and the wider community from avoidable exposure. Proper waste handling relies on immediate segregation at the point of origin rather than sorting items later. Mixing infectious materials with regular household refuse creates a massive biological hazard and puts every handling staff member at risk. Every clinical unit must maintain designated waste receptacles equipped with appropriate liners to ensure this separation happens instantly.

Classification of Medical and Laboratory Waste

Medical waste is not uniform; it spans various categories that demand distinct handling methods. Categorization determines whether an item requires high-temperature incineration, chemical disinfection, or ordinary municipal disposal.

  • Infectious Waste: Materials contaminated with blood, body fluids, or cultures from the laboratory.
  • Sharps: Needles, scalpel blades, lancets, and broken glass capable of causing puncture wounds.
  • Pathological Waste: Human tissues, organs, body parts, and recognizable fetuses.
  • General Waste: Office paper, food wrappers, and non-contaminated packaging materials.

Color-Coding and Segregation Guidelines

Color-coded receptacles provide a universal visual language that prevents sorting errors in busy clinical environments. Staff must match the waste type to the exact color of the container or bag.

  • Yellow Bags or Bins: Reserved for infectious waste, human tissues, and highly contaminated materials destined for incineration.
  • Brown or Black Bins: Designated for non-hazardous general municipal waste, administrative paper, and food scraps.
  • Yellow Safety Boxes: Puncture-resistant containers specifically built for needles, syringes with fixed needles, and small glass ampoules.

Safe Packaging, Transport, and Storage of Laboratory Specimens

Diagnostic specimens like blood, sputum, and stool carry high pathogen loads and require rigorous containment before leaving the ward. Nurses must place primary specimen containers inside leak-proof secondary containers, such as specimen transport bags with zip seals. Request forms must never be placed inside the same compartment as the specimen bottle to prevent paper contamination if a leak occurs. Transport staff should use rigid, labeled transport boxes. If a specimen cannot be sent to the laboratory immediately, store it in a designated clinical refrigerator, never alongside food or drinks.

Methods of Treatment and Decontamination

Decontamination reduces microbial load to a safe level before ultimate disposal or recycling. Autoclaving uses pressurized steam to sterilize reusable laboratory instruments and certain types of pre-treated infectious waste. Chemical disinfection using freshly prepared sodium hypochlorite solutions is common for surface decontamination and instrument pre-soaking. Incineration remains the gold standard for pathological waste and anatomical parts, ensuring complete destruction through high-temperature burning. Every facility must ensure treatment equipment functions correctly through routine biological and chemical monitoring.

Management of Accidental Spills and Exposures

Accidents involving infected fluids or needlestick injuries require immediate, standardized action to minimize infection transmission. For a needlestick injury, encourage bleeding under running water, wash with soap, and report the incident instantly for post-exposure prophylaxis evaluation. For biological fluid spills on floors, staff must don heavy-duty gloves, apply absorbent materials, and flood the area with a chlorine-based disinfectant solution before cleaning. Documentation of every exposure incident protects the worker and helps institutional safety committees prevent future occurrences.
