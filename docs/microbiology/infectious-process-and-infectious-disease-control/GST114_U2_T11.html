Sterilization

Introduction and Definition of Terms

Sterilization is the absolute destruction or elimination of all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial endospores, fungi, viruses, and prions. In clinical nursing practice across Nigeria, achieving true sterility is the cornerstone of preventing hospital-acquired infections, particularly in surgical suites, labour wards, and sterile supply units. To master this topic, several baseline terms require precise understanding: - **Decontamination**: The removal of pathogenic microorganisms from objects to make them safe to handle. - **Sanitization**: A cleaning process that reduces the number of microorganisms to a safe public health level, commonly used in food preparation areas rather than surgical zones. - **Bactericide**: An agent that kills vegetative bacteria, though it does not necessarily destroy bacterial spores. - **Sporicidal**: Capable of killing bacterial endospores, which are the most resilient living entities known to microbiology.

Distinguishing Sterilization, Disinfection, and Antisepsis

Clinical safety relies on knowing when to use sterilization, disinfection, or antisepsis. Mixing up these three processes leads directly to surgical site infections and cross-contamination. | Process | Target Application | Level of Reduction | Common Agent | |---|---|---|---| | **Sterilization** | Critical items entering sterile tissue | 100% elimination of all microbes | Autoclaving, ethylene oxide | | **Disinfection** | Semi-critical items touching mucous membranes | High reduction, spores may survive | Chemical glutaraldehyde, boiling | | **Antisepsis** | Living human skin or mucous membranes | Reduction of transient flora | Chlorhexidine, povidone-iodine | Critical instruments like surgical scalpels and hypodermic needles demand absolute sterilization. Semi-critical items like endoscopy tubes require high-level disinfection. Skin preparation before an injection calls for antisepsis. Applying a skin antiseptic to a surgical blade will not make it sterile.

Physical Methods of Sterilization

Physical methods remain the most reliable, cost-effective, and widely used techniques in Nigerian healthcare institutions. 1. **Steam Under Pressure (Autoclaving)**: The gold standard for heat-stable items. Uses saturated steam at 121°C for 15 minutes at 15 pounds per square inch of pressure, or 134°C for 3 minutes. The moisture coagulates cellular proteins rapidly. 2. **Dry Heat Sterilization**: Relies on ovens utilizing conduction. Requires higher temperatures (e.g., 160°C for two hours or 170°C for one hour) because dry air is a poor thermal conductor. Ideal for anhydrous powders, oils, and sharp instruments that might rust in wet steam. 3. **Incineration**: Complete combustion of infectious waste, anatomical parts, and disposable sharps. This reduces waste to sterile ash. 4. **Boiling Water**: An ancient method that achieves disinfection rather than true sterilization, as bacterial endospores and certain viruses survive prolonged boiling.

Chemical Methods of Sterilization

When instruments are heat-sensitive—such as delicate fibre-optic scopes, plastics, and certain electrical equipment—chemical sterilants are deployed. - **Ethylene Oxide (EtO)**: A colourless, highly penetrative gas used for heat-sensitive equipment. It alkylates cellular proteins and DNA. A major drawback is its toxicity, requiring strict aeration periods before equipment can touch patients. - **Glutaraldehyde (2%)**: Often used for immersion of endoscopes. While powerful, it requires prolonged contact time (up to 10 hours) to achieve true sporicidal action, earning it the label of a liquid chemical sterilant. - **Hydrogen Peroxide Gas Plasma**: Utilises vaporized hydrogen peroxide driven into a plasma state with radiofrequency waves. It operates at low temperatures and leaves no toxic residue, making it safe for delicate electronics. - **Peracetic Acid**: A rapid-acting oxidant that breaks down into safe byproducts (acetic acid and oxygen), widely used in automated endoscope reprocessors.

Radiation and Filtration Sterilization Techniques

Industrial and specialized clinical settings rely heavily on radiation and mechanical filtration when heat and harsh chemicals fail. Gamma radiation, sourced from cobalt-60, and electron-beam radiation destroy microbial DNA through ionization. This method is standard for single-use disposable medical supplies manufactured off-site, such as syringes, suture packs, and intravenous infusion sets. It allows manufacturers to package items before sterilization. Filtration, conversely, does not kill microorganisms; rather, it physically traps them. Membrane filters with pore sizes of 0.22 micrometres are fitted to syringe nozzles or intravenous lines to strain bacteria and fungi out of thermolabile pharmaceutical liquids and intravenous infusions.

Validation and Monitoring of Sterilization Processes

Running a sterilizer does not guarantee sterility. Rigorous monitoring ensures equipment integrity and patient safety. - **Mechanical Monitoring**: Assessing cycle parameters like temperature, pressure gauges, and time readouts displayed on the machine panel during the run. - **Chemical Indicators**: Tape, strips, or special inks that change colour when exposed to specific temperatures or chemical concentrations. They confirm that an item has been processed through a cycle, though they do not guarantee complete sterility. - **Biological Indicators**: The ultimate validation test. They use highly resistant bacterial endospores—such as *Geobacillus stearothermophilus* for steam and *Bacillus atrophaeus* for dry heat and ethylene oxide. If the spores fail to grow after incubation, the sterilization process is officially validated.

Infection Control and Nursing Implications in Clinical Practice

Nurses act as the final line of defense in maintaining sterile integrity. A sterilized instrument pack is rendered useless if handled with contaminated hands or stored in a damp environment. When opening sterile packs, the nurse must inspect external chemical indicators, check for wrapper tears, and examine the pack for moisture strike-through. Wet packs are considered unsterile because water acts as a wick, drawing microorganisms from the surrounding air through the wrapper. Proper storage in clean, dry, and dust-controlled environments protects items until use. In resource-constrained Nigerian clinical settings, nurses must frequently advocate for equipment maintenance and challenge practices where expired sterile packs or compromised instrument trays are brought near a sterile field.
