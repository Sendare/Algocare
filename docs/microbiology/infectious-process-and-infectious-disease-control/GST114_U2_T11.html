Sterilization

Introduction and Principles of Sterilization

Sterilization is the complete destruction or removal of all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial spores. In clinical settings, achieving sterility is non-negotiable for instruments that break the skin, enter sterile body cavities, or touch mucous membranes. A common misconception is that washing an item thoroughly makes it sterile; cleaning only reduces dirt and surface microbes, while sterilization goes a step further to eliminate every single living microorganism. Without proper sterilization, hospitals become breeding grounds for healthcare-associated infections, putting vulnerable patients at severe risk.

Comparing Sterilization, Disinfection, and Antisepsis

Understanding infection control requires knowing how these three terms differ:

  • Sterilization: Destroys all microbial life, including spores. Used on critical items like surgical scalpels and implants.
  • Disinfection: Eliminates most pathogenic microorganisms on inanimate objects, but usually does not kill high numbers of bacterial spores. Used on non-critical surfaces like stethoscope diaphragms or bedside tables.
  • Antisepsis: Uses chemical agents on living tissue, such as skin, to inhibit or destroy microorganisms. Used when cleaning a patient's arm before drawing blood or inserting an intravenous line.

Physical Methods of Sterilization

Physical methods rely on heat, pressure, or steam to destroy microbes.

  • Autoclaving: Uses moist heat under pressure (typically 121 degrees Celsius at 15 pounds of pressure for 15 to 30 minutes). The moisture helps coagulate cellular proteins quickly, making it the gold standard for surgical metals and linens.
  • Dry Heat: Uses ovens operating at higher temperatures (such as 160 to 170 degrees Celsius) for longer periods. It is ideal for glassware, powders, and anhydrous oils that would be ruined by moisture.
  • Incineration: Burns contaminated items to ashes, primarily used for pathological waste and disposable sharps.

Chemical Sterilization Techniques

When instruments cannot withstand the high temperatures of an autoclave, chemical sterilants offer a viable alternative. Ethylene oxide gas is widely used for heat-sensitive and moisture-sensitive medical devices, though items require lengthy aeration times afterward to clear toxic gas residues. Hydrogen peroxide gas plasma is another low-temperature method popular for delicate scopes and electronics. Liquid chemical sterilants, such as activated glutaraldehyde, are also utilized, but they require strict immersion times and careful rinsing with sterile water to prevent chemical burns on patients.

Radiation and Filtration Sterilization

Radiation sterilization employs ionizing energy, such as gamma rays or electron beams, to break down microbial DNA. This method is predominantly used by industrial manufacturers to sterilize single-use disposable medical supplies like syringes, sutures, and catheters. Filtration, on the other hand, does not kill microbes; instead, it physically traps them using membrane filters with microscopic pore sizes (typically 0.22 micrometers). Nurses encounter filtration when preparing heat-sensitive intravenous medications or liquid solutions that would break down if exposed to heat or harsh chemicals.

Validation and Monitoring of Sterilization Processes

Ensuring that a sterilization cycle actually worked requires three distinct types of monitoring:

  1. Mechanical Monitors: Gauges, timers, and printouts on the sterilizer machine itself that show pressure, temperature, and exposure time during the run.
  2. Chemical Indicators: Tape, strips, or special ink on packaging that change color when exposed to specific temperatures or chemical conditions, proving the item went through the machine.
  3. Biological Indicators: Vials containing highly resistant bacterial spores (such as Geobacillus stearothermophilus). If these spores are killed during the cycle, it confirms the sterilization process was truly successful.

Nursing Implications and Infection Control

Nurses act as the final line of defense in protecting patients from contaminated equipment. Before using any sterile pack, nurses must inspect the wrapper for tears, moisture, or compromised seals, because a wet pack is considered contaminated due to wicking. Staff must also verify expiration dates and check that chemical indicator strips have changed color correctly. Any suspicious pack must be rejected immediately. Diligent adherence to these checks prevents healthcare-associated infections and promotes patient safety.
