Application of Asepsis to Sterile Procedures and Operating Theatres

Medical Asepsis Versus Surgical Asepsis

In nursing practice, controlling microorganisms is split into two distinct levels. Medical asepsis, often called clean technique, aims to reduce the total number of pathogens and stop them from spreading. Common examples include handwashing before feeding a patient, wearing clean gloves to empty a bedpan, and wiping down bedside tables with disinfectant.

Surgical asepsis, or sterile technique, is much stricter. It eliminates every single microorganism, including spores, from an object or area. This level of protection is mandatory whenever the skin barrier is broken, a body cavity is entered, or a sterile device is placed into a vascular system. A single lapse in surgical asepsis introduces bacteria directly into vulnerable tissues, leading to severe surgical site infections.

Operating Theatre Design and Environmental Controls

The operating theatre is deliberately constructed to block outside contaminants. The layout follows a zone system:

  • Unrestricted Zone: Street clothes are permitted here. It serves as the transition area for staff and patients entering the surgical suite.
  • Semi-restricted Zone: Staff must wear authorized theatre attire, including scrub suits and hair coverings. This area holds clean storage rooms, corridors, and processing spaces.
  • Restricted Zone: Masks and eye protection are compulsory here alongside scrub suits. This zone includes the actual operating rooms and sterile core where open supplies are kept.

Air pressure plays a vital role inside the operating room. Rooms are kept under positive pressure, meaning air pushes outward into hallways when doors open. This prevents contaminated air from rushing inside when staff enter or leave.

Core Rules of Sterile Technique

Maintaining sterility relies on unyielding adherence to a few non-negotiable rules. If a rule is broken, the item or area is instantly considered contaminated.

  • Sterile to Sterile: Only sterile items can touch other sterile items. Touching a sterile surface with a non-sterile object breaks the barrier instantly.
  • Awareness of Boundaries: Sterile drapes and tables are only considered sterile at table level. Anything hanging below the edge is unsterile because it falls outside the field of vision.
  • Movement Constraints: Scrubbed personnel must stay within the sterile area, facing each other, and avoiding turning their backs on the sterile field. Unscrubbed staff must never walk between two sterile areas.
  • Doubt Resolution: Whenever a nurse doubts whether an item is truly sterile, it must be treated as contaminated and discarded immediately.

Surgical Scrubbing, Gowning, and Gloving

Preparing the surgical team for an operation requires meticulous removal of transient flora and long-term reduction of resident skin bacteria. The surgical hand scrub uses an antiseptic agent and runs from fingertips up to two inches above the elbows. Hands must be kept higher than the elbows at all times during the wash so water drains downward away from the clean hands.

Once scrubbed, personnel enter the room and rely on the circulating nurse to assist with gowning and closed gloving. In closed gloving, hands never emerge from the gown sleeves until the gloves are pulled over the cuffs. This keeps bare skin from ever touching the outside of the sterile glove.

Establishing and Maintaining the Sterile Field

The sterile field acts as a safe working zone during surgery. It is created just before the procedure using sterilized drapes placed over tables and patient skin. Once set up, vigilance is required to keep it intact.

Only the top surface of a draped table is considered sterile. Drapes that slide off the edge or touch the floor are immediately contaminated. Spills on the sterile field present a major hazard because liquids soak through drapes via strike-through contamination, drawing bacteria from non-sterile tables below up into the sterile zone. Wet drapes must be covered with an impermeable sterile barrier immediately.

Roles of the Scrub Nurse and Circulating Nurse

Teamwork between the scrub nurse and circulating nurse keeps the operating theatre safe. Their duties are divided into sterile and non-sterile roles to ensure continuous monitoring.

  • Scrub Nurse: Works within the sterile field, hand-delivers instruments to the surgeon, monitors the exact count of sponges and needles, and guards against breaks in sterile technique.
  • Circulating Nurse: Remains outside the sterile field, manages patient positioning, records intraoperative events, opens sterile supplies for the scrub nurse, and watches the entire room for safety lapses.

Both nurses share equal responsibility for speaking up immediately if they witness a contamination event, regardless of who caused it.

Post-Procedure Decontamination and Waste Management

Infection control does not end when the incision is closed. Proper post-procedure management protects cleaning staff, waste handlers, and future patients.

Instruments are wiped free of gross blood and tissue in the operating room before being sent to central processing. Sharp items like scalpel blades and needles go directly into puncture-resistant sharps containers at the point of use to prevent needle stick injuries. Contaminated dressings and biological waste go into biohazard bags marked with universal warning symbols. Proper segregation ensures that municipal trash is not contaminated with infectious hospital waste.
