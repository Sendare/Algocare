Definition of Microbiological Terms

Introduction to Microbiology and Core Concepts

Microbiology is the study of microorganisms, which are tiny living organisms usually too small to be seen with the naked eye. In clinical practice, understanding these microscopic entities is essential for managing infectious diseases.

  • Microorganism: Any living organism of microscopic size, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa.
  • Pathogen: A microorganism capable of causing disease in a host.
  • Commensal: Microorganisms that live peacefully on or inside the human body without causing harm, often providing some benefit.
  • Opportunistic pathogen: An organism that does not normally cause disease in a healthy person but can trigger an infection when the immune system is weakened.

Classification and Cellular Characteristics

Microorganisms are broadly divided based on their cellular structure. Prokaryotes lack a true membrane-bound nucleus and include bacteria. Eukaryotes possess a defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, grouping together fungi, protozoa, and algae. Viruses stand apart because they are acellular entities that require a living host cell to replicate.

Gram-staining is a primary laboratory technique used to classify bacteria based on their cell wall composition. Gram-positive bacteria retain the crystal violet stain, appearing purple under a microscope due to a thick peptidoglycan layer. Gram-negative bacteria lose the crystal violet, take up the counterstain, and appear pink because of a thin peptidoglycan layer surrounded by an outer membrane.

Bacteriology and Virology Terminology

Bacteriology and virology use specific terms to describe structural features and behaviors. Bacteria exhibit characteristic shapes such as spheres (cocci) and rods (bacilli). They often reproduce through binary fission, a process where a single cell splits into two identical daughter cells.

Viral terminology focuses heavily on the particle structure. A virion is a complete, infectious viral particle outside a host cell, consisting of genetic material enclosed in a protein coat known as a capsid. Some viruses possess an outer lipid membrane called an envelope, which makes them generally easier to destroy with common disinfectants than naked viruses.

Mycology and Parasitology Terms

Mycology is the study of fungi, which include yeasts and molds. Fungi reproduce using microscopic reproductive units called spores. Yeasts are typically single-celled fungi that reproduce by budding, whereas molds grow as multicellular thread-like structures known as hyphae.

Parasitology deals with organisms that live on or inside a host organism at the host's expense. A parasite may be a single-celled protozoan or a multicellular helminth (worm). The organism that harbors the adult sexual stage of the parasite is called the definitive host, while the host that harbors the larval or asexual stage is the intermediate host.

Infection Control and Sterilization Terms

Preventing the spread of microorganisms in healthcare settings relies on strict decontamination practices.

  • Sterilization: The complete destruction or removal of all forms of microbial life, including highly resistant bacterial spores.
  • Disinfection: The elimination of many or all pathogenic microorganisms on inanimate objects, excluding bacterial spores.
  • Antisepsis: The application of chemical agents to living tissue, such as skin, to inhibit or destroy microorganisms.
  • Asepsis: Practices used to prevent the entry of infectious agents into sterile tissues, thereby preventing infection.

Clinical and Diagnostic Microbiology Terms

Diagnostic laboratories rely on specific terms to identify causative agents and guide treatment. Culture refers to growing microorganisms in a nutrient medium from a clinical specimen like blood, urine, or wound swabs. Sensitivity testing determines which antimicrobial drugs will successfully inhibit or kill the isolated pathogen.

Microbial presence without tissue invasion is called colonization. When that colonization begins to damage host tissues and trigger clinical signs, it becomes an infection. Serology tests detect specific antibodies or antigens in a patient's blood serum to confirm immune responses against infectious agents.
