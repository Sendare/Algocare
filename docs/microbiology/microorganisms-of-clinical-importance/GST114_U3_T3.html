Microbiology of Water

Introduction to Water Microbiology

Water microbiology examines the microscopic life inhabiting aquatic ecosystems and its profound impact on human health. While pure water is chemically simple, natural water teems with diverse microorganisms including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and protozoa. Understanding this invisible world helps healthcare professionals trace the origins of community outbreaks and design effective infection control strategies.

Microbial Flora of Natural Water Bodies

Aquatic environments support complex microbial communities adapted to specific physical and chemical conditions. Natural waters are categorized into autochthonous populations, which are native to the aquatic environment, and allochthonous populations, which originate from external sources like soil or sewage. - **Autochthonous Flora**: Indigenous microorganisms such as Pseudomonas, Flavobacterium, and various cyanobacteria that naturally metabolize dissolved minerals and organic matter. - **Allochthonous Flora**: Transient microorganisms washed in from terrestrial runoff, agricultural lands, or human settlements, often including pathogenic species.

Sources and Types of Water Pollution

Water pollution occurs when foreign substances contaminate aquatic bodies, degrading quality and threatening biological life. Point source pollution originates from discrete, identifiable locations like industrial pipe discharges or municipal sewage treatment plants. Conversely, non-point source pollution arises from broad, diffuse areas, including agricultural fertilizer runoff and urban street wash. Organic pollutants introduce high biological oxygen demand, rapidly depleting dissolved oxygen and suffocating aquatic fauna. Chemical contaminants, such as heavy metals and pesticides, persist in the environment and bioaccumulate through the food chain, creating long-term toxic hazards for human consumers.

Water-borne Diseases

Water-borne infections represent a major global health burden, particularly where sanitation infrastructure is inadequate. These illnesses spread primarily through the fecal-oral route when drinking water is contaminated with human or animal excreta. - **Cholera**: Caused by Vibrio cholerae, presenting with profuse watery diarrhea and rapid dehydration. - **Typhoid Fever**: Caused by Salmonella typhi, characterized by sustained high fever, systemic illness, and abdominal pain. - **Amebic Dysentery**: Caused by Entamoeba histolytica, marked by severe bloody diarrhea and cramping. - **Hepatitis A**: A viral infection transmitted via contaminated water that attacks hepatic tissue.

Indicator Organisms and Water Quality Testing

Testing water for every known pathogen is impractical and expensive. Instead, microbiologists rely on indicator organisms—specifically coliform bacteria like Escherichia coli—to signal fecal contamination. The presence of coliforms implies that disease-causing enteric pathogens may also be present. Standard testing methods include the **multiple-tube fermentation test** to estimate the most probable number of coliforms, and **membrane filtration techniques** that capture bacteria on a porous filter for colony counting on selective media.

Water Treatment and Purification Methods

Transforming raw, contaminated water into safe drinking supply requires a multi-step purification process. 1. **Coagulation and Flocculation**: Adding chemicals like alum neutralizes suspended particle charges, causing them to clump together into larger flocs. 2. **Sedimentation**: Heavy flocs settle to the bottom of the basin under gravity. 3. **Filtration**: Water passes through beds of sand, gravel, and anthracite to remove remaining microscopic particulates. 4. **Disinfection**: Chlorine, ozone, or ultraviolet light is applied to eradicate surviving pathogenic bacteria and viruses before distribution.

Public Health Nursing Implications in Nigeria

In many Nigerian communities, limited access to piped treated water forces reliance on boreholes, streams, and shallow wells vulnerable to contamination. Public health nurses play a frontline role in mitigating water-borne disease outbreaks through community health education, promoting household water purification techniques like boiling and chemical disinfection, and collaborating with local authorities to monitor water sanitation standards.
