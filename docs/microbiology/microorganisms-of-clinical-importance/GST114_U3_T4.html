Microbiology of Milk

Introduction to Milk Microbiology

Freshly expressed milk is far from sterile. While it contains natural antimicrobial factors like **lactoferrin** and **lysozyme** that momentarily suppress microbial proliferation, milk remains an exceptionally rich culture medium. It possesses a near-neutral pH, high moisture content, and an abundance of essential nutrients including proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates. These characteristics render it an ideal ecological niche for a vast array of microorganisms, ranging from harmless environmental saprophytes to dangerous human pathogens. Understanding how these microbes enter and multiply within milk is foundational for preventing widespread foodborne outbreaks in public health settings.

Sources of Contamination in Milk

Microbes find their way into milk through multiple distinct routes during production and handling: - **Internal udder infection**: Pathogens such as *Streptococcus agalactiae* shed directly from systemic or localized bovine mastitis. - **External surface of the udder**: Soil particles, dried manure, and bedding material transfer dust-borne spores into the milking pail. - **Milking equipment**: Contaminated stainless steel surfaces, poorly sanitized pipeline clusters, and stagnant rinse water harbor biofilms. - **Human handlers**: Poor personal hygiene introduces transient skin and respiratory flora, notably *Staphylococcus aureus*.

Pathogenic Microorganisms in Milk

Raw milk frequently serves as a vehicle for serious zoonotic and human pathogens. Bacteria such as **Mycobacterium bovis**, **Brucella abortus**, and **Coxiella burnetii** pass directly from diseased animals into the milk supply. Other organisms, including **Salmonella enterica**, **Campylobacter jejuni**, and **Escherichia coli** strains, contaminate milk via faecal-oral routes during unhygienic milking or processing. These pathogens often multiply rapidly without altering the taste, appearance, or smell of the milk, presenting a hidden hazard to unsuspecting consumers.

Milk-borne Diseases and Public Health

Consuming unpasteurized or improperly handled milk exposes populations to a spectrum of severe systemic illnesses. Tuberculosis caused by *Mycobacterium bovis* often manifests as extrapulmonary manifestations, while brucellosis triggers chronic undulating fevers, joint pain, and profound fatigue. Enteric infections from *Salmonella* and *Campylobacter* result in acute gastroenteritis, dehydration, and occasional post-infectious sequelae like reactive arthritis. In resource-constrained settings where raw milk consumption remains culturally entrenched, these preventable infections burden healthcare systems considerably.

Principles of Milk Pasteurization and Sterilization

Thermal processing remains the cornerstone of milk safety. **Pasteurization** uses controlled heat to destroy all vegetative pathogens—most notably *Coxiella burnetii*, the most heat-resistant non-spore-forming pathogen found in milk—without significantly altering nutritional value or flavor. Common methods include **Low-Temperature Long-Time (LTLT)** at 63 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes, and **High-Temperature Short-Time (HTST)** at 72 degrees Celsius for 15 seconds. Conversely, **Ultra-High Temperature (UHT)** processing employs temperatures exceeding 135 degrees Celsius for a few seconds to achieve commercial sterility, destroying both pathogens and spoilage organisms to allow shelf-stable storage without refrigeration.

Quality Control and Microbiological Testing

Rigorous laboratory testing ensures that processed milk meets strict microbiological safety standards. The **standard plate count (SPC)** estimates viable aerobic mesophilic bacteria to indicate general hygiene and sanitation levels. **Coliform counts** act as reliable indicators of post-pasteurization fecal contamination or inadequate equipment sanitization. Additionally, the **phosphatase test** serves as a vital regulatory check; because the enzyme alkaline phosphatase is naturally present in raw milk and is inactivated by temperatures slightly higher than those needed to destroy pathogens, its absence confirms that pasteurization was successfully completed.
