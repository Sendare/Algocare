Sources of Health Care Financing in Nigeria

Introduction and Public Sector Health Financing

Health care financing determines how funds are raised, pooled, and allocated to provide health services within a population. In Nigeria, public sector financing relies primarily on budgetary allocations made by the federal, state, and local government tiers. These funds originate from national revenue, mostly derived from oil proceeds, taxation, and custom duties. Despite policy commitments such as the Abuja Declaration—which recommends allocating at least fifteen percent of the national budget to health—actual budgetary provisions have historically hovered around four to seven percent. Consequently, public facilities frequently experience shortages of essential drugs, inadequate diagnostic equipment, and poor infrastructure, forcing patients to seek alternative funding mechanisms.

Out-of-Pocket Expenditure and Catastrophic Health Costs

Out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) refers to direct payments made by individuals at the point of service where no third-party payer is involved. In Nigeria, OOPE constitutes the largest share of total health expenditures, often exceeding seventy percent. This heavy reliance on user fees creates severe financial barriers for vulnerable populations. When an acute illness or chronic condition strikes, households must liquidate assets, borrow money, or forgo care entirely. This dynamic frequently plunges families into catastrophic health spending and deeper poverty, particularly in rural communities where formal safety nets are absent.

Health Insurance Schemes: NHIS and State Initiatives

To reduce dependence on direct cash payments, the Nigerian government established formal risk-pooling mechanisms. The National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), recently transitioned into the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA), was designed to provide social health insurance for formal sector employees, with provisions for the informal sector. Additionally, state governments have enacted mandatory or contributory state health insurance agency (SHIA) laws to capture informal workers and vulnerable groups. These schemes pool funds so that healthy individuals subsidize the sick, spreading financial risk across a broader demographic.

Donor Funding and International Development Assistance

International development partners, bilateral agencies, and global philanthropic foundations contribute substantially to Nigeria's health sector. Key areas supported by donor funds include immunization programs, the management of infectious diseases like HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria, as well as maternal and child health interventions. While these funds fill critical resource gaps, they introduce sustainability vulnerabilities. Programs heavily reliant on external aid often struggle when donor priorities shift or when transition timelines require domestic ownership and counterpart funding that local budgets fail to provide.

Private Sector and Community-Based Financing Models

Private sector participation encompasses for-profit clinics, pharmaceutical enterprises, and corporate social responsibility (CSR) initiatives that fund health infrastructure and outreach. Simultaneously, community-based health insurance (CBHI) and prepaid micro-health schemes operate at the grassroots level. Managed through cooperatives, faith-based organizations, or village associations, CBHI allows rural and informal workers to pool small, manageable financial contributions for basic healthcare packages. These grassroots models foster community ownership and bridge the gap where formal government and commercial insurance fail to reach.
