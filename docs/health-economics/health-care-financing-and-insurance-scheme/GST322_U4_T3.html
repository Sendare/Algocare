Sources of Health Care Financing in Nigeria

Overview of Health Care Financing in Nigeria

Health care financing refers to how money is raised, pooled, and allocated to pay for health services in a population. In Nigeria, the health system is funded through a mix of public budgets, private payments, health insurance, and foreign aid. Unfortunately, the total money available for health has historically fallen short of international targets, leading to poorly equipped facilities and staff shortages.

A major issue in the Nigerian health sector is fragmentation. Funds come from multiple tiers of government—federal, state, and local—as well as private pockets and international partners, often without proper coordination. This makes it difficult to track spending or ensure that resources reach the primary health care centers where they are needed most. Sustainable financing is essential to achieve universal health coverage and protect families from poverty when illness strikes.

Government and Public Sector Financing

Public sector financing comes from the revenues generated by federal, state, and local governments through taxes, tariffs, and royalties. Governments allocate a portion of these revenues to the health sector through annual budgets. These funds are used to run public hospitals, pay health worker salaries, and procure basic medical supplies for public facilities.

In Nigeria, public health spending has traditionally lagged behind the Abuja Declaration target, which recommended that African governments allocate at least 15 percent of their annual national budget to health. Because actual budgetary allocations often hover below this threshold, public hospitals frequently suffer from infrastructural decay, power outages, and stockouts of essential medicines. Consequently, public facilities rely heavily on user fees to keep operations running.

Out-of-Pocket Expenditure and Its Implications

  • Direct Payment: Patients pay cash directly to healthcare providers for consultations, tests, and medications at the point of service.
  • Regressive Nature: It places the heaviest financial burden on the poor, who spend a much larger share of their income on sickness than the wealthy.
  • Impoverishment: High out-of-pocket costs push vulnerable families into catastrophic health spending, forcing them to sell assets or borrow money.
  • Delayed Care: Many Nigerians delay seeking medical attention until illnesses become severe because they cannot afford immediate cash payments.

Out-of-pocket spending remains the dominant mode of health care financing in Nigeria. This reliance on direct cash payments creates a barrier to accessing timely care, worsening maternal and child mortality rates across the country.

Health Insurance Schemes: NHIS and State Health Insurance

Health insurance is designed to pool financial risks so that the healthy subsidize the sick and individuals do not face ruinous costs during illness. In Nigeria, the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), now regulated by the National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA), was established to secure prepaid health care coverage for citizens.

While the formal sector—such as federal civil servants—has experienced uptake, coverage for the informal sector, which makes up the majority of the Nigerian workforce, remains low. To bridge this gap, many state governments have established their own State Health Insurance Agencies (SHIAs) to provide affordable, decentralized plans tailored to informal workers and rural dwellers. Mandatory health insurance laws are gradually being adopted to increase population coverage.

Donor Funding and International Development Assistance

International development assistance plays a visible role in Nigeria's health financing, particularly in combating infectious diseases like HIV/AIDS, tuberculosis, and malaria, as well as supporting routine immunization and maternal health.

Major partners include global agencies, foreign governments, and philanthropic foundations. While these funds provide life-saving commodities and infrastructure support, heavy reliance on donor funding creates vulnerability. External aid can be unpredictable, subject to foreign policy shifts, and often targets specific vertical programs rather than strengthening the broader, foundational health system. Transitioning toward domestic resource mobilization is necessary for long-term health sovereignty.

Private Sector and Corporate Social Responsibility in Health

The private sector encompasses private hospitals, clinics, pharmacies, laboratories, and pharmaceutical manufacturers. Many Nigerians bypass public facilities to seek care in the private sector, driven by perceptions of better service quality and shorter waiting times, even though they pay entirely out of pocket.

Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) also contributes to health financing. Corporations, banks, and telecommunications companies frequently fund health-related projects as part of their community investments. These initiatives include building primary health care centers, donating medical equipment, sponsoring free medical outreaches, and supporting health education campaigns. Integrating the private sector effectively remains vital for expanding healthcare access.

Community-Based Health Financing and Prepaid Schemes

Community-Based Health Insurance (CBHI) schemes are nonprofit, prepaid financing mechanisms organized and managed by local communities. Members make regular small financial contributions into a communal pool, which is then used to pay for basic health services at designated local facilities.

These schemes are particularly useful for rural communities and informal workers who lack formal employment benefits. CBHIs foster strong community ownership and trust, making healthcare more accessible. However, they can struggle with small risk pools, limited financial reserves, and sustainability challenges if community members lose confidence or face severe economic hardship.

Challenges and Sustainable Financing Solutions

Nigeria's health financing landscape faces systemic hurdles, including endemic corruption, weak accountability frameworks, inflation, and poor infrastructure. These factors waste scarce resources and deter investment.

Achieving sustainable financing requires deliberate structural reforms. Governments must increase public budgetary allocations to meet international benchmarks, expand mandatory health insurance coverage to the informal sector, and strengthen financial management systems to eliminate leakages. Nurses and other health professionals play a crucial role in advocating for better resource allocation and efficient service delivery at all levels of care.
