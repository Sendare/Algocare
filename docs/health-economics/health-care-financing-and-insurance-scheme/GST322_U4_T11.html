Classification of Health Care Providers

Introduction and Primary Level of Care

Health care providers are the individuals, institutions, and organizations licensed or structured to deliver health services. Understanding how these providers are classified helps nurses navigate the health system and guide patients to the appropriate point of entry. The healthcare delivery system is traditionally tiered into levels based on complexity and scope.

The primary level of care is the first point of contact individuals have with the health system. It focuses on preventive services, health promotion, and treatment of common, uncomplicated illnesses.

  • General Practitioners (GPs): Medical doctors who handle everyday health issues, routine check-ups, and minor infections.
  • Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs): Vital members of the primary health team in Nigeria who provide basic care, immunizations, and health education at the grassroots level.
  • Primary Health Centres (PHCs): Facilities located close to residential communities designed to manage maternal and child health, routine immunizations, and basic endemic disease control.

Secondary and Tertiary Levels of Care

When health needs exceed the capacity of primary facilities, patients are referred upward to secondary and tertiary levels.

The secondary level of care involves more specialized services provided by general hospitals, district hospitals, and regional medical centers. Patients here are usually seen after a referral from a primary care provider.

  • General Practitioners and Specialists: Doctors like pediatricians, general surgeons, and obstetricians manage cases requiring admission, routine surgery, and diagnostic imaging.
  • General Hospitals: Facilities equipped with basic surgical theaters, laboratories, and inpatient wards.

The tertiary level of care represents the highest tier of specialized medical attention. It is centered around advanced technology, complex surgical procedures, and sub-specialists.

  • Teaching Hospitals and Federal Medical Centres: Large institutions equipped for neurosurgery, oncology treatments, advanced intensive care, and specialized medical research.

Public versus Private Health Care Providers

Health care providers are also categorized by ownership and funding sources, primarily dividing into public and private sectors.

FeaturePublic Health ProvidersPrivate Health Providers
OwnershipGovernment (Federal, State, or Local)Individuals, corporations, or faith-based groups
FundingTax revenue, government allocations, and public subsidiesOut-of-pocket payments, private health insurance, and fees
Primary GoalPublic health equity, population coverage, and accessible careService delivery, financial sustainability, and operational growth

Public providers strive to ensure healthcare reaches underserved rural populations, though they often face challenges with funding and resource shortages. Private providers range from small proprietary clinics to large specialist hospitals, often praised for efficiency and modern equipment but sometimes criticized for cost barriers.

For-Profit versus Not-for-Profit Organizations

Within the private sector, organizations are further subdivided based on their financial motives.

For-profit providers operate as commercial business enterprises. Their revenues are expected to cover operating costs, pay staff, and generate a financial return or profit for the owners or shareholders. Examples include proprietary clinics, private laboratories, and commercial pharmacies.

Not-for-profit providers do not distribute surplus revenue to owners. Any financial surplus generated is reinvested directly into improving facility infrastructure, buying better equipment, or subsidizing care for poor patients. This category includes mission hospitals, faith-based health clinics, and certain non-governmental organizations (NGOs). Nurses working in faith-based hospitals often observe a strong emphasis on compassionate, community-centered care alongside clinical duties.

Formal and Informal Health Care Providers in Nigeria

In many developing nations like Nigeria, healthcare delivery features a distinct mix of formal and informal systems operating side by side.

Formal health providers consist of government-certified professionals and licensed institutions operating under regulatory oversight. This includes doctors, nurses, pharmacists, and licensed medical laboratories within primary, secondary, and tertiary facilities.

Informal health providers operate outside official government regulation and institutional structures. They remain culturally significant and widely utilized, especially in rural areas.

  • Traditional Birth Attendants (TBAs): Community women who assist with pregnancy and childbirth using indigenous practices.
  • Traditional Bone Setters (TBS): Practitioners who treat fractures and musculoskeletal injuries using herbal concoctions and wooden splints.
  • Herbalists and Patent Medicine Vendors (PMVs): Local drug sellers and practitioners of herbal medicine.

*Clinical Observation*: Nurses must maintain a respectful, non-judgmental attitude toward patients who consult informal providers, as many patients visit them before or alongside orthodox hospital care.

Implications for Health Economics and Nursing Practice

Provider classification directly impacts health economics, resource allocation, and daily nursing practice. When health systems are fragmented across different provider types, inequalities in access and out-of-pocket spending often arise.

For nurses, understanding these categories influences several professional responsibilities:

  • Referral Coordination: Nurses must know local facility tiers to guide patients smoothly from primary health centers to secondary or tertiary hospitals.
  • Resource Awareness: Recognizing whether a facility is public, private, or faith-based helps nurses anticipate available resources, staffing levels, and patient financial constraints.
  • Patient Advocacy: Nurses advocate for vulnerable populations who rely on underfunded public facilities or struggle to afford private care.
  • Interprofessional Collaboration: Working effectively with diverse providers improves continuity of care across different sectors of the health system.
