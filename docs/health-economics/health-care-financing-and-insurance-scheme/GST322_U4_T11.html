Classification of Health Care Providers

Introduction to Health Care Providers

A health care provider is any individual or institution that delivers preventive, curative, promotional, or rehabilitative health care services to individuals and communities. In the Nigerian health system, these providers form a diverse network ranging from orthodox medical practitioners to traditional healers. Understanding this classification helps nursing students navigate interprofessional collaboration and appreciate the different entry points patients use when seeking care.

Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary Levels of Care

Health care delivery is structured into tiers to ensure efficient resource allocation and patient flow.

  • Primary Care: The first point of contact for individuals within the National Health Health Act. Delivered in Primary Health Care (PHC) centers, focusing on basic immunizations, health education, and treatment of minor ailments.
  • Secondary Care: Provided in general hospitals or specialist community hospitals. It handles conditions requiring specialized skills, basic surgeries, and admission facilities.
  • Tertiary Care: Highly specialized medical care provided in teaching hospitals and federal medical centers. Involves advanced diagnostic technology, complex surgeries, and subspecialists.

Public versus Private Health Care Providers

The Nigerian health sector operates on a mixed economy model split between public and private ownership.

FeaturePublic ProvidersPrivate Providers
OwnershipGovernment (Federal, State, Local)Individuals, corporations, or faith-based groups
FundingGovernment budgets, taxes, subventionsOut-of-pocket payments, private health insurance
Primary GoalEquity and broad public accessFinancial sustainability and profit

Public facilities often struggle with funding and equipment shortages, while private facilities may be financially out of reach for low-income earners.

For-Profit versus Not-for-Profit Provider Organizations

Within the private sector, organizations differ fundamentally in their financial objectives.

  • For-Profit Providers: Private clinics, proprietary hospitals, and community pharmacies owned by individuals or business syndicates. Their operational surpluses are distributed to owners or shareholders.
  • Not-for-Profit Providers: Faith-based organizations (FBOs) such as mission hospitals, and non-governmental organizations (NGOs). Any financial surplus is reinvested back into the facility to improve services or subsidize care for the poor.

Formal and Informal Health Care Providers in Nigeria

A significant portion of healthcare delivery in Nigeria occurs outside orthodox settings.

  • Formal Providers: Professionally trained personnel operating within government-approved structures, including registered nurses, doctors, pharmacists, and medical laboratory scientists.
  • Informal Providers: Traditional birth attendants, herbalists, patent medicine vendors, and spiritual healing homes. Many Nigerians consult informal providers first due to cultural beliefs, geographical proximity, or perceived cost advantages.

Implications for Health Economics and Nursing Practice

The fragmentation of health care providers directly affects nursing practice and health economics. Out-of-pocket spending remains the dominant payment method in Nigeria, often leading to catastrophic health expenditures for households. Nurses must develop strong assessment skills to identify when patients have delayed seeking formal care due to financial constraints or patronage of informal providers. Furthermore, nurses working within a mixed provider system must advocate for safe referral networks and patient education regarding orthodox care options.
