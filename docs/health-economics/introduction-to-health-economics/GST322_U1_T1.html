Concepts in Health Economics

Introduction to Health Economics and Scarcity

Health economics is a branch of economics that studies how healthcare resources are allocated among alternative uses. At the core of this discipline lies the fundamental economic problem of scarcity. Resources such as skilled doctors, modern hospital equipment, hospital beds, and government health budgets are always limited, while human wants and healthcare needs are practically infinite. Because society cannot produce every healthcare service desired by everyone, choices must be made regarding which treatments to fund, where to build clinics, and how to distribute medical supplies. Nurses experience scarcity daily when managing heavy workloads with limited staffing or prioritizing patient care supplies during shortages. Understanding scarcity helps healthcare professionals appreciate why efficient resource management matters just as much as clinical bedside skills.

Opportunity Cost

Every time a choice is made in healthcare, something else is given up. Opportunity cost is defined as the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made. For example, if a district health management team decides to spend its limited budget building a new specialized cardiac center, the opportunity cost is the maternal health clinic or the malaria prevention programs that cannot be funded as a result. At the individual level, a patient choosing to spend out-of-pocket savings on elective cosmetic surgery gives up the opportunity to use those funds for nutritional food or school fees. Recognizing opportunity cost forces decision-makers to weigh the true hidden price of every health intervention.

Demand for Healthcare

Unlike ordinary consumer goods, people rarely demand healthcare because they want it for pleasure; they demand it because they want health. Demand refers to the quantity of healthcare services that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various prices over a given period.

Several unique factors influence healthcare demand:

  • Need versus Demand: A clinical need does not automatically translate into economic demand unless the patient has the financial means or insurance coverage to pay for the service.
  • Health Status: As a person ages or contracts chronic illnesses, their demand for medical care naturally rises.
  • Price and Insurance: High out-of-pocket costs reduce demand, while health insurance lowers the perceived price at the point of service, often increasing demand (a phenomenon linked to moral hazard).
  • Information Asymmetry: Doctors know much more about medical treatments than patients, meaning physicians often heavily influence or create the demand for their own services.

Supply of Healthcare

Supply refers to the total amount of a specific healthcare good or service that providers are willing and able to make available to patients at various prices. In the health sector, suppliers include individual doctors, nurses, pharmacists, laboratories, pharmaceutical manufacturers, and entire hospital systems.

Key elements that influence healthcare supply include:

  • Resource Availability: Shortages of trained professionals, such as doctors or specialized nurses, restrict the supply of clinical services.
  • Technology and Infrastructure: Advanced diagnostic machines and well-equipped facilities increase the capacity to deliver care.
  • Government Regulations: Licensing requirements, safety standards, and drug approvals can either encourage or slow down the supply of medical treatments.
  • Cost of Inputs: High wages for medical staff and expensive imported drugs increase the cost of production, which can reduce the quantity of services providers are willing to offer.

Equilibrium in the Healthcare Market

In a free market, prices adjust until the quantity of goods demanded by consumers equals the quantity supplied by producers. This balancing point is known as market equilibrium. When supply equals demand, there are neither frustrating shortages (where patients wait endlessly for scarce beds) nor wasteful surpluses (where expensive clinic rooms sit empty).

However, the healthcare market rarely achieves a true, perfect economic equilibrium. Government interventions, insurance subsidies, patents on life-saving drugs, and medical emergencies constantly distort prices and quantities. For instance, if government price ceilings are placed on essential medicines to make them affordable, a shortage may occur because suppliers reduce production while patient demand spikes.

Externalities of Healthcare

An externality occurs when the production or consumption of a good affects innocent third parties who are not directly involved in the transaction. In health economics, externalities are exceptionally important because health behaviors spill over into the wider community.

  • Positive Externalities: Vaccination is a classic example. When an individual gets vaccinated against an infectious disease like measles or polio, they protect themselves, but they also create a positive externality by helping achieve herd immunity, protecting vulnerable infants and immuno-compromised neighbors who cannot get vaccinated. Because the private market under-produces goods with positive externalities, governments often subsidize or mandate them.
  • Negative Externalities: Smoking tobacco in public places or failing to treat a contagious airborne infection creates negative externalities. The smoker enjoys the product, but second-hand smoke harms bystanders, imposing health costs on society.
