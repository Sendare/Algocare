Methods of Funding Primary Secondary and Tertiary Health Care

Introduction to Healthcare Funding and the Nigerian Health System

Healthcare financing determines how money is raised, pooled, and allocated to health services. Within the Nigerian three-tiered healthcare system, financial responsibility is shared among the federal, state, and local governments, alongside private entities and international partners. Primary healthcare is predominantly managed at the local government level, secondary care falls largely under state jurisdiction, and tertiary care is anchored by the federal government. Adequate funding ensures infrastructure maintenance, drug availability, and retention of skilled personnel. When financing fails, service delivery collapses, directly undermining population health outcomes and increasing disease burden across communities.

Primary Healthcare Funding Mechanisms

Primary healthcare represents the first point of contact for individuals within the community. Funding for this tier primarily flows from statutory allocations to local government councils, supplemented by state grants and occasional federal interventions like the Basic Health Care Provision Fund (BHCPF). Resource allocation at this level targets maternal and child health, routine immunizations, and treatment of common endemic illnesses like malaria. Despite these provisions, many primary health centres suffer from chronic underfunding, leading to dilapidated buildings, lack of cold-chain storage for vaccines, and severe shortages of basic medical consumables.

Secondary Healthcare Funding Structures

Secondary healthcare involves general hospitals that provide specialized services such as general surgery, internal medicine, paediatrics, and obstetrics. State governments carry the primary responsibility for financing this tier through state budgetary allocations, internally generated revenue (IGR), and state health insurance schemes where operational. General hospitals function as referral centres for primary health facilities. Funding at this level covers inpatient admissions, diagnostic laboratories, and emergency surgical care. Financial constraints often manifest as prolonged waiting times, delayed equipment repairs, and shortages of specialized nursing personnel.

Tertiary Healthcare Financing and Specialized Care

Tertiary healthcare encompasses teaching hospitals, federal medical centres, and specialized institutes that manage complex, highly specialized medical conditions. Federal government budgetary allocations constitute the major source of funding for these institutions. Additional revenue is generated through clinical service fees, research grants, and residency training support funds. Financing tertiary care is capital-intensive due to the requirement for advanced diagnostic machinery, intensive care units, and sub-specialist training. Consequently, budgetary cuts directly impair advanced surgical procedures and specialized research output.

Government Budgetary Allocations and Public Sector Financing

Public sector financing relies on tax revenues, customs duties, and natural resource rents pooled into national and sub-national treasuries. Annual budgetary allocations to the health sector reflect government commitment. International benchmarks, such as the Abuja Declaration, recommend that African governments allocate at least 15 percent of their annual budgets to health. Many developing economies fall short of this target, resulting in constrained public health spending. This shortfall places a heavy financial burden on health facilities, forcing them to rely on internally generated revenue collected directly from patients at the point of care.

Health Insurance Schemes: NHIA and State Initiatives

Health insurance pools financial risks across populations to protect individuals from catastrophic medical expenditures. The National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) regulates health insurance operations in Nigeria, catering primarily to federal public servants and organized private sectors, while also expanding coverage to vulnerable groups. Simultaneously, various state governments have established State Health Insurance Agencies (SHIAs) to cover informal sector workers and rural populations. Despite these structured initiatives, formal coverage remains relatively low due to widespread informal employment, low awareness, and administrative bottlenecks in enrolment.

Out-of-Pocket Payments and Their Impact on Access

  • Definition: Direct payment by individuals to healthcare providers at the time of service use without any third-party prepayment mechanism.
  • Dominance: Out-of-pocket spending constitutes the largest share of healthcare financing in Nigeria, accounting for over seventy percent of total health expenditures.
  • Access Barrier: Forces vulnerable households to choose between basic survival needs and paying for essential medications or hospital admissions.
  • Catastrophic Expenditure: Unforeseen medical emergencies frequently drive low-income families into extreme poverty due to lack of financial risk protection.

Donor Funding, NGOs, and Public-Private Partnerships

External aid, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and public-private partnerships (PPPs) supplement domestic health financing. International agencies such as WHO, UNICEF, and global health initiatives provide targeted funding primarily for vertical programs like HIV/AIDS, malaria control, and polio eradication. While donor funds have successfully driven disease-specific interventions, they introduce sustainability risks when grants expire or donor priorities shift. Public-private partnerships leverage private sector capital and management efficiency to upgrade hospital infrastructure, supply medical equipment, and improve service delivery efficiency in public institutions.

Challenges and Sustainable Solutions in Healthcare Financing

Financing Nigerian healthcare faces systemic bottlenecks including poor budget execution, corruption, inefficient resource allocation, and weak data systems. Addressing these challenges requires multi-faceted reforms. Expanding mandatory health insurance coverage to informal sector workers reduces catastrophic out-of-pocket spending. Governments must also increase budgetary allocations, improve financial transparency, and strengthen supply chain management to eliminate wastage. Nurses and health administrators play a vital role in advocating for efficient resource utilization and participating in facility-level financial planning to ensure sustainable care delivery.
