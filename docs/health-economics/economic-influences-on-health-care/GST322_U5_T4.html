Methods of Funding Primary Secondary and Tertiary Health Care

Introduction to Healthcare Funding and the Nigerian Health System

Healthcare financing determines how money is raised, pooled, and allocated to pay for health services in a country. In Nigeria, the health system operates on a three-tier structure: primary, secondary, and tertiary care. Each tier relies on different financial sources, ranging from local government budgets to federal allocations and out-of-pocket spending. Understanding these financial streams helps nurses see why some facilities lack basic supplies while others have advanced equipment. Adequate funding ensures that hospitals have drugs, personnel, and functional infrastructure to deliver safe patient care.

Primary Healthcare Funding Mechanisms

Primary healthcare serves as the first point of contact for individuals and families within the community. In Nigeria, primary health centers are funded primarily through the Basic Health Care Provision Fund (BHCPF), local government allocations, and grants. The BHCPF draws resources from one percent of the Consolidated Revenue Fund of the federal government and contributions from donor agencies. These funds target basic minimum package of health services, focusing on maternal and child health, immunizations, and treatment of common minor ailments at the grassroots level.

Secondary Healthcare Funding Structures

Secondary healthcare involves general hospitals that provide care for patients referred from primary centers. These facilities handle more complex medical, surgical, and emergency cases requiring admission. Funding for secondary health facilities comes predominantly through state government budgetary allocations, internally generated revenue from user fees, and sometimes state-level health insurance schemes. State ministries of health distribute these funds to pay staff salaries, maintain infrastructure, and procure pharmaceuticals for general hospitals.

Tertiary Healthcare Financing and Specialized Care

Tertiary healthcare centers, such as federal teaching hospitals and specialized medical centers, provide highly specialized services, training, and research. Because of the advanced technology and expert personnel required, tertiary care is capital-intensive. Funding comes primarily from the federal government through annual national budgets and capital appropriations. Additional funds are generated through specialized service fees and grants. A major challenge in tertiary financing is that federal allocations often struggle to cover the rising cost of advanced medical equipment maintenance and infrastructural upgrades.

Health Insurance Schemes: NHIA and State Initiatives

Health insurance is a risk-pooling mechanism designed to protect individuals from catastrophic out-of-pocket spending. The National Health Insurance Authority (NHIA) manages the federal scheme, catering largely to formal sector employees, though efforts are expanding toward vulnerable groups. Similarly, many state governments have established State Health Insurance Agencies to cover informal sector workers and rural dwellers. Contributions are pooled so that the healthy subsidize the sick, and the wealthy subsidize the poor. When properly implemented, health insurance guarantees steady cash flow for hospitals and financial protection for patients.

Out-of-Pocket Payments and Their Impact on Access

Out-of-pocket (OOP) payment refers to direct cash disbursements made by patients at the point of service when they visit a clinic or buy medications. In settings without universal health coverage, OOP remains the dominant method of healthcare financing. This financing model creates severe barriers to care, often leading to delays in seeking treatment, worsening of diseases, and impoverishment due to medical bills. Many families sell land or borrow money during health emergencies. Nurses frequently witness patients abandoning prescribed treatments because they cannot afford immediate cash payments.

Donor Funding, NGOs, and Public-Private Partnerships

External aid, international non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and philanthropic foundations play a major role in supplementing domestic health financing in Nigeria. Development partners often fund disease-specific programs such as HIV/AIDS treatment, malaria control, and polio eradication. Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) also help bridge infrastructural gaps by combining government oversight with private sector efficiency for facility management or equipment supply. While donor support saves lives, over-reliance on external funding can create sustainability risks when donor priorities shift or funding cycles end.

Challenges and Sustainable Solutions in Healthcare Financing

Nigeria's healthcare financing faces multiple hurdles, including poor budgetary releases, corruption, inefficient resource allocation, and a heavy reliance on out-of-pocket spending. To achieve universal health coverage, stakeholders advocate for several sustainable solutions. Governments must increase budget allocations to meet international targets like the Abuja Declaration. Furthermore, strengthening mandatory health insurance enrollment, plugging revenue leakages, and ensuring transparent fund management will improve resource availability. Nurses play an advocacy role by highlighting resource shortages and participating in facility-level planning.
