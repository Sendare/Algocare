MRI

Introduction to Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

Magnetic Resonance Imaging, commonly called MRI, is a powerful diagnostic tool used in healthcare to capture detailed pictures of internal body structures. Unlike X-rays and computed tomography (CT) scans, an MRI machine does not use ionizing radiation. Instead, it relies on a strong magnetic field and radiofrequency waves to generate clear images of soft tissues, organs, blood vessels, and bones.

In medical-surgical nursing, you will see MRIs ordered when doctors need a closer look at the brain, spinal cord, joints, muscles, and internal organs like the liver or heart. Because it highlights soft tissue differences much better than standard X-rays, it is especially valuable for spotting tiny injuries, tumors, inflammation, and structural defects.

Underlying Physics and Principles of MRI

The human body is made up mostly of water, which means it is full of hydrogen atoms. Each hydrogen atom contains a tiny particle called a proton that naturally spins and acts like a small compass needle.

When a patient enters the strong magnetic field of an MRI scanner, these hydrogen protons line up in the same direction. The machine then sends out a pulse of radiofrequency energy, which knocks the protons out of alignment. Once the radio pulse is turned off, the protons snap back to their original positions, releasing their own tiny radio signals. A computer picks up these signals, measures how long they take to return to normal based on the type of tissue they are in, and translates them into a detailed 3D image.

Indications and Diagnostic Uses

Doctors order MRIs across many different medical and surgical specialties. Common clinical indications include:

  • Brain and Spinal Cord: Detecting strokes, multiple sclerosis plaques, tumors, herniated discs, and spinal cord compression.
  • Musculoskeletal System: Identifying torn ligaments, meniscus injuries in the knee, rotator cuff tears, and bone infections.
  • Cardiovascular System: Evaluating heart muscle damage after a heart attack, congenital heart defects, and blood vessel diseases.
  • Abdomen and Pelvis: Assessing organs like the liver, kidneys, uterus, and prostate for masses or disease.

Contraindications and Patient Safety

The giant magnet inside an MRI machine is always on, even when no scan is running. This creates serious safety hazards for any metal objects brought near or inside the room. As a nurse, you must screen every patient thoroughly before they enter the suite.

Absolute contraindications include certain metallic foreign bodies in the eye, old types of pacemakers, and some aneurysm clips that can move or heat up in a magnetic field. Relative contraindications include claustrophobia, pregnancy (especially in the first trimester), severe kidney disease if contrast is used, and non-MRI-compatible surgical hardware or insulin pumps. Always remove jewelry, watches, hearing aids, and clothing with metal zippers or snaps.

Patient Preparation and Nursing Actions

Proper preparation reduces patient anxiety and ensures a safe, successful imaging procedure.

Begin by explaining what the patient will experience. The machine makes loud clicking, tapping, and thumping noises as it operates, so earplugs or headphones are always provided. Inform the patient that they must lie completely still for long periods, sometimes up to an hour, because movement blurs the images.

Check the patient's chart for lab results if contrast dye is ordered; specifically, look at creatinine and BUN levels to check kidney function. Ensure the patient empties their bladder right before the scan so they remain comfortable inside the tube. Finally, complete a written safety checklist alongside the patient to confirm no hidden metal is present.

Post-Procedure Care and Complications

Once the MRI scan finishes, most patients require very little special care and can return to their normal diet and activities immediately.

However, if the patient received intravenous contrast dye (usually gadolinium), monitor them closely for allergic reactions such as itching, hives, rash, or trouble breathing. Encourage the patient to drink plenty of fluids after the test to help flush the contrast agent out of their system quickly.

For patients who received mild sedatives to cope with claustrophobia, keep them in bed with side rails up until the medication wears off completely. Assess their vital signs regularly and ensure a safe transition back to the nursing unit.
