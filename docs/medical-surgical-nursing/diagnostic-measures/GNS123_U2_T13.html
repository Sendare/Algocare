CT Scan

Introduction and Principles of Computed Tomography

A computed tomography, commonly known as a CT scan, is a diagnostic imaging procedure that uses X-ray equipment combined with computer processing to create detailed cross-sectional images of the body. Unlike a traditional X-ray which produces a flat, two-dimensional shadow, a CT scanner rotates a narrow beam of X-rays around the patient in a full circle. Detectors on the opposite side measure how much radiation passes through different tissues.

The underlying principle relies on tissue density. Dense structures like bone absorb a large amount of radiation and appear bright white on the monitor, referred to as hyperdense. Less dense tissues like air-filled lungs or fluid-filled ventricles absorb very little radiation and appear dark, or hypodense. Soft tissues fall somewhere in between, showing up in shades of grey.

A computer then takes thousands of these digital readings and performs complex mathematical calculations to reconstruct them into thin, clear 'slices' of the body. Clinicians can view these slices individually or stack them together to form a three-dimensional image. This allows healthcare teams to visualize internal organs, blood vessels, and bones with remarkable precision, helping to locate tumors, internal bleeding, fractures, and infections.

CT Contrast Media and Scan Variations

Many CT scans require the use of contrast media—special substances that temporarily change how X-rays interact with the body. Contrast highlights specific blood vessels, organs, or tissues, making them stand out clearly from surrounding structures.

  • Iodinated Contrast: Usually injected intravenously or given orally, this substance highlights blood vessels, the urinary tract, and organs like the liver and spleen.
  • Oral Contrast: Swallowed by the patient, this outlines the stomach and intestinal loops to help diagnose bowel obstructions or inflammatory conditions.
  • Non-Contrast Scans: Performed without any contrast agent, often used initially to look for acute head injuries, fresh brain bleeding, or kidney stones.

Patients often need to fast for a few hours before receiving intravenous contrast. Nurses must review the patient's allergy history, particularly regarding iodine or shellfish, and check baseline kidney function markers such as serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen, because contrast agents are filtered out by the kidneys and can occasionally cause kidney injury.

Pre-Procedural Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Thorough pre-procedural preparation ensures patient safety and diagnostic accuracy. The nurse must verify that a valid informed consent has been signed, ensuring the patient understands the risks, benefits, and use of contrast if applicable.

Assessment priorities include screening for pregnancy, as ionizing radiation can harm a developing fetus. The nurse must also inquire about implanted metal devices, pacemakers, or surgical clips, although most modern titanium implants do not prevent a CT scan. Asking about claustrophobia is also helpful, as the scanner gantry can feel confined for some individuals.

Practical preparation steps involve instructing the patient to remove all metallic items, jewelry, hairpins, and hearing aids near the scanning area, because metal causes severe artifact streaks on the final images. Patients scheduled for contrast studies are typically instructed to fast for four hours prior, though regular medications can usually be taken with a small sip of water unless ordered otherwise.

Intra-Procedural Care and Patient Safety

During the scan, the nurse or radiographer positions the patient on the motorized examination table, often securing them gently with straps or cushions to prevent involuntary movement. Even slight motion during the rotation can blur the images and require a repeat scan, increasing radiation exposure.

As the table moves through the circular opening of the gantry, the patient must remain completely still. If an intravenous contrast is being injected, the nurse or technologist monitors the access site and informs the patient that they may experience a sudden flushing sensation, a metallic taste in the mouth, or a warm feeling in the groin area. These sensations are completely normal and typically pass in a minute or two.

The technologist operates the scanner from an adjacent control room behind a lead-lined glass window, maintaining constant visual and intercom contact with the patient throughout the entire procedure.

Post-Procedural Management and Complications

Post-procedural care focuses primarily on monitoring for delayed reactions and assisting the body in eliminating contrast media. If the patient received intravenous contrast, the nurse encourages high fluid intake—either oral water or IV fluids if prescribed—to help flush the dye out through the kidneys quickly.

The nurse observes the intravenous site for signs of extravasation, where the contrast fluid leaks into the surrounding subcutaneous tissue instead of the vein, causing localized swelling and pain. Delayed allergic reactions, such as hives, itching, or respiratory discomfort, can occur hours after the procedure, so patient education on warning signs is essential before discharge.

Patients with pre-existing renal impairment require close monitoring of their urine output and follow-up blood work to detect contrast-induced nephropathy early. If no contrast was used and the patient feels well, normal activities and diet can resume immediately.
