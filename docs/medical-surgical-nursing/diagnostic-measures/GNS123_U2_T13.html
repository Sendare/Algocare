CT Scan

Introduction and Definition of Computed Tomography

Computed Tomography, universally known as a CT scan, is an advanced diagnostic imaging technique that combines specialized X-ray equipment with sophisticated computers to generate detailed, cross-sectional images of the body. Unlike traditional X-rays which produce flat, superimposed shadows, a CT scanner creates slice-like views—resembling individual pages sliced from a loaf of bread. This allows healthcare providers to visualize internal organs, blood vessels, bones, and soft tissues with exceptional clarity. Developed in the 1970s, this technology revolutionized medical diagnostics by providing non-invasive anatomical insight that previously required exploratory surgery. In Nigerian tertiary health institutions, CT imaging has become a cornerstone for rapid trauma assessment, neurology, and oncology, bridging the gap between clinical suspicion and definitive diagnosis.

Underlying Principles and How a CT Scan Works

The mechanical operation of a CT scanner relies on differential tissue absorption of ionizing radiation.

  • X-ray Tube and Detectors: An X-ray tube rotates rapidly in a continuous circle around the patient lying on a motorized table. Opposite the tube, electronic X-ray detectors measure the amount of radiation passing through the body.
  • Data Attenuation: Dense structures like cortical bone absorb most radiation and appear bright white, known as hyperdense. Less dense tissues like air or cerebrospinal fluid absorb very little, appearing dark black or hypodense. Soft tissues fall in various shades of grey (isodense).
  • Computer Reconstruction: Advanced algorithms process millions of attenuation measurements from multiple rotational angles, calculating exact tissue density values to construct the final digital image.

Types of CT Scans and Contrast Media Usage

Diagnostic clarity during a CT scan is frequently enhanced through specialized scanning protocols and the administration of contrast media. Contrast agents temporarily alter the density of specific tissues or blood vessels, making pathology stand out distinctly from normal structures.

  • Non-Contrast CT (NCCT): Performed without contrast agents; standard for acute intracranial hemorrhage, kidney stones, and basic skeletal trauma.
  • Contrast-Enhanced CT (CECT): Utilizes iodine-based intravenous contrast to highlight vascular structures, organ perfusion, and hypervascular tumors.
  • Oral Contrast: Barium sulfate or water-soluble iodine swallowed by the patient to opacify the gastrointestinal tract and distinguish bowel loops from adjacent masses.
  • Rectal Contrast: Administered via enema when lower gastrointestinal tract pathology requires precise delineation.

Indications for CT Scanning in Medical-Surgical Conditions

CT imaging plays a vital role across multiple medical and surgical specialties by providing rapid and accurate diagnostic answers. In emergency departments, polytrauma patients undergo swift pan-scan evaluations to detect internal hemorrhage, splenic lacerations, or pelvic fractures. Neurological indications include distinguishing ischemic stroke from hemorrhagic stroke before thrombolytic therapy is initiated. In oncology, CT is indispensable for staging cancers, guiding needle biopsies, and monitoring therapeutic response over time. Surgical teams rely heavily on CT angiography to map complex vascular anomalies, aortic aneurysms, or mesenteric ischemia before operative intervention.

Pre-Procedural Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Thorough nursing preparation ensures patient safety and prevents scan delays. The nurse must first verify the clinical order, obtain informed consent, and screen for potential contraindications.

  • Allergy History: Assess specifically for previous adverse reactions to iodine, seafood, or contrast media.
  • Renal Function: Check baseline serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), as intravenous contrast can precipitate contrast-induced nephropathy in impaired kidneys.
  • Medication Reconciliation: Identify patients taking metformin, which may need temporary cessation to prevent lactic acidosis if renal function declines post-contrast.
  • Patient Instruction: Inform the patient about fasting protocols (usually nothing by mouth for 4 hours if contrast is used) and prepare them for sensations of warmth and a metallic taste during contrast injection.

Intra-Procedural Nursing Care and Patient Safety

During the actual scan, the nurse facilitates a smooth process by addressing patient anxiety and maintaining physical safety. The patient must lie supine on a narrow table that moves through the circular gantry. Because motion artifact severely degrades image quality, clear communication and positioning aids like straps or foam pillows are essential. If intravenous contrast is injected via a secure, patent peripheral line (ideally 20-gauge or larger in an antecubital fossa), the nurse or radiographer monitors the injection site for infiltration. The patient is instructed to report any sudden itching, shortness of breath, or facial swelling immediately, as these signal an acute anaphylactoid contrast reaction.

Post-Procedural Management and Monitoring

Post-procedural nursing care centers on recovery, elimination of contrast agents, and monitoring for delayed adverse reactions. The nurse encourages high fluid intake—either oral water or intravenous fluids if prescribed—to accelerate renal clearance of the contrast dye. For patients who received oral contrast, inform them that stools may appear whitish or pale for the first twenty-four hours as the barium clears. The nurse continues to monitor vital signs and observe for delayed hypersensitivity reactions, which can occasionally manifest hours after the procedure with skin rashes, urticaria, or respiratory wheezing. Review discharge instructions regarding dietary resumption and follow-up clinic appointments.

Complications, Contraindications, and Radiation Safety

Although CT scans provide unmatched diagnostic benefits, nurses must remain mindful of associated risks, primarily ionizing radiation exposure and contrast-related complications. Radiation safety adheres to the ALARA principle (As Low As Reasonably Achievable), minimizing exposure through tailored pediatric and adult scanning protocols. Pregnant women represent a special contraindication: while a single CT scan does not typically deliver enough radiation to harm a fetus, alternative imaging modalities like ultrasound or MRI are preferred whenever possible to eliminate risk entirely. Severe allergic reactions to iodinated contrast—though rare—include bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, and anaphylactic shock, requiring immediate emergency access to crash carts, antihistamines, and epinephrine.
