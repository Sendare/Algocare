CT Scan

Introduction and Principles of Computed Tomography

A Computed Tomography (CT) scan is a sophisticated diagnostic imaging procedure that combines X-ray equipment with advanced computer processing. Unlike a standard two-dimensional X-ray which creates a flat, overlapping projection of body structures, a CT scanner takes multiple images from various angles around the body. The computer then processes these digital slices to generate cross-sectional views of bones, blood vessels, and soft tissues.

The core principle relies on radiation attenuation. As an X-ray beam passes through the body, different tissues absorb varying amounts of radiation based on their density. Bone absorbs a high amount and appears white on the monitor, air absorbs very little and appears black, while soft tissues show up in varying shades of grey. A rotating gantry houses the X-ray tube and detectors, continuously capturing data as the patient lies on a motorized table moving through the scanner.

Contrast Media Usage in CT Imaging

Many CT scans require contrast media to enhance the visibility of specific internal structures, blood vessels, or pathologies. Contrast agents alter how radiation is absorbed, making blood vessels, tumors, or inflammation stand out clearly against surrounding tissue.

  • Iodinated contrast: Administered intravenously, orally, or rectally to highlight blood vessels, organs like the liver and kidneys, and the gastrointestinal tract.
  • Oral contrast: Used to outline the stomach and bowel loops, helping differentiate the intestinal tract from adjacent abdominal organs.
  • Intravenous contrast: Quickly highlights the vascular system and highlights areas of increased blood flow, which often indicate tumors or acute inflammation.

Clinical Indications for CT Scanning

CT scans are indispensable in medical-surgical nursing for rapid and accurate diagnosis across various body systems.

  • Neurology: Rapidly identifies acute ischemic strokes, intracranial hemorrhage, and skull fractures following head trauma.
  • Pulmonology: Detects pulmonary emboli via CT angiography, lung masses, and severe interstitial lung diseases.
  • Abdomen and Pelvis: Diagnoses acute appendicitis, bowel obstructions, renal calculi, and internal organ lacerations after abdominal trauma.
  • Oncology: Stages cancers, monitors tumor responses to chemotherapy, and guides needle biopsy procedures.

Pre-Procedural Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Thorough preparation before a CT scan ensures patient safety, accurate results, and the prevention of adverse reactions. The nurse must conduct a comprehensive assessment focusing on specific risk factors.

  • Allergy Screening: Specifically check for previous reactions to iodine, shellfish, or contrast dye.
  • Renal Function: Review serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels because iodinated contrast is cleared by the kidneys and can precipitate contrast-induced nephropathy.
  • Medication Review: Identify if the patient takes metformin, which may need to be temporarily withheld to prevent lactic acidosis in the event of acute kidney injury.
  • Pregnancy Status: Confirm last menstrual period or perform a pregnancy test to protect a developing fetus from ionizing radiation.

Intra-Procedural Care and Patient Safety

During the scan, the nurse or radiographer ensures the patient remains completely still to prevent motion artifact, which blurs the images and compromises diagnostic accuracy. Patients receiving intravenous contrast often experience a transient flushed feeling, a metallic taste in the mouth, or a warm sensation in the groin; the nurse should prepare the patient for these normal sensations beforehand.

Communication is maintained throughout the procedure via an intercom system while the nurse monitors the patient from the control room or through a window. For pediatric or highly anxious patients, sedation may be required to maintain the immobility necessary for quality imaging.

Post-Procedural Management and Monitoring

Post-procedural nursing care centers on monitoring for delayed adverse reactions and assisting the patient back to baseline status.

  • Encourage high fluid intake to flush the iodinated contrast medium out of the renal system quickly.
  • Monitor the intravenous injection site for signs of infiltration, swelling, or hematoma formation.
  • Observe outpatient clients for delayed hypersensitivity reactions, which can manifest as hives, itching, or respiratory discomfort hours after the scan.
  • Resume pre-procedure medications, such as metformin, only after physician clearance confirms stable renal function.

Complications, Contraindications, and Radiation Safety

Although CT scans provide life-saving diagnostic information, they involve significant exposure to ionizing radiation and potential risks from contrast media.

  • Radiation Exposure: Each scan carries a cumulative dose of radiation, which slightly increases lifetime cancer risk; therefore, scans must be clinically justified.
  • Contrast Reactions: Mild reactions include nausea and hives, while severe anaphylaxis requires immediate resuscitation with epinephrine.
  • Extravasation: Leaking of IV contrast into subcutaneous tissue can cause localized tissue damage and blistering.
  • Contraindications: Severe allergy to iodinated contrast and established pregnancy are major considerations requiring risk-benefit analysis or alternative imaging like ultrasound or MRI.
