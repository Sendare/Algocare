Vital Signs, Weight, Height, Apex Beat and Fetal Heart Beat

Baseline Physical Measurements and Vital Signs Overview

Baseline clinical metrics serve as the foundation of physical assessment in any healthcare setting, whether in a busy Lagos teaching hospital or a rural primary health clinic. These objective indicators reveal real-time changes in a person's physiological status, often signaling distress long before overt symptoms appear. Routine collection includes measuring core vital signs alongside anthropometric parameters like height and weight. Establishing these initial values allows clinicians to track therapy effectiveness, catch sudden deterioration, and make prompt decisions regarding patient care. Every measurement requires accuracy, proper technique, and an understanding of the physiological principles driving human homeostasis.

Core Vital Signs: Temperature, Pulse, Respiration, and Blood Pressure

The four classic vital signs—temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure—offer a snapshot of cardiovascular and metabolic health.

  • Temperature: Reflects the balance between heat production and heat loss in the body, typically measured via oral, tympanic, axillary, or rectal routes. Axillary measurement is most common in routine Nigerian ward practice, though it reads slightly lower than core temperature.
  • Pulse: Represents the tactile expansion of an artery produced by wave pressure from the contracting left ventricle. Assess rate, rhythm, and volume, most frequently at the radial artery.
  • Respiration: Involves observing the rise and fall of the chest, noting rate, depth, and rhythm without alerting the patient, as conscious control alters breathing patterns.
  • Blood Pressure: Measures the lateral pressure exerted by circulating blood on arterial walls during cardiac contraction (systolic) and relaxation (diastolic) using a sphygmomanometer and stethoscope.

Measuring Anthropometric Data: Weight and Height Techniques

Anthropometric measurements provide objective dimensions of the human body, essential for calculating drug dosages, assessing nutritional status, and monitoring fluid balance.

Weight must be measured using a properly calibrated scale. The patient should wear minimal clothing and remove heavy footwear. In bedridden patients, bed scales or sling lifts are utilized. Height is measured with the patient standing straight, looking forward, with heels together and shoulders relaxed against a vertical stadiometer. For individuals unable to stand, alternative metrics such as demispan or knee height can be used to estimate stature. Regular tracking of these parameters helps identify malnutrition, rapid fluid retention, or sudden weight loss linked to chronic pathology.

Assessing the Apex Beat: Location, Technique, and Clinical Significance

The apex beat, also known as the point of maximal impulse (PMI), represents the point where the inferior tip of the left ventricle impacts the chest wall during systole. To locate it correctly, the clinician uses the pads of the fingers, palpating in the fifth left intercostal space along the midclavicular line in adults. In children, it may be located slightly higher and more laterally. Palpation helps determine the location, amplitude, and duration of the impulse. A displaced apex beat—shifting laterally or inferiorly—often indicates cardiac enlargement or hypertrophy, a common finding in long-standing hypertension or heart failure.

Obstetric Assessment: Locating and Monitoring the Fetal Heart Beat

Monitoring fetal wellbeing is a fundamental skill in midwifery and obstetric nursing. The fetal heart beat (FHB) can be detected using a Pinard stethoscope from approximately 20 weeks of gestation, or much earlier—around 10 to 12 weeks—using an ultrasonic Doppler device.

To locate the FHB manually with a Pinard, abdominal palpation using Leopold's manoeuvres is performed first to determine fetal lie, presentation, and position. The bell of the Pinard is placed over the area of the fetal back. The normal fetal heart rate ranges from 110 to 160 beats per minute. Regular auscultation confirms fetal life, while listening for accelerations and decelerations helps evaluate fetal oxygenation during labor.

Documentation, Normal Ranges, and Clinical Interpretation

Accurate recording of physiological measurements is as vital as taking them. Every value must be documented immediately on the patient's chart or electronic health record, noting the exact date, time, and any accompanying symptoms like pain or dizziness.
