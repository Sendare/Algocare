Urine Testing

Introduction and Physiology of Urine Formation

Urine testing remains a cornerstone of basic clinical diagnostics in Nigerian healthcare facilities, offering a swift window into a patient's renal and metabolic health. Urine formation begins in the nephron, the functional unit of the kidney, through three distinct processes.

  • Glomerular filtration: Blood passes through the glomerulus, forcing water, electrolytes, and small molecules into Bowman's capsule while retaining blood cells and large proteins.
  • Tubular reabsorption: Essential substances like glucose, water, and amino acids are reclaimed from the filtrate and returned to the peritubular capillaries.
  • Tubular secretion: Waste products, excess hydrogen ions, and certain drugs are actively secreted from the capillaries into the renal tubule for excretion.

Types of Urine Specimens and Collection Methods

Accurate laboratory results depend entirely on the quality of the specimen collected. Different clinical objectives require specific collection techniques.

  • Random specimen: Collected at any time without prior preparation, primarily used for routine screening.
  • First morning specimen: Most concentrated sample, ideal for detecting pregnancy, nitrites, and protein.
  • Clean-catch midstream specimen: Minimizes microbial contamination from the external genitalia, making it suitable for urinalysis and culture.
  • Timed 24-hour collection: Quantifies specific analytes like creatinine or protein over a full day, requiring strict adherence to timing protocols.

Routine Urinalysis and Dipstick Testing

Routine urinalysis evaluates physical, chemical, and microscopic properties of urine. Reagent strip (dipstick) testing provides rapid semi-quantitative chemical analysis. Nurses must check expiration dates and store strips properly, as humidity and heat degrade the reactive pads.

Microscopic Examination of Urine Sediment

Microscopic analysis of centrifuged urine sediment confirms and expands upon dipstick findings. The specimen is spun down, the supernatant is discarded, and a drop of sediment is examined under a microscope.

  • Red blood cells: Small, round, non-nucleated discs; presence indicates glomerular damage, stones, or trauma.
  • White blood cells: Larger, granular cells with nuclei; high counts signify pyuria and active inflammation or infection.
  • Epithelial cells: Squamous, transitional, or renal tubular cells; excessive renal tubular cells suggest tubular injury.
  • Casts: Cylindrical protein molds formed in the distal nephron; hyaline, granular, or cellular casts point toward specific renal pathologies.

Diagnostic Urine Cultures and Sensitivity Testing

Urine culture and sensitivity (C&S) is ordered when a urinary tract infection is suspected or when routine urinalysis shows unexplained abnormalities. The test isolates the causative microorganism and determines which antimicrobial agents will effectively treat the infection.

A colony count exceeding 10^5 colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL) from a clean-catch sample typically confirms significant bacteriuria. The sensitivity report lists effective antibiotics alongside terms like susceptible, intermediate, or resistant, guiding safe pharmacological intervention.

Specialized Urine Tests and 24-Hour Collections

Certain metabolic disorders and kidney conditions require tests that cannot be measured on a single spot specimen. A 24-hour urine collection measures total daily excretion of substances like protein, cortisol, or electrolytes.

Executing a 24-hour collection requires meticulous patient instruction. The collection period begins by having the patient void and discard that initial urine, noting the exact time. Every drop of urine produced over the next 24 hours is saved in a specialized container, often kept on ice or with a chemical preservative. The final collection occurs at the exact same hour the following day.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Nurses oversee the entire urine testing pathway, from patient preparation and safe collection to specimen transport and result interpretation. Proper labeling at the bedside is mandatory to prevent dangerous lab mix-ups.

When educating patients, clear communication overcomes anxiety and prevents contaminated samples. Female patients need instructions to cleanse the labia from front to back, while uncircumcised male patients must retract the foreskin before cleansing the meatus. Prompt delivery to the laboratory—within two hours or refrigeration—prevents bacterial proliferation and cellular breakdown that ruins diagnostic accuracy.
