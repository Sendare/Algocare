Assessment

Introduction to Comprehensive Patient Assessment

Clinical assessment forms the foundational bedrock of professional nursing practice. It is a systematic, continuous process of collecting, validating, and communicating patient data. By establishing an accurate baseline, nurses can identify actual and potential health problems, guiding clinical decision-making and priority setting. Assessment is not merely a static task performed upon admission; it is an ongoing cycle that adapts as the patient's condition evolves. Data collection is traditionally categorized into two primary types: - **Subjective data**: Symptoms and covert experiences described solely by the patient, such as pain intensity, nausea, or anxiety. - **Objective data**: Signs and overt measurements gathered through observation, physical examination, and diagnostic testing, such as blood pressure, surgical scars, or laboratory values. In busy Nigerian healthcare environments, where nurse-to-patient ratios can be exceptionally high, structured assessment frameworks prevent vital clinical clues from being missed during rapid ward rounds.

Health History and Biographical Data Collection

Gathering a thorough health history unlocks the narrative of the patient's illness. Biographical data—including age, gender, occupation, and residential location—provides essential context for interpreting disease risk. For instance, living in rural agricultural belts or urban centers with heavy traffic congestion directly impacts respiratory health. The chronological narrative of the **presenting complaint** should capture the exact sequence of events, precipitating factors, and relieving measures. Clinicians must explore past medical history, family genetic predispositions, and medication profiles, including the use of local herbal concoctions which frequently interact with orthodox pharmaceuticals. Maintaining a respectful, open-ended interviewing style encourages vulnerable patients to share sensitive information without fear of judgment.

Core Techniques of Physical Examination

Physical examination relies on four primary sensory techniques executed in a specific sequence. Understanding how to apply these methods correctly ensures reliable data extraction while minimizing patient discomfort. 1. **Inspection**: The deliberate, focused visual observation of the patient, noting symmetry, color, movement, and obvious abnormalities. It begins the moment the nurse encounters the patient. 2. **Palpation**: Using the sense of touch through the finger pads and dorsal surface of hands to evaluate texture, temperature, moisture, swelling, tenderness, and pulsations. 3. **Percussion**: Striking the body surface lightly with distinct taps to produce sound vibrations. The resulting tone—tympanic, resonant, hyperresonant, dull, or flat—reveals the density of underlying structures. 4. **Auscultation**: Listening to body sounds generated inside organs, primarily using a stethoscope to evaluate heart, lung, and bowel sounds. *Clinical exception*: When assessing the abdomen, the standard sequence is altered. Auscultation is performed before palpation and percussion to prevent stimulating bowel motility and creating false-positive bowel sounds.

System-by-System Physical Assessment

Organized body system assessments prevent diagnostic oversights. While head-to-toe sequencing is common, regional targeting allows rapid evaluation during acute distress. - **Cardiovascular**: Assessing central and peripheral pulses, checking capillary refill time, inspecting for jugular venous distension, and auscultating heart valves for murmurs or extra gallop rhythms. - **Respiratory**: Evaluating chest expansion symmetry, counting respiratory rate and depth, noting use of accessory muscles, and auscultating bilateral breath sounds for wheezes, crackles, or bronchial echoes. - **Gastrointestinal**: Inspecting abdominal contours, auscultating all four quadrants for bowel activity, light and deep palpation for tenderness or organomegaly, and inquiring about bowel habit changes. - **Neurological**: Assessing level of consciousness using the Glasgow Coma Scale, testing pupillary light reflexes, checking cranial nerve integrity, and evaluating motor strength and sensory responses.

Pain Assessment and Management Strategies

Pain is universally defined as an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience, making it entirely subjective. The cardinal rule of clinical practice is that pain is whatever the experiencing person says it is. Reliance solely on physiological markers like elevated blood pressure or tachycardia will lead to under-treatment, as the human body adapts to chronic discomfort. Clinicians utilize validated tools matched to patient developmental stages: - **Numeric Rating Scale (NRS)**: Rates discomfort from 0 to 10 for cooperative adults. - **Wong-Baker FACES Scale**: Utilizes cartoon facial expressions for children or language-barrier patients. - **Behavioral Pain Scale**: Assesses non-verbal cues in unconscious or intubated patients. Effective management combines pharmacological interventions, such as the WHO analgesic ladder ranging from non-opioids to strong opioids, with non-pharmacological modalities including repositioning, application of heat or cold, and distraction techniques.

Psychosocial, Cultural, and Spiritual Assessment

Holistic nursing mandates looking beyond physical pathology to examine the whole person. Cultural beliefs heavily influence health-seeking behaviors, dietary restrictions, and acceptance of certain medical interventions like blood transfusions. For instance, in many Nigerian communities, family members play a central decision-making role during health crises, requiring nurses to engage the collective family rather than just the individual patient. Psychosocial evaluations screen for anxiety, depression, coping mechanisms, and support networks. Spiritual assessments explore sources of hope, meaning, and distress. Asking simple exploratory questions like 'What helps you cope when things get difficult?' opens avenues for emotional healing, reducing psychological distress that frequently exacerbates physical symptoms.

Interpreting Diagnostic and Laboratory Findings

Laboratory and diagnostic results provide objective biochemical and anatomical data to confirm clinical impressions or monitor therapeutic responses. Nurses must understand normal reference ranges and recognize critical panic values that require immediate medical notification. Interpretation requires clinical context rather than isolated number-crunching. For example, an elevated white blood cell count (leukocytosis) points toward infection, but physiological stress, corticosteroid therapy, or trauma can produce similar elevations. Imaging results, such as chest radiographs or ultrasound reports, must be correlated with physical findings; a clear lung auscultation contrasted with an old radiographic scar requires astute clinical judgment. Recognizing specimen collection errors—such as hemolysis in a blood sample—prevents misdiagnosis and unnecessary therapeutic interventions.

Documentation and Reporting in Medical-Surgical Nursing

An assessment is clinically incomplete if it is not accurately documented. Medical records serve as legal documents, communication tools, and quality assurance audits. The golden rule of nursing documentation dictates that if an assessment or intervention is not recorded, legally it was never performed. Effective charting must be timely, objective, accurate, and concise. Nurses should avoid vague descriptors like 'patient had a good day' or 'wound looks fine' in favor of precise, measurable observations: 'serosanguineous drainage noted on abdominal dressing measuring 2cm by 3cm.' During verbal handovers, structured communication frameworks like **SBAR** (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) ensure critical data is transmitted efficiently between shifts, minimizing errors during transitions of care.
