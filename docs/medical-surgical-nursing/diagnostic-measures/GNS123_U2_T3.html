Physical Examination

Introduction and Purpose of Physical Examination

A physical examination is a systematic data-collection method that uses the senses of sight, hearing, smell, and touch to gather clinical information about a patient. This process forms the physical half of the health assessment, complementing the subjective health history obtained during the interview. The primary purpose is to establish a baseline of physiological function, identify actual or potential health problems, evaluate the effectiveness of nursing interventions, and monitor changes in a patient's condition over time. In Nigerian healthcare settings, where diagnostic equipment may occasionally be limited by power outages or resource constraints, a thorough and skillful physical examination remains an indispensable tool for clinical decision-making. Nurses perform baseline exams upon admission, routine check-ups in outpatient clinics, and ongoing focused assessments during shift handovers. Without this hands-on evaluation, subtle clinical deteriorations can easily go unnoticed until they become medical emergencies.

Preparation and Environment Setup

Clinical accuracy depends heavily on the setting in which the assessment takes place. A proper examination environment prioritizes patient privacy, comfort, and safety. - **Lighting**: Adequate illumination, preferably natural light combined with a focused examination lamp, is necessary to accurately observe skin color, rashes, and subtle jaundice. - **Temperature**: The room must be comfortably warm to prevent shivering, which causes involuntary muscle contractions that can obscure heart and lung sounds during auscultation. - **Privacy**: Use curtains, screens, or dedicated examination rooms to protect the patient's modesty and encourage honest disclosure of physical symptoms. - **Equipment**: Gather and test all necessary instruments—such as stethoscopes, sphygmomanometers, penlights, and tape measures—before approaching the patient to maintain a smooth, professional workflow.

Standard Precautions and Infection Control

Infection prevention is a fundamental responsibility during any physical contact with a patient. Standard precautions apply to all patients receiving care, regardless of their suspected or confirmed infection status. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective measure to stop cross-contamination between patients. Gloves must be worn whenever the nurse anticipates contact with blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, non-intact skin, or contaminated items. However, wearing gloves does not replace the need for handwashing; hands must be washed or sanitized immediately after glove removal. Additional personal protective equipment, such as face masks, eye protection, or gowns, is utilized if splashing or spraying of body fluids is likely during procedures like wound inspection or oral cavity checks. Stethoscope diaphragms and other reusable tools must be wiped with an approved disinfectant wipe between every single patient interaction.

Techniques of Physical Assessment: Inspection, Palpation, Percussion, and Auscultation

Except when examining the abdomen, where auscultation precedes palpation to avoid altering bowel sounds, physical assessment follows a strict, sequential order of four core techniques. | Technique | Primary Sensory Mode | Clinical Purpose | Common Example | | --- | --- | --- | --- | | **Inspection** | Vision and Smell | Observing symmetry, color, shape, and odors | Noting jaundice in the sclera | | **Palpation** | Touch | Evaluating texture, temperature, moisture, and tenderness | Detecting abdominal masses | | **Percussion** | Hearing and Touch | Striking body tissues to produce sound waves indicating density | Assessing for fluid in the lungs | | **Auscultation** | Hearing | Listening to internal body sounds using a stethoscope | Assessing bowel motility or heart valves | Mastering these four skills requires deliberate practice. For instance, light palpation should always precede deep palpation to prevent guarding and reduce patient discomfort.

General Survey and Vital Signs Measurement

The general survey begins the exact moment the nurse first encounters the patient. It is an overall visual appraisal covering physical appearance, body structure, mobility, and behavior. The nurse observes signs of acute distress, facial expression, personal hygiene, speech clarity, and nutritional status before touching the patient. Immediately following the general survey, the nurse measures vital signs: temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation, often accompanied by pain assessment as the fifth vital sign. These numerical parameters offer immediate physiological feedback regarding homeostatic balance. Any acute deviation from the patient's baseline requires immediate clinical reporting and further targeted assessment.

System-Specific Physical Examinations

Once general observations and vitals are recorded, the nurse proceeds to targeted, body-system examinations. A systematic head-to-toe approach ensures no anatomical region is missed. - **Cardiovascular**: Inspecting the precordium for pulsations, palpating the apical pulse, and auscultating heart sounds (S1 and S2) using both the diaphragm and bell of the stethoscope. - **Respiratory**: Assessing chest expansion symmetry, palpating for tactile fremitus, and listening to breath sounds across all lung fields, noting any adventitious sounds like wheezes or crackles. - **Gastrointestinal**: Inspecting abdominal contour, auscultating all four quadrants for bowel motility, and lightly palpating for tenderness, masses, or organomegaly. - **Neurological**: Evaluating level of consciousness, cranial nerve integrity, deep tendon reflexes, and sensory function. Keeping the patient informed about what each step entails helps reduce anxiety and promotes active cooperation.

Documentation and Reporting of Findings

A physical examination is clinically incomplete until all findings are accurately recorded in the patient's medical record. Documentation must be prompt, objective, and factual. Avoid vague terms like "normal" or "fine"; instead, use descriptive measurements and precise anatomical terminology, such as "palpable liver edge 2 cm below the right costal margin" or "clear breath sounds bilaterally in all lobes." Timely reporting is vital when abnormal findings indicate a sudden shift in clinical status. If a patient exhibits acute hypotension, respiratory distress, or sudden neurological deficits, the nurse must communicate these findings immediately to the attending physician or multidisciplinary team, ensuring continuity of safe, effective patient care.
