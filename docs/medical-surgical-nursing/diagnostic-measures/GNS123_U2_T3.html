Physical Examination

Introduction and Purpose of Physical Examination

A physical examination is a systematic data-collection method used by nurses to evaluate a patient's health status. It builds upon subjective data gathered during the nursing health history, providing objective evidence to confirm or rule out clinical suspicions. The primary goals include obtaining baseline health data, identifying early signs of illness, and evaluating the effectiveness of ongoing nursing and medical interventions.

Every clinical encounter requires a clear understanding of why an assessment is performed. Nurses use physical exams to monitor changes in patient conditions over time, ensuring timely adjustments to the care plan.

Preparation and Environment Setup

Conducting an effective physical examination demands careful preparation of both the physical space and the patient. A proper examination environment must ensure privacy, adequate lighting, and a comfortable room temperature to prevent shivering or distress.

  • Lighting: Direct natural light or adjustable exam lamps are essential for observing subtle skin color changes.
  • Privacy: Use curtains, screens, or private rooms to protect patient dignity and encourage open communication.
  • Equipment: Gather all necessary tools—such as stethoscopes, penlights, and sphygmomanometers—before starting to avoid interrupting the workflow.

Standard Precautions and Infection Control

Infection control protects both the patient and the healthcare provider during physical contact. Standard precautions must be applied to every patient encounter, regardless of their suspected or confirmed infectious status.

Hand hygiene remains the single most effective way to prevent the spread of microorganisms. Nurses must perform hand hygiene before and after touching a patient, after removing gloves, and after touching equipment near the bed. Personal protective equipment, such as gloves, gowns, and masks, should be worn whenever there is a risk of exposure to blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin.

Core Techniques: Inspection, Palpation, Percussion, and Auscultation

Physical assessment relies on four foundational techniques performed in a specific sequence, except during an abdominal exam where auscultation precedes palpation to avoid altering bowel sounds.

  1. Inspection: Visual scrutiny of the body, noting symmetry, color, and movement.
  2. Palpation: Using the sense of touch to evaluate texture, temperature, moisture, and tenderness.
  3. Percussion: Tapping body parts to produce sound waves that reveal the density of underlying structures.
  4. Auscultation: Listening to sounds produced by the body, typically using a stethoscope.

General Survey and Vital Signs

The general survey begins the moment the nurse meets the patient, capturing overall physical appearance, body structure, mobility, and behavior. It provides an immediate snapshot of the patient's distress level, nutritional status, and mental state before organ-specific assessments begin.

Vital signs—temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure, and pain assessment—quantify the body's baseline physiological functions. Abnormal vital sign readings often serve as the earliest warning indicators of clinical deterioration, prompting urgent nursing evaluation and intervention.

System-Specific Physical Examinations

System-specific examinations target individual body systems using tailored inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation strategies. For instance, respiratory assessments focus on chest expansion, breath sounds, and symmetry, while cardiovascular evaluations involve locating apical pulses and identifying heart murmurs.

A structured, head-to-toe or body-system approach ensures no critical area is overlooked. Nurses adapt their examination depth based on the patient's chief complaint, acuity level, and age.

Documentation and Reporting of Findings

Accurate documentation converts physical examination findings into a permanent, legal record of patient care. Observations must be recorded promptly, objectively, and without personal bias or vague terminology.

Instead of writing "patient looks normal," the nurse documents specific, measurable data such as "alert and oriented x4, skin pink and warm, clear breath sounds bilaterally." Clear documentation facilitates effective communication among interprofessional healthcare team members, ensuring continuity of care and patient safety. Any critical or unexpected findings must be reported immediately to the attending physician or senior nurse.
