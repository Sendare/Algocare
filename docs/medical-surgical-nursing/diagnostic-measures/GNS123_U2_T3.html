Physical Examination

Introduction and Purpose of Physical Examination

Physical examination is a fundamental clinical skill utilized by nurses to gather objective data regarding a patient's health status. The primary purpose of conducting a systematic physical assessment is to obtain baseline data, identify actual or potential health problems, evaluate the effectiveness of nursing and medical interventions, and formulate appropriate nursing diagnoses. In the Nigerian healthcare context, where resources may sometimes be constrained, a thorough and structured physical examination becomes an even more invaluable diagnostic tool that complements patient history taking. Nurses use their senses of sight, hearing, touch, and smell to detect normal anatomical findings as well as pathological deviations from health. Furthermore, conducting a physical examination fosters a therapeutic nurse-patient relationship, offering an opportunity to educate patients about their bodies and promote healthy lifestyle behaviors. It bridges the gap between subjective complaints reported during the health history interview and measurable, clinical evidence. Mastery of physical examination techniques empowers nursing students to practice safely, make sound clinical judgments, and collaborate effectively with other members of the healthcare team to ensure optimal patient outcomes across diverse clinical settings, including community health centers, primary healthcare clinics, and tertiary hospitals.

Preparation and Environment Setup

Proper preparation and environment setup are essential prerequisites for conducting a reliable, comfortable, and ethical physical examination. Before initiating the assessment, the nurse must ensure the physical environment is warm, well-lit, quiet, and private to protect patient dignity and encourage open communication. Adequate lighting is crucial for accurately observing skin color, rashes, and subtle physical abnormalities. Controlling environmental noise ensures that the nurse can clearly hear bodily sounds during auscultation, such as heart and bowel sounds. Psychological preparation is equally vital; the nurse must introduce themselves, explain each procedure clearly in a language the patient understands, and obtain informed consent to reduce patient anxiety and build trust. Equipment must be gathered, checked, and organized beforehand—including items such as a stethoscope, sphygmomanometer, thermometer, flashlight, and tape measure—to prevent unnecessary interruptions during the examination. Positioning the patient correctly according to the specific body system being examined ensures comfort and optimizes visualization and palpation. For instance, ensuring an empty bladder before abdominal palpation enhances patient comfort and accuracy of findings. Paying meticulous attention to these preparatory steps demonstrates professional competence and respects the patient's physical and emotional boundaries.

Standard Precautions and Infection Control

Standard precautions and infection control are paramount in clinical practice to prevent the transmission of healthcare-associated infections between patients, healthcare workers, and the environment. Standard precautions apply to all patients receiving care, regardless of their diagnosed or suspected infection status. Hand hygiene remains the single most effective intervention for preventing infection spread; nurses must perform hand hygiene before and after direct patient contact, after removing gloves, and after touching potentially contaminated equipment or body fluids. Personal protective equipment (PPE)—such as gloves, gowns, masks, and eye protection—must be selected and worn based on the anticipated exposure risk during the physical examination. For instance, gloves are mandatory whenever the nurse anticipates touching non-intact skin, mucous membranes, blood, body fluids, or contaminated medical instruments. Additionally, proper decontamination of reusable examination tools, such as stethoscopes and otoscope specula, using approved antiseptic wipes between every patient use is non-negotiable. Safe injection practices and proper disposal of sharps into puncture-resistant safety boxes are critical components taught in Nigerian nursing curricula to protect against bloodborne pathogens like Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and HIV. Upholding these stringent infection control protocols safeguards public health and maintains professional nursing standards.

Techniques of Physical Assessment: Inspection, Palpation, Percussion, and Auscultation

Physical assessment relies on four core fundamental techniques performed in a specific sequence, except during an abdominal examination: inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. Inspection involves close, deliberate visual observation of the patient's body, noting size, shape, symmetry, color, and movement, and must be done systematically without rushing. Palpation utilizes the sense of touch through the hands and fingers to evaluate texture, temperature, moisture, swelling, pulsation, tenderness, and masses; light palpation precedes deep palpation to avoid eliciting severe pain or guarding. Percussion involves tapping the body surface with the fingertips to produce vibrations and sound waves, which help determine the density, size, and borders of underlying organs, distinguishing between air-filled, fluid-filled, or solid structures. Auscultation is the act of listening to sounds produced within the body, primarily using a stethoscope, to evaluate the heart, lungs, and abdomen for characteristics such as frequency, intensity, quality, and duration. Notably, when assessing the abdomen, the sequence changes to inspection, auscultation, percussion, and palpation; this ensures that palpation or percussion does not alter or stimulate bowel motility, which would falsely distort auscultation findings of bowel sounds. Mastery of these four sequential techniques provides a robust foundation for clinical data collection.

General Survey and Vital Signs Measurement

The general survey and vital signs measurement represent the initial component of the physical examination, providing an immediate snapshot of the patient's overall health and physiological stability. The general survey begins the moment the nurse encounters the patient, involving systematic observation of general appearance, hygiene, nutritional status, body habitus, posture, gait, level of consciousness, and emotional state or affect. Distress or acute pain should be identified immediately. Following the general survey, the nurse measures the vital signs: body temperature, pulse rate and rhythm, respiratory rate and depth, blood pressure, and pain assessment, often referred to as the fifth vital sign. Each vital sign offers crucial insight into autonomic nervous system function and homeostatic balance. For example, a change in heart rate or blood pressure may indicate early hypovolemic shock, pain, or anxiety. Nurses must use accurate, properly calibrated equipment—such as a stethoscope, sphygmomanometer, and thermometer—and document findings immediately. Recognizing abnormal trends in vital signs allows nurses to initiate timely, life-saving interventions and report critical deviations to the attending physician or primary healthcare provider without delay.

System-Specific Physical Examinations

System-specific physical examinations involve targeted, detailed assessments of individual body systems to uncover specific pathological processes. These include the cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, neurological, musculoskeletal, integumentary, and genitourinary systems. Each system requires specialized knowledge of anatomy, physiology, and specific assessment maneuvers. For instance, the respiratory examination entails inspecting chest symmetry, palpating for tactile fremitus, percussing lung fields for resonance or dullness, and auscultating breath sounds like vesicular, bronchial, or adventitious sounds such as crackles and wheezes. The neurological assessment evaluates mental status, cranial nerve function, deep tendon reflexes, sensory function, and cerebellar coordination. In cardiovascular assessments, the nurse locates anatomical landmarks like the apical impulse (point of maximal impulse) and auscultates heart valves (Aortic, Pulmonic, Tricuspid, Mitral) to detect normal S1 and S2 sounds or abnormal murmurs and gallops. Musculoskeletal exams focus on joint range of motion, muscle strength grading, and spinal alignment. By systematically moving through each body system, nurses gather comprehensive data that helps differentiate normal variants from abnormal pathological conditions, thereby guiding individualized nursing care plans and medical management.

Documentation and Reporting of Findings

Accurate documentation and reporting of physical examination findings are legal, professional, and ethical responsibilities of the nurse. Documentation provides a permanent, chronological record of the patient's health status, clinical progress, and nursing care provided. All findings must be recorded clearly, objectively, and promptly following the assessment, using accepted medical terminology and standard abbreviations without making vague or judgmental statements. For example, instead of writing 'patient looks anxious and weak,' the nurse should document specific, observable data such as 'patient expresses feeling nervous, walks with a slow, unsteady gait, and has a blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg.' Documentation can be structured using formats such as SOAP (Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan) or PIE (Problem, Intervention, Evaluation), depending on the healthcare institution's policy. Beyond written records, effective verbal reporting—such as during handovers or shift changes—ensures continuity of care and highlights urgent or critical findings that require immediate intervention. In Nigerian clinical settings, adherence to proper documentation standards protects the nurse against legal liability and guarantees that all multidisciplinary team members have access to reliable data for ongoing patient management.
