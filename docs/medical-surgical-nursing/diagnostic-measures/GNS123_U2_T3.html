Physical Examination

Introduction and Purpose of Physical Examination

A physical examination is a systematic data-gathering method used by nurses and other healthcare professionals to evaluate a patient's health status. It forms the objective foundation of the nursing process, balancing the subjective history provided by the patient with concrete, observable findings. The main goals include obtaining baseline data, identifying potential health problems, and evaluating the effectiveness of nursing or medical interventions. Nurses rely on these findings to make sound clinical judgments and tailor care plans to individual needs.

Preparation and Environment Setup

Before touching a patient, the nurse must prepare both the physical space and the patient to ensure comfort, accuracy, and privacy.

  • Environment: Ensure a warm, well-lit, and quiet room. Privacy is vital, so pull curtains or close doors.
  • Equipment: Gather all necessary tools—such as a stethoscope, penlight, and sphygmomanometer—and keep them within easy reach.
  • Patient Preparation: Explain every step of the procedure clearly to reduce anxiety, ask the patient to empty their bladder if needed, and position them comfortably.

Standard Precautions and Infection Control

Infection control is critical during any physical examination to protect both the patient and the healthcare provider. Standard precautions must be applied to all patients regardless of their diagnosis.

  • Hand Hygiene: Wash hands with soap and water or use an alcohol-based rub before and after every patient contact.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: Wear gloves when touching blood, body fluids, mucous membranes, or non-intact skin.
  • Equipment Sanitation: Clean stethoscope diaphragms and other reusable tools with antiseptic wipes between patients to prevent cross-contamination.

Core Techniques: Inspection, Palpation, Percussion, and Auscultation

Physical assessment relies on four foundational techniques, usually performed in a specific sequence.

TechniqueDescriptionPrimary Use
InspectionConcentrated visual observationChecking skin color, size, and symmetry
PalpationUsing sense of touch to feel texture and tendernessAssessing lumps, pain, or organ size
PercussionTapping body parts to produce sound wavesDetermining organ density and borders
AuscultationListening to body sounds with a stethoscopeEvaluating heart, lung, and bowel sounds

*Note*: For abdominal assessments, the standard order changes to inspection, auscultation, percussion, and palpation to avoid altering bowel motility.

General Survey and Vital Signs

The general survey begins the moment the nurse meets the patient, capturing overall appearance, behavior, and mobility. Following this initial scan, the nurse measures vital signs: temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure, and often oxygen saturation and pain level. These numbers provide immediate clues about physiological stability and help direct the rest of the physical examination.

System-Specific Physical Examinations

After the general survey and vital signs, the nurse conducts focused system examinations. These include the cardiovascular system (listening for heart murmurs and checking peripheral pulses), respiratory system (checking breath sounds and chest expansion), neurological system (testing reflexes, orientation, and cranial nerves), and musculoskeletal system (assessing range of motion and muscle strength). Each system requires specific tools and targeted techniques.

Documentation and Reporting of Findings

Accurate documentation completes the physical examination process. Findings must be recorded promptly, objectively, and clearly in the patient's medical record. Avoid vague words like 'normal' or 'fine'; instead, describe exact measurements, locations, and characteristics. Any critical or unexpected finding—such as a sudden drop in blood pressure or severe respiratory distress—must be reported immediately to the attending physician or charge nurse.
