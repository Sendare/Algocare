Blood Groups and Cross Matching

Introduction to Blood Groups and Antigens

Blood grouping relies on the presence or absence of specific inherited chemical structures on the surface of red blood cells. These structures, known as antigens (or agglutinogens), act as identification markers for the immune system. When the body encounters foreign antigens through transfusion or pregnancy, it mounts an immune response by producing matching antibodies (agglutinins) in the plasma. This immunological recognition forms the biological foundation of safe blood transfusion practice. In clinical settings across Nigeria, laboratory scientists use these antigen-antibody reactions to determine compatibility before any blood product reaches a recipient.

The ABO Blood Group System

Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO system, which classifies human blood into four main phenotypes based on the presence of A antigens, B antigens, both, or neither. The plasma naturally contains antibodies against the antigens absent from the red blood cells.

  • Group A: Has A antigens on red blood cells and anti-B antibodies in the plasma.
  • Group B: Has B antigens on red blood cells and anti-A antibodies in the plasma.
  • Group AB: Has both A and B antigens on red blood cells and no ABO antibodies in the plasma.
  • Group O: Has neither A nor B antigens on red blood cells but contains both anti-A and anti-B antibodies in the plasma.

Because Group O red cells lack major antigens, individuals with this blood type are historically termed universal red cell donors. Conversely, people with Group AB are considered universal plasma donors because their plasma lacks anti-A and anti-B antibodies.

The Rhesus (Rh) Factor and Its Clinical Significance

The Rhesus system is the second most important blood group system in clinical practice. The most critical antigen in this system is the D antigen. Individuals who express the D antigen on their red blood cells are classified as Rh-positive, while those lacking it are Rh-negative. Unlike the ABO system, antibodies to the Rh factor do not form naturally. Instead, an Rh-negative person only produces anti-D antibodies after direct exposure to Rh-positive blood, either through a mismatched transfusion or during childbirth.

This immunological mechanism underpins Hemolytic Disease of the Newborn (HDON). When an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus, fetal red blood cells can cross into the maternal circulation during delivery or trauma. The mother's immune system sensitizes and produces anti-D IgG antibodies. During a subsequent pregnancy with another Rh-positive fetus, these maternal antibodies cross the placenta, hemolyzing fetal red blood cells and causing severe anemia, jaundice, and hydrops fetalis. Routine administration of Rh immunoglobulin (RhoGAM) to un-sensitized Rh-negative mothers prevents this immune sensitization.

Principles of Blood Typing and Compatibility

Blood typing involves mixing a patient's red blood cell suspension with known commercial antisera containing anti-A, anti-B, and anti-D antibodies. If clumps form—a process called agglutination—the corresponding antigen is present on the cells. For instance, if agglutination occurs only with anti-A serum and anti-D serum, the blood type is A positive.

Compatibility rules dictate that a recipient must not possess plasma antibodies that match the antigens on the donor's red blood cells. Infusing unmatched blood leads to an acute immune attack where recipient antibodies bind to donor red cells, causing hemolysis and potentially fatal renal failure. Careful verification of both forward grouping (testing cells) and reverse grouping (testing serum with known cells) ensures laboratory accuracy before blood leaves the blood bank.

Purpose and Procedure of Blood Cross-Matching

Cross-matching is the final pre-transfusion safety check performed in the blood bank. It physically tests the compatibility between the donor and the recipient before blood is released for infusion. The procedure involves mixing the recipient's serum with donor red blood cells to detect any unexpected antibodies that might cause a transfusion reaction, even if the ABO and Rh types match.

A complete cross-match consists of immediate spin phases and incubation phases using enhancement media. A compatible cross-match shows no agglutination or hemolysis, indicating it is safe to transfuse. An incompatible result means antibodies in the patient's serum are reacting with donor antigens, and that unit must be rejected immediately. In emergency situations where hemorrhagic shock leaves no time for a full cross-match, uncrossmatched Group O negative (or O positive for males and post-menopausal females) red blood cells are issued under strict medical protocol.

Major and Minor Cross-Matching Techniques

Traditional cross-matching techniques are divided into major and minor components to evaluate different immunological risks in whole blood components.

  • Major Cross-Match: Tests recipient serum against donor red blood cells. This is the most crucial test because antibodies in the recipient's plasma can rapidly destroy the newly infused donor red blood cells, causing a life-threatening acute hemolytic reaction.
  • Minor Cross-Match: Tests donor serum against recipient red blood cells. This detects any antibodies present in the small amount of donor plasma remaining in a unit of packed cells or whole blood that might attack the patient's own red cells.

Modern blood banking practices that utilize packed red blood cells with minimal residual plasma have largely replaced traditional minor cross-matching with sensitive antibody screening of all donor units. Nonetheless, understanding both principles remains vital for safe transfusion science.

Nursing Responsibilities During Blood Administration

Administering blood products demands rigorous clinical diligence because clerical errors and procedural shortcuts can lead to fatal hemolytic reactions. The nurse must verify that the informed consent is signed and that patent intravenous access (usually an 18-gauge or 20-gauge cannula) is secured using normal saline. Dextrose solutions must never be used because they cause red blood cell hemolysis.

Before starting the transfusion, a two-person bedside check is mandatory. Two qualified healthcare professionals must independently verify the patient's name and hospital number against the blood bank compatibility tag, the transfusion request form, and the unit label. The nurse records baseline vital signs immediately before starting, stays with the patient for the first 15 minutes (as most severe acute reactions occur during the infusion of the first 50 mL), and monitors vital signs periodically until completion. Blood administration must be completed within four hours from the time it leaves the blood bank refrigerator to prevent bacterial proliferation.

Complications of Blood Transfusion and Management

Blood transfusions carry inherent risks that require immediate recognition and skilled nursing intervention. Complications range from mild allergic responses to catastrophic acute reactions.

  • Acute Hemolytic Reaction: Caused by ABO incompatibility. Symptoms include fever, chills, lumbar pain, chest tightness, and hypotension. The nurse must stop the transfusion immediately, keep the IV line open with normal saline using a new tubing set, notify the physician and blood bank, and send blood samples and urine for analysis.
  • Febrile Non-Hemolytic Reaction: Caused by recipient antibodies reacting against donor white blood cells or platelets. It presents with fever and chills without hemolysis; managed by slowing the infusion and administering antipyretics.
  • Allergic/Anaphylactic Reaction: Caused by donor plasma proteins reacting with IgE antibodies. Manifests as urticaria, pruritus, wheezing, and anaphylaxis.
  • Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO): Occurs when blood is infused too rapidly, leading to pulmonary edema, hypertension, and dyspnea. Managed by positioning the patient upright, administering oxygen, and giving diuretics.
  • Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI): Characterized by non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema, severe hypoxemia, and respiratory failure caused by donor antibodies activating recipient neutrophils in the lungs.
