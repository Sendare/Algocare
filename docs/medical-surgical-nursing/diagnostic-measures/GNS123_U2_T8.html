Blood Groups and Cross Matching

Introduction to Blood Groups and Antigens

Blood typing is a vital process in healthcare that prevents fatal immune reactions during blood transfusions. At the center of this system are antigens and antibodies. Antigens are specific protein or carbohydrate molecules located on the surface of red blood cells. They act like biological name tags that tell the immune system whether a cell belongs in the body or is a foreign intruder. Naturally occurring antibodies float in the liquid part of the blood, the plasma. These antibodies actively search for and attack any red blood cells carrying unfamiliar antigens. If a patient receives blood with antigens their body recognizes as foreign, their antibodies will bind to the donor cells, causing them to clump together in a dangerous process called agglutination. Understanding these basic markers is the first step to safe blood transfusion practice.

The ABO Blood Group System

The ABO blood group system classifies human blood into four main types based on the presence or absence of two specific antigens, designated as antigen A and antigen B.

  • Type A: Red blood cells carry A antigens, and the plasma contains anti-B antibodies.
  • Type B: Red blood cells carry B antigens, and the plasma contains anti-A antibodies.
  • Type AB: Red blood cells carry both A and B antigens, and the plasma contains no ABO antibodies.
  • Type O: Red blood cells carry neither A nor B antigens, but the plasma contains both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.

Because Type AB blood lacks antibodies against A and B, individuals with this type can theoretically receive red blood cells from any ABO group, making them universal red cell recipients. Conversely, Type O red blood cells lack both major antigens, meaning they rarely trigger an immune response in other groups, making Type O individuals universal red cell donors.

The Rhesus (Rh) Factor and Clinical Significance

Beyond the ABO system, the Rhesus (Rh) factor is another critical protein marker found on red blood cells. A person is classified as Rh-positive if they carry the D antigen on their red blood cells, or Rh-negative if they do not. Unlike the ABO system, humans do not automatically form antibodies against the Rh factor unless they have direct exposure to Rh-positive blood.

This becomes clinically significant during pregnancy. If an Rh-negative mother carries an Rh-positive fetus, fetal red blood cells can cross into the maternal circulation during delivery or trauma. The mother's immune system recognizes the Rh antigen as foreign and produces anti-Rh antibodies. In a subsequent pregnancy with another Rh-positive fetus, these maternal antibodies can cross the placenta and destroy the fetal red blood cells, causing hemolytic disease of the newborn. Routine administration of Rho(D) immune globulin prevents this sensitization process in vulnerable mothers.

Principles of Blood Typing and Compatibility

Blood typing involves mixing a patient's blood sample with known laboratory antibodies to see how it reacts. If the blood clumps when mixed with anti-A serum, it means A antigens are present on the cells. Safe transfusion requires matching both ABO and Rh types to prevent severe acute hemolytic reactions. Giving incompatible blood triggers a massive immune attack where red blood cells burst, releasing free hemoglobin into the bloodstream. This can clog the kidneys, cause low blood pressure, and lead to organ failure. Nurses must double-check laboratory reports and patient wristbands to ensure total compatibility before any blood product reaches the bedside.

Purpose and Procedure of Blood Cross-Matching

Blood cross-matching is the final safety check performed in the blood bank before transfusion. While blood typing identifies broad ABO and Rh categories, cross-matching looks for hidden incompatibilities caused by minor blood group antigens.

The procedure involves two main phases:

  1. Major cross-match: Mixing the recipient's serum with the donor's red blood cells to ensure the patient's antibodies will not attack the donated tissue.
  2. Minor cross-match: Mixing the donor's serum with the recipient's red blood cells to check for adverse reactions from donor antibodies.

If agglutination occurs in either test tube, the blood is incompatible and must never be given to that patient. This careful laboratory screening protects patients from hidden immune reactions that basic blood typing might miss.

Nursing Responsibilities During Blood Administration

Administering blood products requires strict adherence to safety protocols to protect the patient from harm. Before the blood leaves the blood bank, the nurse must ensure that an informed consent form is signed and that an intravenous line with a large-bore needle is secured. When the blood unit arrives, two licensed nurses must perform a bedside verification check. They must compare the patient's identification band, hospital number, and blood bag label against the blood bank compatibility report.

Key safety steps during administration include:

  • Using only 0.9% sodium chloride (normal saline) to prime tubing and flush lines.
  • Staying with the patient for the first 15 minutes of the infusion, as most severe acute reactions happen quickly.
  • Monitoring vital signs before starting, 15 minutes after starting, and periodically until completion.
  • Completing the transfusion within a maximum of four hours to prevent bacterial growth in the blood bag.

Complications of Blood Transfusion and Management

Blood transfusions can sometimes trigger adverse reactions ranging from mild rashes to life-threatening emergencies. A hemolytic transfusion reaction occurs when mismatched blood is infused, causing fever, chills, chest pain, and lower back pain. Allergic reactions present with hives, itching, and wheezing. Circulatory overload happens when blood is infused too quickly, leading to shortness of breath, high blood pressure, and crackles in the lungs, especially in elderly or frail patients.

If a transfusion reaction is suspected, the nurse must act immediately:

  1. Stop the transfusion at once.
  2. Disconnect the blood tubing while leaving the IV line open with normal saline.
  3. Notify the attending physician and blood bank immediately.
  4. Recheck all labels, ID bands, and paperwork.
  5. Send the blood bag and a fresh urine sample to the laboratory for evaluation.
