X-Ray

Introduction to X-Ray Imaging and Nursing Responsibilities

X-rays are a form of electromagnetic radiation used to visualize internal structures of the body. As a diagnostic tool, they help identify fractures, infections, fluid accumulation, and foreign bodies. Nursing involvement spans preparation, positioning assistance, safety enforcement, and post-imaging assessment. Understanding the basics of diagnostic imaging enables clinicians to collaborate effectively with radiographers and physicians, ensuring patient safety and diagnostic clarity in acute and chronic care settings across Nigeria.

Principles of Radiation Physics and Safety

X-ray machines emit ionizing radiation that passes through the body, being absorbed differently by various tissues. Dense structures like bone absorb more radiation and appear white (radiopaque), while air-filled spaces appear black (radiolucent). Soft tissues show up in shades of grey. Radiation safety relies on three core principles: - **Time**: Minimize the duration of exposure. - **Distance**: Maximize the physical gap between the radiation source and personnel (Inverse Square Law). - **Shielding**: Utilize lead aprons, thyroid collars, and protective barriers to block stray scatter radiation.

Clinical Indications and Common Uses

Diagnostic radiography is requested to investigate diverse pathological conditions. In emergency departments and general wards, X-rays evaluate respiratory distress, trauma, abdominal pain, and musculoskeletal injuries. - **Chest X-Ray (CXR)**: Assesses for pneumonia, heart failure, pneumothorax, and pleural effusion. - **Abdominal X-Ray (AXR)**: Identifies bowel obstruction, free air, renal calculi, and foreign bodies. - **Musculoskeletal X-Ray**: Detects bone fractures, joint dislocations, osteomyelitis, and degenerative arthritis.

Patient Preparation and Assessment

Pre-procedural nursing assessment ensures that the patient is physically and psychologically ready for the imaging procedure. The Nursing Role During and After the Procedure

During mobile or bedside radiography, the nurse helps maintain sterile fields, supports fragile patients into proper alignment, and ensures visitors step away behind protective screens. After the imaging procedure, the nurse repositions the patient for comfort, ensures lines and drains are secure, and documents the completion of the diagnostic test. Effective communication during this phase prevents delays in medical diagnosis and treatment initiation.

Special Considerations and Nigerian Healthcare Challenges

Conducting diagnostic imaging in resource-constrained environments presents distinct challenges. Frequent power outages necessitate reliance on reliable backup generators or battery-powered portable units. Equipment maintenance backlogs can lead to suboptimal image quality, requiring higher repeat rates and increased patient exposure. Furthermore, patient financial constraints in out-of-pocket payment systems often delay diagnostic investigations, necessitating nurse advocacy to coordinate affordable care pathways.
