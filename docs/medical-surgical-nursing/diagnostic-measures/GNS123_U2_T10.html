X-Ray

Introduction to X-Ray Imaging in Nursing

X-ray imaging remains one of the foundational diagnostic tools utilized in modern healthcare settings, including hospitals across Nigeria. As student nurses, understanding the basics of radiography is crucial because you will frequently coordinate patient care, prepare individuals for examinations, and collaborate closely with radiographers and physicians. Developed by Wilhelm Conrad Roentgen in 1895, X-rays are a form of electromagnetic radiation that can penetrate human tissues to create two-dimensional images of internal anatomical structures. Dense tissues, such as bones, absorb more radiation and appear white on the developed film or digital monitor, while less dense structures, such as air-filled lungs, allow radiation to pass through and appear black. Soft tissues appear in various shades of grey. In nursing practice, recognizing how these images are formed enables you to better explain the procedure to anxious patients, reduce their fears, and ensure compliance with pre-test instructions. Furthermore, basic knowledge of X-ray imaging aids in identifying obvious gross abnormalities when reviewing charts, fostering interdisciplinary communication and enhancing overall patient safety and outcomes.

Principles of X-Ray Physics and Radiation Safety

Radiation safety is a paramount concern for nursing professionals working in diagnostic imaging departments. X-rays utilize ionizing radiation, which possesses enough energy to remove tightly bound electrons from atoms, potentially causing cellular or DNA damage if exposures are excessive or uncontrolled. To protect both patients and healthcare personnel, the fundamental principles of radiation protection must be strictly applied: Time, Distance, and Shielding. Minimizing the time of exposure, maximizing the distance from the radiation source, and utilizing appropriate physical shielding—such as lead aprons, thyroid collars, and gonadal shields—drastically reduce cumulative occupational and patient exposure. Nurses accompanying patients to the radiology suite must wear designated personal protective equipment if they are required to stay in the room to support a confused or pediatric patient. Additionally, female patients of childbearing age must always be assessed for potential pregnancy before undergoing an X-ray to protect the developing fetus from teratogenic risks. Understanding these physics principles ensures that the diagnostic benefits of the imaging procedure consistently outweigh any potential biological risks.

Indications and Clinical Uses of X-Rays

X-rays are ordered across various clinical departments for diverse diagnostic and monitoring purposes. In emergency settings, medical-surgical wards, and outpatient clinics in Nigeria, chest X-rays (CXRs) are frequently indicated to evaluate respiratory complaints such as persistent cough, dyspnoea, chest pain, and suspected pneumonia, tuberculosis, or cardiomegaly. Abdominal X-rays, often referred to as plain abdominal radiographs or KUB (Kidneys, Ureters, and Bladder), are utilized to investigate acute abdominal pain, bowel obstruction, perforation, or to locate radiopaque foreign bodies and renal calculi. Musculoskeletal X-rays are crucial for assessing traumatic injuries, fractures, joint dislocations, and chronic degenerative conditions like osteoarthritis. Furthermore, orthopedic surgeons and general practitioners rely on serial X-rays to monitor the healing progress of bone fractures following reduction or surgical fixation. By understanding these clinical indications, nursing students can better anticipate physician orders, correlate physical assessment findings with imaging requests, and provide targeted patient education regarding why a specific radiological test has been prescribed.

Patient Preparation and Pre-Procedural Assessment

Proper patient preparation and pre-procedural assessment are essential nursing responsibilities that ensure diagnostic image quality and patient safety. Before sending a patient for an X-ray, the nurse must verify the clinical order against the patient's medical chart and correctly identify the patient using at least two identifiers. Assessment must include checking for pregnancy status in females of childbearing potential, as ionizing radiation poses significant risks to embryonic and fetal development. The nurse must instruct the patient to remove all radiopaque items that could obscure anatomical structures or mimic pathology on the film, including jewelry, metal buttons, zippers, belts, dentures, and metallic medical devices. Patient education should be provided in a clear, reassuring manner, explaining what sensations to expect (such as the cold room or the need to hold one's breath for a chest X-ray) to reduce anxiety and promote cooperation. For specialized contrast studies, specific fasting or bowel preparation instructions must be given and confirmed. Thorough preparation minimizes the need for repeat exposures, thereby reducing overall radiation dose to the patient.

The Nursing Role During the X-Ray Procedure

During the actual X-ray procedure, the nurse plays an active role in patient advocacy, positioning support, and maintaining safety standards. Whether the procedure takes place in the main radiology department or at the bedside using a mobile X-ray machine, the nurse ensures that the correct patient is matched with the correct anatomical requisition. If the patient is unstable, unconscious, or uncooperative, the nurse may need to assist the radiographer with safe positioning while strictly adhering to radiation safety protocols by wearing a lead apron and thyroid shield. For bedridden patients in Nigerian hospital wards, the nurse helps position film cassettes correctly beneath the patient and ensures lines, tubes, and drains are secured and kept out of the field of view unless they are specifically being evaluated. Clear communication is vital; the nurse often reinforces the radiographer's instructions, such as telling the patient to take a deep breath and hold still for a chest radiograph. Monitoring the patient's vital signs and emotional well-being throughout the procedure ensures immediate intervention should any clinical deterioration occur.

Post-Procedure Care and Radiation Protection Measures

Post-procedure care following a standard diagnostic X-ray is typically straightforward because basic radiography is non-invasive and requires no recovery time. Once the imaging session is complete, the nurse assists the patient in dressing comfortably, ensures their personal belongings are collected, and safely escorts them back to their ward or discharge area. If contrast media was administered for a specialized gastrointestinal or renal study, post-procedure care includes monitoring for allergic reactions, encouraging increased fluid intake to promote renal clearance of the contrast agent, and documenting bowel elimination patterns. The nurse should provide ongoing reassurance and answer any questions regarding when the results will be reviewed by the primary physician. From a radiation protection standpoint, documentation of the procedure in the patient's nursing notes should include the date, time, anatomical region examined, and any specific safety measures utilized. This complete documentation supports multidisciplinary continuity of care and maintains an accurate lifetime radiation exposure record for the patient.

Interpreting Common Chest and Abdominal X-Ray Findings

While formal interpretation of X-rays is the responsibility of radiologists and attending physicians, student nurses must develop basic visual literacy to identify major gross abnormalities during clinical practice. On a normal chest X-ray, the lungs appear dark (radiolucent) due to air content, the heart shadow is well-defined within the mediastinum, and the costophrenic angles are sharp and clear. Pathological findings on a chest X-ray may include pulmonary infiltrates or consolidation indicative of pneumonia, blunted costophrenic angles suggesting pleural effusion, or a visible pneumothorax showing absent lung markings and a collapsed lung edge. On an abdominal X-ray, abnormal findings can include dilated loops of bowel with multiple air-fluid levels indicating intestinal obstruction, or free air under the diaphragm indicating a gastrointestinal perforation. Recognizing these critical findings allows nurses to promptly report alarming changes in patient status to the medical team, facilitating rapid life-saving interventions in both emergency and ward settings across Nigerian healthcare facilities.

Complications, Risks, and Special Considerations in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Practising nursing and managing diagnostic imaging in Nigerian healthcare settings comes with unique challenges and considerations. Common complications associated with diagnostic X-rays are rare due to low radiation doses, but the stochastic risks of long-term cellular mutation and carcinogenesis remain if exposures are unjustified. Special considerations in resource-constrained environments include managing unreliable power supplies, which can affect digital radiography equipment, and dealing with occasional shortages of protective lead apparel or radiation badges for staff. Furthermore, cultural and religious beliefs among some Nigerian patient populations may cause apprehension regarding radiation exposure or modesty concerns during positioning, requiring nurses to provide sensitive, culturally competent communication and informed consent. Effective nursing leadership in these settings involves advocating for routine equipment maintenance, ensuring strict adherence to safety guidelines, and educating both staff and patients to optimize the diagnostic benefits of X-ray imaging while minimizing associated hazards.
