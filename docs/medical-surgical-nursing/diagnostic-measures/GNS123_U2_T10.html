X-Ray

Introduction to X-Ray Imaging and Nursing Relevance

X-ray imaging stands as one of the most foundational diagnostic tools in modern healthcare. Discovered by Wilhelm Röntgen, these electromagnetic waves pass through bodily tissues to create visual representations of internal structures. Dense structures like bone absorb more radiation and appear white on the final image, whereas air-filled spaces like lungs let radiation pass through and appear dark. Soft tissues show up in various shades of grey.

For nurses, understanding this imaging modality goes far beyond simply reading a radiologist's report. You coordinate patient care, ensure safety protocols are met, and prepare clients physically and emotionally. Many patients harbor deep-seated fears about radiation exposure or feel anxious about entering a cold, unfamiliar radiology suite. Your bedside explanation can turn fear into cooperation, making the diagnostic process smoother for everyone involved.

Principles of Radiation Safety and Protection

Radiation safety is a critical daily responsibility in clinical practice. The cornerstone of radiation protection rests on three basic concepts: time, distance, and shielding.

  • Time: Keep the duration of actual radiation exposure as short as possible.
  • Distance: Increase your physical space from the radiation source. Radiation intensity drops off sharply the further away you stand.
  • Shielding: Use lead aprons, thyroid collars, and mobile barriers to block secondary scatter radiation.

Students often fall into the misconception that stepping behind a standard glass window guarantees complete safety. In truth, structural lead shielding within the walls is what protects personnel stationed outside the control booth. Always respect warning lights outside radiology suites and ensure reproductive organs are shielded whenever it does not obscure the required diagnostic view.

Clinical Indications and Common Uses

Physicians order X-rays to investigate a vast array of clinical complaints. In emergency and medical-surgical wards, chest X-rays remain the gold standard for spotting pneumonia, heart failure, rib fractures, and collapsed lungs. Abdominal X-rays help detect bowel obstructions, free air from a perforated viscus, or large kidney stones.

  • Orthopedics: Identifying bone fractures, joint dislocations, and degenerative bone disease.
  • Chest: Diagnosing pulmonary congestion, consolidation, and cardiomegaly.
  • Abdomen: Locating radiopaque foreign bodies and checking for severe constipation or loops of blocked bowel.

When a patient presents with sudden shortness of breath or chest trauma, anticipating a portable chest X-ray order allows you to prepare the room and equipment before the medical team even arrives.

Patient Preparation and Pre-Procedural Assessment

Proper preparation ensures diagnostic accuracy and prevents unnecessary repeat exposures. Begin your assessment by verifying the clinical order against the patient's bedside chart. Ask female patients of childbearing age about their menstrual history and the possibility of pregnancy. Radiation poses serious teratogenic risks to a developing fetus, particularly during the first trimester.

Next, instruct the patient to remove all metallic objects near the area of interest, including necklaces, bras with underwires, zippers, belts, and hearing aids. Metal creates dense white artifacts on the film that can easily obscure a hidden fracture or lung lesion. Explain the positioning requirements simply, reassuring the client that the procedure is painless and brief.

The Nursing Role During and After the Procedure

Your presence during bedside or transport X-rays keeps the patient secure and aligned. If you accompany a client to the radiology department, bring their medical chart, ensure intravenous lines are secured with enough slack, and manage any ongoing oxygen therapy using portable cylinders.

During image capture, patients often need to hold their breath for a few seconds, particularly during chest radiography, to prevent motion blur. Coach the patient gently: "Take a deep breath, let it out, take another deep breath, and hold still." Once the radiographer confirms the images are clear and diagnostic, help the patient return to a comfortable position, tidy their bed linens, and document the completion of the procedure in the nursing notes.

Special Considerations in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

Practicing nursing in resource-constrained environments introduces unique challenges during diagnostic imaging. Frequent power fluctuations or total grid outages mean that rural clinics may rely on backup generators or portable battery-powered X-ray machines. Ensuring these units are charged and maintained is part of ward safety routines.

Additionally, high patient volumes can lead to long waiting times in crowded radiology waiting areas. Nurses must triage patients properly, ensuring that acutely unstable clients, such as those with severe trauma or respiratory distress, receive priority over stable outpatients. Effective communication with radiology staff helps bridge gaps when equipment undergoes unexpected maintenance or servicing.
