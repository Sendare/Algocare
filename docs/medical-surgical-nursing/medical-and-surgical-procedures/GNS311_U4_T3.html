Suturing of Wounds in Emergencies

Introduction and Assessment of Emergency Wounds

When a patient presents to the emergency room with a laceration, the nurse must first look at the big picture before staring at the cut. Emergency wound management focuses on stopping hemorrhage, preserving tissue function, and preventing infection. Assessment begins with evaluating the patient's overall stability using the primary survey—airway, breathing, and circulation—ensuring that life-threatening injuries take priority over a torn piece of skin.

Once the patient is stable, the nurse and doctor inspect the wound itself. This evaluation involves checking the depth of the injury, testing the distal motor, sensory, and vascular functions to ensure nerves and blood vessels are intact, and asking about the mechanism of injury. A wound caused by a clean kitchen knife requires a very different approach compared to a ragged tear from a dirty farm tool or a bite from an animal.

Infection Control and Local Anesthesia

Creating a sterile field is non-negotiable in emergency wound closure. The nurse prepares the instruments and sets up the tray using strict aseptic technique to keep bacteria out of the damaged tissue. Donning sterile gloves, masks, and gowns protects both the patient and the healthcare worker.

Before any needle touches the skin, pain must be managed. Lignocaine, commonly known as lidocaine, is the standard local anesthetic used. Sometimes adrenaline is mixed with the local anesthetic to constrict blood vessels, which reduces bleeding and makes the medication last longer. However, nurses must remember never to use adrenaline-containing local anesthetics on end-organs like fingers, toes, the nose, or the penis, as the severe blood vessel narrowing can lead to tissue death.

Wound Cleansing, Debridement, and Irrigation

  • Cleansing: Removing dirt and debris from the surrounding skin using mild soap or antiseptic solutions without getting harsh chemicals directly inside the open wound.
  • Irrigation: Flushing the wound cavity vigorously using normal saline via a syringe to lift away microscopic bacteria and loose foreign particles.
  • Debridement: Carefully trimming away dead, ragged, or non-viable tissue using sterile scissors so that healthy edges can knit back together cleanly.

Suturing Materials, Instruments, and Needle Selection

Choosing the correct tools makes the difference between a clean repair and a poor cosmetic outcome. Suturing materials generally fall into two categories: absorbable, which the body naturally breaks down over time, and non-absorbable, which must be removed manually after a few days. Non-absorbable sutures like nylon are ideal for skin closures where they can be taken out once the epidermis heals.

Needles also vary based on the tissue type. Cutting needles feature a sharp edge designed to slice easily through tough, thick skin, whereas round tapered needles are reserved for delicate underlying tissues like fat or muscle to avoid unnecessary tearing. The nurse lays out needle holders, thumb forceps, and suture scissors on the sterile tray, handing them securely to the clinician as requested.

Basic Suturing Techniques and Knot Tying

Putting the needle through the skin requires a steady wrist and precise angle. The needle enters the skin at a ninety-degree angle, following its natural curve through the tissue to match both sides evenly. The simple interrupted suture is the most common technique taught in emergency care; each stitch is placed, tied, and cut individually. If one stitch fails or gets infected, the rest of the wound stays secure.

Knot tying is just as critical as stitch placement. A proper surgical square knot prevents the suture from slipping or coming undone under tension. The nurse must watch that the knots are laid flat to the side of the wound edge rather than directly over the incision line, because pressure from a knot sitting on top of the cut can delay skin healing and leave unsightly marks.

Post-Procedure Care and Complications

Once the suturing is complete, protecting the fresh repair is the final step. The nurse cleans away dried blood, applies a sterile non-adherent dressing, and gives clear home-care instructions to the patient. Keeping the wound clean and dry for the first twenty-four to forty-eight hours significantly lowers the risk of bacterial invasion.

Complications can still arise days after the procedure. Nurses must teach patients to watch for red flags such as spreading redness, warmth, pus draining from the stitch lines, or worsening pain, which indicate infection. Furthermore, patients need to know the exact timeline for suture removal—ranging from three to five days for facial wounds to ten to fourteen days for wounds over joints—to prevent ugly scarring or stitch marks.
