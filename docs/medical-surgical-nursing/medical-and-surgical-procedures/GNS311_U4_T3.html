Suturing of Wounds in Emergencies

Introduction to Emergency Wound Management

Emergency wound management focuses on restoring tissue integrity, preventing infection, and minimizing scarring. When a patient presents to a Nigerian emergency department with a traumatic laceration, the primary clinical objective is rapid stabilization followed by systematic evaluation. Not all wounds require immediate primary closure; understanding the window of opportunity is critical for safe practice. The golden period for primary closure of a clean, uninfected wound is generally within six to eight hours of injury, though vascular structures on the face and scalp can often be closed safely up to twenty-four hours later due to their rich blood supply. Wounds presenting outside these timeframes, or those heavily contaminated with soil, feces, or saliva, carry a significantly elevated risk of deep tissue infection if closed primarily. In such cases, delayed primary closure or healing by secondary intention is preferred to trap pathogenic bacteria inside a closed dead space.

Patient Assessment and Wound Evaluation

A comprehensive patient assessment begins with evaluating the systemic status of the injured individual before inspecting the local trauma. Check airway, breathing, and circulation if the mechanism involves high-impact trauma. Once the patient is stable, examine the wound with adequate lighting and proper positioning. Document neurovascular status distal to the injury site—checking sensation, capillary refill, and motor function—before administering any local anesthetic. Failing to document nerve or tendon damage prior to local infiltration is a common medicolegal pitfall, as sensory deficits induced by the anesthetic can mask pre-existing nerve transections. Measure the exact length, depth, and orientation of the laceration, and inspect the base of the wound for foreign bodies such as glass shards, gravel, or metal fragments.

Infection Control and Local Anesthesia

Maintaining a sterile field prevents iatrogenic contamination during invasive emergency procedures. Practitioners must wear sterile gloves, a mask, and eye protection. Local anesthesia allows painless wound exploration and repair. - **Lidocaine**: The most commonly used agent with a rapid onset and intermediate duration of roughly one to two hours. - **Bupivacaine**: A longer-acting agent providing up to four to eight hours of analgesia, useful for extensive repairs. - **Adinephrine**: Frequently added to local anesthetics to cause vasoconstriction, which prolongs the anesthetic effect and reduces bleeding. *Clinical Caution*: Never use local anesthetics with epinephrine in end-arterial structures such as fingers, toes, the nose, penis, or ears, as severe vasoconstriction can precipitate ischemic necrosis.

Wound Cleansing, Debridement, and Irrigation

The single most effective intervention to prevent wound infection is thorough mechanical cleansing and irrigation. Normal saline is the standard irrigant because it is isotonic and does not damage exposed cellular matrices. Avoid harsh antiseptics like povidone-iodine tincture or hydrogen peroxide directly inside the open wound bed, as these substances are cytotoxic to healthy granulation tissue and impair leukocyte function. High-pressure irrigation using a syringe attached to an angiocatheter generates sufficient psi to dislodge adherent bacteria and particulate debris without driving contaminants deeper into the tissue planes. Debridement involves excising devitalized, non-viable, or ragged tissue margins that would otherwise serve as a medium for bacterial growth.

Suturing Materials, Instruments, and Needle Selection

Choosing the correct suture material and instruments dictates the cosmetic and structural outcome of wound repair. Suture materials are broadly categorized into absorbable and non-absorbable, as well as monofilament and multifilament configurations. | Suture Type | Common Materials | Typical Clinical Use | | --- | --- | --- | | Absorbable | Vicryl, Monocryl, Catgut | Deep dermal layers, mucosal closures, patients unable to return for suture removal | | Non-Absorbable | Nylon, Prolene, Silk | Skin closure requiring high tensile strength, scheduled for later removal | Needles also vary by shape and point geometry. Cutting needles are designed with a sharp edge to easily penetrate tough dermal tissue, whereas tapered needles pass smoothly through delicate, highly vascular tissues such as fascia or bowel without cutting through them.

Basic Suturing Techniques and Knot Tying

Mastering basic hand and instrument skills ensures proper wound edge eversion and tension distribution. The simple interrupted stitch is the workhorse of emergency laceration repair, providing secure closure where each loop is tied and cut independently. If one suture fails or becomes infected, adjacent sutures maintain wound integrity. Vertical and horizontal mattress stitches are particularly useful in high-tension areas or for achieving optimal skin edge eversion, preventing the edges from inverting into a depressed scar. When placing any stitch, enter the skin at a ninety-degree angle, pass through the epidermis and dermis, and match tissue depths symmetrically on both sides of the wound to eliminate dead space.

Post-Procedure Wound Care and Patient Instructions

Clear, written discharge instructions protect the integrity of the repair and reduce secondary complications. Instruct the patient to keep the wound clean and dry for the first twenty-four to forty-eight hours, after which gentle washing with soap and clean water is generally permitted. Advise patients to watch for red flags indicating infection, such as spreading erythema, purulent drainage, worsening pain, or fever. Suture removal timing depends heavily on the anatomical location due to varying rates of epithelialization and tension: - **Face**: Three to five days to minimize track marks. - **Scalp and Upper Extremities**: Seven to ten days. - **Trunk and Lower Extremities**: Ten to fourteen days, especially over joints where tension is high.

Complications of Emergency Wound Suturing

Complications can arise despite meticulous technique, and nurses must monitor for them during recovery. Wound dehiscence—the partial or total separation of previously approximated wound edges—usually results from premature suture removal, excessive tension, or underlying infection. Hematoma and seroma formation occur when blood or serous fluid collects in unclosed dead space beneath the skin, creating an ideal breeding ground for bacteria. Furthermore, tetanus is a severe, vaccine-preventable neurotoxin-mediated disease associated with contaminated puncture or laceration wounds; clinicians must assess the patient's immunization history and administer a tetanus toxoid booster, with or without tetanus immune globulin, based on the wound classification and vaccination status.
