Male Circumcision

Introduction and Definition of Male Circumcision

Male circumcision is the surgical removal of the prepuce, commonly known as the foreskin, which covers the glans penis. This practice holds deep historical, religious, cultural, and medical significance across various global populations, including widespread routine performance in many Nigerian communities for traditional or religious reasons. From a public health perspective, international health organizations endorse the procedure as a biomedical intervention for human immunodeficiency virus prevention in high-prevalence settings. Understanding the background of this procedure requires recognizing that motivations vary from ritualistic obligations in neonates to therapeutic management of pathological conditions in older males.

Anatomy and Physiology of the Male Genitalia

A firm grasp of penile anatomy is vital for safe surgical intervention and effective postoperative observation. The penis consists of three cylindrical columns of erectile tissue: two corpora cavernosa dorsolaterally and one corpus spongiosum ventrally, which encloses the urethra.

  • Glans Penis: The sensitive conical head of the penis, formed by the expanded distal end of the corpus spongiosum.
  • Corona: The projecting posterior border of the glans, possessing a rich network of nerve endings.
  • Frenulum: A sensitive fold of skin on the ventral aspect connecting the glans to the inner prepuce, housing a prominent blood vessel.
  • Prepuce: The retractable double-layered fold of skin that covers and protects the glans.

Indications and Contraindications

While cultural and religious preferences drive the vast majority of circumcisions, distinct clinical indications justify the procedure across various age groups. Therapeutic reasons include recurrent balanoposthitis, chronic urinary tract infections linked to abnormal foreskin anatomy, and scar tissue formation from trauma.

  • Phimosis: Inability to retract the foreskin over the glans penis, which can obstruct urine flow or cause painful erections.
  • Paraphimosis: A surgical emergency where a retracted foreskin becomes trapped behind the corona, leading to venous congestion and edema.
  • Bleeding Disorders: Conditions such as hemophilia represent absolute relative contraindications until clotting factors are optimized.
  • Hypospadias: A congenital anomaly of the urethral meatus where circumcision is contraindicated because the foreskin tissue may be needed for future reconstructive surgery.

Preoperative Preparation and Assessment

Thorough preoperative evaluation minimizes surgical risks and reassures parents or adult patients. The nursing team verifies informed consent, ensuring that parents understand potential risks, benefits, and alternative options for neonatal procedures. A baseline physical examination must check for anatomical anomalies, diaper dermatitis, or signs of systemic infection that could delay the procedure. Laboratory investigations, particularly bleeding profiles, are secured if there is any family history of coagulopathies. Fasting guidelines must be strictly communicated and confirmed prior to any procedure requiring sedation or general anesthesia in infants and children.

Surgical Techniques and Equipment

Several established surgical devices are utilized globally to achieve safe and cosmetically acceptable circumcision outcomes. The choice of technique often depends on the patient's age and the clinician's preference.

Intraoperative Nursing Responsibilities and Safety

During the surgical procedure, the circulating and scrub nurses maintain a sterile environment and ensure patient safety. Maintaining infant normothermia is critical because operating rooms are cool, and infants lose heat rapidly through exposed skin and immature thermoregulation; radiant warmers and warm blankets are standard adjuncts. The nurse assists with positioning, secures the infant safely to prevent sudden movements that could cause surgical trauma, and tracks all surgical sponges and instruments. Local anesthetic administration, such as a dorsal penile nerve block or ring block using lidocaine without epinephrine, requires vigilant monitoring for systemic toxicity symptoms like central nervous system changes.

Postoperative Nursing Care and Pain Management

Postoperative nursing vigilance centers on monitoring for hemorrhage, assessing pain, and evaluating urinary output. Nurses inspect the surgical site frequently for active oozing or hematoma formation, especially within the first few hours after the procedure. Effective analgesia involves multimodal approaches, combining paracetamol syrup with topical anesthetic creams or non-pharmacological comfort measures like sucrose pacifiers for infants. The nurse must confirm that the patient voids successfully within a reasonable timeframe post-procedure, as localized swelling or psychological reluctance can temporarily impede micturition.

Potential Complications and Emergency Management

Although generally safe when performed by trained personnel, male circumcision carries risks of acute and delayed complications. Immediate complications include excessive hemorrhage from the frenular artery, localized infection, and accidental injury to the glans or urethra. In cases where a Plastibell device is used, the ring can slip proximally onto the shaft, causing severe strangulation and edema. Nurses must recognize signs of excessive bleeding, apply gentle direct pressure, and notify the surgical team immediately. Long-term complications such as meatal stenosis, skin bridges, or unsatisfactory cosmetic results require specialized urological referral.

Patient Education and Discharge Instructions

Comprehensive discharge education empowers caregivers to manage home recovery and recognize warning signs requiring medical evaluation. Caregivers are instructed to keep the diaper area clean and dry, applying petroleum jelly liberally to prevent the healing wound from sticking to clothing or diapers. For Plastibell procedures, parents are taught that the ring typically falls off within five to ten days and should never be pulled off forcibly. Warning signs that necessitate prompt return to the health facility include persistent bright red bleeding, foul-smelling purulent discharge, spreading redness, fever, or the inability to pass urine.
