Male Circumcision

Introduction and Definition of Male Circumcision

Male circumcision is the surgical removal of the prepuce, commonly known as the foreskin, which covers the glans of the penis. This procedure is one of the oldest and most common surgical interventions globally, performed for a variety of cultural, religious, hygienic, and medical reasons. In many African communities, including Nigeria, it holds deep traditional and religious significance, often marking a rite of passage into adulthood or performed during infancy as a covenant or health practice.

From a public health perspective, voluntary medical male circumcision has gained significant recognition for its role in reducing the transmission of certain sexually transmitted infections, most notably HIV. Understanding the procedure requires looking beyond the surgical cut to appreciate the cultural and psychosocial context of the patient and family. As healthcare providers, nurses must approach circumcision with cultural sensitivity while prioritizing patient safety, informed consent, and pain control.

Anatomy and Physiology Relevant to Circumcision

  • Prepuce (Foreskin): A double-layered fold of skin and mucous membrane that covers and protects the glans penis.
  • Glans Penis: The sensitive head of the penis, rich in nerve endings, which is exposed after the foreskin is removed.
  • Frenulum: A V-shaped band of skin on the underside of the glans connecting to the inner prepuce; it contains a prominent blood artery and requires careful handling during surgery to prevent excessive bleeding.
  • Dorsal Nerves: The primary sensory nerves supplying the penis, located near the top surface, which are targeted during local anesthesia blocks.

An understanding of this local vascular and nerve supply is essential for safe surgical outcomes and effective pain management.

Indications and Contraindications

Medical indications for circumcision include phimosis (where the foreskin cannot be retracted over the glans), recurrent balanitis (inflammation of the glans), and paraphimosis (a medical emergency where a retracted foreskin becomes trapped behind the glans and swells). Religious and cultural traditions remain the most frequent reasons for the procedure in many populations worldwide.

Contraindications must be carefully screened before scheduling the surgery.

  • Bleeding disorders: Conditions like hemophilia can cause severe, life-threatening hemorrhage.
  • Congenital anomalies: Abnormalities such as hypospadias (where the urethral opening is on the underside of the penis) mean the foreskin may be needed later for reconstructive surgery.
  • Active local infection: Surgery should be delayed until any acute inflammation or infection is fully resolved.

Preoperative Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Thorough preoperative preparation safeguards the patient and reassures the family. For infants, the nurse checks feeding schedules to ensure the baby is not overly full or at risk of regurgitation during sedation or handling. For older boys and adults, verifying informed consent is paramount; the patient or legal guardian must understand the risks, benefits, and alternative choices.

The nurse assesses baseline vital signs, inspects the genital area for rashes, structural defects, or signs of infection, and reviews the personal and family bleeding history. In pediatric cases, educating parents on what to expect post-procedure helps reduce anxiety and prepares them for home care.

Surgical Techniques and Equipment

Several standard instruments and techniques are utilized for male circumcision, varying by the patient's age and clinical preference. Common devices include the Gomco clamp, the Plastibell, and the Mogen clamp.

  • Gomco Clamp: A bell-shaped metal device placed over the glans to protect it while the foreskin is drawn over the bell and clamped, cutting off blood supply before excision.
  • Plastibell: A plastic ring tied tightly over the foreskin with a suture string; the excess foreskin is trimmed off, and the plastic ring naturally falls off within a week as the tissue heals.
  • Freehand Technique: Often performed using surgical scissors and scalpel, commonly practiced in many local and traditional setups as well as adult surgeries, requiring precise suturing for hemostasis.

Intraoperative Nursing Responsibilities and Safety

During the procedure, the scrub and circulating nurses maintain a sterile field and ensure patient safety. Proper positioning is vital; infants are gently secured to a specialized restraint board using Velcro straps to prevent sudden movements that could cause accidental injury from surgical instruments.

The nurse monitors the patient's color, breathing, and vital signs, especially if mild sedation or sweet-tasting sucrose solutions are used to soothe an infant. Instrument counts and checks for tissue integrity are maintained throughout. Promptly identifying and reporting any signs of local anesthetic toxicity, such as tremors or unusual drowsiness, remains a critical safety duty.

Postoperative Nursing Care and Pain Management

Immediate postoperative care focuses on monitoring for hemorrhage and managing pain effectively. The nurse checks the surgical site frequently for active bleeding or expanding hematomas. For infants, non-pharmacological comfort measures such as skin-to-skin contact, swaddling, and oral sucrose are combined with prescribed analgesics like paracetamol.

The nurse instructs caregivers on how to keep the area clean. If a Gomco or freehand method was used, a loose gauze with petroleum jelly is typically applied to prevent the raw wound from sticking to the diaper. For a Plastibell procedure, caregivers are taught not to pull on the plastic ring and to watch for normal healing changes.

Potential Complications and Emergency Management

Although usually straightforward, circumcision carries potential risks that nurses must recognize promptly.

  • Hemorrhage: The most common immediate complication, requiring direct firm pressure and notification of the surgeon.
  • Infection: Redness, foul-smelling discharge, or pus at the surgical site indicates bacterial contamination.
  • Meatal Stenosis: Narrowing of the urethral opening, sometimes caused by irritation after the glans is exposed.
  • Incomplete or Excessive Tissue Removal: Uneven cosmetic results or accidental removal of too much skin requiring specialist review.

Emergency management for severe bleeding involves applying direct pressure with sterile gauze and preparing suture materials or electrocautery equipment for the medical team.

Patient Education and Discharge Instructions

Clear discharge instructions ensure safe healing at home and reduce parental anxiety. Caregivers must be taught normal healing signs, such as a yellowish exudate or crust that forms over the wound; this is a normal part of granulation and should not be scrubbed off, as it is not pus.

Instructions should cover:

  • Gentle daily cleaning with warm water without harsh soaps.
  • Diaper changes should be frequent to keep the area clean and dry.
  • Warning signs to report immediately, including persistent heavy bleeding, spreading redness, fever, or an inability to urinate for several hours.
  • Confirmation that the Plastibell ring usually drops off within 5 to 10 days, and that a doctor should be called if it remains attached after two weeks.
