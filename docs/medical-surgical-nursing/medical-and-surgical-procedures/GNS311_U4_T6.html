Intravenous Infusion

Introduction and Purpose of Intravenous Infusion

Intravenous (IV) infusion involves the direct administration of fluids, electrolytes, medications, or blood products into a patient's venous system. By bypassing the digestive tract, this route achieves immediate therapeutic blood concentrations, making it indispensable in emergency care, surgical procedures, and severe dehydration management. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, IV therapy is frequently utilized for rapid fluid resuscitation in severe malaria, gastroenteritis, and trauma cases where oral rehydration is impossible or insufficient. The primary goals include restoring fluid and electrolyte balance, administering continuous or intermittent pharmacotherapy, delivering total parenteral nutrition, and supplying blood components during acute hemorrhage.

Anatomy and Selection of Peripheral Veins

Selecting an appropriate vein determines the success of cannulation and minimizes patient discomfort. The ideal site is easily visualized and palpated, straight, and resilient. - **Metacarpal veins**: Located on the dorsal aspect of the hand; excellent for initial attempts in adults, though hand movement can easily dislodge the cannula. - **Cephalic vein**: Runs up the lateral aspect of the arm; offers a large, straight pathway suitable for various catheter sizes. - **Basilic vein**: Located on the medial aspect of the arm; accommodates larger cannulas but requires careful maneuvering due to proximity to nerves and arteries. - **Median cubital vein**: Situated in the antecubital fossa; wide and stable, making it the preferred choice for emergency blood draws and rapid fluid boluses, though flexion of the elbow can occlude flow. Avoid selecting sites over areas of joint flexion, inflamed tissue, scarred skin, or limbs affected by previous mastectomy or hemiplegia.

Types of Intravenous Solutions and Fluids

Intravenous fluids are categorized based on their tonicity relative to human plasma, which dictates how fluid shifts between the intravascular space and the cells. | Fluid Category | Mechanism | Common Clinical Example | | --- | --- | --- | | **Isotonic** | Expands extracellular fluid volume without causing significant fluid shifts into or out of cells | 0.9% Normal Saline, Ringer's Lactate | | **Hypotonic** | Shifts fluid out of the intravascular space and into intracellular and interstitial spaces | 0.45% Normal Saline, 5% Dextrose in Water (after metabolism) | | **Hypertonic** | Draws water out of intracellular and interstitial compartments into the vascular space | 3% Normal Saline, 10% Dextrose in Water | Selecting the wrong tonicity can lead to severe cellular damage, cerebral edema, or circulatory overload. Always verify the medical prescription against the patient's fluid and electrolyte status.

Equipment Needed for IV Cannulation and Infusion

Gathering all necessary supplies before initiating the procedure ensures efficiency and minimizes infection risks. - **IV Cannula**: Selected based on patient age and vein caliber (e.g., 18G for rapid blood administration, 20G-22G for standard infusions). - **Infusion Set**: Standard gravity tubing or specialized pump tubing with a drip chamber. - **IV Fluid Bag**: Checked for clarity, integrity, correct formulation, and expiration date. - **Skin Antiseptic**: Alcoholic chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine swabs. - **Tourniquet**: To engorge peripheral veins. - **Transparent Dressing**: Sterile, semi-permeable film to secure the site and allow visual monitoring. - **Personal Protective Equipment**: Clean gloves to maintain aseptic technique. - **Sharps Container**: For immediate, safe disposal of the used needle stylet.

Step-by-Step Procedure for IV Cannulation

Executing IV cannulation requires meticulous attention to hygiene and patient communication. Begin by verifying the medical order and explaining the procedure to reduce anxiety. Sanitize hands and don clean gloves. Apply the tourniquet 10 to 15 centimeters above the chosen site and palpate for a bouncy, unobstructed vein. Cleanse the skin using a friction scrub with an antiseptic swab and allow it to dry completely to ensure antimicrobial efficacy. Anchor the skin gently below the site to stabilize the vein. Insert the cannula with the bevel facing upward at a 15- to 30-degree angle. Observe for flashback of blood in the chamber, confirming vascular entry. Lower the angle nearly parallel to the skin and advance the catheter slightly off the stylet. Release the tourniquet, occlude the vein lightly above the tip, withdraw the needle completely while activating the safety device, and attach the infusion tubing or saline lock. Secure the catheter with a transparent dressing and document the procedure accurately.

Calculating and Regulating Infusion Flow Rates

Regulating the rate at which fluids enter the circulatory system prevents both dehydration and fluid overload. The infusion rate depends on the total volume prescribed and the specified total time, or drop factor of the administration set. To calculate drops per minute (gtt/min), use the standard formula: $$\text{Flow Rate (gtt/min)} = \frac{\text{Total Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Time (minutes)}}$$ Standard gravity tubing typically comes in two drop factors: macro-drip sets (usually 10, 15, or 20 gtt/mL) used for routine adult infusions, and micro-drip sets (60 gtt/mL) used for pediatric or precise medication administration. Always count drops manually over a full minute if an electronic infusion pump is unavailable, and re-check flow rates frequently throughout the shift.

Ongoing Nursing Monitoring and Maintenance

Continuous surveillance preserves site integrity and prevents systemic complications. Inspect the insertion site every hour for signs of swelling, coolness, pain, or leakage, which indicate infiltration. Palpate along the vein path for warmth or tenderness, suggestive of phlebitis. Ensure the infusion tubing remains unkinked and connections are secure. Change peripheral site dressings according to institutional policy—typically every 72 to 96 hours or whenever integrity is compromised. Monitor vital signs regularly and assess intake and output charts meticulously to evaluate the patient's fluid balance response.

Complications of Intravenous Therapy and Management

Intravenous therapy carries risks of local and systemic adverse events that require immediate identification and intervention. - **Infiltration**: Fluid enters surrounding subcutaneous tissue due to catheter dislodgement. Manifests as swelling, coolness, and taut skin. Stop infusion and remove the device immediately. - **Phlebitis**: Inflammation of the vein wall caused by chemical, mechanical, or bacterial irritation. Characterized by redness, warmth, tenderness, and a palpable cord. Discontinue infusion and apply warm compresses. - **Air Embolism**: Air enters the circulatory system through unsecured tubing. Causes sudden dyspnea, cyanosis, and hypotension. Place the patient in left lateral Trendelenburg position and administer oxygen. - **Fluid Overload**: Excess intravascular volume leading to hypertension, crackles in lungs, and respiratory distress. Slow the infusion rate and notify the prescriber.

Discontinuation of Intravenous Infusion

When intravenous therapy is no longer required or complications demand site removal, discontinuation must be performed safely. Confirm the medical order to cease the infusion. Gather clean gloves, gauze, and adhesive bandage tape. Clamp the infusion tubing and gently remove the transparent dressing while stabilizing the cannula. Place sterile gauze lightly over the insertion site without applying pressure, then withdraw the catheter smoothly along the axis of the vein. Apply firm, direct pressure with the gauze for at least one to two minutes until hemostasis is achieved, especially if the patient has a bleeding disorder or is on anticoagulants. Inspect the removed catheter tip to verify it is intact, apply a secure adhesive bandage, and document the date, time, reason for discontinuation, and condition of the site.
