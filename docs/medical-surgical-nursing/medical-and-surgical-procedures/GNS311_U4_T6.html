Intravenous Infusion

Introduction and Purpose of Intravenous Infusion

An intravenous (IV) infusion is the direct delivery of fluids, medications, blood products, or nutrients straight into a patient's venous circulation. By bypassing the digestive tract completely, the IV route ensures rapid onset of action, making it the gold standard in emergency situations, surgical suites, and critical care units. Nurses rely on this method when patients cannot take anything by mouth, require precise blood concentration levels of drugs, or need urgent volume resuscitation.

The main goals of IV therapy include:

  • Fluid and electrolyte replacement: Restoring hydration and chemical balance after heavy losses from vomiting, diarrhea, or trauma.
  • Medication administration: Giving antibiotics, pain relief, or emergency drugs that demand immediate systemic effects.
  • Blood component therapy: Transfusing packed red cells, platelets, or plasma to restore oxygen-carrying capacity or clotting factors.
  • Nutritional support: Delivering total parenteral nutrition (TPN) to patients with non-functioning digestive systems.

Anatomy and Selection of Peripheral Veins

Choosing the right vein for cannulation is a foundational nursing skill that directly influences how long an IV line will last. Optimal peripheral veins are usually located in the upper extremities. They should feel bouncy, straight, and refill quickly after gentle pressure is released. A common clinical mistake is choosing a vein solely because it looks visible, ignoring how fragile or curved it might actually be.

Key veins commonly used in adults include:

  • Metacarpal veins: Found on the back of the hand; great for short-term use, but hand movement can dislodge the cannula easily.
  • Cephalic vein: Runs up the lateral side of the arm; offers a large, sturdy path but can be challenging to stabilize near the wrist.
  • Basilic vein: Located on the medial side of the arm; large and easy to cannulate, but care must be taken near the inner elbow where nerves and arteries run close by.
  • Median cubital vein: Sits right in the antecubital fossa; wide and stable, making it ideal for blood draws and emergency access, though bending the elbow can occlude the line.

Types of Intravenous Solutions and Fluids

Intravenous fluids are categorized based on how they affect the osmolality of body fluids. Understanding these categories helps nurses prevent dangerous fluid shifts inside the patient's cells.

Equipment Needed for IV Cannulation and Infusion

Gathering the correct equipment before entering the patient's room promotes efficiency and reduces the risk of contaminating the sterile site. A well-prepared tray prevents frantic searching mid-procedure.

  1. Personal Protective Equipment (PPE): Clean non-sterile gloves to protect the nurse and patient from cross-contamination.
  2. Skin Antiseptic: Chlorhexidine in alcohol or 70% isopropyl alcohol swabs to thoroughly disinfect the insertion site.
  3. Tourniquet: Applied temporarily to distend the veins by restricting venous blood return.
  4. Peripheral IV Catheter: Appropriate gauge size (e.g., 18G for rapid fluids/blood, 20G/22G for general infusions).
  5. IV Administration Set: Primary tubing set, either gravity-based or compatible with an infusion pump.
  6. IV Fluid Bag: The prescribed solution, checked against the medication ticket.
  7. Transparent Dressing: Sterile dressing to secure the cannula and allow continuous visual inspection of the site.
  8. Flushing Supplies: A syringe filled with sterile normal saline to confirm patency after insertion.

Step-by-Step Procedure for IV Cannulation

Performing safe IV cannulation requires strict adherence to aseptic technique and clear communication with the patient throughout the process.

Begin by verifying the medical order, explaining the procedure, and positioning the patient comfortably. Wash your hands and apply a tourniquet about 4 to 6 inches above your chosen site. Palpate the vein, release the tourniquet temporarily if needed, and don your gloves. Cleanse the skin vigorously with an antiseptic wipe, allowing it to dry completely—blowing on the site to dry it defeats the disinfection process.

Reapply the tourniquet, pull the skin taut below the site to stabilize the vein, and insert the needle at a 15- to 30-degree angle with the bevel facing upward. Watch for a flash of dark red blood in the flashback chamber, which confirms entry into the vessel lumen. Lower the catheter almost parallel to the skin, advance the plastic cannula slightly further into the vein while withdrawing the needle stylet, release the tourniquet, connect the extension set or IV tubing, and flush gently with normal saline to check for patency before securing with a transparent dressing.

Calculating and Regulating Infusion Flow Rates

Ensuring that IV fluids infuse at the correct speed prevents dangerous complications like fluid overload or dehydration. Nurses calculate flow rates using either an electronic infusion pump (measured in milliliters per hour, mL/hr) or a manual gravity tubing set (measured in drops per minute, gtt/min).

To calculate gravity flow rates, the nurse must know the drop factor of the administration set, which is printed on the packaging (e.g., standard sets usually have 10, 15, or 20 gtt/mL, while blood tubing or microdrip sets usually deliver 60 gtt/mL).

The Gravity Flow Rate Formula: $$\text{Flow Rate (gtt/min)} = \frac{\text{Total Volume (mL)} \times \text{Drop Factor (gtt/mL)}}{\text{Total Time (minutes)}}$$

Alternatively, when using hours, multiply the hours by 60 to get total minutes, or use the shortcut: $$\text{Gtt/min} = \frac{\text{Volume in mL} \times \text{Drop Factor}}{\text{Time in hours} \times 60}$$

Regularly check the drip chamber and roller clamp to ensure the rate remains steady throughout the shift.

Ongoing Nursing Monitoring and Maintenance

An active IV line demands constant vigilance to catch minor problems before they turn into major emergencies. Maintenance protocols protect the insertion site and ensure the prescribed therapy flows without interruption.

  • Site Inspection: Inspect the IV site at least every shift—and more frequently for high-risk medications—checking for pain, redness, swelling, or coolness.
  • Tubing Changes: Primary continuous infusion sets are typically changed every 96 hours to prevent bacterial colonization. Intermittent infusion sets or blood tubing require more frequent changes.
  • Flushing Protocols: Flush intermittent lines with normal saline before and after medication administration to prevent drug incompatibility and clotting.
  • Dressing Integrity: Change transparent dressings immediately if they become loose, damp, or visibly soiled.

Complications of Intravenous Therapy and Management

Intravenous therapy carries inherent risks that every nurse must recognize immediately. Prompt intervention prevents severe tissue damage or systemic infection.

  • Infiltration: Occurs when non-vesicant fluid leaks into the surrounding tissue. The site becomes cool, pale, and swollen, with sluggish flow. *Action*: Stop the infusion, remove the catheter, and elevate the limb.
  • Extravasation: Similar to infiltration, but the leaked fluid is a vesicant medication (like chemotherapy) that causes severe tissue necrosis. *Action*: Stop the infusion, leave the catheter in place temporarily to aspirate residual drug if possible, notify the prescriber, and apply the specific antidote.
  • Phlebitis: Inflammation of the vein wall marked by pain, erythema, warmth, and a hard streak along the vein. *Action*: Stop the infusion, remove the device, and apply warm compresses.
  • Fluid Overload: Excess intravascular fluid causing hypertension, shortness of breath, and crackles in the lungs. *Action*: Slow the infusion rate, sit the patient upright, and notify the physician.
  • Air Embolism: Air entering the circulatory system through the tubing. *Action*: Turn the patient onto their left side in the Trendelenburg position and administer oxygen.

Discontinuation of Intravenous Infusion

Discontinuing an IV line is a straightforward procedure that should be performed with care to prevent bleeding, air embolism, or site trauma. Nurses remove peripheral lines when therapy is finished, when signs of complications appear, or when ordered by the physician.

To discontinue an IV safely, turn off any electronic pump or close the roller clamp. Perform hand hygiene, put on clean gloves, and gently remove the transparent dressing and adhesive tape while holding the catheter steady. Place a sterile gauze pad or cotton ball directly over the insertion site without applying pressure into the wound channel. Withdraw the cannula smoothly, keeping it parallel to the skin to avoid scraping the vessel wall. Immediately apply gentle pressure to the site with the gauze for at least one to two minutes until bleeding stops. Inspect the removed catheter tip to ensure it is intact—a missing tip indicates a potential catheter embolism. Finally, apply a clean dry bandage and document the time, reason for removal, and condition of the site.
