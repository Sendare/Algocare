Suturing of Simple Lacerations

Introduction and Patient Assessment

Suturing is a fundamental clinical skill used to close simple skin lacerations, reduce healing time, and minimize scarring. Before reaching for a needle holder, you must thoroughly evaluate the wound and the patient. Check the time of injury, as most simple lacerations should ideally be closed within a specific golden window of six to eight hours to prevent trapped bacteria from causing deep infections. Assess the depth of the cut, check for active bleeding, and test the function of nerves, tendons, and blood vessels distal to the injury site. Always take a complete medical history, noting any bleeding disorders, current medications like anticoagulants, and the date of the patient's last tetanus toxoid injection.

Equipment, Instruments, and Local Anesthesia

Performing a proper closure requires an organized sterile field and the correct tools. Your standard instrument tray should contain a needle holder, suture scissors, tissue forceps with or without teeth, and a scalpel handle. Local anesthesia is essential to keep the patient comfortable throughout the procedure. Lignocaine, commonly known as lidocaine, is the most frequently used local anesthetic.

  • Plain Lidocaine: Acts quickly with a shorter duration of action.
  • Lidocaine with Adrenaline (Epinephrine): Constricts blood vessels to reduce bleeding and prolongs the numbing effect.

Never use lidocaine containing adrenaline on end-organs such as fingers, toes, the nose, or the earlobes, as the severe vessel constriction can lead to tissue death.

Wound Preparation and Aseptic Technique

Preparation of the wound bed directly dictates whether the skin will heal cleanly or develop a deep infection. Begin by cleansing the surrounding skin with an antiseptic solution like chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine, taking care not to pour harsh chemicals directly into an open wound. High-pressure irrigation using normal saline from a syringe and splash guard is the single most effective step to wash away dirt and loose bacteria. Carefully inspect the wound base for hidden foreign bodies such as glass shards or gravel. Maintain a strict sterile field throughout this entire process to protect the patient from outside contamination.

Suture Materials and Needle Selection

Choosing the right suture material depends on whether the stitches are meant to stay inside the body or be removed from the skin surface later. Sutures are generally divided into two main categories:

  • Absorbable Suture: Materials like catgut or polyglycolic acid that break down naturally inside the body over time; used mostly for deep dermal layers.
  • Non-Absorbable Suture: Materials like nylon or polypropylene that do not break down; used for outer skin closure and removed after healing begins.

Needles also vary by shape and point. Cutting needles are standard for tough skin because their sharp edge easily pierces through the tough outer dermal layer without bending.

Step-by-Step Technique for Simple Interrupted Sutures

The simple interrupted suture is the workhorse of skin closure because it is reliable and easy to adjust.

  1. Grasp the needle holder roughly one-third to one-half of the distance from the needle attachment point.
  2. Use tissue forceps to gently stabilize the skin edge.
  3. Enter the skin at a 90-degree angle, rotating your wrist so the needle travels in a smooth arc through the tissue.
  4. Take equal bites of tissue on both sides of the wound, ensuring the depth matches the width to prevent the skin edges from overlapping.
  5. Tie a secure square knot using the instrument tie technique, laying the knot down to the side of the incision line rather than directly over the cut.

Post-Procedure Care and Suture Removal Timeline

Proper aftercare ensures the wound continues to heal cleanly after the patient leaves the clinic. Instruct the patient to keep the site clean and dry for the first 24 to 48 hours, after which gentle washing with mild soap and water is usually permitted. Advise them to watch for warning signs of trouble, such as spreading redness or pus.

The timeline for removing sutures depends heavily on where the injury is located on the body because blood supply varies greatly by region:

  • Face: 3 to 5 days, due to excellent blood supply and to avoid permanent track marks.
  • Scalp and Trunk: 7 to 10 days, where skin tension is moderate.
  • Limbs and Joints: 10 to 14 days, where movement puts constant stress on the healing skin.

Recognizing Wound Complications and Infection

Even with careful preparation and technique, complications can occasionally develop during the healing process. You must be able to recognize early signs of wound infection, which include increasing pain, swelling, warmth, and purulent drainage. Mild redness immediately after the procedure is a normal inflammatory response, but expanding erythema and red streaks spreading away from the wound suggest cellulitis. Other potential problems include wound dehiscence, where the edges pull apart due to too much tension or premature suture removal, and hematoma formation underneath the skin from inadequate bleeding control before closure.
