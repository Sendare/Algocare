Management of Patients on Mechanical Ventilation

Introduction to Mechanical Ventilation and Indications

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving supportive therapy that physically moves air in and out of the lungs when an individual is unable to maintain adequate spontaneous ventilation or gas exchange. It does not cure the underlying disease process; rather, it buys time for medical treatments to reverse respiratory failure. Indications for initiating ventilatory support fall into two main categories: - **Acute Respiratory Failure Type 1 (Hypoxemic)**: Characterized by a low arterial oxygen tension, seen in conditions such as severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and pulmonary edema. - **Acute Respiratory Failure Type 2 (Hypercapnic)**: Characterized by an elevated carbon dioxide tension due to inadequate alveolar ventilation, common in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations, drug overdoses, and myasthenic crises. In busy critical care units across Nigeria, patients with severe tetanus, late-stage malaria with cerebral complications, or profound polytrauma frequently present with exhausted respiratory muscles and require prompt artificial airway placement and ventilator attachment.

Ventilator Modes and Settings

Understanding the interplay between patient effort and machine delivery is essential for safe critical care nursing. Ventilator modes dictate how much work the machine performs relative to the patient. - **Controlled Mandatory Ventilation (CMV)**: The machine delivers breaths at a set rate and volume regardless of patient effort. Rarely used unless the patient is deeply sedated or paralyzed. - **Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV)**: Delivers preset mandatory breaths synchronized with the patient's spontaneous breathing efforts, allowing the patient to breathe spontaneously between machine breaths. - **Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV)**: Augments the patient's spontaneous breaths with a preset amount of positive pressure to decrease the work of breathing through the endotracheal tube. Key settings adjusted by the multidisciplinary team include **FiO2** (fraction of inspired oxygen), **tidal volume** (volume of gas delivered per breath, usually calculated based on ideal body weight), **respiratory rate**, and **PEEP** (positive end-expiratory pressure), which keeps alveoli open at the end of expiration to prevent collapse.

Initial Setup, Baseline Assessment, and Safety Checks

When a patient is newly connected to a mechanical ventilator, immediate verification of correct tube placement and machine function is mandatory. Auscultation of bilateral breath sounds and epigastric sounds ensures the endotracheal tube has not entered the esophagus. A **chest X-ray** is subsequently performed to confirm that the tip of the tube sits approximately two to six centimeters above the carina. Baseline vital signs, continuous pulse oximetry, and end-tidal carbon dioxide (capnography) monitoring must be established instantly. The nurse must verify that alarm limits are active and audible, never silenced or turned down. Emergency equipment—including a manual resuscitation bag (Ambu bag) with a mask, a working suction apparatus, and an appropriately sized spare endotracheal tube—must remain at the bedside throughout the duration of mechanical ventilatory support.

Routine Nursing Care and Patient Monitoring

Caring for a mechanically ventilated patient requires systematic, round-the-clock observation. Hemodynamic instability can occur rapidly due to positive pressure intrathoracic effects reducing venous return to the heart, which subsequently drops cardiac output and blood pressure. Comfort and synchrony are managed using sedation and analgesia scales, such as the **Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS)**, ensuring the patient is neither dangerously agitated nor unnecessarily comatose. Daily spontaneous awakening trials are integrated into care plans to evaluate neurological status. Nutritional support is initiated early via enteral routes to preserve gut integrity, while frequent eye care, oral hygiene with chlorhexidine, and passive range-of-motion exercises protect against secondary complications of immobility.

Airway Management and Endotracheal Suctioning

An artificial airway bypasses the upper airway's natural defense mechanisms, making proactive airway clearance vital. Endotracheal suctioning removes pulmonary secretions, maintains airway patency, and prevents atelectasis caused by mucous plugging. However, suctioning is not benign; it can induce severe hypoxemia, cardiac dysrhythmias, mucosal trauma, and elevated intracranial pressure. To minimize risks, nurses must adhere to strict aseptic technique and hyperoxygenate the patient before and after the procedure. Suction pressure should be maintained between 80 and 120 mmHg in adults. The catheter is inserted gently without suction until resistance or coughing occurs, then withdrawn while applying intermittent suction in a rotating motion for no longer than 10 to 15 seconds. Saline instillation prior to routine suctioning is discouraged because it causes oxygen desaturation and pushes pathogens deeper into the lungs.

Prevention of Ventilator-Associated Complications

Patients on mechanical ventilation face severe hospital-acquired threats, chief among them being **ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)**. VAP develops when bacteria aspiration occurs around the endotracheal tube cuff or via contaminated condensate in circuit tubing. Implementing a strict **ventilator bundle** drastically reduces complication rates: - **Head-of-bed elevation**: Maintain the patient at 30 to 45 degrees to prevent aspiration of gastric contents. - **Daily sedation breaks and readiness assessments**: Minimize the duration of mechanical support. - **Oral care protocols**: Brush teeth and clean oral mucosa with antiseptic solutions every few hours. - **Peptic ulcer and deep vein thrombosis prophylaxis**: Prevent secondary systemic complications. Barotrauma is another significant risk, manifested by pneumothorax or subcutaneous emphysema, caused by excessive airway pressures overdistending fragile alveoli.

Ventilator Weaning and Extubation Protocols

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilatory support back to spontaneous breathing. The multidisciplinary team evaluates readiness daily using objective criteria such as improving arterial blood gases, stable hemodynamics, adequate cough reflex, and resolution of the acute phase of the underlying illness. A common weaning method is the **spontaneous breathing trial (SBT)**, where the patient breathes with minimal or no ventilator support via a T-piece or low continuous positive airway pressure for 30 to 120 minutes. Throughout the trial, the nurse monitors closely for signs of weaning failure, including tachypnea, tachycardia, diaphoresis, agitation, and oxygen desaturation. If the patient tolerates the trial, extubation is performed after explaining the procedure, suctioning the airway and oropharynx, deflating the cuff, and removing the tube at peak inspiration.

Patient and Family Support in Critical Care

The intensive care environment is profoundly disorienting and terrifying for a conscious patient trapped behind an endotracheal tube. Inability to speak impairs basic communication, frequently inducing panic, anxiety, and feelings of helplessness. Nurses must establish alternate communication methods, such as alphabet boards, writing pads, picture cards, or eye-blinking signals. Family members also experience acute crisis when witnessing a loved one tethered to complex machinery. Clear, compassionate communication, transparent updates, and structured visitation policies alleviate fear. In many Nigerian hospitals, families play a direct supportive role in bedside care, making their inclusion and education on infection control precautions—such as hand hygiene and gowning—integral to holistic recovery.
