Management of Patients on Mechanical Ventilation

Introduction to Mechanical Ventilation and Indications

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving supportive therapy that takes over or assists a patient's breathing when they can no longer maintain adequate gas exchange on their own. The primary goals are to deliver adequate oxygenation, eliminate carbon dioxide, and reduce the physical work of breathing. Nurses caring for these patients must understand why the therapy was initiated, as this guides ongoing clinical monitoring.

Common indications for mechanical ventilation include:

  • Acute respiratory failure: Inability to maintain normal oxygen or carbon dioxide levels, often due to pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), or severe asthma.
  • Airway protection: Inability to protect the airway due to decreased level of consciousness, head injury, or drug overdose.
  • Hemodynamic instability: Severe shock or cardiac arrest where excessive metabolic work of breathing compromises vital organs.
  • Neuromuscular compromise: Conditions like Guillain-Barré syndrome or myasthenic crisis that paralyze the breathing muscles.

Understanding Ventilator Modes and Settings

Mechanical ventilators offer different modes that determine how much work the machine does compared to the patient. Knowing these modes helps nurses anticipate the patient's breathing pattern and detect problems early.

  • Controlled Ventilation: The machine delivers breaths at a set rate and volume regardless of the patient's effort. Used mainly for heavily sedated or paralyzed patients.
  • Assist-Control (AC): The machine delivers a preset volume or pressure whenever the patient initiates a breath, but also guarantees a minimum breath rate if the patient is too weak.
  • Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV): Delivers preset mandatory breaths synchronized with the patient's spontaneous breaths, allowing the patient to breathe independently between machine breaths.
  • Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV): The machine applies pressure to assist the patient's own spontaneous breaths, commonly used during weaning.

Key settings prescribed by the doctor or respiratory therapist include FiO2 (fraction of inspired oxygen), tidal volume (amount of air per breath), and PEEP (positive end-expiratory pressure, which keeps small air sacs open at the end of exhalation).

Initial Setup, Verification, and Baseline Assessment

When a patient is newly intubated and connected to a ventilator, immediate verification of tube placement and baseline assessment are critical nursing responsibilities. Mistakes at this stage can be life-threatening.

First, confirm endotracheal tube placement using end-tidal carbon dioxide detectors, auscultation of bilateral breath sounds (checking for equal air entry and ensuring the tube hasn't slipped into the right mainstem bronchus), and checking a chest X-ray. Secure the tube firmly with tape or a commercial holder, and document the centimeter mark at the lip or teeth.

Perform a rapid baseline assessment of vital signs, oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry, and the patient's comfort and sedation level. Check that emergency equipment—including a manual bag-valve-mask (Ambu bag) connected to oxygen and working suction—is immediately available at the bedside at all times.

Routine Nursing Care and Patient Monitoring

Caring for a ventilated patient requires structured, continuous nursing vigilance. The ventilator is a complex machine, but the human response is what demands primary nursing attention.

Monitor vital signs frequently, keeping a close eye on blood pressure because positive pressure ventilation increases chest pressure, which can decrease venous return to the heart and cause hypotension. Assess sedation levels regularly using standard scales (such as the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale) to prevent both accidental extubation from agitation and prolonged coma from over-sedation.

Provide meticulous oral care every two to four hours with chlorhexidine to drastically reduce the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia. Ensure proper positioning by keeping the head of the bed elevated between 30 and 45 degrees unless medically contraindicated. Perform routine eye care, prevent pressure injuries with regular turns and skin checks, and monitor nutritional status through enteral tube feedings as prescribed.

Airway Management and Endotracheal Suctioning

Artificial airways bypass the upper airway's natural ability to warm, filter, and humidify inspired air, leading to thick secretions and potential blockages. Endotracheal suctioning removes these secretions to maintain a patent airway, but it must be performed carefully because it can cause hypoxemia, trauma, and cardiac arrhythmias.

Suctioning should be done based on clinical need—such as visible secretions in the tube, coarse crackles over the trachea, coughing, or a sudden drop in oxygen saturation—rather than on a rigid routine schedule. Hyperoxygenate the patient before and after the procedure with 100% oxygen when necessary. Insert the suction catheter gently without applying suction, and apply suction only while withdrawing the catheter in a rotating motion. Limit each suction pass to 10 to 15 seconds to avoid dangerously depleting the patient's oxygen reserves.

Prevention of Ventilator-Associated Complications

Patients on mechanical ventilation face significant risks of secondary complications. Vigilant nursing care forms the front line of defense in preventing these adverse events.

  • Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP): Prevented through rigorous oral hygiene, keeping the head of the bed elevated, daily spontaneous breathing trials to assess readiness for weaning, and avoiding unscheduled circuit changes.
  • Barotrauma and Volutrauma: High pressures or excessive tidal volumes can rupture delicate lung tissue, leading to pneumothorax. Nurses must monitor peak inspiratory pressures closely.
  • Oxygen Toxicity: Prolonged exposure to high FiO2 levels can damage lung tissue. Wean oxygen as soon as clinical condition permits.
  • Stress Ulcers and Aspiration: Administer prescribed proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers, and monitor residual gastric volumes.
  • Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT): Apply sequential compression devices and administer prophylactic anticoagulants since mobility is absent.

Ventilator Weaning and Extubation Protocols

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilator support back to spontaneous breathing. The process begins as soon as the underlying medical reason for ventilation starts to resolve.

Multidisciplinary weaning protocols typically involve daily screening for readiness, such as stable vital signs, adequate oxygenation, reversal of the acute disease process, and the ability to breathe spontaneously. If the patient passes the screening, a Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT) is performed using minimal support like T-piece or low continuous positive airway pressure for 30 to 120 minutes.

During the trial, the nurse carefully monitors for signs of weaning failure, including rising heart rate, drop in oxygen saturation, increased respiratory rate, use of accessory breathing muscles, agitation, or severe sweating. If failure signs appear, the trial is stopped and ventilator support is restored. If the patient tolerates the trial well, the tube is removed (extubation), followed by careful post-extubation monitoring for airway swelling or respiratory fatigue.

Patient and Family Support in Critical Care

Being on mechanical ventilation is a terrifying experience for patients. Unable to speak due to the endotracheal tube, patients often experience severe anxiety, frustration, and helplessness. Nurses must find alternative ways to communicate and provide constant reassurance.

Provide communication aids such as magic slates, picture boards, alphabet boards, or electronic writing tablets. Ask simple yes-or-no questions that the patient can answer with a head nod or hand squeeze. Explain all care procedures before performing them, even if the patient appears sedated or unresponsive.

Families also experience profound stress when a loved one is intubated in the intensive care unit. Offer clear, compassionate explanations of equipment, monitors, and the plan of care. Encourage family involvement in simple care tasks when appropriate, and facilitate regular visiting times to reduce anxiety for both patient and family.
