Basic Life Support

Introduction and the Chain of Survival

Basic Life Support (BLS) encompasses foundational interventions aimed at sustaining life during cardiac arrest, respiratory failure, or airway obstruction before advanced medical help arrives. The primary objective is to maintain minimal perfusion and oxygenation to the brain and heart.

The American Heart Association structures community and in-hospital emergency care through the Chain of Survival, a sequence of interdependent steps that significantly alter patient outcomes:

  • Early Recognition and Prevention: Identifying emergencies like chest pain or stroke signs before cardiac arrest occurs.
  • Immediate High-Quality CPR: Initiating rapid chest compressions to maintain artificial blood flow.
  • Rapid Defibrillation: Delivering a shock via an AED to reset lethal cardiac rhythms like ventricular fibrillation.
  • Advanced Resuscitation: Transitioning care to paramedics or emergency department teams equipped with advanced airway and pharmacological tools.
  • Post-Cardiac Arrest Care: Managing systemic recovery, neurological protection, and rehabilitation in specialized units.

In Nigerian clinical and community settings, bridging the gap between collapse and the initiation of the first chain link remains a major hurdle due to traffic delays and limited pre-hospital infrastructure. Bystander education is therefore a public health priority.

Scene Safety and Initial Assessment

Before touching a collapsed individual, the rescuer must conduct a rapid environmental scan. Rushing blindly into an unsafe zone—such as an area with live electrical wires, active traffic, or toxic chemical spills—creates secondary casualties.

Once the environment is deemed safe, assess the victim's responsiveness. Tap the shoulders firmly and shout loudly, "Are you okay?" Simultaneously check for normal breathing by scanning the chest for rise and fall over no more than ten seconds. Gasping is not normal breathing; it is agonal respiration and serves as a direct indicator of cardiac arrest.

If the victim is unresponsive and not breathing normally, immediately activate the emergency response system or instruct a specific bystander to call for help and fetch an Automated External Defibrillator before returning to start chest compressions.

High-Quality Chest Compressions

Chest compressions create artificial circulation by increasing intrathoracic pressure and directly compressing the heart. The technique must be precise to be effective:

  • Hand Placement: Place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest (lower half of the sternum) and put the other hand on top, interlocking your fingers.
  • Rate: Deliver compressions at a frequency of 100 to 120 per minute.
  • Depth: Depress the adult sternum at least 5 centimeters (2 inches) but not exceeding 6 centimeters (2.4 inches).
  • Recoil: Allow full chest recoil between compressions to let the heart chambers refill with venous blood.
  • Interruptions: Minimize pauses in compressions; limit any interruption, such as for pulse checks or rhythm analysis, to under 10 seconds.

Airway Management and Rescue Breaths

Once chest compressions are underway, airway management ensures oxygen enters the lungs. The standard approach for an uninjured victim is the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver. Place one hand on the forehead and tilt the head back while lifting the chin with two fingers of the other hand, moving the tongue away from the back of the throat.

If trauma or a cervical spine injury is suspected, use the jaw-thrust maneuver instead to open the airway without extending the neck.

For coordinated CPR, deliver breaths using a barrier device or bag-valve-mask at a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 30:2 for adults. Each rescue breath should last about one second, just enough to make the chest visibly rise. Excessive volume or overly forceful breaths can force air into the stomach, leading to gastric distension, regurgitation, and subsequent aspiration.

Automated External Defibrillator (AED) Operation

An Automated External Defibrillator analyzes the heart's electrical rhythm and delivers a corrective shock when a shockable rhythm is detected. Operating an AED is straightforward, and the device provides verbal or visual prompts throughout the process.

  1. Power On: Turn the AED on immediately upon arrival.
  2. Attach Pads: Peel and place the adhesive pads on the bare chest. For adults, place one pad on the upper right chest below the right clavicle and the second pad on the lower left lateral chest wall below the left armpit.
  3. Clear the Patient: Ensure no one is touching the victim while the AED analyzes the rhythm. Shout a clear warning such as "Everybody clear!"
  4. Deliver Shock: If prompted by the device that a shock is advised, press the shock button.
  5. Resume CPR: Immediately resume chest compressions after the shock is delivered, starting with compressions.

Never use an AED in standing water or on someone lying in a pool of conductive fluid, and wipe the chest dry if the patient is excessively sweaty.

Foreign-Body Airway Obstruction Management

Choking occurs when a foreign object lodges in the upper airway, blocking airflow. Obstruction can be partial or complete. A person with a mild, partial obstruction can cough forcefully; encourage them to keep coughing and do not interfere.

If the obstruction is severe and complete—indicated by the universal sign of choking (hands clutching the throat), inability to speak, weak cough, or cyanosis—immediate intervention is required:

  • Abdominal Thrusts (Heimlich Maneuver): Stand behind the conscious victim, wrap your arms around their waist, make a fist with one hand, place it just above the navel and well below the breastbone, grasp the fist with your other hand, and press hard into the abdomen with a quick, upward thrust.
  • Unresponsive Choking Victim: If the choking person loses consciousness, gently lower them to the floor, activate emergency services, and begin CPR. Each time you open the airway to give breaths, look inside the mouth for the foreign object and remove it only if it is clearly visible. Never perform blind finger sweeps.

Special Considerations in Resuscitation

Certain clinical scenarios modify standard BLS protocols.

  • Pregnant Patients: The gravid uterus compresses the inferior vena cava, significantly reducing venous return to the heart. Manual left uterine displacement—pushing the uterus to the left side of the abdomen—relieves this pressure during resuscitation efforts.
  • Infants and Children: For pediatric victims, compressions are performed using two fingers (for single rescuers) or the two thumb-encircling hands technique (for two rescuers), depressing the chest by about one-third of its depth (approximately 4 cm for infants and 5 cm for children). The compression-to-ventilation ratio for two-rescuer pediatric CPR is 15:2.
  • Hypothermia and Drowning: Resuscitation efforts for cold-water drowning victims should be prolonged, as cold water can induce a protective metabolic diving reflex, delaying anoxic brain injury.
