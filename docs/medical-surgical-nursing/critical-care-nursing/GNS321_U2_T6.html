Basic Life Support

Introduction and Chain of Survival

Basic Life Support, commonly called BLS, is a foundational level of medical care provided to victims of life-threatening illnesses or injuries until they can be given full medical care at a hospital. It involves prompt recognition of cardiac arrest, activation of the emergency response system, performing high-quality chest compressions, and using an Automated External Defibrillator (AED).

The American Heart Association structures the emergency response through the Chain of Survival, a sequence of interconnected steps that gives the patient the best chance of survival.

  • Early Recognition and Prevention: Spotting warning signs like chest pain or unsafe environments before an arrest occurs.
  • Early CPR: Immediate, high-quality chest compressions to keep oxygen-rich blood flowing to the brain and heart.
  • Rapid Defibrillation: Applying an AED quickly to shock abnormal heart rhythms back to a normal pattern.
  • Advanced Life Support: Taking over with medications and advanced airway tools by paramedics or hospital staff.
  • Post-Cardiac Arrest Care: Specialized hospital care to stabilize body functions and promote neurological recovery.

Scene Safety and Initial Assessment

Before touching any collapsed person, a rescuer must perform a rapid visual check of the environment. Scene safety is paramount; rushing into a dangerous area like a room with a gas leak, live electrical wires, or active violence only creates another casualty.

Once the area is deemed safe, tap the victim's shoulder and shout loudly, "Are you okay?" Simultaneously, scan the chest for normal movement to check for breathing. This entire assessment phase should take no longer than 10 seconds. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing normally—or only gasping—you must activate the emergency response system immediately and retrieve an AED if one is nearby.

High-Quality Chest Compressions

Chest compressions are the engine of CPR. They artificially pump blood to vital organs, especially the brain and heart. To be effective, compressions must follow strict mechanical standards. Place the heel of one hand on the center of the victim's chest (lower half of the sternum) and put your other hand on top, interlocking your fingers. Keep your arms completely straight and position your shoulders directly over your hands.

For an adult, push hard and push fast: compress at least 2 inches deep at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Allowing full chest recoil between compressions is crucial because it lets the heart refill with blood before the next push. Minimizing interruptions in compressions directly correlates with higher survival rates.

Airway Management and Rescue Breaths

Once chest compressions are underway, the rescuer must open the airway to deliver rescue breaths. The standard method for an unresponsive medical patient is the head-tilt, chin-lift maneuver. Place one hand on the forehead and tilt the head back, lifting the chin with the fingers of your other hand. If a neck injury is suspected, use the jaw-thrust maneuver instead to avoid moving the cervical spine.

To deliver breaths, pinch the victim's nose shut, create a tight seal over their mouth with yours or a barrier device, and blow into the mouth for about one second. Watch for the chest to rise. The standard compression-to-ventilation ratio for adult single-rescuer CPR is 30 compressions to 2 breaths.

Automated External Defibrillator (AED) Operation

An AED is a computerized device that analyzes the heart rhythm and delivers an electrical shock to reset the heart if a shockable rhythm like ventricular fibrillation is detected. Time is critical: every minute defibrillation is delayed reduces survival.

Using an AED is straightforward because the machine provides voice and visual prompts:

  1. Turn on the AED: Open the lid or press the power button.
  2. Attach pads: Place the adhesive pads on the victim's bare chest according to the picture diagrams on the pads.
  3. Clear the patient: Shout "Clear!" and ensure nobody is touching the patient so the machine can analyze the rhythm accurately.
  4. Deliver shock: If prompted, press the shock button, then immediately resume CPR starting with chest compressions.

Managing Foreign-Body Airway Obstruction (Choking)

Choking occurs when a foreign object blocks the upper airway, preventing airflow. A mild obstruction allows the person to cough forcefully; rescuers should encourage them to keep coughing and monitor them closely.

If the obstruction becomes severe—indicated by a weak, ineffective cough, inability to speak or breathe, and the universal clutching sign at the throat—intervention is required. For a responsive adult or child, perform abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich maneuver). Stand behind the person, wrap your arms around their waist, make a fist with one hand and place it just above the navel well below the breastbone. Grasp your fist with your other hand and press hard into the abdomen with a quick upward thrust. Repeat until the object is expelled or the person becomes unresponsive.
