Assessment of Critically Ill Patients

Introduction to Critical Care Nursing and Patient Assessment

Critical care nursing is a specialized field dedicated to the management of patients experiencing life-threatening health conditions. In the intensive care unit (ICU) or emergency setting across Nigeria, student nurses must understand that caring for the critically ill requires a systematic, rigorous approach to assessment. Patient assessment forms the bedrock of clinical decision-making, allowing nurses to detect subtle physiological deterioration long before overt clinical collapse occurs. Unlike routine ward care, critical care assessment is continuous, dynamic, and multi-system oriented. Nurses must synthesize clinical data from physical examinations, advanced monitoring technologies, and laboratory diagnostics to formulate immediate nursing interventions. Furthermore, cultural competence, effective multidisciplinary collaboration, and clear communication with anxious family members are integral components of holistic critical care nursing. By mastering the fundamentals of patient assessment, student nurses develop the clinical judgment necessary to navigate high-stress environments, prioritize urgent interventions, and ultimately improve patient survival rates in resource-limited or advanced healthcare settings alike.

Initial Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The initial primary survey of a critically ill patient follows the universally recognized ABCDE approach: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, and Exposure. This rapid, life-saving assessment is designed to identify and immediately treat life-threatening conditions within minutes of patient contact. 'A' stands for Airway; the nurse must ensure the airway is patent and clear of obstructions, maintaining cervical spine precautions if trauma is suspected. 'B' evaluates Breathing by checking respiratory rate, depth, chest symmetry, and oxygen saturation. 'C' assesses Circulation, checking pulse rate, blood pressure, capillary refill, skin color, and active bleeding, while simultaneously securing intravenous or intraosseous access for fluid resuscitation. 'D' focuses on Disability, providing a rapid neurological evaluation utilizing tools like the AVPU scale (Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive) or the Glasgow Coma Scale to check pupillary response and consciousness levels. Finally, 'E' involves Exposure and Environmental control, requiring the nurse to briefly undress the patient to inspect for hidden injuries, rashes, or signs of trauma while actively preventing hypothermia through the use of warm blankets. Mastering this systematic framework ensures that the most critical threats to life are addressed first.

Comprehensive Secondary Survey and System-Wise Evaluation

Once immediate life threats identified during the primary survey are stabilized, the nurse conducts a comprehensive secondary survey and system-wise evaluation. This detailed, head-to-toe physical examination provides baseline data and uncovers secondary injuries or underlying medical conditions. The nurse begins with a thorough history using the SAMPLE acronym (Signs and symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, and Events leading to the illness). Following the history, a systematic head-to-toe assessment is performed. The head and neck are checked for trauma or jugular venous distension. The thorax is auscultated for abnormal breath sounds and heart murmurs. The abdomen is inspected, auscultated for bowel sounds, and gently palpated for tenderness, distension, or rigidity. The extremities are evaluated for peripheral pulses, edema, color, and motor-sensory function. Skin integrity is meticulously checked for pressure injury risks, especially over bony prominences. In critical care settings, this comprehensive evaluation must be repeated at regular intervals, comparing current findings with previous baseline data to evaluate treatment efficacy and identify emerging complications promptly.

Neurological Assessment and Consciousness Scales

Neurological assessment in the critically ill patient is crucial for detecting intracranial pressure changes, cerebral hypoperfusion, and acute encephalopathy. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is the gold standard tool used to evaluate a patient's level of consciousness by assessing three behavioral responses: eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. A lower GCS score indicates severe neurological impairment and a high risk for airway compromise. In addition to GCS, nurses must assess pupillary size, symmetry, and direct and consensual light reflexes to evaluate brainstem integrity. Cranial nerve evaluations and assessments of peripheral motor and sensory function further help localize neurological deficits. In sedated or mechanically ventilated patients where verbal assessment is impossible, specialized tools such as the Full Outline of UnResponsiveness (FOUR) score are often utilized. Nurses must also watch closely for signs of increased intracranial pressure, such as Cushing's triad (systolic hypertension, widened pulse pressure, and bradycardia combined with irregular respirations), which represents a dire neurosurgical emergency requiring immediate medical escalation.

Cardiovascular and Hemodynamic Monitoring

Cardiovascular and hemodynamic monitoring provides real-time data regarding tissue perfusion, cardiac output, and vascular tone in critically ill patients. Non-invasive monitoring includes frequent blood pressure checks, continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) rhythm analysis, and pulse oximetry. However, hemodynamically unstable patients often require advanced invasive monitoring. An arterial line provides continuous, beat-to-beat blood pressure measurement and easy access for frequent arterial blood gas sampling. Central venous catheters (CVCs) measure central venous pressure (CVP), reflecting right ventricular preload and fluid status. In more complex cases, pulmonary artery catheters (Swans-Ganz catheters) or advanced minimally invasive cardiac output monitors are used to measure stroke volume, systemic vascular resistance, and cardiac index. Critical care nurses must master transducer calibration, zeroing, and leveling (at the phlebostatic axis) to ensure measurement accuracy. Furthermore, nurses must correlate numerical hemodynamic data with physical assessment findings—such as peripheral pulse quality, skin temperature, capillary refill time, and urine output—to obtain a complete picture of the patient's circulatory adequacy.

Respiratory Assessment and Airway Management

Respiratory assessment and airway management are paramount in critical care nursing, as oxygen failure is a primary cause of cardiac arrest. The nurse assesses the respiratory system by inspecting chest excursion symmetry, monitoring respiratory rate and pattern, using pulse oximetry for oxygen saturation, and interpreting arterial blood gas (ABG) results. Auscultation reveals breath sounds such as wheezes, crackles, or diminished air entry, guiding clinical interventions. For patients with compromised airways or respiratory failure, advanced airway management is required, ranging from simple oropharyngeal airways and endotracheal intubation to surgical cricothyroidotomy or tracheostomy care. Nurses caring for mechanically ventilated patients must closely monitor ventilator settings, peak inspiratory pressures, and end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) levels. Maintaining a patent artificial airway necessitates meticulous endotracheal suctioning using sterile technique, regular oral hygiene with chlorhexidine to prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), and monitoring cuff pressures to prevent tracheal necrosis. The nurse must also be vigilant for signs of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) or tension pneumothorax.

Renal, Gastrointestinal, and Fluid Balance Monitoring

Renal, gastrointestinal, and fluid balance monitoring are vital components of critical care that help prevent multi-organ dysfunction syndrome. Fluid balance is meticulously tracked through strict intake and output (I&O) recording. Hourly urine output measurement via an indwelling urinary catheter attached to a urometer is the most sensitive indicator of renal perfusion and overall circulatory status; an output of less than 0.5 mL/kg/hour typically indicates oliguria and impending acute kidney injury. Daily weights provide additional insight into fluid retention or third-spacing. Gastrointestinal assessment involves inspecting abdominal contour, listening for bowel sounds in all four quadrants, and monitoring for abdominal distension or tenderness. In critically ill patients, enteral nutrition should be initiated early to preserve gut mucosal integrity and prevent bacterial translocation, provided the patient is hemodynamically stable. Nurses must monitor gastric residual volumes when appropriate, assess for feeding intolerance, and observe for gastrointestinal bleeding by testing stool or nasogastric aspirates for occult blood. Laboratory values such as serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and electrolyte panels must be monitored daily to guide fluid and pharmacological management.

Pain, Agitation, and Delirium Assessment in the ICU

Pain, agitation, and delirium are prevalent and distressing complications in the intensive care unit that negatively impact patient outcomes, length of stay, and long-term cognitive function. Effective critical care nursing requires routine, validated assessment of these parameters using standardized tools. For pain assessment in communicating patients, numeric rating scales are used, while behavioral pain scales (such as the Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool, CPOT) are utilized for non-verbal or mechanically ventilated patients. Agitation and sedation depth are frequently monitored using the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) or the Sedation-Agitation Scale (SAS) to avoid both oversedation and undersedation. Furthermore, delirium—an acute disturbance of consciousness and cognition—must be screened for at least once per shift using validated instruments like the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) or the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC). Non-pharmacological interventions, such as reorientation, early mobilization, natural light exposure, and minimizing nighttime noise, are essential first-line strategies to prevent and manage ICU delirium.

Documentation, Communication, and Continuous Reassessment

Accurate documentation, effective multidisciplinary communication, and continuous reassessment complete the cycle of safe critical care nursing practice. Given the rapidly changing condition of ICU patients, documentation must be timely, precise, and objective, utilizing electronic health records or structured flowsheets to record vital signs, medication administration, fluid balance, and clinical observations. Standardized communication frameworks, such as the ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) tool, ensure vital information is conveyed clearly during handovers, interprofessional rounds, and medical emergency call-outs. Continuous reassessment is the hallmark of expert critical care nursing; no assessment is a one-time event. The nurse must constantly evaluate the patient's response to interventions—such as noticing whether blood pressure improved following fluid boluses or if oxygenation worsened after position changes. By combining diligent documentation, clear communication, and ongoing evaluation, the nurse safeguards patient safety, fosters teamwork, and ensures high-quality, evidence-based care in the critical care environment.
