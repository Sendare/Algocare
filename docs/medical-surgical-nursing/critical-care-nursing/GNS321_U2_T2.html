Assessment of Critically Ill Patients

Introduction to Critical Care Nursing and Patient Assessment

Critical care nursing demands rapid identification of life-threatening instabilities before overt physiological collapse occurs. Assessment in this specialized environment is not a static, once-per-shift duty but a continuous cycle of observation, data interpretation, and clinical intervention. The underlying principle relies on prioritizing immediate threats to life over chronic or secondary abnormalities. Patient instability in Nigerian tertiary intensive care units often stems from delayed recognition of subtle physiological decline, making systematic bedside evaluation an essential safeguard. Establishing a baseline establishes a reference point for all subsequent interventions, allowing the nursing team to evaluate whether a therapy is succeeding or failing.

Initial Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The ABCDE framework provides a standardized, rapid sequence to identify and treat immediate threats to life. Each letter directs attention to a specific physiological system:

  • Airway: Assess patency, listen for stridor or gurgling, and check for obstructions. Immediate intervention involves manual maneuvers, suctioning, or artificial airway insertion.
  • Breathing: Evaluate respiratory rate, depth, symmetry of chest expansion, and oxygen saturation. Administer high-flow oxygen or provide assisted ventilation if inadequate.
  • Circulation: Check central pulses, capillary refill time, skin temperature, and blood pressure. Address hemorrhage or hypoperfusion aggressively with IV access and fluid resuscitation.
  • Disability: Perform a quick neurological check using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale alongside pupillary light reflexes.
  • Exposure: Fully expose the patient to locate hidden injuries, rashes, or bleeding sites while actively preventing hypothermia with warm blankets.

Comprehensive Secondary Survey and System-Wise Evaluation

Once immediate life threats are controlled during the primary survey, a systematic secondary survey uncovers less acute injuries or underlying medical problems. This head-to-toe clinical examination includes reviewing every body system, inspecting surgical incisions, checking drainage tubes, and verifying the correct placement of all invasive lines.

Reviewing the patient chart for laboratory values, diagnostic imaging reports, and medication history completes the picture. In resource-constrained Nigerian facilities, where advanced imaging may be delayed, meticulous physical palpation and auscultation become even more critical for detecting subtle signs like abdominal guarding, crepitus, or adventitious breath sounds.

Neurological Assessment and Consciousness Scales

Accurate neurological monitoring detects early signs of cerebral hypoxia, intracranial pressure spikes, or metabolic encephalopathy. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) remains the gold standard, evaluating eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. A sudden drop of two or more points on the GCS demands immediate medical notification, as it frequently signals neurological deterioration.

Pupil assessment evaluates size, symmetry, and reactivity to light; sluggish or fixed pupils often indicate brainstem compression or severe hypoxia. In sedated intensive care patients where verbal scores cannot be assessed, nurses rely on motor response testing and brainstem reflex evaluations.

Cardiovascular and Hemodynamic Monitoring

Cardiovascular assessment ensures adequate tissue perfusion and cellular oxygen delivery. Continuous electrocardiogram (ECG) monitoring tracks heart rate and detects life-threatening dysrhythmias. Blood pressure measurement can be non-invasive via automated cuffs or invasive through an arterial line, which provides real-time systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) readings.

Evaluating peripheral perfusion involves checking skin color, temperature, peripheral pulses, and capillary refill time. A persistently low MAP despite fluid administration typically signals the need for vasoactive infusions such as adrenaline or noradrenaline to maintain organ perfusion.

Respiratory Assessment and Airway Management

Respiratory evaluation focuses on effective gas exchange and work of breathing. Nurses inspect chest wall symmetry, monitor respiratory rate, and auscultate lung fields for adventitious sounds like crackles, wheezes, or diminished air entry. Pulse oximetry provides continuous estimation of arterial oxygen saturation, though poor peripheral perfusion can cause inaccurate readings.

Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis offers precise data on pH, oxygenation, and ventilation status. For mechanically ventilated patients, nurses monitor tidal volumes, peak inspiratory pressures, and ventilator synchrony to prevent barotrauma and oxygen toxicity.

Renal, Gastrointestinal, and Fluid Balance Monitoring

Maintaining fluid and electrolyte homeostasis requires rigorous intake and output tracking. Urinary output serves as a sensitive indicator of renal perfusion and overall circulatory status; an output below 0.5 mL/kg/h for consecutive hours signals acute kidney injury risk or inadequate volume replacement. Indwelling urinary catheters with hourly urometers are standard in critical care.

Gastrointestinal assessment involves checking bowel sounds, monitoring abdominal distension, and assessing feeding tolerance. In settings where enteral nutrition is delayed, nurses watch closely for signs of ileus, stress ulcer bleeding, or malabsorption.

Pain, Agitation, and Delirium Assessment in the ICU

Pain, agitation, and delirium are prevalent in intensive care units and profoundly affect recovery. Pain assessment utilizes validated tools such as the Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT) for non-verbal patients. Agitation is monitored using scales like the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) to avoid over-sedation while keeping patients comfortable and safe from self-extubation.

Delirium—an acute fluctuating brain dysfunction—is frequently missed. Tools like the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) help identify hyperactive, hypoactive, or mixed delirium subtypes so that non-pharmacological reorientation and targeted interventions can be implemented.

Documentation, Communication, and Continuous Reassessment

Accurate, timely documentation ensures continuity of care and provides a legal record of nursing interventions and patient responses. Critical care charts must reflect dynamic physiological trends rather than static numbers. Effective interprofessional communication using structured tools like ISBAR (Introduction, Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) prevents errors during emergency handovers or medical consultations.

Because a stable patient can deteriorate rapidly, reassessment is the final linchpin of critical care. Every medication, fluid bolus, or respiratory intervention must be evaluated for its clinical effect shortly after administration.
