Assessment of Critically Ill Patients

Introduction to Critical Care Nursing and Patient Assessment

Critical care nursing demands rapid, systematic evaluation of patients facing life-threatening organ failure. Unlike general ward care where assessments might occur every four hours, the unstable patient requires continuous observation and split-second clinical judgment. A nurse in this environment acts as the primary sentinel, detecting subtle physiological changes before overt collapse occurs. Assessment here is not a single event but a continuous loop of data collection, interpretation, intervention, and re-evaluation. Mastering this workflow prevents complications and directly impacts survival outcomes.

Initial Primary Survey: ABCDE Approach

The ABCDE approach provides a standardized framework to quickly identify and manage immediate threats to life. Every second counts, and the nurse must address each letter sequentially before moving forward.

  • Airway: Check for patency, remove foreign bodies, and ensure the tongue is not obstructing the throat.
  • Breathing: Assess respiratory rate, chest rise, depth, and oxygen saturation levels.
  • Circulation: Evaluate pulse rate, skin temperature, capillary refill, and blood pressure.
  • Disability: Quickly check neurological status using basic responsiveness scales like AVPU or GCS.
  • Exposure: Expose the body to check for hidden injuries or rashes while maintaining patient dignity and warmth.

Comprehensive Secondary Survey and System-Wise Evaluation

Once immediate life threats are controlled during the primary survey, the nurse performs a detailed secondary survey. This head-to-toe examination uncovers less obvious injuries or underlying medical issues. It involves a systematic review of the head, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, and extremities. A thorough medical history, often gathered from family members or old charts using the SAMPLE history framework (Symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past medical history, Last oral intake, Events leading up to illness), is integrated here. This step bridges emergency resuscitation and ongoing daily ICU management.

Neurological Assessment and Consciousness Scales

Neurological monitoring detects early signs of cerebral hypoxia, increased intracranial pressure, or drug toxicity. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) remains the gold standard, evaluating eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. Nurses must look past the total number; a sudden drop in the motor score is an urgent red flag. Pupil size, symmetry, and reactivity to light offer quick clues about brainstem function. For sedated patients, specialized tools like the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) help determine if sedation levels are appropriate.

Cardiovascular and Hemodynamic Monitoring

Cardiovascular assessment goes far beyond taking a standard blood pressure cuff reading. Nurses monitor continuous telemetry rhythms, peripheral pulses, skin perfusion, and capillary refill to gauge tissue oxygen delivery. In advanced critical care, invasive lines such as arterial lines and central venous catheters provide real-time blood pressure and fluid status data. A drop in urine output or cool, mottled extremities often signals failing cardiac output even when the blood pressure monitor appears normal. Integrating these physical and technological findings prevents missed shock states.

Respiratory Assessment and Airway Management

Respiratory failure is among the most frequent reasons for intensive care admission. The nurse assesses work of breathing by observing accessory muscle use, nasal flaring, and paradoxical chest wall movement. Auscultation reveals adventitious sounds like crackles, wheezes, or diminished breath areas. For patients on mechanical ventilators, checking endotracheal tube placement, ventilator pressures, and end-tidal carbon dioxide levels is essential. A common misconception is that high oxygen saturation readings guarantee adequate ventilation; however, a patient can have good oxygen numbers while retaining dangerous levels of carbon dioxide.

Renal, Gastrointestinal, and Fluid Balance Monitoring

Accurate fluid balance tracking prevents dangerous fluid overload or dehydration. Every drop of fluid entering the body through intravenous lines, medications, and enteral feeds must be meticulously recorded alongside outputs from urine, drains, and gastrointestinal losses. Hourly urine output serves as a sensitive indicator of kidney perfusion, with anything less than 0.5 mL/kg/hour signaling potential acute kidney injury. Gastrointestinal assessment involves checking bowel sounds, abdominal distension, and monitoring for feeding intolerance or gastric residual volumes.

Pain, Agitation, and Delirium Assessment in the ICU

Pain and anxiety are universal stressors in the intensive care unit, often worsened by mechanical ventilation and constant noise. If communicating verbally is impossible, nurses use behavioral tools like the Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS) to evaluate discomfort through facial expressions and muscle tension. Beyond pain, delirium is a frequent, life-threatening complication characterized by sudden shifts in mental status, confusion, and hallucinations. Using validation tools like the Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) ensures early detection, allowing the team to treat underlying triggers rather than just sedating the patient.

Documentation, Communication, and Continuous Reassessment

All assessment data must be communicated clearly and documented promptly to ensure safe multidisciplinary care. Structured handover methods, such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation), keep nursing reports concise during shift changes or emergency doctor calls. Because a critical patient's condition changes rapidly, no single assessment is ever final. The nurse must re-evaluate interventions regularly—such as checking response times after giving a blood pressure medication or confirming ventilator settings after patient repositioning—closing the loop on safe clinical practice.
