Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Cardiac Arrest

Cardiac arrest occurs when the heart abruptly ceases to pump blood effectively, depriving vital organs of oxygen. This state differs fundamentally from a heart attack, although a myocardial infarction can trigger it. Without immediate intervention, irreversible brain damage begins within four to six minutes. Understanding this timeline underpins every protocol in emergency resuscitation.

During cardiac arrest, cellular hypoxia develops rapidly. The cessation of coronary and cerebral perfusion forces cells into anaerobic metabolism, leading to a swift accumulation of lactic acid and metabolic acidosis. Cellular energy stores deplete, membrane pumps fail, and intracellular calcium accumulates, causing cell death. The primary electrical rhythms observed at this critical juncture include ventricular fibrillation, pulseless ventricular tachycardia, pulseless electrical activity, and asystole.

The Chain of Survival

The chain of survival represents a sequence of coordinated events that maximizes the likelihood of intact neurological recovery from cardiac arrest. A weak link anywhere in this chain dramatically reduces patient survival rates.

  • Early Recognition and Prevention: Identifying risk factors and spotting pre-arrest warning signs to prevent catastrophic collapse.
  • Immediate High-Quality CPR: Initiating chest compressions swiftly to generate minimal but vital blood flow to the heart and brain.
  • Rapid Defibrillation: Applying an automated or manual defibrillator promptly to reset chaotic heart rhythms like ventricular fibrillation.
  • Advanced Life Support: Delivering specialized medical care, advanced airway management, and pharmacotherapy by trained personnel.
  • Post-Cardiac Arrest Care: Managing systemic reperfusion, temperature regulation, and hemodynamic stability in a specialized critical care unit.

Assessment and Recognition

Quick and accurate assessment saves precious seconds. Clinical evaluation begins by ensuring scene safety, followed by checking the patient for responsiveness by tapping their shoulder and shouting. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing—or only gasping, which is a sign of agonal respiration—the clinician must immediately declare cardiac arrest.

Simultaneously, pulse checks should take no longer than ten seconds, palpating the carotid artery in adults or the brachial artery in infants. Agonal gasps are frequently misinterpreted by bystanders as normal breathing, delaying vital care. Professional healthcare personnel must recognize agonal respiration as an indicator of cardiac arrest and act without hesitation.

High-Quality CPR Techniques and Compression-Ventilation Ratios

High-quality chest compressions are the cornerstone of successful resuscitation. Poor technique drastically reduces coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure.

  • Rate: Push hard and fast, maintaining a frequency of 100 to 120 compressions per minute.
  • Depth: Depress the sternum at least 5 centimeters (2 inches) in adults, but no more than 6 centimeters to avoid structural trauma.
  • Recoil: Allow complete chest recoil after every compression to let the heart chambers refill with blood.
  • Interruptions: Minimize pauses in chest compressions; interruptions must never exceed ten seconds.
  • Ratio: The standard compression-to-ventilation ratio for untrained bystanders and single rescuers is 30:2.

Use of Automated External Defibrillators

An automated external defibrillator analyzes the heart rhythm and delivers an electrical shock if a shockable rhythm is detected. Immediate access to a defibrillator greatly increases survival rates for ventricular fibrillation.

Upon arrival of the device, power it on immediately and attach the adhesive pads to the patient's bare chest. Follow the visual and voice prompts provided by the machine. Ensure that no one is touching the patient during rhythm analysis and prior to pressing the shock button. Once the shock is delivered, resume high-quality chest compressions immediately, starting with compressions rather than checking for a pulse.

Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support Interventions

Advanced life support builds upon basic life support by introducing definitive airway control, vascular access, and targeted drug therapy. Endotracheal intubation or supraglottic airway placement isolates the airway and permits continuous chest compressions synchronized with ventilation.

Intravenous or intraosseous access enables the administration of medications such as epinephrine, which increases coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure via vasoconstriction. Antiarrhythmic agents like amiodarone are administered for refractory ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia. Reversible causes, often referred to as the H's and T's—such as hypoxia, hypovolemia, hydrogen ions, and tension pneumothorax—must be systematically investigated and treated.

Post-Resuscitation Care

Restoring spontaneous circulation marks only the first milestone; post-resuscitation care focuses on preventing secondary organ injury and managing the underlying etiology. The systemic ischemia-reperfusion response often triggers multi-organ dysfunction syndrome and neurological damage.

Optimizing hemodynamics involves maintaining adequate mean arterial pressure using intravenous fluids and vasoactive infusions. Targeted temperature management is often implemented to cool patients who remain comatose, reducing cerebral metabolic demand and neuronal apoptosis. Continuous monitoring of oxygenation, ventilation, and blood glucose prevents secondary insults to the recovering brain and myocardium.

Ethical Considerations and Termination of Resuscitation

Resuscitation efforts involve complex ethical decisions regarding when to start, continue, or stop interventions. Healthcare professionals must respect valid advance directives and Do Not Resuscitate orders. In the absence of documentation, the general presumption favors initiating life-saving measures.

Deciding when to terminate resuscitation depends on factors such as the initial cardiac rhythm, downtime prior to CPR, patient comorbidities, and response to advanced therapies. Prolonged resuscitation in the absence of return of spontaneous circulation or a shockable rhythm rarely results in favorable neurological survival, prompting multidisciplinary consensus to cease efforts with compassion and dignity.
