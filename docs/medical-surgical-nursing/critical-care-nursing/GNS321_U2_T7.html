Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

Introduction to Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) is an emergency, lifesaving procedure performed when the heart stops beating effectively. Cardiac arrest is a critical medical emergency where the heart abruptly ceases to pump blood, depriving vital organs like the brain and kidneys of oxygenated blood. For nursing students in Nigeria, mastering CPR is foundational to clinical competency because prompt intervention can mean the difference between life and death in hospital wards, emergency departments, and community settings. The primary goal of CPR is to manually maintain circulatory flow and oxygenation until advanced medical care can restore spontaneous circulation. Basic Life Support (BLS) involves recognizing sudden cardiac arrest, activating the emergency response system, performing high-quality chest compressions, and delivering rescue breaths. Effective CPR slows down tissue necrosis and buys precious time for healthcare providers to apply defibrillation or administer resuscitative medications. Understanding the history, rationale, and clinical indicators for initiating CPR empowers nurses to act swiftly and decisively without hesitation. Through structured training and regular practice simulations, student nurses develop the muscle memory and clinical judgment required to lead or assist effectively during resuscitation emergencies in any healthcare environment.

Chain of Survival in Basic and Advanced Life Support

The Chain of Survival represents a sequence of integrated actions that, when executed promptly and in order, maximize the probability of survival from cardiac arrest. For out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, the links include early recognition and prevention, activation of emergency response services, immediate high-quality CPR, rapid defibrillation with an automated external defibrillator (AED), advanced life support, and integrated post-cardiac arrest care. In-hospital settings have a parallel chain that emphasizes surveillance, prevention, and rapid response teams to intercept clinical deterioration before cardiac arrest occurs. Nursing students must understand that every link is interconnected; a failure in any single link drastically reduces the patient's chances of neurologically intact survival. Rapid activation of emergency systems brings skilled personnel and life-saving tools like the AED closer to the victim faster. Defibrillation is especially crucial for shockable rhythms such as ventricular fibrillation, terminating the chaotic electrical activity and allowing the heart's natural pacemaker to resume normal rhythm. Advanced Life Support (ALS) builds upon Basic Life Support by introducing advanced airway management, intravenous or intraosseous drug administration, and continuous cardiac monitoring. By mastering these links, nurses contribute to a cohesive multidisciplinary approach that optimizes patient outcomes across various clinical environments.

Pathophysiology of Cardiac Arrest

Understanding the pathophysiology of cardiac arrest is essential for nurses to appreciate why certain resuscitative interventions are performed. Cardiac arrest typically results from primary cardiac arrhythmias, most commonly ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (VT), where the heart muscle quivers incoherently instead of contracting synchronously. Other non-shockable rhythms include pulseless electrical activity (PEA) and asystole, which represent a total lack of organized mechanical cardiac contraction despite organized electrical activity or flatline, respectively. When the heart stops pumping, cellular hypoxia ensues rapidly, particularly in oxygen-sensitive tissues like the brain, leading to irreversible brain damage within four to six minutes if perfusion is not restored. Anaerobic metabolism takes over, producing lactic acid and resulting in severe metabolic acidosis, which compromises myocardial contractility and cellular enzyme function. Coronary perfusion pressure drops significantly during cardiac arrest, making chest compressions vital to generate minimal blood flow to the heart muscle itself. Reversible causes, often known as the 'Hs and Ts' (Hypoxia, Hypovolemia, Hydrogen ion acidosis, Hypo/Hyperkalemia, Hypothermia, Toxins, Tamponade cardiac, Tension pneumothorax, Thrombosis pulmonary, Thrombosis coronary), must be systematically identified and treated during resuscitation. Nurses must stay vigilant, as identifying these underlying triggers can reverse the arrest and save the patient's life.

Assessment and Recognition of Cardiac Arrest

Early and accurate assessment is the gateway to effective resuscitation. When a nurse encounters an unresponsive patient, the initial step is to ensure scene safety, followed by checking for responsiveness by gently tapping the patient's shoulders and shouting, 'Are you okay?' Next, the nurse must simultaneously check for normal breathing and assess the carotid pulse for no more than 10 seconds. Gasping, also known as agonal respirations, is not normal breathing and must be recognized as a sign of cardiac arrest, prompting immediate action. If the patient is unresponsive, not breathing normally, and has no definite pulse, the nurse must immediately activate the emergency response team or call for help and retrieve an AED. Time is of the essence; prolonged assessment delays vital chest compressions and worsens patient prognosis. Nurses should avoid wasting time checking peripheral pulses like the radial or brachial pulse, as vasoconstriction during cardiac arrest makes them unreliable. Regular simulation practice helps student nurses perform these assessments efficiently and calmly under pressure. Once cardiac arrest is confirmed, high-quality chest compressions must begin without delay to restore minimal systemic perfusion.

High-Quality CPR Techniques and Compression-Ventilation Ratios

High-quality CPR is the single most important determinant of survival in cardiac arrest. Key components of high-quality CPR include pushing hard and fast: compressions should be at a depth of at least 2 inches (5 cm) for adults, with a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Allowing complete chest recoil after each compression is critical because it allows the heart to refill with blood between compressions. Rescuers must minimize interruptions in chest compressions, keeping pauses to less than 10 seconds, even when inserting advanced airways or analyzing heart rhythms. For adult victims, the compression-ventilation ratio for single rescuers or untrained bystanders is 30 compressions to 2 breaths. When an advanced airway (such as an endotracheal tube or supraglottic device) is in place, rescuers can deliver continuous compressions at 100 to 120 per minute while providing one breath every 6 seconds without pausing compressions. Avoid excessive ventilation, as too much volume or rate increases intrathoracic pressure, decreases venous return to the heart, and reduces overall survival rates. Nursing students must practice proper body mechanics—kneeling close to the patient, locking elbows, and using upper body weight—to maintain effective compression depth and prevent physical exhaustion during prolonged resuscitation attempts.

Use of Automated External Defibrillators (AED)

An Automated External Defibrillator (AED) is a computerized, portable medical device that analyzes heart rhythms and delivers an electrical shock to restore normal heart rhythm in victims of sudden cardiac arrest. As soon as an AED is available, nurses must turn it on and follow its visual and voice prompts immediately. The device will instruct the user to attach adhesive electrode pads to the patient's bare chest—one pad below the right collarbone and the other on the lower left rib cage. Once the pads are applied, the AED commands everyone to stand clear while it analyzes the underlying cardiac rhythm. If a shockable rhythm like ventricular fibrillation is detected, the AED charges and advises the operator to press the shock button, ensuring no one is touching the patient. Following the shock delivery, or if no shock is advised, the rescuer must immediately resume CPR, starting with chest compressions. AEDs are designed to be extremely user-friendly, allowing trained healthcare providers and laypersons alike to safely operate them in emergency settings. Prompt use of an AED alongside high-quality CPR dramatically increases the chances of survival for patients experiencing sudden cardiac arrest.

Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support Interventions

Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ALS) interventions take over when basic life support alone is insufficient to achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). ALS includes advanced airway management (such as endotracheal intubation or supraglottic airway insertion) to secure a patent airway and deliver mechanical ventilation with supplemental oxygen. Vascular access, via intravenous (IV) or intraosseous (IO) routes, is established promptly to administer life-saving emergency medications. Epinephrine is the primary vasopressor used in cardiac arrest, administered every 3 to 5 minutes to vasoconstrict peripheral vessels and improve coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure. Antiarrhythmic drugs like amiodarone or lidocaine are administered for refractory ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia that does not respond to initial shocks. Team leadership, clear communication, and structured role delegation (using the Crew Resource Management model) are vital components during ALS implementation to minimize errors and chaos. Nurses participating in ALS teams must be skilled in medication administration, preparation of intubation equipment, and continuous monitoring of rhythm strips. Working cohesively ensures that advanced interventions run smoothly alongside ongoing, high-quality chest compressions.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Nursing Management

Post-resuscitation care begins immediately after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) is achieved. The goals of post-resuscitation care are to optimize cardiopulmonary function, transport or transfer the patient to an intensive care unit (ICU), identify and treat the precipitating cause of arrest, and preserve neurological function. Nurses must continuously monitor vital signs, maintaining adequate blood pressure and oxygen saturation while avoiding hypoxemia and hyperoxia. Targeted Temperature Management (TTM), maintaining a constant core body temperature between 32°C and 36°C for at least 24 hours, is frequently implemented for comatose adult patients to improve neurological outcomes and reduce brain injury. Hemodynamic instability is common after cardiac arrest; therefore, nurses may need to titrate vasoactive infusions (such as norepinephrine or dopamine) under medical direction to maintain organ perfusion. Identifying the underlying cause—such as acute coronary syndrome, electrolyte imbalances, or pulmonary embolism—guides definitive medical or surgical therapies like percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). Meticulous nursing documentation, ongoing neurological assessments, and family support are critical pillars of comprehensive post-cardiac arrest nursing management in critical care settings.

Ethical Considerations and Termination of Resuscitation

Resuscitation efforts involve profound ethical considerations regarding the sanctity of life, patient autonomy, and the allocation of healthcare resources. Healthcare providers must honor valid Advance Directives, Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) orders, and specific living wills that express a patient's clear wishes regarding end-of-life care. In the absence of clear documentation, the default clinical standard is to initiate resuscitation immediately while attempting to verify any known patient preferences with family members or legal surrogates. Deciding when to terminate resuscitation is challenging; factors influencing this decision include the initial cardiac rhythm, downtime before CPR started, patient age, comorbidities, and the duration of resuscitation efforts without ROSC. Nurses play a crucial advocacy and supportive role, ensuring that ethical principles such as beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice are upheld throughout the crisis. Clear, compassionate communication with grieving family members is essential when resuscitation efforts are unsuccessful or stopped. Nursing students must reflect on these ethical dilemmas to build resilience, professional integrity, and sensitivity toward patients and families facing terminal health events.
