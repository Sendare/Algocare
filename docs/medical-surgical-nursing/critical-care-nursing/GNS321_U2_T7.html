Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

Introduction and Chain of Survival

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, commonly known as CPR, is an emergency lifesaving procedure performed when someone's breathing or heartbeat has stopped. The primary goal is to maintain a flow of oxygenated blood to the brain and vital organs until advanced medical help arrives or normal heart function is restored. The Chain of Survival represents the series of critical events that must occur rapidly to maximize the chance of survival from cardiac arrest.

  • Early recognition and prevention: Spotting danger and calling for help before cardiac arrest occurs.
  • Early CPR: Immediate chest compressions to buy vital time.
  • Rapid defibrillation: Using an AED to shock a shockable heart rhythm.
  • Advanced life support: Specialized medical care by trained health personnel.
  • Post-cardiac arrest care: Comprehensive hospital treatment to improve long-term recovery.

Pathophysiology and Assessment

When sudden cardiac arrest strikes, the heart abruptly ceases to pump blood effectively. The most common initial rhythm in adult sudden cardiac arrest is ventricular fibrillation, where the heart muscle quivers uselessly instead of contracting. Without blood flow, cellular hypoxia sets in rapidly, leading to irreversible brain damage within minutes.

Nurses must quickly assess the situation by checking for responsiveness, shouting for help, and simultaneously checking for normal breathing and a carotid pulse for no longer than 10 seconds. Agonal gasps—which sound like occasional, snorting, or labored breaths—do not count as normal breathing and are a common sign of cardiac arrest.

High-Quality CPR Techniques

Performing effective chest compressions is the single most important determinant of survival. High-quality CPR requires strict adherence to specific biomechanical parameters to ensure adequate artificial perfusion.

  • Rate: Push hard and fast at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute.
  • Depth: Compress the adult chest at least 2 inches (5 centimeters), avoiding depths greater than 2.4 inches.
  • Recoil: Allow complete chest recoil after every compression to let the heart refill with blood.
  • Interruptions: Minimize pauses in chest compressions, keeping interruptions under 10 seconds.
  • Ratio: Maintain a compression-to-ventilation ratio of 30:2 for single rescuers in adults.

Automated External Defibrillator (AED) Operation

An AED is a computerized device that analyzes the heart rhythm and delivers an electrical shock if a shockable rhythm is detected. As soon as an AED arrives, the rescuer should turn it on and follow the clear visual and voice prompts.

  1. Power on: Open the case and switch the device on.
  2. Attach pads: Place adhesive electrode pads on the bare chest according to the diagram on the pads—one on the upper right chest and the other on the lower left side.
  3. Clear the patient: Ensure nobody is touching the patient while the machine analyzes the rhythm.
  4. Deliver shock: If advised, push the flashing shock button, then immediately resume high-quality CPR starting with chest compressions.

Advanced Life Support and Pharmacology

When advanced medical personnel arrive, basic life support transitions to Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS). This phase includes secure airway management via endotracheal intubation or supraglottic devices, continuous electrocardiogram monitoring, and intravenous or intraosseous drug administration.

Epinephrine is the primary vasoactive drug used in cardiac arrest, administered every 3 to 5 minutes to vasoconstrict blood vessels and improve coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure. For shock-refractory ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia, antiarrhythmic medications such as amiodarone or lidocaine are given after defibrillation attempts.

Post-Resuscitation Care and Nursing Management

Return of spontaneous circulation is a major milestone, but the patient remains in a fragile, highly unstable state. Post-resuscitation care focuses on optimizing ventilation, maintaining hemodynamic stability, and identifying the precipitating cause of the arrest.

Nurses closely monitor vital signs, arterial blood gases, and urine output. Maintaining normothermia or targeted temperature management is often implemented to protect neurological function in comatose patients. Continuous monitoring for re-arrest, arrhythmias, and multi-organ dysfunction requires vigilant nursing assessment in an intensive care unit environment.

Ethical Considerations and Termination

Deciding when to start or stop resuscitation involves complex ethical and clinical judgments. Healthcare providers must respect valid advanced directives, do-not-resuscitate orders, or clear expressions of patient autonomy.

When resuscitation efforts are underway, the healthcare team must consider whether further intervention is futile. Prolonged CPR with no return of spontaneous circulation, absence of a shockable rhythm, and asystole lasting beyond standard timeframes without reversible causes often guide the physician's decision to terminate efforts. Nurses play a crucial role in supporting grieving families and documenting the timeline of events accurately.
