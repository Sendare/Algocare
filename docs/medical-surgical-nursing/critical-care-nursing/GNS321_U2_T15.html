Boat Nurses

Introduction to Boat Nursing and Maritime Healthcare

Boat nursing, also known as maritime or cruise nursing, involves providing professional healthcare services aboard watercraft ranging from small research vessels and offshore oil rigs to large commercial cruise liners and naval ships. In West Africa, particularly in the Niger Delta region of Nigeria, maritime nurses play a critical role aboard tugboats, supply vessels, and floating production storage and offloading (FPSO) units. These practitioners bridge the gap between land-based medical facilities and isolated maritime workers, managing everything from routine occupational health checks to acute traumatic injuries miles away from the nearest hospital.

Roles and Responsibilities Aboard Watercraft

The responsibilities of a maritime nurse extend far beyond standard bedside care. A typical day combines occupational health, primary care, emergency response, and public health management. Duties include:

  • Primary Care Delivery: Treating common ailments such as seasickness, gastroenteritis, heat exhaustion, and upper respiratory tract infections.
  • Occupational Health: Monitoring potable water safety, inspecting galley hygiene, and ensuring compliance with maritime health regulations.
  • Emergency Triage: Acting as the first responder for industrial accidents, machinery injuries, or man-overboard recovery scenarios.
  • Administrative Tasks: Managing shipboard medical inventories, documenting treatments in official ship logs, and coordinating radio communications with shore-side physicians.

Environmental Challenges and Constraints

Providing care on a moving platform introduces unique physiological and logistical hurdles. Constant pitching, rolling, and vibration complicate physical examinations, IV cannulation, and medication administration. Space limitations inside shipboard sickbays mean equipment must be securely stowed to prevent projectiles during rough seas. Furthermore, limited freshwater supplies impact hygiene protocols, and chronic noise from ship engines creates barriers to effective verbal communication during patient assessments.

Patient Assessment and Triage at Sea

Triage in a maritime setting requires immense clinical judgment because definitive care may be days away. Nurses utilize systematic assessment frameworks to categorize injuries rapidly. When multiple casualties occur due to an engine room fire or deck mishap, differentiating between immediate, urgent, and delayed cases ensures that limited medical stores and oxygen supplies are allocated to those with the highest chance of survival.

Emergency Management and Trauma Care

Maritime trauma often stems from heavy machinery accidents, falls from rigging, burns from engine room flares, or near-drowning incidents. The boat nurse must initiate advanced first aid, control severe hemorrhaging using tourniquets, manage airway patency, and treat hypothermia. Unlike land-based nursing where backup arrives within minutes, the boat nurse must function autonomously for hours or days, stabilizing the patient until rescue coordination centers can intervene.

Medical Equipment and Telehealth Utilization

Modern vessel sickbays are equipped with specialized telemedicine kits, satellite communication devices, automated external defibrillators (AEDs), and portable diagnostic tools like point-of-care ultrasound. Telehealth allows boat nurses to transmit vital signs, ECG tracings, and digital imagery directly to land-based emergency physicians (often via services like TMAS - Telemedical Maritime Assistance Service). Knowing how to operate and troubleshoot these communication technologies is just as important as knowing how to administer medications.

Medical Evacuation (Medevac) and Patient Transport Protocols

When a patient's condition exceeds the scope of shipboard care, a medical evacuation (medevac) must be organized. This process involves coordinating with maritime rescue coordination centers (MRCC), coast guards, or helicopter rescue teams. Preparing a patient for a hoist operation or transfer to a smaller pilot boat requires securing lines, packaging the patient safely against environmental exposure, and handing over a concise, written SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) report.
