Fever

Normal Body Temperature Regulation and Thermoregulation

Human core body temperature is tightly controlled within a narrow range, typically between 36.5°C and 37.5°C, to ensure optimal enzymatic and metabolic function. The hypothalamus, specifically the preoptic area, acts as the body's central thermostat. It continuously compares thermal input from peripheral thermoreceptors in the skin and central thermoreceptors in the spinal cord, abdominal organs, and the hypothalamus itself against a baseline set point.

When ambient temperature drops or infection occurs, thermoregulatory mechanisms are triggered to generate and conserve heat. These physiological responses include:

  • Vasoconstriction: Constriction of blood vessels in the skin minimizes cutaneous blood flow, reducing radiative and convective heat loss.
  • Shivering: Involuntary rhythmic skeletal muscle contractions rapidly increase cellular metabolism and heat production.
  • Behavioral changes: Putting on warmer clothing or curling up to minimize body surface area exposed to the environment.

Conversely, when core temperature rises, the hypothalamus stimulates vasodilation to increase blood flow to the skin, promoting heat dissipation through radiation and convection. Sweat glands are simultaneously activated, allowing evaporative cooling to lower temperature back to baseline.

Definition and Pathophysiology of Fever

Fever, medically termed pyrexia, is a temporary elevation in body temperature above the normal daily variation, driven by an upward resetting of the hypothalamic thermostat. This elevation is distinct from hyperthermia, an uncontrolled rise in body temperature caused by environmental heat load or metabolic dysfunction where the hypothalamic set point remains normal.

The pathophysiology of fever involves a cascade of immune signaling molecules:

  1. Exogenous pyrogens: Pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, or their toxins enter the body and stimulate immune cells.
  2. Cytokine release: Macrophages and monocytes release endogenous pyrogens, primarily interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha).
  3. Prostaglandin E2 synthesis: These cytokines travel to the brain, where endothelial cells of the vascular organ of the lamina terminalis synthesize prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) via the cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) enzyme pathway.
  4. Set point elevation: PGE2 binds to receptors in the preoptic area of the hypothalamus, raising the thermal set point. The body responds as if it is hypothermic, initiating heat-generating mechanisms until core temperature matches the new, higher target.

Etiology and Common Causes in Nigerian Clinical Practice

In Nigerian clinical settings, fever is the most frequent presenting complaint across emergency departments, pediatric wards, and outpatient clinics. While infectious etiologies predominate, recognizing local epidemiological patterns is vital for accurate diagnosis and prompt management.

  • Malaria: Caused predominantly by *Plasmodium falciparum* and transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes, it remains a leading cause of febrile illness at all ages.
  • Typhoid Fever: A systemic infection caused by *Salmonella typhi*, typically acquired through ingestion of contaminated food or water, characterized by step-ladder temperature charts and abdominal discomfort.
  • Upper and Lower Respiratory Tract Infections: Ranging from viral common colds to bacterial pneumonias and bronchiolitis, particularly prevalent during the harmattan season.
  • Urinary Tract Infections: Frequently encountered, especially in pregnant women and young children, often presenting with fever as the primary or sole systemic sign.
  • Viral Hemorrhagic Fevers: Outbreaks of Lassa fever require high clinical suspicion in endemic zones to ensure strict barrier nursing and early antiviral intervention with ribavirin.
  • Meningitis: Bacterial meningitis presents unique challenges, particularly during dry season outbreaks in the meningitis belt, necessitating rapid lumbar puncture and empirical antibiotics.

Classification and Patterns of Fever

Documenting the pattern of temperature fluctuations over time can offer valuable diagnostic clues for the physician. Fever is generally classified by duration as acute (lasting less than 7 days, typically infectious), subacute, or chronic/fever of unknown origin (FUO), which persists for more than three weeks without an identified cause after standard diagnostic workup.

Specific temperature charting patterns include:

  • Sustained or Continuous Fever: Temperature remains elevated with minimal diurnal fluctuation of less than 1°C, commonly seen in lobar pneumonia or typhoid fever.
  • Remittent Fever: Temperature fluctuates over the course of 24 hours but never returns to the normal baseline, characteristic of many bacterial infections and infective endocarditis.
  • Intermittent Fever: Temperature spikes are interspersed with periods of normal or subnormal temperature each day, observed in sepsis, abscesses, or malaria.
  • Relenting or Pel-Ebstein Fever: Periods of high fever lasting several days alternate with afebrile periods lasting days to weeks, classically associated with Hodgkin lymphoma.

Clinical Assessment and Vital Signs Monitoring

Comprehensive assessment of a febrile patient requires accurate measurement of core or proxy body temperatures using reliable routes such as oral, tympanic, axillary, or rectal methods. The nurse must note accompanying signs and symptoms, including chills, rigors, diaphoresis, flushing, tachycardia, and tachypnea. Physiological rule of thumb dictates that for every 1°C rise in body temperature above 37°C, the heart rate increases by approximately 10 beats per minute.

Thorough evaluation also involves screening for localized sources of infection. Nurses should inspect the oropharynx for exudates, palpate lymph nodes, auscultate lung fields for adventitious sounds, and inquire about urinary or gastrointestinal symptoms. Documenting the exact timing and character of chills is crucial, as violent rigors typically coincide with rapid upward shifts in core temperature during bacteremic episodes.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Tests

Laboratory investigations for a febrile patient are guided by history and physical examination findings to identify the underlying pathology. In high-prevalence regions like Nigeria, initial screening commonly includes a Rapid Diagnostic Test (RDT) for malaria or a thick and thin blood film for microscopic parasite visualization.

Additional baseline diagnostics include:

  • Complete Blood Count (CBC): Evaluates total white blood cell count and differential to differentiate between bacterial infections (typically leukocytosis with a left shift) and viral infections (often leukopenia or normal counts).
  • Blood Cultures: Serial aerobic and anaerobic cultures drawn before initiating antibiotic therapy when systemic bacterial sepsis or endocarditis is suspected.
  • Urinalysis and Urine Culture: Essential for detecting occult urinary tract infections, pyuria, and bacteriuria.
  • Widal Test or Serology: Used alongside clinical findings to support enteric fever diagnosis, keeping in mind regional baseline titers.
  • C-Reactive Protein (CRP) and Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR): Non-specific inflammatory markers that indicate the presence and severity of systemic inflammation.

Nursing Interventions and Medical Management

Medical management of fever focuses primarily on treating the underlying cause, such as prescribing antimalarials, antibiotics, or antivirals. Antipyretic medications like paracetamol (acetaminophen) inhibit central prostaglandin synthesis, lowering the hypothalamic set point and easing patient discomfort.

Nursing interventions aim to optimize comfort and safety while reducing metabolic demand:

  • Administering Antipyretics: Give prescribed analgesics and antipyretics on a scheduled basis rather than strictly PRN if fevers are recurrent and distressing.
  • Promoting Hydration: Encourage frequent oral fluid intake to replace losses from sweating and increased metabolic rate, monitoring intravenous infusion rates when oral routes are impaired.
  • Environmental Adjustment: Maintain a well-ventilated room, adjust bed linens, and remove excessive clothing to facilitate natural heat loss.
  • Avoiding Harmful Practices: Refrain from using ice-water immersion, cold alcohol rubs, or rigid physical cooling blankets, as these induce shivering, vasoconstriction, and dramatic spikes in core temperature.

Potential Complications and Patient Education

While fever is primarily a protective immune response, high elevations or prolonged states can lead to significant complications. Febrile seizures are a notable risk in young children between six months and five years of age, occurring during rapid temperature spikes. Dehydration and electrolyte imbalance frequently develop due to insensible fluid losses from tachypnea and diaphoresis combined with inadequate oral intake. Severe hyperpyrexia exceeding 41.1°C can cause direct cellular damage, delirium, and neurological impairment.

Discharge and home-care education must empower patients and caregivers to manage low-grade fevers safely and recognize danger signs. Nurses should instruct families on correct paracetamol dosage calculations based on weight rather than age, emphasize the importance of completing full antibiotic courses, and outline warning signs that require immediate return to a healthcare facility, such as persistent vomiting, stiff neck, altered consciousness, or respiratory distress.
