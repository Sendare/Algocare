Fever

Definition and Pathophysiology of Fever

Fever, medically referred to as pyrexia, is defined as a temporary elevation in the body's core temperature above the normal daily variation, typically triggered by the hypothalamus. This physiological response is not a disease in itself, but rather a vital clinical sign that the body's immune system is actively combating an internal threat, such as an infection, inflammation, or malignancy. The pathophysiology of fever begins when exogenous pyrogens, which include microbial toxins, bacteria, viruses, and fungi, invade the body. These exogenous substances stimulate host immune cells, specifically macrophages and monocytes, to synthesize and release endogenous pyrogens, most notably cytokines such as Interleukin-1 (IL-1), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), Tumor Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-a), and interferons. Once released into the bloodstream, these cytokines travel to the brain, specifically targeting the vascular organ of the lamina terminalis (VOLT) and the preoptic area of the anterior hypothalamus. In the hypothalamus, these inflammatory mediators stimulate the local synthesis of Prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) through the arachidonic acid cascade, mediated by the enzyme cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2). PGE2 acts upon the thermoregulatory center to effectively elevate the hypothalamic set-point to a higher temperature level. In response to this newly established higher set-point, the hypothalamus perceives the normal baseline body temperature as being too cold. It consequently initiates autonomic and behavioral mechanisms to generate and conserve heat. These compensatory mechanisms include peripheral vasoconstriction to minimize heat loss through the skin, shivering to actively generate muscular heat, and behavioral changes such as seeking warmth or putting on extra blankets. Once the underlying trigger is resolved and endogenous pyrogen production ceases, PGE2 levels drop, the hypothalamic set-point returns to its normal baseline, and heat-loss mechanisms such as vasodilation and sweating (defervescence) are activated to rid the body of excess heat.

Normal Body Temperature Regulation and Thermoregulation

Body temperature regulation is a tightly controlled physiological process managed primarily by the thermoregulatory center located in the preoptic area of the anterior hypothalamus. This central control system acts much like a domestic thermostat, continuously receiving input signals from peripheral thermal receptors located in the skin and mucous membranes, as well as central thermal receptors situated in the spinal cord, abdominal organs, and the hypothalamus itself. These receptors monitor changes in ambient and core temperatures, transmitting sensory information via nerve pathways to the hypothalamic integration center. To maintain thermal homeostasis within a narrow normal range, typically between 36.5°C and 37.5°C, the hypothalamus balances heat production (thermogenesis) with heat loss (thermolysis). Heat production is primarily a byproduct of cellular metabolism, occurring continuously in organs such as the liver and skeletal muscles. When the body requires additional heat, mechanisms like involuntary skeletal muscle contractions (shivering), increased cellular metabolic rate mediated by thyroid hormones and catecholamines, and voluntary physical activity are engaged. Conversely, when core body temperature rises above the normal set-point, the hypothalamus initiates mechanisms to dissipate excess heat. These include cutaneous vasodilation, which increases blood flow to the skin surface to facilitate radiant and convective heat loss, and eccrine sweat gland activation, which promotes evaporative cooling through perspiration. Behavioral adaptations, such as removing heavy clothing, moving to a cooler environment, or drinking cool fluids, also play a crucial role in supporting physiological thermoregulation. Disruptions in this delicate balance can lead to pathological states such as hyperthermia—where the body temperature rises uncontrolled without an alteration in the hypothalamic set-point—or fever, where the set-point is intentionally elevated by pyrogens.

Etiology and Common Causes of Fever in Nigerian Clinical Practice

In Nigerian clinical practice, fever is one of the most frequent presenting complaints across all age groups in both primary healthcare centers and tertiary hospitals. The etiology of fever in this geographical region is diverse, heavily influenced by tropical infectious diseases, environmental factors, and local epidemiological patterns. Malaria, caused predominantly by Plasmodium falciparum, remains the single most common cause of fever in Nigeria, presenting with periodic chills, rigors, and systemic symptoms. Other notable vector-borne and zoonotic infections include Lassa fever, an acute viral hemorrhagic fever endemic in parts of West Africa, and Dengue fever. Bacterial infections are also extremely prevalent; typhoid fever, caused by Salmonella typhi, is frequently encountered due to contaminated water and food sources, while bacterial meningitis, tuberculosis, urinary tract infections, and community-acquired pneumonia contribute significantly to the disease burden. Viral illnesses such as measles, viral gastroenteritis, and acute respiratory tract infections are common, particularly among pediatric populations. Furthermore, emerging and re-emerging infectious threats, including seasonal outbreaks of cholera and viral respiratory illnesses, require nurses to maintain a high index of suspicion. Non-infectious etiologies of fever must not be overlooked in Nigerian settings and include malignancies such as lymphomas and leukemias, connective tissue disorders, acute inflammatory conditions, and adverse drug reactions. Nursing students and practicing clinicians must conduct thorough patient histories—including recent travel, occupational exposure, and vaccination status—combined with appropriate diagnostic testing to accurately identify the underlying etiology and initiate timely, evidence-based management.

Classification and Patterns of Fever

Fever can be classified and described using various patterns based on how body temperature fluctuates over a period of 24 hours. Recognizing these distinct fever patterns can provide vital clues to the underlying etiology, assisting the healthcare team in formulating an accurate differential diagnosis. A sustained or continuous fever is characterized by a persistent elevation in body temperature with little or no fluctuation (less than 1°C variation) over a 24-hour period, commonly seen in conditions such as typhoid fever. A remittent fever exhibits marked temperature fluctuations throughout the day, where the temperature drops significantly but never reaches the normal baseline; this pattern is frequently observed in infective endocarditis and certain bacterial respiratory infections. An intermittent fever features temperature elevations that are present only for certain periods during the day, alternating with normal or subnormal temperatures, a classic pattern seen in malaria (such as quotidian, tertian, or quartan paroxysms). A relenting or relapsing fever is characterized by febrile episodes lasting for a few days alternating with periods of normal temperature lasting for days or weeks, which can be seen in conditions like brucellosis or Pel-Ebstein fever associated with Hodgkin's lymphoma. Clinicians also classify fevers by their duration: acute fevers last less than seven days and are usually self-limiting viral infections, subacute fevers last up to two weeks, and fevers of unknown origin (FUO) are defined as elevated temperatures exceeding 38.3°C for more than three weeks with no clear diagnosis despite initial outpatient or inpatient evaluation. Understanding these classifications empowers nursing students to document temperature charts meticulously and communicate meaningful clinical trends to the medical team.

Clinical Assessment and Vital Signs Monitoring

Clinical assessment of a patient with fever requires a systematic approach that combines a comprehensive health history with precise vital signs monitoring. The nurse must first evaluate the subjective symptoms reported by the patient or caregivers, which often include chills, rigors, malaise, generalized body aches (myalgia), headache, anorexia, and diaphoresis. A thorough history should capture the onset, duration, and pattern of the fever, recent travel history, medication use, immunization history, and any localized symptoms such as cough, dysuria, abdominal pain, or skin rashes. Objective assessment centers on accurate measurement and continuous monitoring of vital signs. Core body temperature can be assessed via various routes including oral, tympanic, axillary, and rectal measurements, with rectal being the most reliable reflection of core temperature in clinical settings, though axillary is widely used in routine practice in Nigeria. Accompanying vital signs must be closely monitored because fever directly impacts cardiovascular and respiratory parameters. Specifically, for every 1°C rise in body temperature above 37°C, the heart rate typically increases by approximately 10 beats per minute (a physiological phenomenon known as Faget's sign or general tachycardic response), and the respiratory rate increases correspondingly to meet the heightened metabolic oxygen demand. Blood pressure must also be monitored, as severe infections can lead to septic shock characterized by hypotension. Nurses must document all findings meticulously on the patient's observation chart, noting any signs of neurological compromise such as lethargy, confusion, or febrile seizures in young children, and promptly report critical deviations to the attending physician.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Tests

Diagnostic investigations for a febrile patient are directed toward identifying the underlying etiology and assessing the severity of the systemic inflammatory response. In Nigerian clinical practice, where infectious diseases are paramount, specific laboratory tests are routinely ordered based on clinical suspicion. A complete blood count (CBC) with differential is fundamental; leukocytosis typically indicates a bacterial infection, leukopenia or neutropenia may suggest certain viral infections, typhoid fever, or overwhelming sepsis, and anemia is common in chronic infections and malaria. Diagnostic tests for malaria, such as the rapid diagnostic test (RDT) for malaria parasite antigens and thick/thin blood films viewed under microscopy, are standard components of the initial workup for any fever. To investigate suspected bloodstream infections or typhoid, blood cultures and sensitivity tests are performed, ideally before initiating antimicrobial therapy. Urinalysis and urine microscopy, culture, and sensitivity (MCS) are essential to rule out urinary tract infections, which are frequent causes of fever across various patient demographics. Other relevant investigations include Widal test or blood cultures for typhoid, sputum microscopy and geneXpert for suspected tuberculosis, lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis when meningeal irritation is suspected, and chest radiographs to detect pulmonary infections like pneumonia. Inflammatory markers such as Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-Reactive Protein (CRP) are helpful non-specific indicators that can differentiate between inflammatory/infectious processes and non-inflammatory states, as well as monitor treatment efficacy over time. Nurses play a crucial role in preparing patients for these tests, ensuring proper specimen collection technique, and labeling and transporting samples promptly to the laboratory.

Nursing Interventions and Medical Management

Nursing interventions and medical management of fever aim to relieve patient discomfort, reduce excessive metabolic demands, treat the underlying cause, and prevent complications. Medical management primarily involves addressing the root cause, such as prescribing antimalarial drugs for malaria, targeted antibiotics for bacterial infections, or antiviral agents where applicable. Antipyretic medications, such as paracetamol (acetaminophen), are commonly prescribed to lower the hypothalamic set-point and alleviate discomfort, headache, and myalgia. Nurses must administer medications safely, calculating correct pediatric or adult dosages and monitoring for potential adverse effects. Non-pharmacological nursing interventions are equally important and focus on promoting patient comfort and facilitating heat loss. These include encouraging adequate oral fluid intake to prevent dehydration caused by increased metabolic rate, sweating, and tachypnea, or administering intravenous fluids if oral intake is impaired. Environmental modifications should be implemented by keeping the patient's room well-ventilated, dressing the patient in lightweight clothing, and using bed linens sparingly. Tepid sponging with lukewarm water may be used to promote physical cooling through evaporation and conduction, though cold water baths or ice packs should be avoided as they induce shivering, which paradoxically increases core body temperature and metabolic heat production. Continuous monitoring of temperature trends, vital signs, and neurological status allows the nurse to evaluate the effectiveness of the implemented interventions and detect any sudden clinical deterioration early.

Potential Complications and Patient Education

Potential complications of fever must be anticipated and closely monitored by the nursing team, especially when temperatures reach high levels or persist unchecked. One of the most critical complications in pediatric patients aged six months to five years is febrile seizures, which are brief convulsions triggered by a rapid rise in body temperature. Other serious complications include severe dehydration and electrolyte imbalances resulting from increased insensible fluid loss through respiration and diaphoresis, delirium, confusion, and in extreme cases, cardiovascular collapse or progression to septic shock. Patient and family education is a cornerstone of holistic nursing care and empowers individuals to manage fevers safely at home while recognizing warning signs that necessitate immediate professional medical attention. Nurses should educate patients and caregivers on accurate temperature measurement techniques, the appropriate use and dosage calculation of home antipyretics, and the critical importance of maintaining adequate hydration with clean water, oral rehydration salts (ORS), and nutrient-rich fluids. Furthermore, education must emphasize environmental prevention strategies relevant to the Nigerian context, such as sleeping under insecticide-treated mosquito nets (ITNs) to prevent malaria, maintaining proper hand hygiene and safe drinking water to prevent typhoid and diarrheal diseases, and ensuring complete routine immunizations for children. Caregivers must be clearly instructed on 'red flag' symptoms—such as persistent high fever unresponsive to medications, difficulty breathing, stiff neck, lethargy or refusal to feed, persistent vomiting, or skin rashes—that require an immediate return to the healthcare facility.
