Fever

Normal Body Temperature Regulation

The human body maintains its internal temperature within a tight, healthy range, usually between 36.5°C and 37.5°C. This stability is managed primarily by the hypothalamus, a small structure at the base of the brain that acts as the body's internal thermostat. When environmental or internal conditions change, the hypothalamus activates mechanisms to either generate heat or release it. Heat is produced mainly through cellular metabolism and muscular activity, such as shivering. Conversely, the body loses heat through radiation, conduction, convection, and evaporation via sweating and vasodilation of blood vessels near the skin. Understanding this baseline balance is essential for recognizing when a true thermal imbalance occurs.

Definition and Pathophysiology of Fever

Fever, medically known as pyrexia, is a temporary elevation in body temperature above the normal daily variation, usually triggered by the hypothalamus resetting its set point upward. This upward shift is mediated by signaling molecules called pyrogens. Exogenous pyrogens come from outside the body, such as bacteria, viruses, or toxins. When immune cells encounter these invaders, they release endogenous pyrogens, including cytokines like interleukin-1 and tumor necrosis factor. These cytokines prompt the hypothalamus to synthesize prostaglandins, particularly PGE2, which directly raises the thermal set point. Consequently, the body feels cold and initiates shivering to reach this new, higher target temperature.

Common Causes of Fever in Nigerian Practice

In local clinical settings across Nigeria, infectious diseases remain the leading driver of febrile illnesses among patients of all ages. Malaria, caused by Plasmodium species and transmitted by female Anopheles mosquitoes, is perhaps the most frequent culprit behind acute fevers. Other prevalent infectious causes include typhoid fever linked to contaminated water or food, acute respiratory tract infections, urinary tract infections, and viral illnesses such as Lassa fever or dengue. A thorough clinical history must explore recent travel, environmental exposures, and local disease outbreaks to narrow down these possibilities.

Patterns and Types of Fever

  • Sustained fever: A constant, elevated temperature with little or no fluctuation over a 24-hour period.
  • Intermittent fever: Temperature spikes interspersed with normal temperature readings at least once every 24 hours.
  • Remittent fever: Fluctuating temperature that remains above normal throughout the day without ever touching baseline.
  • Relenting or Relapsing fever: Episodes of fever alternating with days of completely normal temperature readings.

Clinical Assessment and Vital Signs

Accurate assessment of a febrile patient goes beyond a single thermometer reading. Nurses must evaluate the full picture by monitoring all vital signs, including heart rate, respiratory rate, and blood pressure. As a general physiological rule, for every 1°C rise in body temperature, the heart rate increases by about 10 beats per minute, a phenomenon known as Relative Tachycardia. Nurses should also observe skin color, check for diaphoresis or shivering, and assess the patient's level of consciousness. Identifying accompanying symptoms such as headache, neck stiffness, or rash provides crucial clues for medical diagnosis.

Diagnostic Investigations

Laboratory investigations help uncover the underlying pathology driving a patient's fever. A complete blood count is standard, often revealing leukocytosis in bacterial infections or leukopenia in certain viral illnesses. In regions where malaria is endemic, a blood smear or rapid diagnostic test is performed promptly. Other useful tests include urinalysis, chest radiography, and blood cultures if a systemic bloodstream infection is suspected. Selecting the appropriate test depends on the patient's symptoms, physical findings, and local epidemiological patterns.

Nursing Interventions and Management

Managing a fever involves balancing comfort measures with targeted medical treatments. Nurses administer prescribed antipyretics, such as paracetamol, to lower the hypothalamic set point. Non-pharmacological measures include encouraging adequate oral hydration to offset fluid losses from sweating and keeping the room well-ventilated. Tepid sponging may offer temporary comfort, though harsh cold baths should be avoided because they induce shivering, which paradoxically drives core temperature back up. Nurses must also monitor the patient's response to interventions and report persistent high readings to the attending physician.

Complications and Patient Education

While most fevers are a natural, self-limiting part of the immune response, very high temperatures can lead to complications. Severe dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, and febrile seizures in young children are notable risks. Patient and caregiver education is vital for safe home management. Nurses should teach families how to correctly read a thermometer, administer antipyretic doses based on weight rather than age, and recognize red-flag symptoms. Warning signs that require immediate return to a healthcare facility include persistent vomiting, difficulty breathing, altered mental state, or a stiff neck.
