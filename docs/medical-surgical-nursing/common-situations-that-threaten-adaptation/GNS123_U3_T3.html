Fatigue

Definition and Pathophysiology of Fatigue

Fatigue is a subjective, multidimensional state characterized by an overwhelming, sustained sense of exhaustion and decreased capacity for physical and mental work that is not relieved by usual rest. Unlike transient tiredness, clinical fatigue alters functional status and persists across time.

The exact biological mechanisms remain complex and overlapping. Central fatigue originates in the central nervous system, involving impaired neural drive from the motor cortex and alterations in neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine. Peripheral fatigue occurs at or distal to the neuromuscular junction, driven by the depletion of energy substrates such as glycogen and the accumulation of metabolic byproducts like hydrogen ions and inorganic phosphate. Chronic systemic inflammation also plays a major role, where circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6 cross the blood-brain barrier, altering neuroendocrine function and inducing sickness behaviors that mimic severe exhaustion.

Differentiating Fatigue from Somnolence and Tiredness

  • Tiredness: An adaptive, transient response to exertion, poor sleep, or minor stress that resolves quickly with restorative rest or sleep.
  • Somnolence: Excessive daytime sleepiness characterized by a strong biological drive to fall asleep, frequently linked to sleep deprivation, obstructive sleep apnea, or circadian rhythm disorders.
  • Fatigue: A subjective lack of physical and/or mental energy that impairs a person's ability to initiate or sustain voluntary activities, independent of the desire to sleep.

A patient complaining of somnolence will often fall asleep mid-conversation or during quiet activities, whereas a fatigued patient may lie awake in bed feeling too exhausted to move their limbs, demonstrating that sleep does not cure true pathological fatigue.

Etiology and Underlying Causes of Acute and Chronic Fatigue

Fatigue stems from a diverse spectrum of physiological, psychological, and environmental triggers. Clinicians categorize fatigue temporally: acute fatigue lasts less than one month and typically serves a protective, identifiable purpose, while chronic fatigue persists for six months or longer and severely disrupts daily functioning.

  1. Infectious and Inflammatory: Post-viral syndromes, chronic infections like tuberculosis, and autoimmune disorders such as systemic lupus erythematosus.
  2. Metabolic and Endocrine: Hypothyroidism, diabetes mellitus, adrenal insufficiency, and chronic kidney disease.
  3. Hematological: Iron-deficiency anemia, which is highly prevalent in resource-constrained settings due to nutritional deficits and parasitic infections like malaria.
  4. Psychological: Major depressive disorder, generalized anxiety, and prolonged emotional stress.
  5. Iatrogenic: Side effects from medications including antihypertensives, antihistamines, and cancer chemotherapy regimens.

Clinical Assessment and Screening Tools

Evaluating fatigue requires a systematic approach because the symptom is entirely subjective and invisible to objective laboratory tests. The nursing assessment begins with a thorough health history exploring the onset, duration, severity, and relieving or aggravating factors. Utilizing validated rating scales helps quantify the subjective experience and monitor therapeutic progress over time.

Commonly utilized instruments include the Fatigue Severity Scale and the Brief Fatigue Inventory, which ask patients to rate how exhaustion interferes with daily activities, work, and social relationships on a numerical scale. Laboratory investigations are guided by clinical findings but typically include a complete blood count to check for anemia, fasting blood glucose, thyroid-stimulating hormone levels, and inflammatory markers.

Nursing Interventions and Energy Conservation Strategies

When managing persistent fatigue, nursing care focuses on optimizing remaining energy reserves rather than attempting to eliminate the exhaustion entirely. Energy conservation teaches patients how to pace activities, prioritize daily tasks, and eliminate non-essential movements to prevent total exhaustion.

  • Pacing: Breaking large tasks into smaller, manageable chunks with mandatory rest intervals scheduled before fatigue sets in.
  • Prioritizing: Arranging the daily schedule so high-energy activities occur during peak physiological energy windows.
  • Positioning: Utilizing ergonomic principles, sitting rather than standing during household chores or hygiene care, and organizing workspaces to minimize bending and reaching.

Environmental modifications, such as reducing bright lights and loud noises, also decrease sensory overload which contributes to central nervous system fatigue.

Patient Education and Lifestyle Management

Empowering patients with self-management techniques is essential for long-term coping. Education must address sleep hygiene, nutritional support, and gradual physical reconditioning. Many patients mistakenly believe that complete bed rest cures fatigue, when in reality, physical deconditioning worsens the symptom cycle.

Nurses should guide patients in establishing consistent sleep-wake routines, limiting caffeine and alcohol intake in the evening, and engaging in low-impact aerobic exercises such as short walks, as tolerated. In the Nigerian clinical context, nutritional counseling must emphasize affordable, locally available iron-rich and protein-dense foods to combat nutritional deficiencies contributing to weakness. Maintaining an open dialogue helps patients cope with the psychosocial toll of chronic fatigue.
