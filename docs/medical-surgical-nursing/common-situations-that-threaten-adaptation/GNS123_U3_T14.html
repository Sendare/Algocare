Oedema

Introduction and Definition of Oedema

Oedema is the palpable swelling produced by an expansion of the interstitial fluid volume. While fluid constantly moves between the vascular space and the interstitium, a disruption in this balance leads to accumulation. This excess fluid can manifest subtly, such as tight shoes by evening, or profoundly, as massive generalised swelling known as anasarca. In busy Nigerian clinical wards, noticing early dependent swelling around the ankles during routine morning bed-making can prevent severe fluid overload states.

Pathophysiology and Starling Forces

Fluid movement across the capillary membrane is governed by Starling forces, which balance hydrostatic and oncotic pressures. Normally, capillary hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out, while plasma oncotic pressure—primarily driven by albumin—pulls fluid back in.

  • Capillary hydrostatic pressure: Increases due to venous obstruction or heart failure, forcing excess fluid outward.
  • Plasma oncotic pressure: Drops when serum albumin is depleted, preventing adequate fluid reabsorption.
  • Capillary permeability: Rises during inflammation or trauma, allowing proteins to leak into the interstitium.
  • Lymphatic drainage: Impairment prevents the clearance of normal interstitial protein and fluid loads.

Localised versus Generalised Oedema

Distinguishing between localised and generalised oedema directs the entire diagnostic workup. Localised swelling typically stems from an acute, site-specific insult such as venous thrombosis, localized trauma, or localized inflammation. Conversely, generalised oedema reflects a systemic disturbance affecting fluid homeostasis across the entire body, commonly linked to cardiac, renal, or hepatic failure. For instance, a swollen, painful left calf points toward a deep vein thrombosis, whereas bilateral, shifting ankle swelling points toward systemic pathology.

Common Causes and Etiology

Multiple underlying disease states disrupt Starling forces. In heart failure, the pump fails, elevating venous pressure. In nephrotic syndrome, massive urinary protein loss plummets oncotic pressure. Liver cirrhosis impairs albumin synthesis and alters portal hemodynamics. Additionally, malnutrition—still encountered in under-resourced regions—leads to severe kwashiorkor, where dietary protein deficiency directly triggers systemic fluid shifts.

Clinical Assessment and Pitting Test

Evaluating oedema requires systematic physical examination, beginning with the pitting test. Firm thumb pressure is applied over a bony prominence—such as the tibial shaft or medial malleolus—for at least five seconds. The depth of the resulting depression determines the grading score, ranging from 1+ (barely perceptible indentation) to 4+ (deep indentation lasting more than two minutes). Documenting the exact anatomical level to which swelling extends, such as mid-calf or knee joint, provides a baseline for tracking diuretic therapy efficacy.

Diagnostic Investigations

Uncovering the root cause of oedema demands targeted laboratory and imaging investigations. Initial blood work generally includes a complete blood count, serum electrolytes, liver function tests, and a serum albumin level to evaluate for systemic protein deficits. Urinalysis is essential to screen for proteinuria. Depending on the clinical suspicion, an electrocardiogram or echocardiogram assesses cardiac function, while a venous duplex ultrasound rules out deep vein thrombosis in asymmetric limb presentations.

Medical and Nursing Management

Managing oedema requires treating the underlying pathology alongside symptom relief. Diuretics, such as furosemide, help mobilize excess extracellular fluid through renal excretion. Nursing interventions focus on skin integrity protection, daily weight monitoring, and strict fluid balance charting. Elevating dependent limbs above the level of the heart harnesses gravity to promote venous return. In addition, fitted compression stockings can prevent fluid pooling in chronic venous insufficiency, provided arterial supply remains intact.

Complications and Patient Education

Unmanaged chronic oedema carries severe risks, including skin breakdown, ulceration, and secondary bacterial infections like cellulitis due to compromised local tissue perfusion and immune defense. Patient education forms a cornerstone of recovery. Instruct individuals to inspect their skin daily for cracks or redness, limit dietary sodium to prevent fluid retention, and avoid standing or sitting motionless for long stretches. Empowering patients to recognize early weight gains prevents repeated emergency hospital admissions.
