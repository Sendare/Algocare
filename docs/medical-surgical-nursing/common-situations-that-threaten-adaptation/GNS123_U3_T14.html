Oedema

Introduction and Definition of Oedema

Oedema is the medical term for swelling caused by a buildup of fluid trapped in the body's tissues. While it can affect any part of the body, it is most commonly noticed in the hands, arms, feet, ankles, and legs. Fluid accumulation happens when tiny blood vessels, known as capillaries, leak fluid into surrounding tissues. This excess fluid overwhelms the local drainage systems, causing the tissue to puff up. Understanding this fluid shift is the first step in recognizing why patients present with swollen limbs or tight skin.

Pathophysiology and Starling Forces

The movement of fluid across capillary walls is governed by Starling forces, which balance hydrostatic pressure and oncotic pressure. Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out of the capillaries, while oncotic pressure, driven mostly by plasma proteins like albumin, pulls fluid back in. When capillary hydrostatic pressure increases or plasma oncotic pressure drops, fluid escapes into the tissues faster than it can be reabsorbed.

  • Increased capillary hydrostatic pressure: Often caused by venous congestion or heart failure, pushing excess fluid out.
  • Decreased plasma oncotic pressure: Seen in liver disease or malnutrition when blood protein levels fall too low to hold fluid inside vessels.
  • Increased capillary permeability: Occurs during inflammation or allergic reactions, letting fluid and proteins leak freely.
  • Lymphatic obstruction: Happens when blocked lymph channels fail to drain interstitial fluid back to the bloodstream.

Classification: Localised versus Generalised Oedema

Oedema is generally categorized based on how much of the body it affects. Localised oedema is restricted to a specific organ or limb, usually resulting from local trauma, venous blockage, or a localized allergic reaction, such as a bee sting on a single hand.

In contrast, generalised oedema, also known as anasarca, is a widespread accumulation of fluid affecting the entire body. This happens when systemic conditions, like severe heart failure, kidney disease, or advanced liver failure, alter fluid balance across the whole circulatory system. Patients with anasarca often show prominent swelling in the face, abdomen, and lower limbs simultaneously.

Clinical Assessment and Pitting Test

Assessing fluid retention requires careful physical examination, with the pitting test being the standard bedside method. To perform the test, the nurse presses a finger firmly against the swollen tissue—usually over a bony prominence like the tibia or dorsum of the foot—for about five seconds. If an indentation remains after the finger is removed, the swelling is classified as pitting oedema.

Clinicians often grade pitting oedema from 1+ to 4+ based on the depth of the indentation and how quickly the skin bounces back. Non-pitting oedema, which does not leave an indentation, is typically associated with lymphatic blockage or severe inflammation where trapped proteins gel the interstitial fluid.

Medical and Nursing Management

Managing oedema involves treating the underlying root cause while providing symptomatic relief to reduce fluid overload. Diuretics, commonly known as water pills, are frequently prescribed to help the kidneys excrete excess sodium and water. Nursing care focuses heavily on monitoring fluid balance, tracking daily weights, and inspecting the skin for breakdown.

Non-pharmacological interventions are equally vital for patient comfort and safety. Elevating swollen limbs above the level of the heart uses gravity to assist venous return. Compression stockings can prevent fluid from pooling in the lower extremities, provided arterial circulation is intact. Nurses must also educate patients on restricting dietary sodium intake to prevent further fluid retention.

Complications and Patient Education

Unmanaged chronic oedema can lead to serious local and systemic complications. Swollen tissues stretch the skin, making it fragile, dry, and prone to cracking, which increases the risk of painful skin infections like cellulitis. Chronically restricted blood flow can also cause delayed wound healing and tissue death.

Patient education is essential for preventing these complications at home. Nurses should teach patients to inspect their skin daily for redness, warmth, or breaks, and to avoid tight clothing or restrictive shoes that impede circulation. Emphasizing adherence to low-salt diets and scheduled medication routines helps patients manage their condition and avoid hospital readmission.
