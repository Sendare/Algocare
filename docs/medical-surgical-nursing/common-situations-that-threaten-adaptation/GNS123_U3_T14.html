Oedema

Introduction and Definition of Oedema

Oedema is the abnormal accumulation of fluid in the interstitial spaces or body cavities. It occurs when the normal homeostatic balance between fluid filtration and absorption is disrupted, leading to tissue swelling. While it is a clinical sign rather than a disease itself, oedema signals an underlying physiological imbalance that requires nursing investigation. The excess fluid mostly consists of water, electrolytes, and plasma proteins that have escaped the vascular network.

Pathophysiology and Starling Forces

Fluid movement across the capillary wall is governed by Starling forces, which balance hydrostatic and oncotic pressures.

  • Capillary Hydrostatic Pressure: Pushes fluid out of the blood vessel into the tissues.
  • Interstitial Hydrostatic Pressure: Pushes fluid back into the vessel, though its effect is usually minimal.
  • Plasma Oncotic Pressure: Pulls fluid back into the capillary via circulating proteins like albumin.
  • Interstitial Oncotic Pressure: Pulls fluid out into the interstitial space when proteins leak through the membrane.

When capillary hydrostatic pressure rises or plasma oncotic pressure drops, fluid shifts overwhelmingly into the interstitium, causing swelling.

Classification: Localised versus Generalised Oedema

Oedema presents in different distributions depending on whether the trigger is a regional event or a systemic failure.

  • Localised Oedema: Confined to a specific organ or body part. Common causes include venous thrombosis, local trauma, or insect bites that trigger acute inflammation and histamine release.
  • Generalised Oedema (Anasarca): Widespread accumulation of fluid throughout the entire interstitial space. This points to systemic disorders such as congestive heart failure, nephrotic syndrome, or severe hepatic cirrhosis.

Recognising the distribution helps the nurse narrow down the diagnostic possibilities during the initial patient assessment.

Common Causes and Etiology

Multiple medical conditions disrupt fluid dynamics. Heart failure increases venous pressure, forcing fluid into the lungs or lower limbs. Kidney diseases, such as glomerulonephritis, cause sodium and water retention while spilling vital proteins into the urine. Liver cirrhosis impairs the synthesis of albumin, dropping oncotic pressure. Additionally, malnutrition creates a protein deficit that mimics hepatic failure. Local blockages, such as filariasis or surgical removal of lymph nodes, prevent fluid return via the lymphatic system, resulting in firm, non-pitting swelling.

Clinical Assessment and Pitting Test

Assessing oedema requires systematic physical examination techniques. The nurse inspects the skin for tautness, shininess, and discolouration. To grade pitting oedema, firm pressure is applied with a thumb over a bony prominence, such as the tibia or medial malleus, for at least five seconds.

  • 1+ Grade: Mild indentation, barely perceptible.
  • 2+ Grade: Moderate indentation, subsides rapidly.
  • 3+ Grade: Deep indentation, remains for a short time.
  • 4+ Grade: Very deep indentation, lasts for a prolonged period.

Documenting the depth and duration of the pit helps evaluate the effectiveness of ongoing diuretic therapy.

Diagnostic Investigations

Laboratory and imaging tests help pinpoint the root cause of fluid accumulation. Urinalysis checks for proteinuria, which suggests glomerular damage. Serum electrolyte and blood urea nitrogen panels evaluate renal function. Liver function tests, including serum albumin and bilirubin levels, assess hepatic synthetic capacity. Electrocardiograms and echocardiograms reveal cardiac chamber enlargement or pump failure. Venous duplex ultrasound is ordered if deep vein thrombosis is suspected as the source of unilateral leg swelling.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management strategies target both the underlying cause and the relief of fluid overload. Diuretics, such as furosemide, are commonly prescribed to promote renal excretion of excess water and sodium. Dietary sodium restriction prevents further water retention. Elevating swollen limbs above the level of the heart encourages venous and lymphatic return. Compression stockings help prevent fluid pooling in chronic venous insufficiency. Nurses must regularly monitor daily weights, strict fluid intake and output charts, and watch for electrolyte imbalances like hypokalaemia during diuretic therapy.

Complications and Patient Education

Unmanaged oedema creates significant tissue risks. Chronic swelling impairs local microcirculation, leading to skin breakdown, delayed wound healing, and ulcers. In severe cases, stagnant fluid increases the risk of local infections such as cellulitis. Patient education focuses on dietary modifications, proper skin care to prevent cracking, and recognizing danger signs. Patients must report sudden weight gain, worsening shortness of breath, or increased pain immediately, as these may signal worsening cardiac or renal function.
