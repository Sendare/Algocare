Stress

Introduction to Stress and General Adaptation Syndrome

Stress is any physical, emotional, or psychological demand that disrupts the body's natural balance, known as homeostasis. When the body encounters a stressor, it responds through a predictable three-stage process called the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS), formulated by Hans Selye.

  1. Alarm Reaction: The initial, sudden shock phase. The sympathetic nervous system fires up rapidly, mobilizing immediate energy through the classic fight-or-flight response.
  2. Resistance (Adaptation): If the stressor continues, the body tries to cope and adapt. Hormone levels remain elevated, and the body uses up significant energy reserves trying to maintain stability.
  3. Exhaustion: The final stage occurs when chronic stress depletes all physical and mental resources. Adaptation fails, leading to organ wear and tear, immune breakdown, and severe vulnerability to illness.

Pathophysiology and Neuroendocrine Response to Stress

When a person perceives a threat, the brain's hypothalamus acts as the main command center, triggering two major physiological pathways.

The first pathway is the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) system. The hypothalamus sends nerve signals to the adrenal medulla, causing a sudden release of catecholamines—mostly adrenaline and noradrenaline. This causes immediate changes: heart rate spikes, blood pressure climbs, airways dilate, and blood rushes to the major skeletal muscles.

The second pathway is the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone, which prompts the pituitary gland to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone. This hormone tells the adrenal cortex to release cortisol, the primary stress hormone. Cortisol boosts blood glucose by breaking down stored nutrients, ensuring the brain and muscles have enough fuel. However, prolonged cortisol release suppresses the immune system, lowers inflammation resistance, and alters mood.

Types and Sources of Stress in Clinical and Daily Life

Stress comes from many different directions and affects people in unique ways. Nurses must understand these sources because hospitalized patients face distinct pressures alongside their regular life challenges.

  • Eustress: Positive, motivating stress that encourages growth, excitement, and achievement, such as planning a wedding or starting a new job.
  • Distress: Negative, damaging stress that causes anxiety, poor performance, and health breakdown, such as financial loss or bereavement.
  • Environmental Stressors: External factors like loud hospital monitors, bright lights, extreme room temperatures, and unfamiliar surroundings.
  • Physiological Stressors: Internal physical demands such as severe pain, massive blood loss, malnutrition, or major surgery.
  • Psychosocial Stressors: Emotional pressures related to job loss, family conflicts, chronic illness diagnosis, or fear of the unknown.

Systemic Physiological and Psychological Effects of Chronic Stress

When stress refuses to go away, the body stays on high alert for months or years. This constant wear and tear is known as allostatic load. Over time, elevated cortisol and blood pressure damage almost every organ system.

Cardiovascular issues include chronic hypertension, plaque buildup in arteries, and a much higher risk of myocardial infarction. The immune system suffers greatly; white blood cell production drops, leaving the body vulnerable to infections and slowing down wound healing. Gastrointestinal problems surface as ulcers, irritable bowel syndrome, and acid reflux because blood is diverted away from the gut.

Psychologically, chronic stress exhausts neurotransmitters, paving the way for persistent anxiety, clinical depression, emotional numbing, irritability, and memory lapses.

Nursing Assessment and Screening Tools for Stress Levels

Assessing a patient's stress level requires both sharp clinical observation and structured screening tools. Nurses look for physical signs like trembling hands, rapid shallow breathing, flushed skin, restlessness, and muscle tension. Behavioral cues include withdrawal, tearfulness, defensiveness, or snapping at staff.

Standardized rating scales help quantify subjective feelings. The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) measures how unpredictable and overloaded individuals find their lives. The Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale evaluates the severity of psychological and somatic anxiety symptoms. During assessment, the nurse must also explore the patient's support systems, coping strategies, and recent major life events.

Coping Mechanisms, Defense Mechanisms, and Adaptation

People handle stress using conscious and unconscious strategies. Healthy coping mechanisms involve problem-solving, seeking emotional support, and reframing negative thoughts. These strategies help resolve the root issue or manage the emotional pain constructively.

When stress becomes overwhelming, the ego uses unconscious defense mechanisms to protect the mind from severe anxiety:

  • Denial: Refusing to accept painful reality or facts.
  • Projection: Attributing one's own unacceptable thoughts or impulses to someone else.
  • Rationalization: Creating logical, false excuses to justify unacceptable behaviors or feelings.
  • Regression: Returning to an earlier, more childish stage of development during times of stress.
  • Displacement: Transferring emotions from the true source of stress to a safer, vulnerable target, such as yelling at a nurse because of a frightening diagnosis.

Nursing Management and Stress Reduction Interventions

Nursing management focuses on lowering environmental triggers, teaching relaxation techniques, and empowering the patient. Simple actions make a big difference: dimming lights at night, grouping nursing tasks to allow uninterrupted sleep periods, and speaking in a calm, reassuring voice.

Nurses also guide patients through evidence-based relaxation interventions. Deep breathing exercises stimulate the vagus nerve, slowing the heart rate and activating the parasympathetic nervous system. Progressive muscle relaxation helps patients identify and release physical tension muscle group by muscle group. Encouraging journaling, guided imagery, and open therapeutic communication helps patients regain a sense of control over their healing journey.

Complications of Unmanaged Stress in Medical-Surgical Patients

In medical-surgical settings, unmanaged stress is more than just feeling uncomfortable; it directly threatens recovery. High stress hormones increase blood glucose levels, impairing leukocyte function and leading to higher rates of hospital-acquired infections. Chronic vasoconstriction and elevated heart rate put extra strain on a recovering heart, increasing the risk of arrhythmias or heart failure exacerbation.

Furthermore, severe anxiety and stress significantly amplify pain perception, requiring higher doses of analgesics which bring their own side effects like respiratory depression or severe constipation. Psychological distress also breeds non-compliance with treatment plans, prolonging hospital stays and increasing readmission rates.
