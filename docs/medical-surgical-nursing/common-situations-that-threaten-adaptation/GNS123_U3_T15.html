Stress

Introduction to Stress and General Adaptation Syndrome

Stress is a universal human experience defined as any disturbance in homeostasis that places a demand on physical or psychological well-being. Hans Selye formulated the **General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)** to explain how the body responds structurally and functionally to noxious stimuli. This framework outlines three sequential stages: 1. **Alarm Reaction**: The immediate fight-or-flight response mediated by the sympathetic nervous system and the sympathomedullary pathway. Heart rate, blood pressure, and blood glucose spike rapidly. 2. **Stage of Resistance**: The body attempts to adapt to the persistent stressor. Parasympathetic activity tries to balance remaining physiological changes, while cortisol levels remain elevated to sustain energy. 3. **Stage of Exhaustion**: If the stressor continues unabated, adaptive energy is depleted. The body can no longer maintain structural or chemical equilibrium, leading to systemic breakdown, chronic illness, or death.

Pathophysiology and Neuroendocrine Response

When a stressor is perceived, the amygdala signals the hypothalamus, initiating two primary pathways: the sympathetic-adrenal-medullary (SAM) axis and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. The SAM axis triggers a rapid release of **epinephrine** and **norepinephrine** from the adrenal medulla, elevating cardiac output and bronchodilation within seconds. Meanwhile, the HPA axis acts more slowly. The hypothalamus secretes corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), stimulating the anterior pituitary to release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH then commands the adrenal cortex to synthesize and release **cortisol**. Cortisol alters metabolism by increasing hepatic gluconeogenesis and suppressing non-essential functions such as digestion, reproduction, and immune surveillance. While vital for acute survival, prolonged activation of these neuroendocrine axes damages target tissues and suppresses inflammatory responses.

Types and Sources of Stress

Stress originates from diverse external and internal stimuli. Categorizing these sources helps clinicians identify targeted interventions. - **Eustress**: Positive, motivating stress that enhances performance, such as preparing for a professional nursing board examination or starting a new job. - **Distress**: Negative, overwhelming stress that exceeds coping abilities, leading to anxiety, somatic complaints, and burnout. - **Acute Stress**: Sudden, short-lived events like a sudden trauma or code blue emergency. - **Chronic Stress**: Ongoing, unrelenting pressures such as long-term poverty, chronic illness, or caregiver fatigue. In Nigerian clinical and academic settings, financial constraints, overcrowded hospital wards, erratic power supply during shifts, and heavy patient-to-nurse ratios frequently contribute to chronic distress among healthcare professionals and patients alike.

Systemic Effects of Chronic Stress

| Body System | Effects of Chronic Stress | |---|---| | **Cardiovascular** | Sustained hypertension, endothelial damage, increased risk of myocardial infarction and stroke. | | **Immune** | Suppression of white blood cell function, delayed wound healing, increased susceptibility to infections. | | **Gastrointestinal** | Exacerbation of peptic ulcers, irritable bowel syndrome, alterations in gut microbiome. | | **Neurological** | Hippocampal atrophy, impaired memory consolidation, anxiety, and depressive disorders. | Chronic activation wears down biological systems through **allostatic load**—the cumulative wear and tear on the body from repeated cycles of stress response activation. In medical-surgical patients, this systemic suppression impairs recovery from surgery and prolongs hospital stays.

Nursing Assessment and Screening Tools

Accurate identification of stress requires both subjective questioning and objective clinical observation. Nurses must evaluate behavioral cues such as restlessness, insomnia, changes in affect, or tachycardia. Standardized screening tools assist in quantifying stress levels: - **Perceived Stress Scale (PSS)**: Measures the degree to which situations in one's life are appraised as stressful. - **Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A)**: Assesses the severity of psychic and somatic anxiety symptoms. - **General Health Questionnaire (GHQ)**: Screens for psychological distress in clinical and community populations. During assessment, the nurse should explore the patient's primary stressors, available support systems, and cultural interpretations of stress, as health beliefs significantly influence coping styles.

Coping Mechanisms and Defense Mechanisms

Individuals utilize various cognitive and behavioral strategies to manage internal and external demands. **Coping mechanisms** can be problem-focused, addressing the root cause of stress, or emotion-focused, regulating the emotional response to distress. When stress becomes overwhelming, the ego employs unconscious **defense mechanisms** to protect the psyche from anxiety: - **Denial**: Refusing to acknowledge painful realities or facts. - **Rationalization**: Creating logical, acceptable excuses for unacceptable behaviors or feelings. - **Projection**: Attributing one's own unacceptable thoughts or impulses to another person. - **Regression**: Reverting to an earlier, more childish developmental stage during times of crisis. Recognizing these mechanisms helps nurses differentiate between adaptive coping and maladaptive avoidance.

Nursing Management and Stress Reduction Interventions

Effective nursing management of stress combines pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches tailored to the patient's needs. Non-pharmacological interventions form the foundation of nursing care: - **Relaxation Techniques**: Guided imagery, deep breathing exercises, and progressive muscle relaxation to reduce sympathetic tone. - **Environmental Modification**: Reducing noise, dimming harsh lights, and promoting undisturbed rest periods in clinical wards. - **Therapeutic Communication**: Active listening and encouraging verbalization of fears and anxieties. When anxiety is severe, physicians may prescribe anxiolytics or sedatives, requiring the nurse to monitor for sedation, respiratory depression, and dependence. Promoting patient autonomy and involving family caregivers also builds resilience against stress.

Complications of Unmanaged Stress in Medical-Surgical Patients

Unmanaged stress directly compromises clinical outcomes in medical-surgical nursing. Prolonged elevation of catabolic hormones impairs wound healing, increasing the risk of surgical site infections and dehiscence. Furthermore, persistent sympathetic stimulation precipitates cardiovascular complications such as perioperative hypertension, arrhythmias, and myocardial ischemia. Gastrointestinal mucosal ischemia from stress-related vasoconstriction can lead to stress ulcers and gastrointestinal bleeding. Psychologically, patients may develop acute stress disorder, depression, or intensive care unit (ICU) delirium. Recognizing these risks allows nurses to advocate for early psychological consultation and comprehensive holistic care protocols.
