Haemorrhage

Introduction and Definition of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is the escape of blood from the vascular system, occurring when a blood vessel wall is damaged. It can happen internally inside body cavities and tissues, or externally where blood escapes through a break in the skin or a natural body orifice. Understanding this loss of circulating volume is foundational for nurses because it directly threatens tissue perfusion and oxygen delivery. Without prompt intervention, unchecked bleeding quickly leads to inadequate cellular oxygenation and organ failure. The body constantly relies on an intact, closed plumbing system to maintain blood pressure and deliver nutrients. When that system ruptures, the priority shifts immediately to stopping the leak and restoring volume.

Classification and Types of Haemorrhage

Bleeding is categorized based on the type of blood vessel involved and where the blood goes.

  • Arterial haemorrhage: Blood is bright red and spurts out in jets corresponding to the heartbeat, indicating high pressure.
  • Venous haemorrhage: Blood is dark red and flows in a steady, continuous stream under lower pressure.
  • Capillary haemorrhage: Blood oozes slowly from minor cuts, typically stopping on its own due to normal clotting.
  • Internal haemorrhage: Blood collects inside the body within cavities like the abdomen, chest, or cranium, making it invisible from the outside.
  • External haemorrhage: Blood is visible escaping from a wound on the body surface or through body openings.

Pathophysiology and Body Compensatory Mechanisms

When blood volume drops sharply, the body immediately activates compensatory mechanisms to protect vital organs like the brain and heart. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinuses and aortic arch detect the sudden fall in blood pressure. They send distress signals to the brain's vasomotor center, which triggers the sympathetic nervous system. The heart beats faster and contracts harder to squeeze out remaining blood. Blood vessels in the skin, gut, and extremities constrict tightly to redirect whatever blood is left toward the core organs. Hormones like adrenaline, aldosterone, and antidiuretic hormone are released to retain water and salt, boosting blood volume. However, if bleeding continues unchecked, these reserves eventually fail, leading to decompensated shock.

Causes and Risk Factors

Haemorrhage stems from many different origins, ranging from physical trauma to chronic disease processes.

  • Trauma: Sharp force injuries, blunt force impacts, and motor vehicle accidents directly tear blood vessels.
  • Medical conditions: Peptic ulcers, ruptured aneurysms, and severe liver disease weaken vessel walls or impair clotting.
  • Coagulation disorders: Conditions like hemophilia or low platelet counts prevent normal blood clotting.
  • Pharmacological agents: Anticoagulant medications like warfarin or antiplatelet drugs like aspirin increase bleeding risks.

Clinical Assessment and Grading of Blood Loss

Assessing a bleeding patient requires watching for subtle signs long before blood pressure crashes. Early indicators include restlessness, mild anxiety, a slightly fast pulse, and cool, pale skin. As blood loss worsens into class III or IV shock, blood pressure drops significantly, breathing becomes rapid, and confusion or lethargy sets in due to poor brain perfusion. Nurses must monitor vital signs trendlines rather than relying on a single reading, because young, healthy patients can compensate remarkably well until they suddenly collapse. Urine output is another gold standard: dropping below 30 mL per hour signals poor kidney perfusion from low blood volume.

Nursing Interventions and Emergency Management

Immediate nursing care focuses on stopping the bleeding and restoring tissue oxygenation. For external bleeding, apply firm, direct pressure over the wound using a clean pad or bandage. Elevate bleeding limbs if no fractures are present. Administer high-flow oxygen via a face mask to maximize the oxygen saturation of any remaining red blood cells. Establish large-bore intravenous access quickly to infuse warmed isotonic crystalloid fluids or blood products. Position the patient flat on their back with legs slightly raised to help blood flow back to the heart and brain, unless head or breathing injuries forbid it. Continuously monitor vital signs and watch for signs of worsening shock.

Medical and Surgical Treatments

Medical management goes beyond basic first aid to address the root cause of the bleeding. Doctors order blood transfusions, packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets to replace missing components. Clotting factor concentrates or medications like tranexamic acid may be given to help the blood clot faster. When bleeding is internal and cannot be stopped with pressure, surgical intervention becomes mandatory. Surgeons perform emergency operations to tie off bleeding vessels, repair damaged organs, or remove ruptured tissues. Minimally invasive procedures like interventional radiology coil embolization are also used to block bleeding vessels from the inside.

Complications of Haemorrhage

Uncontrolled or prolonged bleeding triggers dangerous domino effects throughout the body. The most immediate threat is hypovolemic shock, where the cardiovascular system fails to perfuse vital organs, leading to multi-organ dysfunction syndrome. Prolonged low blood pressure deprives the kidneys of blood, causing acute kidney injury or complete renal failure. When massive amounts of blood are lost and replaced rapidly with cold stored blood or fluids, patients can develop hypothermia, metabolic acidosis, and coagulopathy—a dangerous triad that further stops blood from clotting. Nurses must be vigilant for these complications during the recovery phase to prevent permanent disability or death.
