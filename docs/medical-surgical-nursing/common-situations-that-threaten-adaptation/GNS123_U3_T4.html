Haemorrhage

Introduction and Definition of Haemorrhage

Haemorrhage is defined as the escape of blood from the vascular system, resulting from damage to blood vessels such as arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules, or veins. This blood loss can occur internally into body cavities and tissues, or externally through natural body orifices or breaks in the skin. In a standard adult body containing approximately 5 litres of circulating volume, even modest reductions can destabilize hemodynamic parameters. Understanding the acute volume status of a patient prevents rapid progression to circulatory collapse and tissue hypoxia.

Classification and Types of Haemorrhage

Bleeding is categorized by the injured vessel type and anatomical location. Vessel classification includes arterial, venous, and capillary bleeding, each displaying distinct flow characteristics:

  • Arterial haemorrhage: Bright red blood spurting in rhythm with the heartbeat due to high pressure.
  • Venous haemorrhage: Dark red blood flowing in a steady, continuous stream under lower pressure.
  • Capillary haemorrhage: Oozing of red blood from minor superficial vessel damage, easily controlled.

Anatomically, bleeding is further divided into external (visible outside the body) and internal (concealed within cavities such as the peritoneum, pleural space, or cranial vault).

Pathophysiology and Body Compensatory Mechanisms

When acute blood loss occurs, the body immediately activates neuroendocrine and cardiovascular responses to maintain perfusion to vital organs like the brain and heart. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch detect the drop in blood pressure and signal the vasomotor center in the medulla. This triggers sympathetic nervous system activation, causing vasoconstriction, tachycardia, and increased myocardial contractility. Simultaneously, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is stimulated to conserve sodium and water, while the posterior pituitary releases antidiuretic hormone to limit urine output.

Causes and Risk Factors

Haemorrhage stems from numerous traumatic and medical etiologies. Traumatic causes involve blunt or penetrating injuries that physically sever vessel walls, common in road traffic accidents prevalent across Nigerian highways. Non-traumatic or medical causes include peptic ulcer disease, ruptured ectopic pregnancies, postpartum hemorrhage, and coagulopathies. Risk factors that exacerbate bleeding tendencies include anticoagulant therapy, inherited bleeding disorders like haemophilia, severe liver disease affecting clotting factor synthesis, and chronic hypertension weakening vessel walls.

Clinical Assessment and Grading of Blood Loss

Clinical assessment requires evaluating vital signs, level of consciousness, and peripheral perfusion. Blood loss is traditionally graded into four classes based on physiological parameters:

  • Class I: Loss up to 15% (minimal tachycardia, normal blood pressure).
  • Class II: Loss 15-30% (tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure, anxious).
  • Class III: Loss 30-40% (marked tachycardia, hypotension, confused).
  • Class IV: Loss greater than 40% (profound shock, lethargy, weak or absent pulses).

In early hemorrhage, compensatory mechanisms can maintain normal blood pressure despite significant volume loss, making pulse rate and capillary refill more sensitive indicators than blood pressure alone.

Nursing Interventions and Emergency Management

Immediate nursing care for an actively bleeding patient follows the ABCDE framework. Apply direct manual pressure with a sterile dressing over external bleeding sites. Secure large-bore peripheral intravenous lines promptly to facilitate aggressive fluid resuscitation and blood product administration. Position the patient flat or in a modified Trendelenburg position if appropriate, administer high-flow oxygen to maximize tissue oxygenation, and continuously monitor vital signs and urine output via an indwelling catheter to assess renal perfusion.

Medical and Surgical Treatments

Definitive management aims to stop the source of bleeding and restore circulating volume. Medical therapy involves the transfusion of packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets to correct coagulopathies. Pharmacological agents such as tranexamic acid may be administered to inhibit fibrinolysis. Surgical intervention is frequently required for internal bleeding, ranging from exploratory laparoscopy or thoracotomy to vessel ligation, embolization by interventional radiologists, or surgical repair of damaged organs.

Complications of Haemorrhage

Uncorrected or severe haemorrhage leads to devastating systemic complications. The most critical is haemorrhagic shock, where inadequate tissue perfusion causes widespread cellular hypoxia and multiple organ dysfunction syndrome. Prolonged low flow can result in acute kidney injury, ischemic encephalopathy, and myocardial ischemia. Additionally, massive transfusion protocols carry risks such as hypothermia, hypocalcemia due to citrate binding, and transfusion-related acute lung injury.
