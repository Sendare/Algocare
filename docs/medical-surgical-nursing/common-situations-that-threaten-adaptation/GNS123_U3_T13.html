Dyspnoea

Definition and Pathophysiology of Dyspnoea

Dyspnoea is the subjective experience of breathing discomfort that consists of qualitatively distinct sensations that vary in intensity. It arises from interactions among multiple physiological pathways, signaling a mismatch between the ventilatory demand and the capacity of the respiratory system to meet that demand.

Afferent signals from chemoreceptors, mechanoreceptors in the lungs and chest wall, and higher cortical centers converge in the sensory cortex. When the motor output from the respiratory center in the medulla does not match the sensory feedback from the respiratory apparatus, the conscious perception of shortness of breath occurs. This mismatch is often driven by three main physiological abnormalities:

  • Increased respiratory work or load: Conditions like asthma or upper airway obstruction increase the resistive work of breathing.
  • Decreased ventilatory capacity: Muscle weakness, chest wall deformities, or restrictive lung diseases reduce the physical ability to expand the lungs.
  • Stimulation of respiratory receptors: Hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, or pulmonary congestion stimulate central and peripheral chemoreceptors and J-receptors, intensifying the sensation of breathlessness.

Etiology and Common Clinical Contexts

Breathing difficulties stem from a wide array of pulmonary, cardiac, systemic, and psychological origins. In Nigerian healthcare settings, nurses frequently encounter acute and chronic presentations linked to both communicable and non-communicable diseases. Recognizing these underlying etiologies guides immediate triage and targeted interventions.

  • Pulmonary Causes: Asthma exacerbations, community-acquired pneumonia, pulmonary tuberculosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and pleural effusion.
  • Cardiovascular Causes: Heart failure, hypertensive heart disease, rheumatic heart disease, and pulmonary embolism.
  • Systemic and Metabolic Causes: Severe anaemia, diabetic ketoacidosis causing Kussmaul breathing, and chronic kidney disease with uraemic acidosis.
  • Environmental and Psychogenic Causes: Biomass smoke inhalation from indoor cooking stoves, panic disorders, and acute anxiety states.

Classification and Clinical Presentation

Classifying breathlessness helps determine the urgency of care and tracks disease progression over time. Dyspnoea is broadly categorized as acute when it develops over minutes to hours, or chronic when it persists for weeks or longer.

The severity of chronic dyspnoea is frequently graded using standardized functional scales, such as the Medical Research Council (MRC) dyspnoea scale, which ranges from grade 1 (breathlessness only with strenuous exercise) to grade 5 (too breathless to leave the house).

Clinically, patients manifest distinct physical signs depending on the root cause. Tachypnoea, use of accessory muscles of respiration, nasal flaring, and intercostal retractions point toward significant respiratory distress. Cyanosis of the lips and nail beds indicates profound hypoxaemia. Furthermore, auditory cues like wheezing suggest bronchospasm, while crackles point toward fluid accumulation in the alveoli.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Evaluation

A systematic nursing assessment begins the moment the patient comes into view, evaluating general appearance, level of consciousness, and posture. Many individuals with severe respiratory compromise automatically adopt a tripod position—leaning forward with hands braced on the knees—to enhance the mechanical efficiency of their accessory muscles.

The history-taking process should capture the onset, duration, precipitating triggers, and relieving factors. Nurses must assess the exact character of the discomfort, as patients describe air hunger, tightness, or a smothering sensation differently depending on the underlying pathology.

Objective evaluation relies on vital signs and targeted physical examination. Oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry provides an immediate measure of oxygenation status. Auscultation of breath sounds across all lung fields identifies asymmetry, diminished air entry, adventitious sounds like crackles and wheezes, or a pleural friction rub.

Emergency Management and Interventions

Acute severe dyspnoea constitutes a medical emergency requiring immediate, synchronized action to secure airway patency, restore adequate oxygenation, and stabilize vital parameters. Nurses must act swiftly using standard emergency algorithms.

Initial care focuses on positioning the patient upright in a high Fowler's position to lower the diaphragm and reduce venous return to an overburdened heart. Supplemental oxygen therapy is initiated immediately via nasal cannula or face mask to maintain target oxygen saturations above 92% in most non-hypercapnic patients, or 88-92% in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Emergency pharmacological agents are administered promptly based on the suspected trigger. Rapid-acting bronchodilators via a spacer or nebulizer relieve bronchospasm, while intravenous loop diuretics like furosemide address acute pulmonary oedema. In cases of impending respiratory failure, preparations for endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation must begin without delay.

Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Nursing Care

Beyond acute stabilization, ongoing nursing care involves administering prescribed medications and implementing supportive therapies that conserve patient energy and reduce the sensation of breathlessness.

Pharmacological management relies on bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, mucolytics, and systemic anti-inflammatory agents. Nurses must verify the correct technique for inhaler devices, as improper usage limits drug delivery to the lower airways. For chronic, refractory dyspnoea associated with terminal illnesses, low-dose oral opioids may be prescribed to reduce the sensation of air hunger by blunting respiratory center sensitivity.

Non-pharmacological strategies are equally vital. Paced breathing techniques, such as pursed-lip breathing, help keep airways open longer during exhalation, preventing airway collapse. Energy conservation education teaches clients to schedule daily activities around rest periods, minimizing oxygen demand.

Complications, Patient Education, and Home Care

Long-standing or poorly managed dyspnoea carries substantial physical and psychological tolls, often culminating in complications like chronic hypoxaemia, secondary polycythaemia, cor pulmonale, and profound physical deconditioning. Anxiety and depression are also prevalent among affected individuals, creating a vicious cycle where fear of breathlessness triggers panic, which in turn worsens breathing difficulties.

Comprehensive patient education is essential for successful home management. Nurses must instruct patients and their families on recognizing early warning signs of worsening respiratory status, such as increased resting respiratory rate, dusky nail beds, or inability to complete full sentences. Clients should receive clear instructions regarding medication adherence, proper inhaler cleaning, and avoidance of triggers like smoke, dust, and damp living conditions.
