Dyspnoea

Definition and Pathophysiology of Dyspnoea

Dyspnoea is the subjective clinical sensation of difficult, labored, or uncomfortable breathing. It is important to separate dyspnoea from tachypnoea, which is simply a rapid breathing rate; a patient can experience severe dyspnoea without breathing fast, and conversely, hyperventilate without feeling breathless.

The pathophysiology involves a mismatch among three components: respiratory motor drive from the brainstem, neural output to the respiratory muscles, and afferent sensory feedback from receptors in the lungs, airways, and chest wall. When the respiratory centers in the medulla oblongata demand increased ventilation, but the mechanical system fails to match this demand—due to airway obstruction, poor lung compliance, or weak respiratory muscles—afferent signals via the vagus nerve and spinal pathways alert the cerebral cortex. The patient perceives this sensory conflict as shortness of breath.

Common Causes and Etiology in Nigerian Clinical Practice

In Nigerian clinical wards and emergency departments, dyspnoea presents secondary to a mix of infectious, chronic, and cardiovascular diseases. Malaria with severe anaemia frequently causes acute breathlessness due to reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. Cardiac conditions, particularly hypertensive heart failure and rheumatic heart disease, are major drivers of left ventricular failure leading to pulmonary congestion.

  • Infectious diseases: Lobar pneumonia, pulmonary tuberculosis, and severe acute respiratory infections are widespread triggers.
  • Obstructive airway diseases: Bronchial asthma exacerbations and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
  • Haematological conditions: Severe iron-deficiency anaemia and sickle cell vaso-occlusive crises with acute chest syndrome.
  • Environmental factors: Domestic exposure to biomass smoke from firewood cooking in poorly ventilated kitchens contributes significantly to chronic respiratory morbidity.

Classification and Clinical Presentation

Clinicians grade dyspnoea to assess disease severity and monitor treatment response. The Modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale grades breathlessness from grade 0 (breathless only with strenuous exercise) to grade 4 (too breathless to leave the house or breathless when dressing).

Patients exhibit various clinical signs depending on the underlying etiology:

  • Orthopnoea: Difficulty breathing when lying flat, forcing the patient to use multiple pillows; highly characteristic of left heart failure.
  • Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea: Sudden awakening from sleep gasping for air, relieved by sitting upright or standing.
  • Wheezing: A high-pitched whistling sound during expiration, pointing toward bronchospasm as seen in asthma.
  • Stridor: A harsh, high-pitched musical sound heard on inspiration, indicating upper airway obstruction which requires immediate intervention.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Evaluation

A systematic nursing assessment begins with the primary survey: airway patency, effective breathing rate and depth, and circulatory status. Nurses must observe for accessory muscle use, nasal flaring, and cyanosis of the lips or nail beds, which indicate severe hypoxia.

Obtain a focused history regarding the onset, duration, and precipitating factors of the breathlessness. Use a numerical rating scale from 0 to 10 to quantify the patient's perceived breathing difficulty. Auscultate the lung fields systematically from apex to base, comparing side to side, listening for vesicular breath sounds, crackles, wheezes, or reduced air entry. Document vital signs meticulously, paying special attention to oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry, blood pressure, and pulse rate.

Diagnostic Investigations and Interpretation

Diagnostic tests help confirm the root cause of dyspnoea. A bedside chest X-ray reveals pulmonary infiltrates in pneumonia, cardiomegaly and pleural effusion in heart failure, or hyperinflation in asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Laboratory evaluations provide biochemical clues:

  • Full blood count: Identifies severe anaemia or leukocytosis suggesting infection.
  • Arterial blood gases: Measures partial pressures of oxygen and carbon dioxide, assessing gas exchange efficiency and acid-base balance.
  • Sputum microscopy, culture, and sensitivity: Essential in tropical settings to rule out acid-fast bacilli for tuberculosis or identify bacterial pathogens.
  • Electrocardiogram: Rules out acute coronary syndromes or cardiac arrhythmias contributing to breathlessness.

Emergency Management and Interventions

Acute severe dyspnoea is a medical emergency requiring rapid intervention to prevent respiratory arrest. Immediately position the patient in a high Fowler's or tripod position to maximize chest expansion and diaphragm descent. Administer supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula or face mask to maintain oxygen saturation above 92% (or above 88-92% for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease at risk of carbon dioxide retention).

If bronchospasm is present, deliver rapid-acting bronchodilators using a nebulizer or metered-dose inhaler with a spacer. For acute heart failure with pulmonary oedema, administer prescribed diuretics like intravenous furosemide to reduce preload. Stay with the patient, offer calm reassurance to reduce anxiety, and prepare suction equipment and emergency airway trays in case deterioration occurs.

Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Nursing Care

Comprehensive nursing care combines medication administration with supportive physical therapies. Nurses must administer prescribed drugs accurately—such as bronchodilators, corticosteroids, antibiotics, or diuretics—while monitoring for adverse reactions like tachycardia from beta-agonists or electrolyte imbalances from diuretics.

Non-pharmacological techniques help reduce the sensation of breathlessness:

  • Pursed-lip breathing: Teaches the patient to inhale through the nose and exhale slowly through pursed lips, creating back-pressure to keep small airways open.
  • Diaphragmatic breathing: Guides the patient to use the abdominal muscles rather than upper chest muscles to breathe efficiently.
  • Energy conservation: Helps chronic respiratory patients pace daily activities like bathing and dressing to avoid severe exhaustion.
  • Cool airflow: Directing a small electric fan toward the patient's face stimulates trigeminal nerve receptors, which can significantly lessen the subjective feeling of dyspnoea.

Complications, Patient Education, and Home Care

Unmanaged or chronic dyspnoea can lead to severe physical and psychological complications, including chronic hypoxia, respiratory failure, cor pulmonale, and profound anxiety or depression. Patients and their families require structured education before discharge.

Teach patients to recognize early warning signs of deterioration, such as increased respiratory rate, darker coloration of the lips or fingers, inability to speak in full sentences, or swelling in the lower limbs. Ensure proper inhaler technique is demonstrated and return-demonstrated before discharge. Encourage smoking cessation and advise relatives to avoid indoor biomass smoke exposure by improving kitchen ventilation or transitioning to cleaner cooking fuels. Regular follow-up appointments ensure long-term stability and reduce hospital readmissions.
