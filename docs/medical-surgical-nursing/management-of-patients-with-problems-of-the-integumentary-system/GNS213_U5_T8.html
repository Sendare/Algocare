Clinical Manifestations of Skin Disorders

Introduction to Dermatological Assessment

Assessing the integumentary system requires a systematic approach using good lighting and proper exposure. Nurses must inspect the entire skin surface, noting color, moisture, temperature, and texture. Primary lesions are the initial skin changes caused directly by a disease process, while secondary lesions develop later from changes to the primary lesions or from external trauma. Accurate documentation uses precise medical terminology to describe the size, shape, color, and distribution of any abnormality observed.

Primary Skin Lesions

Primary lesions appear on previously normal skin. Recognizing their distinct physical characteristics helps nurses identify underlying dermatological conditions.

  • Macule: A flat, distinct, discolored area less than 1 cm in size, such as a freckle.
  • Papule: A small, raised, solid pimple or swelling less than 1 cm across.
  • Plaque: A broad, raised, solid, scaly skin patch greater than 1 cm in diameter.
  • Vesicle: A small, fluid-filled blister containing serum, less than 1 cm wide.
  • Bulla: A large, fluid-filled blister measuring greater than 1 cm across.
  • Pustule: A small, raised skin lesion filled with purulent cloudy fluid or pus.

Secondary Skin Lesions

Secondary lesions evolve from primary lesions or are created by external actions like scratching, picking, or natural healing processes.

  • Crust: Dried blood, serum, or pus on the skin surface, commonly known as a scab.
  • Excoriation: A hollowed-out, crusted erosion of the epidermis caused by scratching or scraping.
  • Lichenification: Rough, thickened epidermis with accentuated skin markings resulting from chronic rubbing.
  • Fissure: A linear crack or break extending from the epidermis down into the dermis.
  • Scar: Fibrous connective tissue that replaces normal skin following injury or deep ulceration.
  • Ulcer: A deep loss of skin tissue extending past the epidermis into the dermis or subcutaneous layer.

Vascular and Purpuric Lesions

Vascular lesions involve blood vessels and can be flat or raised. Petechiae are tiny, flat, pinpoint red or purple spots caused by minor capillary bleeding. Purpura are larger flat patches formed by similar bleeding under the skin. A critical clinical test for these spots is blanching. Pressing a glass slide or finger against the lesion is called diascopy. Vascular lesions and inflammatory rashes generally blanch, meaning they turn white when pressed because blood is pushed out of the vessels. Purpuric and petechial lesions do NOT blanch because the red blood cells have leaked completely outside the blood vessels into the surrounding tissue.

Subjective Symptoms: Pruritus, Pain, and Sensory Alterations

Skin disorders often present with uncomfortable sensations reported by the patient. Pruritus, or itching, is the most common and distressing symptom in dermatology. It triggers a physical scratch reflex that unfortunately damages the skin barrier, leading to secondary lesions and potential infection. Pain or burning sensations can indicate nerve involvement, inflammation, or tissue ischemia. Sensory alterations like tingling or numbness often accompany conditions affecting superficial nerves, such as herpes zoster outbreaks before the rash even appears.

Systemic Manifestations of Dermatological Conditions

The skin often reflects internal disease. A yellow discoloration of the skin and sclera points to jaundice, indicating liver dysfunction or hemolysis. Generalized pallor suggests anemia, while cyanosis reveals poor oxygenation. Widespread rashes can signal systemic drug reactions, autoimmune diseases, or infectious illnesses like measles. Nurses must look beyond the skin surface, assessing vital signs, body temperature, and nutritional status whenever a complex dermatological presentation is identified.

Nursing Assessment and Documentation of Skin Disorders

Thorough nursing documentation requires clear, objective descriptions. Nurses should record the exact anatomical location, color, configuration, and approximate dimensions of any skin finding. Avoid vague terms like "rash" without context; instead, specify whether the lesions are clustered, linear, annular, or generalized. Documenting the progression of lesions over time helps the healthcare team evaluate whether treatments are working effectively or if the disease is worsening.
