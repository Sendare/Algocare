Clinical Manifestations of Skin Disorders

Introduction to Dermatological Assessment

Evaluating the integumentary system requires a systematic approach, adequate lighting, and proper exposure of the patient's skin. A complete dermatological examination begins with general inspection before moving to palpation of specific lesions, skin temperature, and turgor. Assessment also demands a thorough patient history, including the timeline of the rash, exposure to potential allergens, occupational hazards, and travel history. Nigerian climate factors, such as high humidity and intense sunlight, heavily influence the presentation of many dermatoses, making local epidemiological context vital during clinical intake.

Primary Skin Lesions

Primary lesions are the initial, unaltered manifestations of a pathological skin process. Recognizing these structures accurately forms the foundation of dermatological diagnosis. - **Macule**: A flat, circumscribed change in skin color measuring less than one centimeter in diameter, such as a freckle. - **Papule**: A solid, elevated, palpable lesion less than one centimeter across, commonly seen in early acne or insect bites. - **Plaque**: A broad, elevated, superficial solid lesion greater than one centimeter, typical of psoriasis. - **Vesicle**: A small, fluid-filled blister containing serous fluid that measures less than one centimeter, as observed in herpes simplex. - **Bulla**: A fluid-filled blister greater than one centimeter in diameter, characteristic of second-degree burns or pemphigus. - **Pustule**: A discrete, elevated cavity filled with purulent exudate, seen in folliculitis. - **Nodule**: A solid, firm lesion extending deeper into the dermis than a papule, measuring greater than one centimeter.

Secondary Skin Lesions

Secondary lesions evolve from primary lesions due to natural disease progression, scratching, trauma, or therapeutic interventions. Documenting these features reveals how a skin condition has changed over time. - **Scale**: Accumulations of keratinized stratum corneum cells forming flaky heaps, seen in dry eczema. - **Crust**: Dried serum, blood, or exudate on the skin surface, commonly known as a scab. - **Excoriation**: Linear or hollowed-out crusted abrasions caused by scratching or picking. - **Fissure**: A linear crack or break extending from the epidermis into the dermis, often occurring over joints or dry heels. - **Ulcera**: A deep loss of epidermis and dermis, healing with scar formation. - **Scar**: Fibrous connective tissue replacing normal skin after an injury or deep ulceration.

Vascular and Purpuric Lesions

Vascular lesions involve altered blood vessel structures or bleeding into the skin. A critical clinical skill when assessing purpuric lesions is performing diascopy—pressing a glass slide against the lesion. Non-blanching lesions, such as purpura and petechiae, indicate extravasation of red blood cells out of the vascular network and do not fade under pressure. Conversely, erythematous inflammatory lesions blanch when compressed. Petechiae are tiny pinpoint purpuric spots, while ecchymoses represent larger bruises. Spider angiomas feature a central pulsating arteriole with radiating capillary branches, often linked to liver disease.

Subjective Symptoms: Pruritus, Pain, and Sensory Alterations

Dermatological manifestations are not purely visual; subjective sensations profoundly impact patient well-being. Pruritus, or intense itching, is the most common symptom of skin disease and can trigger a self-perpetuating itch-scratch cycle that worsens secondary lesions. Pain and burning sensations often accompany inflammatory or infectious processes like herpes zoster. Sensory alterations, including paresthesias or numbness, suggest underlying neuropathic involvement. Evaluating the exact distribution and timing of these symptoms helps differentiate local dermatologic conditions from systemic or neurologic disorders.

Systemic Manifestations and Diagnostic Signs

Skin disorders frequently mirror internal systemic pathology. Jaundice indicates hyperbilirubinemia manifesting in the skin and sclera. Cyanosis reveals poor tissue oxygenation. Nail changes, such as clubbing, point toward chronic hypoxia or cardiovascular disease. Specific clinical signs also aid physical evaluation, such as Auspitz sign in psoriasis, where gentle removal of scales reveals pinpoint bleeding points. Recognizing these cutaneous markers enables nurses to detect systemic diseases early during routine physical examinations.

Nursing Documentation of Dermatological Findings

Precise documentation ensures continuity of care and allows clinical teams to track disease progression or treatment response over time. A complete skin record must specify lesion morphology, exact anatomical location, color, texture, configuration, and distribution pattern (e.g., localized, generalized, annular, or linear). Vague terms like 'bad rash' are clinically unhelpful. Instead, nurses must use standardized dermatological terminology to describe size, border regularity, and whether lesions are discrete or confluent.
