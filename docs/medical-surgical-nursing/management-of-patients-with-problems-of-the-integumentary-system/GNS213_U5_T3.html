Skin Biopsy

Introduction and Indications for Skin Biopsy

A skin biopsy is a fundamental diagnostic procedure performed in dermatological and clinical nursing practice to obtain tissue samples for microscopic examination. In the Nigerian healthcare context, student nurses must understand that skin biopsies are critical for confirming diagnoses of various dermatological conditions, including malignant neoplasms such as basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and malignant melanoma. Furthermore, they are indicated for inflammatory skin disorders like psoriasis, lichen planus, and lupus erythematosus, as well as chronic infectious diseases such as leprosy and deep fungal infections that are prevalent in tropical regions. The primary goal is to secure a definitive histological diagnosis when visual inspection and clinical history are inconclusive. Nurses play a vital role in identifying patients who require this diagnostic intervention, explaining the rationale behind the procedure to alleviate patient anxiety, and ensuring that informed consent is properly obtained by the attending physician or trained clinician before the procedure begins. Thorough baseline assessments help determine if the patient has any pre-existing conditions that might complicate healing or alter tissue integrity, making clinical judgment essential for safe nursing care.

Types of Skin Biopsies: Shave, Punch, and Excisional

Understanding the different types of skin biopsies is essential for nurses assisting in dermatological procedures. A shave biopsy utilizes a flexible surgical blade or scalpel to remove a small superficial sample of the epidermis and upper dermis, making it ideal for raised lesions such as seborrheic keratoses or superficial skin cancers, though it is not recommended for suspected melanomas requiring deep tissue evaluation. A punch biopsy employs a circular, cookie-cutter-like blade ranging from 2mm to 8mm in diameter to obtain a full-thickness cylindrical core of tissue extending through the epidermis, dermis, and into the subcutaneous fat. This method is exceptionally useful for inflammatory dermatoses and deep lesions, and it typically requires one or two simple sutures for closure. An excisional biopsy involves the complete surgical removal of an entire lesion along with a border of normal-appearing surrounding skin down to the subcutaneous fat, using an elliptical incision. This method is the gold standard for completely excising suspected melanomas or large dermal nodules. Knowing the specific technique helps the nurse prepare the correct tray setup, anticipate the provider's needs, and provide accurate post-procedure instructions tailored to the specific wound closure method utilized.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Patient Assessment

Pre-procedure preparation and comprehensive patient assessment are critical nursing responsibilities that ensure patient safety and optimize healing outcomes. The nurse must conduct a thorough health history, specifically asking about bleeding disorders, anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications (such as aspirin, warfarin, or clopidogrel), and known drug allergies, particularly to local anesthetics like lidocaine or antiseptic solutions like chlorhexidine and povidone-iodine. Assessing the patient's baseline vital signs and emotional state helps identify anxiety or unstable medical conditions. The nurse must also inspect the target skin site for signs of active local infection, inflammation, or anatomical landmarks that might complicate the procedure. Patient education is a major component of this phase; the nurse should explain what the procedure entails, the expected sensations (such as a brief stinging from the local anesthetic injection), and potential scarring. Obtaining written informed consent is a mandatory legal and ethical step, and the nurse must verify that the consent form is signed and placed in the chart before any medication is administered. Proper preparation builds trust, reduces patient fear, and prevents avoidable clinical complications.

Equipment and Local Anesthesia Administration

Proper equipment assembly and safe administration of local anesthesia are vital for a successful skin biopsy. The nurse is responsible for gathering and organizing sterile instruments, which typically include a sterile drape, gauze pads, a scalpel or specific biopsy punch tool, forceps, needle drivers, suture material, and specimen containers filled with fixative such as 10% neutral buffered formalin. Skin antisepsis is achieved using an approved antiseptic agent like chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine. Local anesthesia is almost universally achieved using 1% or 2% lidocaine, frequently combined with epinephrine (1:100,000) to provide vasoconstriction that minimizes local bleeding and prolongs the anesthetic effect. However, student nurses must remember that lidocaine with epinephrine should be avoided in end-arterial structures such as fingers, toes, nose, and ears due to the risk of severe vasoconstriction-induced tissue necrosis. The nurse assists by maintaining sterile technique, opening supplies aseptically, and supporting the clinician during the local infiltration, while carefully monitoring the patient for any signs of adverse reactions or systemic local anesthetic toxicity.

Step-by-Step Procedure Execution

Executing a skin biopsy step-by-step requires precision, adherence to strict aseptic technique, and seamless collaboration between the clinician and the assisting nurse. First, the biopsy site is thoroughly cleansed with an antiseptic solution and allowed to dry, followed by the placement of sterile fenestrated drapes to create a sterile field. The clinician then administers the local anesthetic subcutaneously or intradermally, and the nurse observes the patient for any signs of vasovagal response or allergic reaction. Once anesthesia is confirmed, the clinician performs the chosen technique: shaving the lesion with a blade, punching a core of tissue, or excising an ellipse with a scalpel. If a punch or excisional biopsy is performed, the nurse anticipates the need for hemostasis—often using direct pressure, chemical cautery agents like silver nitrate or Monsel's solution, or electrocautery—and assists with tissue handling. Following hemostasis, wound closure is achieved using non-absorbable or absorbable sutures (such as nylon or polypropylene) or sterile adhesive strips depending on the wound tension and depth. Throughout the procedure, the nurse maintains the sterile field, manages sharps safely in accordance with standard precautions, and provides reassurance to the patient.

Post-Procedure Wound Care and Patient Education

Post-procedure wound care and thorough patient education are essential to prevent infection, minimize scarring, and ensure optimal healing. Immediately following the procedure, the nurse cleanses the site gently and applies a sterile, non-adherent dressing and tape to protect the wound from external contamination. The nurse must provide clear, verbal and written discharge instructions to the patient or caregiver. Key instructions include keeping the dressing clean, dry, and intact for the first 24 to 48 hours, after which the site can be gently washed with mild soap and water. Patients should be instructed to apply a thin layer of petroleum jelly or prescribed antibiotic ointment to keep the wound moist and prevent crusting. The nurse must also inform the patient about signs of normal healing versus abnormal complications, such as increasing redness, swelling, purulent discharge, or worsening pain. If non-absorbable sutures were placed, the patient must be given a specific appointment time for suture removal, which typically ranges from 5 to 7 days for facial lesions and 10 to 14 days for lesions on the trunk or extremities.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although skin biopsies are generally safe and minor procedures, nursing students must be prepared to recognize and manage potential complications. Common complications include localized wound infection, excessive bleeding or hematoma formation, wound dehiscence, persistent pain, allergic reactions to topical or local anesthetic agents, and abnormal scarring such as hypertrophic scars or keloids—the latter being particularly important to monitor in certain populations. The nurse must intervene promptly if complications arise. For bleeding, applying continuous, firm direct pressure for 15 to 20 minutes is the initial nursing action. If signs of infection appear—such as spreading erythema, localized warmth, purulent exudate, or fever—the nurse should notify the clinician, obtain wound cultures if ordered, and prepare the patient for potential antibiotic therapy. Providing empathetic emotional support and accurate guidance helps reassure patients who experience anxiety over unexpected complications like excessive bruising or visible scarring.

Specimen Handling, Labeling, and Laboratory Transport

Accurate specimen handling, meticulous labeling, and safe laboratory transport are critical responsibilities that directly impact patient diagnosis and safety. Once the tissue specimen is excised, it must be handled with extreme care using blunt instruments (such as forceps) rather than surgical forceps that could crush and distort the cellular architecture. The tissue is immediately placed into a specimen container filled with an appropriate fixative, most commonly 10% neutral buffered formalin, which preserves cellular morphology and prevents tissue autolysis and decay. The volume of formalin should be at least ten times the volume of the tissue specimen. The nurse must ensure that the specimen container is labeled accurately and immediately at the bedside, including the patient's full name, hospital or identification number, specific anatomical site of the biopsy, date, and time of collection. This container must be accompanied by a properly completed pathology requisition form detailing the clinical history, differential diagnosis, and any pertinent medical background. Finally, the specimen must be securely packaged in a biohazard bag and transported promptly to the histopathology laboratory following institutional protocols to prevent loss, mislabeling, or compromised diagnostic results.
