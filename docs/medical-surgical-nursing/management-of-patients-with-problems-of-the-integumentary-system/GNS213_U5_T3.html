Skin Biopsy

Introduction and Clinical Indications

A skin biopsy is a minor surgical procedure where a small tissue sample is removed from the cutaneous layer, preserved, and sent for microscopic examination. It serves as a definitive diagnostic tool when visual inspection alone cannot differentiate between malignant neoplasms, inflammatory dermatoses, and persistent infections.

Common indications include:

  • Suspected skin malignancies: Evaluating basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and malignant melanoma.
  • Inflammatory skin disorders: Diagnosing conditions like psoriasis, lupus erythematosus, and bullous pemphigoid.
  • Persistent cutaneous infections: Identifying chronic fungal, bacterial, or atypical mycobacterial infections that fail empirical treatment.

Types of Skin Biopsies

Selecting the correct biopsy technique depends on the suspected pathology, lesion depth, and anatomical location.

  • Shave Biopsy: Utilizes a scalpel blade or flexible razor to remove superficial lesions elevated above the skin surface, such as seborrheic keratoses or superficial basal cell carcinomas. Subcutaneous fat is avoided.
  • Punch Biopsy: Employs a circular, cookie-cutter-like blade ranging from 2mm to 8mm in diameter to obtain a full-thickness cylindrical core of tissue, reaching down to the subcutaneous fat. Ideal for inflammatory and deep dermal disorders.
  • Excisional Biopsy: Removes the entire lesion along with a narrow margin of normal surrounding skin down to the subcutaneous layer, typically using an elliptical incision. This is the gold standard for suspected melanomas.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Patient Assessment

Thorough pre-procedure preparation minimizes complications and ensures patient cooperation. Begin by taking a detailed medication history, specifically screening for anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy such as aspirin, warfarin, or clopidogrel, which can increase bleeding risks. Assess for known allergies to local anesthetics, latex, or antiseptic solutions like chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine.

Explain the procedure steps and potential scarring to the patient, then obtain written informed consent. Inspect the target site, check for local signs of active infection, and cleanse the skin thoroughly before marking the excision or punch boundaries with a surgical marker.

Equipment, Anesthesia, and Setup

A sterile field must be established with all necessary instruments organized before the procedure begins. Common equipment includes a surgical marker, sterile gloves, antiseptic solution, local anesthetic (typically 1% or 2% lidocaine with or without epinephrine), a small syringe with a 25G to 27G needle, biopsy blades or punch tools, forceps, needle drivers, suture material, and specimen containers.

Local anesthesia is achieved by injecting the anesthetic intradermally and subcutaneously to create a dermal wheal. The addition of epinephrine helps cause vasoconstriction, which minimizes bleeding and prolongs anesthetic duration, though it should be avoided in end-arterial areas such as fingers, toes, nose, and the tip of the penis to prevent tissue ischemia.

Step-by-Step Procedure Execution

Executing a skin biopsy requires precision and strict adherence to aseptic technique. After draping the area and wearing sterile gloves, the clinician anesthetizes the site.

For a punch biopsy, the instrument is held perpendicular to the skin surface, gently rotated with downward pressure until it penetrates the subcutaneous fat, and withdrawn. The core is gently lifted with fine forceps and severed at the base with iris scissors to avoid crushing artifact.

For an excisional biopsy, an ellipse is incised with a scalpel, undermining the edges slightly if tension is high, followed by closure using interrupted sutures to align skin tension lines properly.

Post-Procedure Wound Care and Patient Education

Proper post-procedure care prevents infection and promotes optimal cosmetic healing. The wound should be covered with a sterile non-adherent dressing and kept dry for the first 24 to 48 hours. Patients must be instructed on gentle cleansing with mild soap and water once the initial dressing is removed, followed by the application of a thin layer of petroleum jelly to keep the wound moist and prevent crusting.

Provide clear instructions regarding physical activity restrictions to avoid putting tension on the suture line. Inform the patient when sutures are scheduled for removal based on anatomical location, such as 5 to 7 days for the face versus 10 to 14 days for the lower extremities.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although skin biopsies are minor procedures, nurses must monitor for and manage potential complications.

  • Bleeding and Hematoma: Apply direct firm pressure for 15 to 20 minutes if bleeding occurs. Patients on anticoagulants are at higher risk.
  • Infection: Educate patients on recognizing early signs of infection, including spreading erythema, purulent discharge, warmth, and worsening pain.
  • Dehiscence: Occurs when wound edges separate due to excessive tension or early suture loss; advise avoidance of heavy lifting and strenuous stretching.
  • Scarring and Keloids: Inform prone patients about the risk of hypertrophic scarring or keloid formation.

Specimen Handling, Labeling, and Transport

Accurate pathology results depend heavily on meticulous specimen handling. The specimen bottle must be labeled at the bedside with the patient's full name, hospital number, exact anatomical site of the biopsy, and date of collection.

The tissue core or ellipse must be placed immediately into a container filled with 10% neutral buffered formalin to prevent cellular autolysis and degradation. Ensure the container lid is tightly secured to prevent leakage or formalin evaporation during transport to the histology laboratory.
