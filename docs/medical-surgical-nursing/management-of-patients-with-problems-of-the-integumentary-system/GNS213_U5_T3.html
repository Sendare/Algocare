Skin Biopsy

Introduction and Indications for Skin Biopsy

A skin biopsy is a minor surgical procedure where a small sample of skin tissue is removed, sent to a laboratory, and examined under a microscope by a pathologist. Nurses encounter this procedure frequently in dermatology clinics, general surgery wards, and primary care settings.

Clinicians order a skin biopsy to confirm a suspected diagnosis, rule out malignancy, or monitor the progression of inflammatory skin diseases. Common indications include:

  • Persistent rashes: Eczema, psoriasis, or drug eruptions that do not respond to standard treatment.
  • Suspicious lesions: Moles showing changes in color, border, or size, which may indicate melanoma or basal cell carcinoma.
  • Blistering disorders: Pemphigus and other autoimmune conditions requiring immunofluorescence studies.
  • Infections: Deep fungal or bacterial skin infections that resist routine scraping or cultures.

Types of Skin Biopsies: Shave, Punch, and Excisional

Different clinical scenarios require different biopsy techniques. Selecting the right method ensures the pathologist gets enough tissue while minimizing scarring.

  • Shave biopsy: Uses a surgical blade or dermablade to slice off a lesion elevated above the skin surface. Ideal for superficial lesions like seborrheic keratoses or warts, but generally not used for suspected melanoma because it leaves deep tissue unexamined.
  • Punch biopsy: Uses a circular, cookie-cutter-like instrument to remove a full-thickness core of skin, including the epidermis, dermis, and superficial fat. Excellent for inflammatory rashes and small lesions, often requiring one or two simple sutures to close.
  • Excisional biopsy: Uses a scalpel to remove the entire lesion down to the subcutaneous fat, including a margin of normal surrounding skin. Used when complete removal of a suspicious lesion like a suspected melanoma is required.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Patient Assessment

Before any skin biopsy, the nurse conducts a thorough patient assessment. Gathering a complete medical history prevents unexpected complications during or after the procedure.

The nurse must specifically screen for bleeding disorders and medication use. Patients taking anticoagulant medications, such as warfarin, aspirin, or direct oral anticoagulants, have an increased risk of prolonged bleeding, though stopping these medications is rarely necessary for small skin biopsies unless specified by the provider. Documenting known allergies to local anesthetics, such as lidocaine, or topical antiseptics like chlorhexidine or iodine, keeps the patient safe.

Addressing patient anxiety is another vital nursing responsibility. Many patients fear the word biopsy because they associate it automatically with cancer. Clear, reassuring explanations about the procedure steps and expected sensations help ease tension.

Equipment and Local Anesthesia Administration

A well-organized tray streamlines the procedure and maintains a sterile field. The nurse gathers the required sterile instruments, personal protective equipment, cleaning agents, and local anesthetic supplies.

Local anesthesia is achieved using an injectable agent, most commonly lidocaine 1% with or without epinephrine. Epinephrine constricts local blood vessels, reducing bleeding and making the local anesthetic last longer, but it should be avoided in areas with end-arterial circulation like fingers, toes, nose, and ears due to the risk of tissue necrosis.

The nurse assists with or administers the injection using a fine-gauge needle, warning the patient that a brief stinging or burning sensation will occur as the medication enters the tissue.

Step-by-Step Procedure Execution

Executing a skin biopsy requires precision, strict sterile technique, and close collaboration between the clinician and the assisting nurse.

  1. Skin Preparation: Clean the site with an antiseptic solution like chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine and let it dry completely.
  2. Anesthesia: Inject the local anesthetic to numb the area, verifying numbness before proceeding.
  3. Tissue Acquisition: The clinician performs the chosen biopsy technique (shave, punch, or excision).
  4. Hemostasis: Control minor bleeding using direct pressure, chemical styptics like Monsel's solution, or electrocautery if necessary.
  5. Wound Closure: If a punch or excisional biopsy was performed, close the defect with simple interrupted non-absorbable or absorbable sutures.

Post-Procedure Wound Care and Patient Education

Clear home care instructions prevent secondary infection and promote optimal cosmetic healing. The nurse provides both verbal and written guidance before the patient leaves the clinic.

  • Dressing Management: Keep the initial pressure bandage clean and dry for the first 24 to 48 hours. After this period, patients can gently wash the area with mild soap and water.
  • Ointment Application: Apply a thin layer of petroleum jelly or prescribed antibiotic ointment to keep the wound moist and prevent hard scabs from forming, which aids re-epithelialization.
  • Activity Restrictions: Advise the patient to avoid strenuous physical exercise or heavy lifting that pulls on the wound edges, especially if sutures are present.
  • Suture Removal: Remind patients when to return for suture removal, which typically ranges from 5 to 7 days for the face and 10 to 14 days for the back or extremities.

Potential Complications and Nursing Interventions

Although skin biopsies are minor procedures, complications can occasionally occur. Recognizing early signs of trouble ensures prompt intervention.

  • Infection: Patients may develop localized cellulitis characterized by spreading erythema, warmth, purulent drainage, and worsening pain. Nurses instruct patients to report these signs immediately.
  • Bleeding and Hematoma: Minor oozing is normal, but persistent bleeding or a firm, painful swelling indicates a hematoma. Applying prolonged direct pressure usually resolves superficial bleeding.
  • Scarring and Dehiscence: Hypertrophic scars, keloids, or wound breakdown can occur, particularly in areas of high skin tension or over joints.
  • Allergic Reactions: Contact dermatitis from topical antibiotics or hypersensitivity to local anesthetics can manifest as pruritic rashes around the site.

Specimen Handling, Labeling, and Laboratory Transport

Accurate pathology results depend entirely on proper specimen collection, labeling, and handling. A labeling error can lead to misdiagnosis and severe patient harm.

The nurse ensures the specimen container is pre-filled with the correct fixative, usually 10% neutral buffered formalin, unless special studies like direct immunofluorescence require specific transport media or frozen sections. The container label must include at least two patient identifiers, the exact anatomical site of the biopsy, and the date and time of collection.

The accompanying pathology requisition form must match the container label perfectly and provide a detailed clinical history, including the patient's age, clinical impression, and the appearance of the lesion.
