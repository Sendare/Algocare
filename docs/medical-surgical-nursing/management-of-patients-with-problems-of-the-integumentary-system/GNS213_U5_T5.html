Leprosy

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Leprosy

Leprosy, also known as Hansen's disease, is a chronic infectious condition caused by the obligate intracellular bacterium Mycobacterium leprae. The organism has a notoriously slow replication rate, with a generation time of about 12 to 14 days, which explains the exceptionally long incubation period ranging from several months to upwards of twenty years. Transmission primarily occurs through prolonged, close droplet contact with untreated individuals via the upper respiratory tract. Once inside the host, *M. leprae* exhibits a distinct thermal preference for cooler peripheral tissues, specifically targeting Schwann cells of peripheral nerves and macrophages in the skin and mucosa. This tropism dictates the hallmark clinical presentation of localized cutaneous lesions and progressive peripheral nerve damage.

Classification and Clinical Manifestations

Clinical presentation depends heavily on the cell-mediated immune response of the infected host. The disease is broadly categorized into two polar forms using the Ridley-Jopling system, alongside operational classifications by the World Health Organization:

  • Tuberculoid Leprosy (Paucibacillary): Associated with a strong cellular immune response. Patients present with few, well-demarcated, hypopigmented skin patches that are markedly anesthetic. Peripheral nerve involvement is typically asymmetric and localized.
  • Lepromatous Leprosy (Multibacillary): Characterized by a weak cellular immune response, resulting in widespread bacterial proliferation. Manifests as numerous symmetrical macules, papules, nodular infiltrates, and the classic thickening of facial features known as facies leonina. Nerve damage is widespread and symmetrical.

A cardinal diagnostic sign across forms is the loss of thermal, pain, and tactile sensation within skin lesions.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Clinical Assessment

Diagnosing leprosy relies heavily on careful physical examination and clinical suspicion rather than complex laboratory assays. A definitive diagnosis requires identifying at least one of three cardinal signs: hypopigmented or reddish skin patches with definite sensory loss, thickened or enlarged peripheral nerves with tenderness, and the presence of acid-fast bacilli on skin slit smears.

During assessment, health workers gently palpate major peripheral nerve trunks—such as the ulnar, median, radial, posterior tibial, and great auricular nerves—checking for enlargement, tenderness, or sensory deficits in their distribution fields. Skin sensation testing uses a wisp of cotton for light touch, a warm and cold test tube for thermal appreciation, and a blunt pin for pain perception. When available, skin slit smears taken from earlobes or lesions provide a bacteriological index, though facilities in resource-limited settings rely heavily on clinical criteria.

Pharmacological Management and Multidrug Therapy

The introduction of Multidrug Therapy (MDT) by the World Health Organization revolutionized leprosy management, successfully preventing widespread antimicrobial resistance and curing millions of individuals. Regimens vary depending on whether the infection is classified as paucibacillary or multibacillary.

ClassificationCore MedicationsDuration
Paucibacillary (PB)Rifampicin and Dapsone6 months
Multibacillary (MB)Rifampicin, Dapsone, and Clofazimine12 to 24 months

Rifampicin is administered as a monthly supervised dose to ensure rapid bactericidal action. Dapsone is taken daily, with routine monitoring for potential hematological side effects like hemolytic anemia. Clofazimine, included in multibacillary regimens, has anti-inflammatory properties alongside its mild antimycobacterial action, though it causes reversible brownish-black skin discoloration that requires advance patient counseling.

Nursing Care and Prevention of Disabilities

Nursing care for individuals with leprosy centers heavily on preventing permanent physical impairments resulting from nerve anesthesia and muscle weakness. Because patients cannot reliably feel pain, heat, or pressure, they are at high risk for traumatic injuries, burns, and chronic plantar ulcerations. Nursing interventions include teaching daily self-inspection of the hands and feet using a mirror to spot early cuts, blisters, or redness before they ulcerate.

Protective measures involve wearing well-fitting, thick-soled footwear to distribute plantar pressure and using gloves when handling hot objects. Joint stiffness and claw hand deformities are mitigated through regular, gentle range-of-motion exercises and oil massage to keep dry skin pliable. Regular assessment of visual acuity is also essential, as lagophthalmos—the inability to fully close the eyelids due to facial nerve damage—can lead to corneal exposure, ulceration, and blindness if protective eye drops or glasses are neglected.

Complications and Reactional States

Immune-mediated reactional episodes can occur before, during, or even after the completion of multidrug therapy, often causing rapid nerve damage and acute tissue destruction. These episodes are classified into two major types:

  • Type 1 Reaction (Reversal Reaction): A delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction occurring mainly in borderline leprosy. Existing skin lesions suddenly become inflamed, swollen, and red, and previously unaffected nerves swell rapidly, causing sudden pain and loss of function.
  • Type 2 Reaction (Erythema Nodosum Leprosum): An immune-complex mediated response seen in multibacillary patients. It presents with painful, tender, subcutaneous red nodules, accompanied by systemic symptoms such as fever, malaise, neuritis, orchitis, and iridocyclitis.

Prompt recognition and treatment with systemic corticosteroids, such as prednisolone, are vital to prevent irreversible nerve paralysis and permanent deformities.

Patient Education, Rehabilitation, and Stigma Reduction

Beyond biological cure, managing leprosy requires addressing profound psychosocial challenges driven by historical stigma and misconceptions. Many communities harbor deep-seated fears that the disease is violently contagious or a curse, leading to social isolation, job loss, and delayed health-seeking behavior. Nurses play a frontline role in community health education, emphasizing that leprosy has a low level of contagiousness, is entirely curable with standard multidrug therapy, and that patients on treatment pose no risk to household members.

Rehabilitation extends to supporting physical recovery through reconstructive surgeries for claw hand or foot drop, alongside vocational training that empowers affected individuals to regain financial independence. Integrating leprosy care into general health services rather than isolating treatment in specialized colonies helps normalize the condition and dismantle institutionalized stigma.
