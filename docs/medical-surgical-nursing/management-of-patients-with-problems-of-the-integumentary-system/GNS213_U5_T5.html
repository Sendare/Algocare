Leprosy

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Leprosy

Leprosy, also known as Hansen's disease, is a chronic infectious condition caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium leprae. This slow-growing organism targets primarily the peripheral nerves and the cooler superficial tissues of the skin, mucous membranes, and eyes. Transmission occurs mainly through prolonged, close contact via respiratory droplets from untreated individuals.

Once inside the body, the bacterium has a remarkably long incubation period, often lasting anywhere from three to five years, though symptoms can take decades to appear.

  • Neurotropism: The organism has a unique affinity for Schwann cells, which form the protective myelin sheath around peripheral nerves.
  • Demyelination: Bacterial invasion leads to chronic inflammation, localized nerve swelling, and gradual destruction of nerve fibers.
  • Anesthesia: Loss of sensory function occurs in affected skin patches as nerve damage progresses, making the patient vulnerable to unnoticed trauma and burns.

A common misconception is that leprosy causes body parts to literally rot and fall off. In reality, the tissue loss and deformities stem from secondary injuries, infections, and bone absorption occurring because the patient cannot feel pain or pressure in damaged extremities.

Classification and Clinical Manifestations

Clinical classification of leprosy helps guide treatment duration and predicts how the patient's immune system is handling the infection. The two main operational categories defined by the World Health Organization are based primarily on skin lesion counts and slit-skin smear results.

  • Paucibacillary (PB) Leprosy: Characterized by one to five skin lesions with negative skin smears. The patient's cell-mediated immune response is relatively strong, keeping the bacterial load low.
  • Multibacillary (MB) Leprosy: Involves more than five skin lesions, widespread nerve involvement, and positive skin smears. The immune response fails to contain the bacteria, resulting in a high bacterial load.

Physical signs vary widely. You will see hypopigmented or reddish skin patches that do not itch and have a distinct loss of sensation. As nerve trunks like the ulnar, median, or common peroneal nerves become enlarged and tender, characteristic motor deficits appear, such as claw hand or foot drop.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Clinical Assessment

Diagnosing leprosy relies heavily on physical and neurological examinations rather than complex laboratory tests alone. A definitive diagnosis requires finding at least one of the three cardinal signs: definite loss of sensation in a pale or reddish skin patch, thickened or enlarged peripheral nerves with accompanying tenderness, and acid-fast bacilli found in slit-skin smears.

When performing a clinical assessment, inspect the skin thoroughly under good lighting. Gently test light touch, pain, and temperature over skin lesions using cotton wisps or test tubes filled with warm and cold water. Palpate major peripheral nerve trunks—including the ulnar nerve at the elbow, the median nerve at the wrist, and the lateral popliteal nerve behind the knee—noting any thickening, nodularity, or tenderness. Slit-skin smears taken from earlobes and lesions can confirm the bacterial index in multibacillary cases, while skin biopsy remains a helpful confirmatory tool when clinical signs are uncertain.

Pharmacological Management and Multidrug Therapy

The introduction of Multidrug Therapy (MDT) revolutionized leprosy care by curing the infection quickly and preventing drug resistance. Monotherapy is strictly avoided because Mycobacterium leprae rapidly develops resistance to single agents like dapsone.

MDT combines specific bactericidal and bacteriostatic medications taken over fixed durations depending on the classification:

  • Rifampicin: A powerful bactericidal agent given monthly under supervision and daily in self-administered blister packs.
  • Dapsone: A bacteriostatic sulfone medication taken daily by the patient at home.
  • Clofazimine: Both bactericidal and anti-inflammatory, taken daily with a higher supervised monthly dose, particularly in multibacillary regimens.

Treatment duration lasts six months for paucibacillary cases and typically twelve to twenty-four months for multibacillary cases. Patients must understand that while MDT kills the bacteria and halts transmission, nerve damage that has already occurred may not fully reverse.

Nursing Care and Prevention of Disabilities

Nursing care for patients with leprosy centers on protecting anesthetic extremities, monitoring for drug side effects, and supporting self-management. Because patients cannot feel pain, minor trauma easily turns into severe chronic ulcers.

Nurses also monitor for medication side effects, such as the reddish-brown skin discoloration caused by clofazimine or the hemolytic anemia sometimes linked to dapsone. Encouraging consistent daily routines for eye and skin care is vital for maintaining functional independence.

Complications and Reactional States

Leprosy reactions are acute inflammatory episodes that can happen before, during, or even after successful completion of multidrug therapy. These episodes threaten nerve function and require urgent medical intervention to prevent permanent paralysis.

  • Type 1 Reaction (Reversal Reaction): A delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction occurring mostly in borderline leprosy. Existing skin lesions become red and swollen, and nerves swell rapidly, causing sudden pain and loss of function.
  • Type 2 Reaction (Erythema Nodosum Leprosum - ENL): An immune-complex mediated reaction seen in multibacillary patients. It presents with painful, tender subcutaneous red nodules, fever, malaise, and sometimes iridocyclitis or orchitis.

Corticosteroids, such as prednisolone, are the mainstay of treatment to reduce severe inflammation and save nerve function. Nurses must assess motor and sensory function regularly during treatment so that sudden deterioration is caught and treated promptly.

Patient Education, Rehabilitation, and Stigma Reduction

Beyond curing the bacterial infection, comprehensive leprosy care addresses the deep social and psychological burdens associated with the disease. Centuries of misunderstanding have generated intense stigma, often causing patients to hide symptoms, delay treatment, and face social isolation.

Nursing interventions must include compassionate counseling for both the patient and their family. Explain clearly that leprosy is curable with standard multidrug therapy, that treatment renders the person non-infectious very quickly, and that casual contact does not spread the disease. Physical rehabilitation programs, reconstructive surgeries for claw hands or foot drop, and peer support groups help restore functional ability and self-esteem. Empowering patients with self-care knowledge transforms them from dependent victims into active participants in their own healing.
