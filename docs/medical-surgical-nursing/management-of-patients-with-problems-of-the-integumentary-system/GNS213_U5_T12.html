Mantoux Test

Introduction to the Mantoux Test and Tuberculosis

The Mantoux test, widely known as the tuberculin skin test (TST), serves as a primary screening tool for infection with *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. Tuberculosis remains a significant public health burden across Nigeria, making reliable screening essential in both community health outreaches and hospital settings. Identifying latent tuberculosis infection before it progresses to active disease prevents widespread transmission.

The test does not distinguish between latent tuberculosis infection and active disease. Instead, it detects a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction indicating that the immune system has encountered the tuberculosis bacterium.

Principles of the Tuberculin Skin Test

The immunological basis of the TST relies on a delayed-type hypersensitivity (Type IV) reaction. When purified protein derivative (PPD) is injected intradermally, sensitized T-lymphocytes that previously encountered the bacterium migrate to the site of the injection.

These activated T-cells release lymphokines, which induce local vasodilation, edema, and infiltration of other inflammatory cells over 48 to 72 hours. This visible and palpable local immune response creates the characteristic induration measured by healthcare providers.

Equipment and PPD Preparation

Administering a precise Mantoux test requires specific equipment to ensure the antigen is placed exactly within the dermal layer.

  • PPD Antigen: Standardized 5 tuberculin units (TU) of Purified Protein Derivative in 0.1 mL, kept refrigerated between 2°C and 8°C.
  • Syringe: A specialized tuberculin syringe calibrated in tenths and hundredths of a milliliter.
  • Needle: A short, fine-gauge needle, typically 27-gauge with a length of one-quarter to one-half inch.
  • Supplies: Alcohol swabs, non-sterile gloves, a sharp container, and cotton balls.

*Clinical Pitfall*: Never expose PPD to direct sunlight or allow it to freeze, as the protein molecules will denature and yield false-negative outcomes.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administration

  1. Site Selection: Choose the inner surface of the forearm, free of scars, tattoos, or prominent veins, approximately 5 to 10 centimeters below the elbow.
  2. Skin Preparation: Cleanse the chosen area with an alcohol swab using a circular motion and allow it to dry completely.
  3. Needle Orientation: Hold the tuberculin syringe with the bevel facing upward, nearly parallel to the skin surface.
  4. Injection: Insert the needle slowly into the epidermis until the bevel is completely covered, then inject the 0.1 mL dose smoothly.
  5. Blister Formation: Verify the immediate formation of a pale, raised, taut bleb or wheal measuring 6 to 10 millimeters in diameter.
  6. Safety Disposal: Engage safety devices immediately and discard the syringe into a rigid sharp box without recapping.

Timing and Technique for Result Reading

Reading a Mantoux test requires precise timing and specialized tactile assessment skills. The reading must be performed between 48 and 72 hours after injection when the delayed hypersensitivity reaction peaks. If a patient fails to return within this 72-hour window, the test must be rescheduled because the reaction diminishes over time.

When evaluating the site, do not measure the area of visible redness or erythema. Instead, use your fingertips to gently palpate the skin to find the edges of the firm, hard core beneath the surface. Measure only the transverse diameter of this induration across the forearm using a flexible millimeter ruler. Document the exact measurement in millimeters, noting zero millimeters if no induration is felt.

Interpretation Criteria and Cut-off Readings

The interpretation of induration size depends entirely on the patient's individual risk factors, immune status, and recent exposures. A single cut-off measurement does not apply to everyone.

Induration SizeRisk Group & Criteria
≥ 5 mmHIV-positive individuals, recent close contacts of active TB cases, organ transplant recipients, and patients with chest radiograph findings consistent with old healed TB.
≥ 10 mmRecent arrivals from high-prevalence countries, injection drug users, residents and employees of high-risk congregate settings, children under four years of age, and individuals with chronic medical conditions like diabetes or renal failure.
≥ 15 mmIndividuals with no known risk factors for tuberculosis or those belonging to populations with low baseline infection rates.

False-Positive and False-Negative Results

Diagnostic accuracy can be compromised by various biological and technical factors, leading to misinterpreted data.

  • False-Positive Causes: Previous vaccination with Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) is a common cause of cross-reactivity, especially in nations like Nigeria where BCG immunization is routine in infancy. Infection with non-tuberculous mycobacteria can also trigger reactivity.
  • False-Negative Causes: Severe immunosuppression from advanced HIV, overwhelming active tuberculosis, malnutrition, recent viral infections, or improper storage of the PPD antigen can impair the immune response, yielding a falsely clear test despite infection.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Nurses play a vital role across the entire screening continuum, ensuring accuracy, safety, and patient adherence. Before administration, the nurse must assess previous skin test reactions to prevent severe local hypersensitivity reactions, known as blistering or necrosis, which can occur if re-tested unnecessarily.

Education must emphasize the absolute necessity of returning within the 48-to-72-hour window. Patients should be instructed to keep the injection site clean and dry, avoiding scratching, scrubbing, or applying bandages, creams, or ointments over the bleb. Clear documentation of the lot number, expiration date, exact administration site, and date ensures continuity of care.
