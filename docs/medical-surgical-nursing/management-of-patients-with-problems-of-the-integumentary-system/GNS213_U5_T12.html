Mantoux Test

Introduction to Tuberculosis and the Mantoux Test

Tuberculosis (TB) is a chronic, infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It primarily affects the lungs, though it can attack other parts of the body such as the kidneys, spine, and brain. Transmission occurs through airborne droplets when an infected person coughs, sneezes, or speaks.

The Mantoux test, also known as the tuberculin skin test (TST), is a standard screening tool used to determine if a person has been infected with the tuberculosis bacterium. It does not differentiate between a latent TB infection (LTBI) and active TB disease; rather, it measures the immune system's delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction to a specific bacterial protein extract.

Principles of the Tuberculin Skin Test

The underlying mechanism of the Mantoux test relies on cell-mediated immunity. When Purified Protein Derivative (PPD) is injected intradermally, memory T-lymphocytes that were previously sensitized to tubercle proteins migrate to the site of the injection if an infection is present.

This cellular recruitment causes a localized inflammatory response, clinically visible and palpable as an induration (a hard, raised area). This is a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction, meaning the peak immune response takes 48 to 72 hours to develop fully. Nurses must understand that this is not an immediate antibody response, which explains why the patient must return within a specific window for reading.

Equipment and PPD Preparation

Performing a proper Mantoux test requires specific equipment to ensure the injection is delivered into the correct skin layer.

  • Tuberculin syringe: A calibrated 1-mL syringe specifically designed for intradermal dosing.
  • Needle: A short, fine needle (usually 27-gauge, 1/4 to 1/2 inch in length).
  • PPD solution: Contains 5 tuberculin units (TU) per 0.1 mL of standard antigen.
  • Antiseptic swab: Alcohol swab for cleaning the injection site.
  • Sharps container: For safe disposal of needles.

*Clinical observation:* Nurses should always check the expiration date on the PPD vial and ensure it has been refrigerated properly, as heat or age degrades the protein potency.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Administration

Administering the Mantoux test correctly demands precision. Choose a clean, hairless site on the inner surface of the forearm, roughly 2 to 4 inches below the elbow. Clean the skin with an alcohol swab and allow it to dry completely.

  1. Hold the patient's forearm and stretch the skin taut with your free hand.
  2. Position the needle almost parallel to the skin surface, with the bevel facing upward.
  3. Insert the needle slowly about 1/8 of an inch into the epidermis, until the bevel is just covered.
  4. Inject the 0.1 mL of PPD slowly and steadily.
  5. Withdraw the needle promptly and apply gentle pressure with cotton, but do not massage the site.

A successful injection produces a distinct pale elevation of the skin, known as a wheal or bleb, measuring 6 to 10 millimeters in diameter.

Timing and Technique for Result Reading

Reading a Mantoux test requires both visual inspection and tactile palpation. The reading must take place between 48 and 72 hours after administration. If a patient fails to return within this window, the test is considered invalid, and a repeat test must be scheduled.

To measure the reaction correctly, run your fingertips lightly across the forearm to find the edges of the induration. Induration is the hard, dense swelling under the skin, distinct from simple redness (erythema). Measure the diameter of the induration *transverse* to the long axis of the arm using a flexible millimeter ruler. Never measure the red, flat area of inflammation.

Interpretation Criteria and Cut-off Readings

Interpreting a Mantoux test is not based on a single universal number. The size of the induration required to call a test "positive" depends heavily on the patient's risk factors for tuberculosis.

Induration SizeRisk Group and Clinical Criteria
>= 5 mmHIV-positive, recent close contacts of active TB cases, persons with fibrotic changes on chest radiograph, organ transplant recipients.
>= 10 mmRecent immigrants from high-prevalence countries, injection drug users, residents/employees of high-risk congregate settings, children under 4 years old.
>= 15 mmPersons with no known risk factors for tuberculosis.

Nurses must carefully review patient history to apply the correct millimeter threshold before determining the clinical implication.

False-Positive and False-Negative Results

No diagnostic screening test is perfect, and the Mantoux test can yield misleading results if immune function or administration technique is compromised.

  • False-Negative Results: Occur when an infected person fails to react. Common causes include severe viral infections, recent live-virus vaccinations (e.g., MMR, varicella), overwhelming TB disease, malnutrition, and immunosuppression (such as corticosteroids or HIV).
  • False-Positive Results: Occur when a person without TB infection shows a reaction. This is frequently caused by prior vaccination with the Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccine, or infection with non-tuberculous mycobacteria.

*Clinical misconception:* A common myth is that a history of the BCG vaccine means a person can never be tested; however, many years after vaccination, BCG-induced reactions wane, and a large induration often points to a true TB infection.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Education

Nurses play a vital role throughout the Mantoux testing process, from assessment to follow-up care. Before administering the test, the nurse must assess the patient for previous severe reactions to PPD, current immunosuppressive therapy, and recent vaccinations.

Patient education is crucial for compliance. The nurse must instruct the patient to return strictly within 48 to 72 hours for the reading. Patients should be taught to keep the injection site clean and dry, avoid scratching or applying tight clothing, and never cover the bleb with adhesive bandages. If the test is positive, the nurse prepares the patient for next steps, which typically include a chest radiograph and a clinical evaluation for active disease.
