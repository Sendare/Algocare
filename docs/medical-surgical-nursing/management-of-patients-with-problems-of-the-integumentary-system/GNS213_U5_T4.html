Burns

Pathophysiology and Classification of Burns

A burn is a traumatic injury to the skin and underlying tissues caused by thermal, electrical, chemical, or radiation energy. The severity of the injury depends on the temperature of the burning agent, duration of contact, and the anatomical site involved. Pathophysiologically, a major burn is divided into three distinct zones based on Jackson's thermal wound theory: - **Zone of coagulation**: The area of irreversible tissue destruction and cell death located at the core of the wound. - **Zone of stasis**: The surrounding area characterized by compromised microcirculation and potentially salvageable tissue, highly vulnerable to hypoperfusion and infection. - **Zone of hyperemia**: The outermost area showing minimal cell injury and increased blood flow, which typically recovers spontaneously. Burns are further classified by depth: **superficial (first-degree)** damage only the epidermis, presenting as painful, red, unblistering skin; **partial-thickness (second-degree)** involve the epidermis and varying depths of the dermis, characterized by extreme pain, blisters, and weeping; and **full-thickness (third- and fourth-degree)** destroy the entire dermis and epidermis, often extending to subcutaneous fat, muscle, or bone, resulting in a painless, leathery, white or charred appearance.

Estimation of Burn Extent and Systemic Response

Calculating the total body surface area (TBSA) affected is critical for guiding resuscitation and determining prognosis. For adult patients, the **Rule of Nines** is the standard clinical tool, dividing the body into multiples of nine: head and neck account for 9%, each upper extremity 9%, the anterior trunk 18%, the posterior trunk 18%, each lower extremity 18%, and the perineum 1%. For irregular or scattered burns, the patient's palm, excluding fingers, represents approximately 1% of their TBSA. When TBSA exceeds 20%, local inflammatory responses escalate into a massive systemic reaction. Inflammatory mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins, and cytokines are released into the systemic circulation, causing profound capillary permeability. Plasma shifts from the intravascular space into the interstitium, resulting in severe generalized edema, hypovolemia, and **burn shock**, a form of distributive and hypovolemic shock. Concurrently, hematologic shifts cause hemoconcentration, elevated hematocrit, and acute hyperkalemia due to massive cellular destruction.

Initial Emergency Management and Resuscitation

Immediate management of a burn victim prioritizes stopping the burning process and securing physiological stability. Rescuers must remove the patient from the source of injury, extinguish flames, and cool thermal burns using cool, running water—avoiding ice, which causes vasoconstriction and deepens tissue damage. Chemical burns require immediate, prolonged irrigation with copious amounts of water. The primary survey follows standard trauma protocols focusing on **Airway, Breathing, and Circulation (ABC)**: - **Airway**: Inhalation injury must be suspected if burns occurred in an enclosed space, facial burns are present, or soot is found in the oropharynx. Early endotracheal intubation is vital before upper airway edema causes complete obstruction. - **Circulation**: Large-bore peripheral intravenous lines must be established, ideally through unburned skin, to initiate aggressive fluid resuscitation. Fluid replacement formulas, most notably the **Parkland formula**, dictate resuscitation volumes. The formula calculates 4 mL of lactated Ringer's solution per kilogram of body weight per percentage of TBSA burned. Half of this calculated volume is administered within the first eight hours post-injury, and the remaining half is delivered over the subsequent sixteen hours.

Hemodynamic Monitoring and Complications

Monitoring the physiological response to fluid resuscitation prevents both under-resuscitation, which leads to organ ischemia, and over-resuscitation, which causes pulmonary edema and compartment syndromes. The most reliable indicator of adequate end-organ perfusion is **urine output**, which is monitored via an indwelling urinary catheter connected to a urometer. Target urine output parameters: - Adults: 0.5 to 1.0 mL/kg/hour - Pediatric patients: 1.0 to 1.5 mL/kg/hour Other vital parameters include mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate, and mental status. In circumferential burns of the extremities or torso, tissue edema within unyielding scars or tight skin can restrict blood flow or ventilation. This requires prompt bedside **escharotomy**, a surgical incision through the rigid burn eschar to relieve pressure and restore distal perfusion or chest wall expansion.

Wound Management, Debridement, and Grafting

Wound care in burn management aims to prevent infection, promote healing, and prepare the wound bed for closure. Initial care involves gentle cleansing with mild soap and water, followed by debridement of loose, non-viable tissue and ruptured blisters. Topical antimicrobial agents, such as **silver sulfadiazine** or **mafenide acetate**, are routinely applied to reduce bacterial colonization. For deep partial-thickness and full-thickness burns, spontaneous healing is impossible due to the destruction of regenerative epithelial elements. These wounds require surgical excision of necrotic tissue followed by skin grafting: - **Autografts**: The gold standard, utilizing the patient's own healthy skin harvested from a donor site. - **Allografts (homografts)**: Temporary skin grafts obtained from human donors or cadavers. - **Xenografts (heterografts)**: Temporary biological dressings sourced from non-human species, such as pigs. Meshing an autograft allows the graft to expand to cover a larger surface area while permitting wound exudate to drain through the perforations.

Metabolic Support and Nutritional Management

Severe thermal injury triggers an unprecedented hypermetabolic and hypercatabolic state. Catecholamines, cortisol, and glucagon surge, driving up resting energy expenditure to as high as double normal baseline levels. This massive catabolism leads to rapid skeletal muscle wasting, visceral protein depletion, and delayed wound healing. Nutritional support must be initiated aggressively, preferably via the enteral route through a small-bore feeding tube if the patient cannot take adequate oral calories. Early enteral feeding preserves gut mucosal integrity, prevents bacterial translocation from the gastrointestinal tract, and mitigates the hypermetabolic response. Diets must be high in protein to support tissue synthesis and high in non-protein calories to meet energy demands.

Rehabilitation and Long-Term Nursing Care

Rehabilitation begins on the first day of admission and extends long after wound closure. The primary long-term challenge is the development of **hypertrophic scars** and **contractures**, which form as collagen matures and tightens across joints. Nursing interventions and physical therapy focus on maintaining functional range of motion through aggressive splinting, positioning, and early ambulation. Patients are fitted with custom-made **pressure garments** worn twenty-three hours a day for up to a year or more. Continuous, uniform pressure flattens collagen bundles and reduces scar maturation. Psychological support is equally essential, as survivors frequently face chronic pain, severe body image alterations, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder.
