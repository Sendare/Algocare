Burns

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Burns

A burn is a traumatic injury to the skin and underlying tissues caused by thermal, electrical, chemical, or radiation sources. When skin is exposed to extreme heat or damaging agents, cellular proteins denature, leading to cell death and tissue destruction.

Pathophysiologically, a major burn is divided into three concentric zones as described by Jackson's Burn Wound Theory:

  • Zone of Coagulation: The central area of maximum irreversible tissue destruction where cells are completely necrotic.
  • Zone of Stasis: The surrounding area characterized by decreased tissue perfusion and microvascular damage; these cells are salvageable if adequate resuscitation and blood flow are promptly restored.
  • Zone of Hyperemia: The outermost zone where minimal cell injury occurs, and tissue recovery is spontaneous through local inflammatory vasodilation.

Clinically, nurses must understand that the zone of stasis is vulnerable to secondary ischemia. Inadequate fluid resuscitation or infection can easily convert a salvageable zone of stasis into permanent necrosis, deepening the total burn wound.

Classification by Depth and Total Body Surface Area

Burn depth determines the regenerative potential of the skin, while total body surface area (TBSA) guides systemic fluid resuscitation. Burns are historically classified by degrees, though modern practice prefers descriptive terminology.

  • Superficial (First-Degree): Involves only the epidermis. Skin is red, painful, and blanches with pressure, healing within a week without scarring (e.g., mild sunburn).
  • Partial-Thickness (Second-Degree): Extends into the dermis. Divided into superficial partial-thickness (painful, weeping blisters, pink/red) and deep partial-thickness (mottled red/white, less painful due to nerve ending damage). Superficial heals in 2 weeks; deep may require grafting.
  • Full-Thickness (Third- and Fourth-Degree): Destroys the epidermis, dermis, and extends into subcutaneous fat, muscle, or bone. Skin appears leathery, dry, charred, or pearly white with no sensation to pinprick. Requires surgical excision and grafting.

To estimate TBSA in adults, the Rule of Nines is widely used:

  • Head and neck: 9%
  • Each upper limb: 9%
  • Anterior trunk: 18%
  • Posterior trunk: 18%
  • Each lower limb: 18%
  • Perineum: 1%

Systemic Responses and Burn Shock

Major burns exceeding 20% TBSA trigger a massive systemic inflammatory response that affects every organ system. Capillary permeability increases dramatically across the entire body, not just at the burn site.

Fluid shifts rapidly from the intravascular space into the interstitial space within the first 12 to 24 hours. This massive fluid loss results in burn shock, a specific form of hypovolemic shock characterized by:

  • Severe hypotension
  • Tachycardia
  • Decreased cardiac output
  • Hemoconcentration and high hematocrit
  • Oliguria

Simultaneously, the respiratory system reacts to smoke inhalation or systemic mediators with bronchospasm and pulmonary edema. The hypermetabolic state sets in shortly after, driving up oxygen consumption and core body temperature as the body enters a profound catabolic phase.

Initial Assessment and Emergency Management

Emergency management of a burn victim begins with the standard Primary Survey (ABCDE), adapted for thermal trauma:

  1. Airway: Assess for inhalation injury (facial burns, singed nasal hairs, carbonaceous sputum, hoarseness). Early endotracheal intubation is critical if airway edema is suspected, because swelling progresses rapidly.
  2. Breathing: Check chest wall excursion. Circumferential full-thickness chest burns restrict expansion, requiring emergency escharotomies.
  3. Circulation: Evaluate pulses and remove all rings, watches, and tight clothing before edema develops. Establish large-bore peripheral intravenous lines.
  4. Disability: Assess neurological status using the AVPU scale or Glasgow Coma Scale.
  5. Exposure: Stop the burning process by removing the heat source and cool the burn with cool water—never ice, which causes vasoconstriction and deepens tissue injury. Cover the wound with a clean, dry sheet to prevent hypothermia.

Fluid Resuscitation and Hemodynamic Monitoring

Fluid resuscitation is the cornerstone of survival during the first 24 hours post-burn. The most widely utilized formula in clinical practice is the Parkland Formula:

$$\text{Total Fluid (mL)} = 4 \times \text{Weight (kg)} \times \text{TBSA}(\%)$$

  • Administer Lactated Ringer's solution.
  • Give the first half of the calculated volume over the first 8 hours from the time of injury, not from the time of hospital arrival.
  • Give the remaining half over the subsequent 16 hours.

The gold standard for monitoring the adequacy of resuscitation is urine output, measured via an indwelling urinary catheter:

  • Target urine output for adults is 0.5 to 1.0 mL/kg/h.
  • Target urine output for electrical burn patients with rhabdomyolysis is 1.0 to 1.5 mL/kg/h to clear myoglobin and prevent acute kidney injury.

Wound Care, Debridement, and Grafting

Burn wound management aims to prevent infection, promote rapid healing, and restore skin integrity.

  • Cleansing and Debridement: Wounds are gently washed with mild soap and water. Loose, necrotic epidermis and ruptured blisters are debrided to remove bacterial breeding grounds.
  • Topical Antimicrobials: Agents like silver sulfadiazine, mafenide acetate, or bacitracin are applied to reduce bacterial colonization. Silver-impregnated dressings are also common.
  • Surgical Excision and Grafting: Deep partial-thickness and full-thickness burns require early surgical excision of dead tissue (eschar) followed by skin grafting.
  • Autograft: Permanent skin graft taken from the patient's own unburned donor site (the gold standard).
  • Allograft (Homograft): Temporary graft from human cadavers.
  • Xenograft (Heterograft): Temporary graft from animal sources, usually pigs.

Nurses must monitor grafts closely for signs of failure, such as hematoma or seroma accumulation underneath the graft, shear movement, or localized infection.

Nutritional Support and Metabolic Management

Severe burns induce the most hypermetabolic and hypercatabolic state known in clinical medicine. Resting energy expenditure can increase by up to 100% above normal baseline levels.

If nutritional demands are not met immediately, patients experience rapid muscle wasting, delayed wound healing, impaired immune function, and organ failure.

  • Enteral Nutrition: Early enteral feeding via a small-bore nasoenteric tube should be initiated as soon as bowel sounds return, typically within hours of admission. Enteral nutrition preserves gut mucosal integrity and prevents bacterial translocation.
  • Caloric and Protein Goals: Formulas must be high in protein (for tissue repair) and high in total calories, calculated using predictive equations like the Curreri formula or indirect calorimetry.
  • Adjunctive Therapies: Medications such as propranolol (a beta-blocker) or oxandrolone (an anabolic steroid) are frequently used to blunt the hypermetabolic response and promote lean muscle mass retention.

Complications of Major Burn Injuries

Major burn injuries present numerous systemic and local complications that require vigilant nursing oversight throughout the hospital stay.

  • Infection and Sepsis: Burn wounds are sterile upon creation but quickly colonized by skin flora and opportunistic pathogens like *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *Staphylococcus aureus*. Sepsis remains the leading cause of death in burn units.
  • Curling's Ulcer: Acute gastric or duodenal erosion caused by reduced gastrointestinal blood flow during hypovolemic shock. Prevented with proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers.
  • Compartment Syndrome: Occurs when unyielding, circumferential eschar acts like a tourniquet as sub-fascial edema accumulates, compromising distal blood flow. Managed with urgent escharotomy or fasciotomy.
  • Contractures: Permanent shortening of scar tissue crossing joints, leading to severe mobility limitation if physical therapy and splinting are neglected.

Rehabilitation and Long-Term Nursing Care

Rehabilitation begins on the day of admission and extends long after the patient leaves the hospital. The primary goals are to minimize permanent physical deformities, restore functional independence, and provide psychological support.

  • Positioning and Splinting: Joints must be positioned in functional, extended alignments (anti-deformity positions) to counteract the natural tendency of healing skin to flex toward the wound.
  • Pressure Therapy: Custom-fitted elastic pressure garments are worn for 23 hours a day for up to 12 to 18 months to flatten hypertrophic scars and minimize keloid formation.
  • Psychological Support: Burn survivors face high rates of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), depression, and body image distress. Nurses facilitate holistic recovery by connecting patients with support groups and mental health professionals.
