Pathophysiology of Skin Disorders

Introduction to Skin Structure and Barrier Function

The integumentary system serves as the primary physical boundary separating internal homeostasis from external environmental hazards. Structurally, the skin is divided into three distinct layers: the epidermis, the dermis, and the subcutaneous tissue. - **Epidermis**: The outermost cellular layer, predominantly composed of **keratinocytes**, which undergo continuous differentiation from the basal layer upward. It also houses melanocytes for photoprotection and Langerhans cells for immune surveillance. - **Dermis**: A fibrous, vascularized connective tissue matrix rich in **collagen** and **elastin**, providing tensile strength, elasticity, and nutritional support to the avascular epidermis via the papillary loops. - **Subcutaneous Tissue (Hypodermis)**: A layer of adipose tissue and loose connective tissue that insulates the body, stores energy, and cushions underlying mechanical structures. The epidermal barrier, specifically the stratum corneum, relies on a "brick and mortar" model where corneocytes act as bricks embedded in a lipid-rich extracellular matrix composed of ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids. Disruption of this lipid matrix compromises the water-retaining capacity of the skin, leading to increased transepidermal water loss, allergen penetration, and clinical presentations of xerosis or inflammatory flare-ups.

Cellular Mechanisms and Inflammatory Responses in the Skin

When cutaneous tissue experiences injury, infection, or allergen exposure, resident cells initiate a coordinated immune and inflammatory cascade. Keratinocytes are no longer viewed as passive physical barriers; they actively secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL-1) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-alpha) when disturbed. Dermal mast cells degranulate, releasing preformed **histamine**, tryptase, and vasoactive amines that rapidly dilate local microvasculature, causing erythema and edema. Concurrently, endothelial cells upregulate adhesion molecules, capturing circulating neutrophils and lymphocytes, leading to cellular infiltration of the tissue. Chronic stimulation of these pathways shifts the cellular profile from acute neutrophilic infiltration to chronic lymphohistiocytic dominance, underlying the persistent plaque formation seen in chronic inflammatory dermatoses.

Pathophysiology of Primary Skin Lesions and Eruptions

Gross morphological changes on the skin reflect underlying microscopic processes. Primary lesions develop directly from a pathologic process rather than evolving from trauma to secondary lesions. - **Macule and Patch**: Localized changes in skin color without elevation or depression. A macule is small (under 1 cm), while a patch is larger, often driven by pigment deposition or localized vascular dilation. - **Papule and Plaque**: Solid, elevated lesions. Papules are superficial and small (under 1 cm), whereas plaques are broader, plateau-like elevations typically formed by the coalescence of multiple papules or deep epidermal thickening (acanthosis). - **Vesicle and Bulla**: Fluid-filled cavities within or beneath the epidermis. Vesicles are small blister-like formations containing serous fluid, while bullae exceed 1 cm, often caused by autoimmune cleavage at the dermo-epidermal junction (e.g., pemphigus or bullous pemphigoid). - **Pustule**: A circumscribed, elevated cavity containing purulent exudate made up of degenerated neutrophils and inflammatory debris.

Mechanisms of Pruritus and Pain in Dermatological Conditions

Itching, or **pruritus**, is a distinct sensory sensation mediated by unmyelinated type C nerve fibers located at the dermo-epidermal junction. Unlike pain, which is transmitted by both myelinated and unmyelinated fibers, pruritus involves specialized pruritoreceptors that respond to chemical mediators such as **histamine**, serotonin, substance P, and notably, **interleukin-31 (IL-31)**. When these chemical mediators bind to receptors on sensory nerve endings, an action potential travels via the spinothalamic tract to the cerebral cortex. Scratching provides temporary relief by activating thicker mechanoreceptive A-beta fibers, which transiently inhibit the transmission of the itch signal through spinal cord gating mechanisms. However, chronic scratching damages the epidermal barrier, releasing more cytokines and establishing an intense itch-scratch cycle that exacerbates inflammation and leads to lichenification.

Pathophysiology of Infectious and Parasitic Skin Disorders

The skin hosts a diverse resident microbiome, but disruption of barrier integrity or host immunosuppression permits pathogenic invasion. Bacterial infections, most commonly caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus pyogenes*, involve toxin-mediated damage or direct enzymatic destruction of tissue planes, resulting in impetigo, folliculitis, or deeper necrotizing fasciitis. Fungal infections, or **dermatophytoses**, are caused by keratinophilic fungi that secrete keratinases to digest the stratum corneum, hair, and nails, spreading outward in annular patterns. Parasitic infestations introduce distinct pathophysiological changes; for instance, *Sarcoptes scabiei* mites burrow directly into the stratum corneum. The host's delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction to mite feces and saliva drives the intense nocturnal pruritus and generalized papular eruption characteristic of scabies.

Pathophysiology of Immune-Mediated and Inflammatory Skin Diseases

Many chronic dermatologic conditions stem from aberrant immune activation. In **psoriasis**, a T-cell-mediated autoimmune disorder, CD4+ and CD8+ T cells infiltrate the dermis and release cytokines such as **interleukin-17 (IL-17)** and interferon-gamma. This signaling cascade dramatically accelerates keratinocyte proliferation, shortening the epidermal turnover time from weeks to just a few days. Consequently, cells fail to shed normally, forming the thick, silvery-white scaly plaques characteristic of the condition. In contrast, **atopic dermatitis** is driven by T-helper type 2 (Th2) immune responses, featuring elevated levels of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13. This Th2 polarization impairs the expression of structural barrier proteins like **filaggrin**, leaving the skin intrinsically porous and dry, which allows environmental allergens to penetrate easily and fuel chronic inflammation.

Wound Healing Processes and Pathological Scarring

Cutaneous wound healing proceeds through four overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. | Phase | Primary Cellular and Biochemical Events | Clinical Objective | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Hemostasis** | Platelet aggregation, fibrin clot formation, release of growth factors | Stop active bleeding and create a provisional matrix | | **Inflammation** | Neutrophil and macrophage influx, phagocytosis of debris | Clear pathogens and dead tissue | | **Proliferation** | Fibroblast migration, angiogenesis, collagen deposition, re-epithelialization | Fill the tissue defect and restore the barrier | | **Remodeling** | Replacement of type III collagen with type I collagen, matrix cross-linking | Increase tensile strength of the scar | When the delicate balance of extracellular matrix synthesis and degradation goes awry during the remodeling phase, pathological scarring results. **Keloids** extend beyond the original borders of the wound and rarely regress spontaneously due to prolonged fibroblast activity and excessive type I and type III collagen production. **Hypertrophic scars**, conversely, remain confined to the original wound boundaries and often improve over time as abnormal tension resolves.
