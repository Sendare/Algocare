Pathophysiology of Skin Disorders

Introduction to Skin Structure and Barrier Function

The skin serves as the primary protective interface between the internal organs and the external environment. Structurally, it consists of three main layers: the outer epidermis, the underlying dermis, and the deep hypodermis or subcutaneous tissue. The epidermis is largely composed of keratinocytes, which continuously migrate upward from the basal layer, flattening and dying to form the tough, protective stratum corneum.

Interspersed among these keratinocytes are melanocytes providing pigment, Langerhans cells acting as immune sentinels, and Merkel cells responsible for light touch sensation. Beneath this lies the dermis, a fibrous network of collagen and elastin fibers embedded in a gel-like ground substance that houses blood vessels, nerve endings, hair follicles, and sweat glands.

The barrier function relies heavily on the stratum corneum, often described as a 'brick and mortar' arrangement where corneocytes act as bricks and intercellular lipids form the mortar. When this lipid matrix is disrupted by friction, harsh chemicals, or inflammatory processes, transepidermal water loss increases, allowing allergens and microbial pathogens to penetrate deeper tissue layers.

Cellular Mechanisms and Inflammatory Responses

When the skin encounters physical trauma, microbial invasion, or chemical irritation, resident immune cells trigger a localized inflammatory cascade. Mast cells residing in the dermis rapidly degranulate, releasing preformed histamine and other vasoactive mediators into the surrounding tissue.

  • Vasodilation: Local blood vessels expand, increasing blood flow to the affected site and causing visible erythema and warmth.
  • Increased Vascular Permeability: Endothelial gaps widen, allowing plasma proteins and fluid to leak into the interstitial space, resulting in local edema or swelling.
  • Leukocyte Recruitment: Circulating neutrophils and later monocytes migrate toward the chemical signal, a process called chemotaxis, to phagocytize debris and pathogens.

This classic sequence accounts for the cardinal signs of inflammation: redness, heat, swelling, and pain. While protective, prolonged or dysregulated cellular responses drive chronic dermatoses.

Pathophysiology of Primary Skin Lesions and Eruptions

Dermatological pathology is largely interpreted through the visual inspection of primary lesions, which represent the initial physical response to underlying disease processes.

  • Macule: A flat, non-palpable change in skin color, such as a freckle, caused by localized pigment alterations.
  • Papule: A small, solid, elevated bump less than one centimeter across, resulting from cellular infiltration or proliferation in the dermis.
  • Vesicle: A small, fluid-filled blister caused by intraepidermal or subepidermal fluid accumulation, common in viral infections.
  • Plaque: A broad, elevated, plateau-like lesion often formed by the coalescence of multiple papules, typical in psoriasis.
  • Pustule: A visible collection of purulent exudate containing neutrophils and cellular debris within or beneath the epidermis.

The specific depth, cellular content, and distribution of these lesions guide clinical diagnosis and reflect distinct underlying histopathological changes.

Mechanisms of Pruritus and Pain

Pruritus, or severe itching, is a uniquely distressing sensation mediated by specialized unmyelinated C-fiber nerve endings located in the dermal-epidermal junction. These sensory fibers respond to chemical mediators such as histamine, bradykinin, prostaglandins, and various cytokines released by damaged or inflamed cells.

Interestingly, itch and pain share similar neural pathways, but itch is transmitted at a slower velocity along specialized pruriceptors. Scratching provides temporary relief by activating thicker, fast-conducting mechanoreceptors that override the itch signal in the spinal cord through the gate control theory of pain. However, this mechanical trauma further injures the epidermis, releasing more inflammatory cytokines and initiating an aggravating itch-scratch cycle that worsens the underlying dermatosis.

Pathophysiology of Infectious and Parasitic Skin Disorders

Infectious skin disorders arise when microbial pathogens overcome the skin's physical and chemical defenses. Bacterial infections, such as impetigo or cellulitis, typically begin when breaks in the skin barrier allow staphylococci or streptococci to colonize deep tissues, provoking intense neutrophilic infiltration and localized pus formation.

Fungal infections, including dermatophytoses, remain restricted to the keratinized layers of the epidermis, hair, and nails because dermatophytes secrete keratinases that digest structural proteins.

Parasitic infestations involve organisms like *Sarcoptes scabiei*, which burrow directly into the stratum corneum. The female mite deposits eggs and waste products along her tunnel, prompting a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction characterized by severe nocturnal pruritus and erythematous papules.

Mechanisms of Immune-Mediated Skin Diseases

Chronic inflammatory skin conditions often stem from aberrant immune system activation. In psoriasis, T-helper cells (specifically Th1 and Th17) infiltrate the dermis and release pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-17. This chemical signaling dramatically accelerates keratinocyte turnover, reducing the normal maturation cycle from a month down to just a few days. Immature keratinocytes fail to shed properly, accumulating into thick, silvery plaques.

In contrast, atopic dermatitis involves a T-helper 2 immune skewing combined with a primary genetic defect in filaggrin, a structural protein essential for skin barrier integrity. The resulting defective barrier allows environmental allergens to penetrate easily, driving chronic inflammation, severe dryness, and localized immune reactivity.

Wound Healing Processes and Pathological Scarring

Skin repair follows a coordinated sequence of overlapping phases: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. During hemostasis, platelets form a plug and release growth factors. The inflammatory phase clears debris, while the proliferative phase features fibroblasts migrating into the wound bed to synthesize a temporary extracellular matrix rich in type III collagen and new blood vessels.

In the final remodeling phase, this weak matrix is gradually replaced by stronger type I collagen, and excess capillaries regress. However, when this delicate balance falters, pathological scarring occurs. Keloids extend far beyond the original wound boundaries due to prolonged, unchecked fibroblast activity and excessive collagen production. Hypertrophic scars, meanwhile, remain confined to the original wound margins and often regress spontaneously over time.
