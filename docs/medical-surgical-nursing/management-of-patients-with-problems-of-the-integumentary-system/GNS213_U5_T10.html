Nursing Management of Integumentary Disorders

Integumentary Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures

A systematic skin assessment forms the cornerstone of dermatological nursing. Inspection and palpation require adequate lighting and exposure of the entire integumentary system, including mucous membranes, scalp, palms, and soles. The nurse evaluates lesions for symmetry, border irregularity, color variation, diameter, and evolution, commonly referred to as the ABCDE criteria. Secondary lesions such as crusts, scales, scars, and ulcerations must be documented with precise measurements of length, width, and depth. Diagnostic procedures translate visual findings into definitive pathology. Common tests include: - **Skin Scrapings**: Used to diagnose fungal infections or scabies by examining epidermal scales under a microscope. - **Wood's Light Examination**: Utilizes ultraviolet light in a darkened room to identify bacterial or fungal fluorescence, particularly useful for erythrasma or tinea capitis. - **Skin Biopsy**: Involves obtaining a small tissue sample via punch, shave, or excisional methods for histological examination, requiring sterile technique and local anesthesia. - **Patch Testing**: Identifies delayed-type hypersensitivity reactions by applying suspected allergens to the back under occlusion for 48 hours.

Principles of Topical and Systemic Drug Therapy

Administering medications for skin disorders demands careful attention to vehicle formulations, absorption rates, and systemic side effects. Topical treatments range from drying lotions and cooling wet dressings to occlusive ointments that enhance penetration in thick, lichenified plaques. Nurses must apply topical agents with clean gloves or wooden applicators, gently smoothing them in the direction of hair growth to prevent folliculitis. Systemic therapies, including corticosteroids, retinoids, and biologic agents, introduce complex monitoring requirements. Long-term systemic corticosteroids demand vigilance for hyperglycemia, immunosuppression, and adrenal suppression. When caring for patients on potent dermatological drugs, baseline laboratory parameters such as liver function tests or complete blood counts are routinely checked, reflecting the high systemic load of many dermatological medications used in specialized Nigerian teaching hospitals.

Wound Care, Dressings, and Debridement Techniques

Effective wound management relies on maintaining a moist wound bed while preventing infection and maceration of surrounding periwound skin. Modern dressings vary widely based on exudate levels, ranging from highly absorbent hydrofibers and foams to hydrating hydrogels and transparent films. Nurses evaluate the wound bed for granulation tissue, slough, and necrotic eschar. When non-viable tissue impedes healing, debridement becomes necessary. Methods include: - **Autolytic Debridement**: Using moisture-retentive dressings to let the body's own enzymes break down necrotic tissue. - **Enzymatic Debridement**: Applying topical chemical agents that selectively digest collagen in necrotic tissue. - **Mechanical Debridement**: Physical removal of debris using wet-to-dry dressings or wound irrigation, though this can inadvertently damage healthy new tissue. - **Sharp Debridement**: Using sterile scissors and scalpels to excise dead tissue quickly, typically performed by physicians or advanced practice nurses.

Nursing Management of Inflammatory and Infectious Skin Conditions

Inflammatory dermatoses such as atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, and contact dermatitis present with intense pruritus, erythema, and barrier disruption. Nursing interventions focus on breaking the itch-scratch cycle by administering antihistamines, applying emollients immediately after bathing to lock in moisture, and teaching patients about identified triggers. Infectious skin disorders, whether bacterial like impetigo and cellulitis, fungal like candidiasis, or viral like herpes zoster, require strict adherence to infection control practices. Standard precautions, proper hand hygiene, and safe disposal of contaminated dressings prevent nosocomial spread. For patients with systemic symptoms like fever or malaise, monitoring vital signs and watching for signs of sepsis remain crucial clinical responsibilities.

Care and Resuscitation in Burns and Severe Trauma

Burn injuries disrupt fluid homeostasis, thermoregulation, and barrier protection. The immediate priority in the emergency phase is stopping the burning process, securing a patent airway, and initiating aggressive fluid resuscitation using formulas such as the Parkland formula to prevent hypovolemic shock. Nurses closely monitor urine output via an indwelling urinary catheter, targeting 0.5 to 1.0 mL/kg/hour as the primary indicator of adequate renal perfusion. As the patient transitions to the acute and rehabilitative phases, priorities shift toward preventing infection, managing severe pain, and maintaining joint mobility. Escharotomies or fasciotomies may be performed for circumferential burns of the extremities or chest to relieve compartment syndrome caused by unyielding, burned tissue. Nutritional support featuring high protein and caloric intake is essential to meet the massive hypermetabolic demands of severe burn healing.

Prevention and Management of Pressure Injuries

Pressure injuries develop when sustained mechanical pressure, friction, or shear forces compromise tissue perfusion, most frequently over bony prominences such as the sacrum, heels, and ischial tuberosities. Comprehensive prevention protocols utilize validated risk assessment tools like the Braden scale upon admission and regularly thereafter. Prevention strategies hinge on frequent repositioning every two hours, utilizing specialized support surfaces, keeping skin clean and dry, and maintaining optimal nutritional status with adequate protein and hydration. When managing established pressure injuries, staging guides clinical interventions from Stage 1 through unstageable or deep tissue injuries. Wounds must be cleansed with gentle, non-cytotoxic solutions, necrotic tissue debrided, and appropriate dressings chosen based on moisture control. Continuous interprofessional collaboration involving dietitians, physical therapists, and wound care specialists is vital for healing complex wounds.

Patient Education, Psychosocial Support, and Home Care Integration

Chronic integumentary conditions impose significant psychological and social burdens. Disfiguring lesions, chronic pain, and persistent itching frequently lead to social withdrawal, anxiety, and depression. Nurses provide empathetic listening, encourage participation in support groups, and address body image concerns directly. Empowering patients through detailed education regarding self-care routines, medication administration, and recognizing early signs of infection fosters adherence and independence. Preparing for discharge requires bridging hospital care with the home environment. Nurses assess home resources, coordinate durable medical equipment such as specialized mattresses or wound care supplies, and involve family caregivers in training sessions before discharge. Clear written instructions regarding warning signs—such as expanding redness, foul odor, or fever—ensure prompt reporting and prevent costly hospital readmissions.
