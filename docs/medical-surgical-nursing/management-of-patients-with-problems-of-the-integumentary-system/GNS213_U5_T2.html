Assessment of Clients with Problems of the Skin Hair and Nails

Introduction to Integumentary Assessment in Nursing

The integumentary system, consisting of the skin, hair, and nails, serves as the body's first line of defense against external pathogens, regulates body temperature, facilitates sensory perception, and synthesizes vitamin D. For nursing students in Nigeria, conducting a thorough integumentary assessment is a foundational skill vital for detecting local skin diseases as well as systemic illnesses such as severe anemia, jaundice, cyanosis, and nutritional deficiencies. The skin is the largest organ in the human body, and changes in its color, texture, moisture, and integrity often mirror internal pathological processes. Assessment requires a systematic approach, combining effective communication, observation, and palpation. Furthermore, cultural competence is crucial during assessment; student nurses must understand how to accurately evaluate skin color variations across diverse African skin tones, looking beyond simple erythema to notice subtle changes like duskiness, pallor in the conjunctiva or nail beds, or localized hyperpigmentation. Recognizing normal variants versus abnormal findings ensures timely nursing interventions, prevents complications, and promotes optimal patient outcomes across clinical settings in Nigeria.

Health History

Gathering a comprehensive health history is the critical first step in integumentary assessment. For nursing practice in Nigeria, the history must explore both presenting complaints and environmental or occupational exposures. The nurse should inquire about the onset, duration, location, and character of any rash, pruritus (itching), pain, or changes in hair and nail texture. It is essential to ask about a personal or family history of skin disorders such as eczema, psoriasis, allergies, or skin cancers. Environmental history is equally important; nurses must ask about prolonged sun exposure, use of bleaching creams or harsh chemical cosmetics (which are prevalent and can cause severe dermatological damage), occupational hazards like handling chemicals or agricultural agents, and living conditions that might predispose the client to fungal or bacterial infections. Additionally, the nurse should review the client's current medications, travel history, and nutritional status. Documenting a detailed history guides the subsequent physical examination, helps identify causative agents, and ensures individualized, culturally sensitive nursing care plans.

Preparation and Equipment for Physical Examination

Proper preparation and equipment are essential for a reliable and comfortable physical examination of the skin, hair, and nails. The examination room should be warm, quiet, and well-lit, preferably utilizing natural daylight to ensure accurate evaluation of skin color and subtle lesions. Privacy must be maintained at all times through the use of drapes or screens, respecting the client's dignity. The nurse should explain each step of the procedure to alleviate anxiety and obtain informed cooperation. Essential equipment for a comprehensive integumentary assessment includes a good light source (penlight), a ruler or tape measure to measure lesion dimensions, clean gloves (which must be worn if there is any risk of contact with non-intact skin, drainage, or infectious lesions), a magnifying glass for inspecting fine details, and a Wood's lamp if fungal or bacterial infections are suspected. Palpation requires clean, warm hands and short fingernails to prevent trauma to the client's skin. Ensuring proper infection control practices, such as hand hygiene before and after the examination, protects both the nurse and the patient from cross-contamination.

Physical Assessment

The physical assessment of the integumentary system relies primarily on inspection and palpation, moving systematically from head to toe. The nurse inspects overall skin color, noting any pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, erythema, or pigmentation irregularities. Moisture and texture are evaluated through palpation; normal skin should feel dry to touch with appropriate oiliness, while abnormal findings might include excessive diaphoresis or extreme dryness and scaling. Skin turgor is assessed by gently pinching a fold of skin (usually over the clavicle or forearm) to evaluate hydration status and elasticity; prolonged tenting indicates dehydration. Temperature is checked using the dorsal surface of the hands, comparing symmetrical body parts for warmth or coolness. Hair is inspected and palpated for distribution, quantity, texture, and presence of infestations like lice or nits. Nails are examined for color, shape, angle of attachment (assessing for clubbing), and capillary refill time, which should normally be under two seconds. Systematically examining these components allows nursing students to detect early signs of localized trauma, systemic disease, or fluid and electrolyte imbalances.

Assessing Primary and Secondary Skin Lesions

Accurate identification and description of skin lesions are core competencies in nursing. Lesions are broadly categorized into primary and secondary types. Primary lesions develop on previously unaltered skin and include macules (flat, non-palpable, colored spots like freckles), papules (elevated, solid palpable masses like warts), patches, plaques, vesicles (fluid-filled blisters like in chickenpox), bullae, wheals (hives), and pustules (pus-filled lesions like acne). Secondary lesions result from changes over time to primary lesions or external trauma, such as crusts, scales, fissures (linear cracks in the epidermis), erosions, ulcers, scars, and lichenification (thickening of the skin due to chronic scratching). When documenting lesions, the nurse must note their exact location, distribution (localized or generalized), color, size (using a ruler), shape, elevation, and pattern (e.g., linear, clustered, annular). Differentiating between primary and secondary lesions helps medical and nursing teams formulate accurate diagnoses and track the progression or healing of dermatological conditions.

Diagnostic Tests and Laboratory Investigations

When physical assessment findings are inconclusive or require further investigation, diagnostic tests and laboratory investigations are ordered. For nursing students, understanding these tests ensures proper patient preparation, specimen collection, and post-procedure care. Common diagnostic procedures include skin scrapings, which are examined under a microscope with potassium hydroxide (KOH) to detect fungal elements like ringworm or candidiasis. Bacterial cultures and sensitivity tests are performed on exudate from infected lesions or ulcers to identify specific pathogens and determine effective antibiotic therapy. Skin biopsies (punch, shave, or excisional) may be performed under local anesthesia to diagnose suspicious pigmented lesions, malignancies, or chronic inflammatory conditions. Additionally, Wood's lamp examination uses ultraviolet light to highlight specific fluorescence in fungal or bacterial infections and pigmentary disorders. Blood tests, such as complete blood counts (CBC), allergy panels, or specific serology, may be ordered when skin manifestations are secondary to systemic diseases. Nurses play a vital role in educating clients, relieving anxiety, assisting the physician during procedures, and ensuring proper labeling and transport of laboratory specimens.

Documentation and Reporting Findings

Accurate documentation and reporting of integumentary findings are legal and professional responsibilities in nursing. Documentation must be objective, concise, clear, and detailed, using precise anatomical locations and standard medical terminology. Instead of vague descriptions like 'skin looks bad,' the nurse should record specific data such as: 'A 2x3 cm erythematous plaque with silvery scales located on the extensor surface of the left elbow.' The nurse must document skin color, temperature, moisture, turgor, integrity, and the exact characteristics of any primary or secondary lesions, hair distribution, and nail bed condition. Any changes in the client's condition must be reported promptly to the attending physician or multidisciplinary team, particularly signs of worsening infection, spreading necrosis, skin breakdown (pressure ulcers), or adverse drug reactions involving the skin (such as Stevens-Johnson syndrome). Good documentation ensures continuity of care, facilitates accurate shift handovers, provides legal protection, and supports clinical audit and research within healthcare facilities across Nigeria.
