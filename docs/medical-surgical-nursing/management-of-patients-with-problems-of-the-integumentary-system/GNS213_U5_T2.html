Assessment of Clients with Problems of the Skin Hair and Nails

Introduction to Integumentary Assessment

The integumentary system serves as a primary protective barrier against environmental pathogens, mechanical trauma, and fluid loss, while also playing vital roles in temperature regulation, vitamin D synthesis, and sensory perception. Because the skin, hair, and nails continuously reflect both localized dermatological conditions and systemic physiological changes, nursing assessment of these structures yields critical baseline data. Conditions such as severe anemia, chronic renal failure, or hepatic dysfunction frequently manifest through visible cutaneous alterations long before other organ systems show overt distress. Clinical examination requires systematic inspection and palpation, integrating anatomical knowledge with careful observation of color, texture, and moisture.

Comprehensive Health History

Gathering a targeted health history uncovers clues regarding dermatological complaints, occupational exposures, and genetic predispositions. The nurse inquires about the onset, duration, and exact location of any rash, lesion, or pruritus, alongside aggravating or relieving factors. Environmental exposures to harsh chemicals, prolonged ultraviolet radiation, or allergens in Nigerian workplaces heavily influence skin pathology. Reviewing past medical history, family history of skin malignancies, current prescription drugs, and over-the-counter herbal preparations provides vital context, as numerous medications trigger photosensitivity or drug eruptions.

Preparation, Environment, and Tools

An accurate physical examination requires proper preparation of both the environment and equipment. Natural sunlight or adequate overhead lighting is non-negotiable for discerning subtle color variations, cyanosis, or jaundice. Privacy and patient comfort remain paramount, necessitating a warm room and exposing only the body parts currently under examination. Essential tools include: - **Penlight**: For illuminating cavities, mucous membranes, and oblique lighting to reveal lesion elevation. - **Ruler or measuring tape**: For recording the exact dimensions of lesions, ulcers, or areas of alopecia. - **Magnifying glass**: For closely inspecting fine details of primary lesions or parasitic infestations. - **Gloves**: For universal precautions, especially when palpating moist lesions, exudate, or mucous membranes.

Physical Assessment Techniques: Inspection and Palpation

Inspection and palpation form the bedrock of clinical evaluation. The nurse inspects the entire integumentary expanse systematically from head to toe, noting general pigmentation, vascularity, and lesions. When evaluating skin color in individuals with darker pigmentation, the nurse inspects areas with less melanin, such as the sclera, conjunctiva, nail beds, and palms, to detect pallor or jaundice. Palpation evaluates texture, moisture, temperature, turgor, and edema. Skin turgor, assessed by gently pinching skin over the clavicle or forearm, checks for hydration status, noting whether the skin returns promptly to its original contour or exhibits tenting.

Assessing Primary and Secondary Skin Lesions

Dermatological lesions are categorized into primary lesions, which develop on previously unaltered skin, and secondary lesions, which evolve from primary lesions or result from external trauma. Accurate documentation requires noting size, shape, color, elevation, texture, distribution pattern, and exudate. - **Macule**: Flat, circumscribed area of color change less than 1 cm (e.g., freckles). - **Papule**: Elevated, firm, circumscribed area less than 1 cm (e.g., warts). - **Vesicle**: Elevated cavity containing free fluid up to 1 cm (e.g., early chickenpox). - **Pustule**: Elevated lesion containing purulent fluid (e.g., acne). - **Excoriation**: Loss of the epidermis representing a hollowed-out, crusted area from scratching. - **Lichenification**: Rough, thickened epidermis resulting from chronic rubbing or scratching.

Assessing Hair and Nails

Hair assessment evaluates quantity, distribution, texture, and hygiene. Inspecting the scalp reveals scaling, lesions, or evidence of parasitic infestations like lice. Diffuse hair thinning can indicate endocrine disorders, nutritional deficiencies, or severe systemic stress. Nail assessment focuses on color, shape, texture, and capillary refill. The nurse inspects the angle between the nail base and the skin, which normally measures approximately 160 degrees. Clubbing, characterized by an angle exceeding 180 degrees and a spongy nail base, strongly suggests chronic hypoxia or pulmonary disease. Capillary refill, tested by compressing the nail bed until blanching occurs and timing the return of pink color, assesses peripheral perfusion.

Diagnostic Tests and Collaborative Investigations

When physical assessment findings remain inconclusive, diagnostic investigations help confirm underlying pathologies. Common bedside and laboratory procedures include skin scrapings examined with potassium hydroxide (KOH) for fungal elements, Tzanck smears for viral infections, and Wood's lamp examination using ultraviolet light to reveal fluorescent fungal or bacterial activity. Skin biopsies—shave, punch, or excisional—provide definitive histological confirmation for suspicious pigmented lesions or malignancies. Complete blood counts and inflammatory markers are ordered when systemic dermatological disorders are suspected.

Documentation and Clinical Reporting

Accurate, objective documentation completes the integumentary assessment process. Nurses must record findings using clear descriptive terminology rather than vague labels like 'rash' or 'sore.' Documentation includes exact anatomical locations, color, dimensions in centimeters, elevation or depression, exudate characteristics, and client-reported symptoms such as pain or pruritus. Timely reporting of abnormal findings—such as spreading cellulitis, sudden purpura, or rapidly changing nevi—ensures prompt medical intervention and maintains continuity of safe nursing care across shifts.
