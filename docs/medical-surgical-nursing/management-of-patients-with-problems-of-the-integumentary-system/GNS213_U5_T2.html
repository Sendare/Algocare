Assessment of Clients with Problems of the Skin Hair and Nails

Introduction to Integumentary Assessment in Nursing

The integumentary system, comprising the skin, hair, and nails, serves as the body's primary protective barrier against environmental pathogens, mechanical injury, and fluid loss. For nursing students, evaluating this system goes beyond simply noting a patient's cosmetic appearance. The skin is a dynamic mirror reflecting internal health status, systemic disease, nutritional deficiencies, and psychological wellbeing. Changes in color, texture, moisture, or integrity frequently signal underlying pathologies before other organ systems manifest symptoms. A thorough integumentary assessment requires systematic observation, precise vocabulary, and an understanding of normal anatomical variations across different age groups and skin tones.

Gathering the Health History

A comprehensive health history provides the essential context needed to interpret physical findings accurately. Begin by exploring chief complaints related to the skin, such as pruritus, rashes, lesions, hair loss, or brittle nails. Ask targeted questions about onset, duration, progression, and relieving or aggravating factors.

  • Past Medical History: Inquire about chronic illnesses like diabetes, kidney disease, or liver disorders, which profoundly affect skin appearance.
  • Medications: Document prescription drugs, over-the-counter remedies, and herbal supplements, as many cause photosensitivity or drug-induced rashes.
  • Environmental Exposures: Assess occupational hazards, sun exposure habits, chemical contacts, and recent travel history.
  • Family History: Note genetic predispositions to skin cancers, eczema, psoriasis, or allergies.

Preparation and Environment for Physical Examination

Conducting an effective physical assessment of the skin requires minimal equipment but careful environmental control. Ensure the examination room is warm, quiet, and well-lit. Natural daylight is ideal, but if unavailable, bright, color-corrected artificial lighting prevents misinterpretation of skin tones and subtle cyanosis or jaundice.

Gather your tools beforehand:

  • Ruler or Tape Measure: To accurately measure the size of lesions, wounds, or rashes in centimeters.
  • Penlight: To inspect cavities, oral mucosa, and provide tangential lighting across raised skin lesions.
  • Magnifying Glass: To examine fine details of borders and structures within skin lesions.
  • Gloves: To maintain standard precautions when touching non-intact skin or mucous membranes.

Inspection and Palpation Techniques

Physical assessment relies heavily on inspection and palpation, moving systematically from head to toe while ensuring the patient's privacy.

Inspect the entire integument, paying close attention to skin folds, pressure points, and areas exposed to the sun. Note overall skin color, looking for pallor, cyanosis, jaundice, or erythema.

Use palpation to assess temperature, moisture, texture, turgor, and edema. Temperature is best checked using the dorsal (back) surface of your hands, which is more sensitive to thermal changes. Skin turgor evaluates hydration status; gently pinch a fold of skin over the forearm or clavicle and observe how quickly it returns to normal. Sluggish recoil indicates dehydration.

Assessing Primary and Secondary Skin Lesions

When abnormal growths or changes appear on the skin, nurses must categorize them as primary or secondary lesions. Primary lesions develop on previously unaltered skin, whereas secondary lesions evolve from primary lesions due to trauma, scratching, or natural disease progression.

  • Primary Lesions: Macules (flat, non-palpable spots like freckles), papules (raised, solid bumps like moles), vesicles (fluid-filled blisters like in chickenpox), and pustules (pus-filled sacs like acne).
  • Secondary Lesions: Crusts (dried residue of serum or blood), scales (shedding epidermal flakes like in psoriasis), excoriations (abrasive scratches from scratching), and scars or ulcers.

Always document the exact size, shape, color, elevation, texture, distribution, and anatomical location of any lesion.

Hair and Nail Assessment

Hair and nail examinations complete the integumentary survey.

For hair, inspect color, distribution, texture, and quantity. Note any patchy hair loss (alopecia), excessive body hair growth in women (hirsutism), or scalp lesions, scaling, and infestations like lice.

For nails, observe nail plate shape, color, thickness, and cleanliness. Assess capillary refill by pressing firmly on the nail tip until it blanches, then timing how many seconds it takes for pink color to return—normally less than two seconds. Inspect for clubbing, characterized by an increased angle of the nail bed greater than 180 degrees, which frequently indicates chronic hypoxia or pulmonary disease.

Documentation and Reporting Findings

Accurate, objective documentation is vital for tracking changes in skin integrity over time and communicating effectively with the healthcare team. Avoid vague terms like 'bad rash' or 'sores present.' Instead, use precise anatomical descriptions, measurements in centimeters, and clear clinical terminology regarding color, exudate, and border characteristics.

If pressure injuries or wounds are present, document their staging, size, presence of tunneling or undermining, and surrounding tissue condition. Promptly report any sudden changes, spreading erythema, signs of infection, or unexplained skin breakdown to the primary provider to ensure timely therapeutic intervention.
