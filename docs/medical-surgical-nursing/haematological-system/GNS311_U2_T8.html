Polycythaemia

Introduction and Definition of Polycythaemia

Polycythaemia is a hematological condition characterized by a significant, abnormal increase in the number of circulating red blood cells in the bloodstream. Along with the elevated red cell count, nurses often observe a rise in hemoglobin levels and packed cell volume, commonly measured as the hematocrit. When blood becomes packed with too many cells, its viscosity increases, making it thicker and slower to move through the circulatory system. This sluggish blood flow impairs tissue perfusion and forces the cardiovascular system to work much harder to deliver oxygen to vital organs.

Classification: Primary versus Secondary Polycythaemia

Medical classification divides polycythaemia into two broad categories based on the root cause of the red blood cell overproduction.

  • Primary Polycythaemia: Also known as polycythaemia vera, this is a stem cell disorder where the bone marrow produces red blood cells autonomously, without needing normal hormonal signals like erythropoietin.
  • Secondary Polycythaemia: This occurs as a reaction to something else in the body, usually chronic low oxygen levels. The kidneys respond to the hypoxia by releasing excess erythropoietin, which stimulates the bone marrow to pump out more red blood cells.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Understanding why a patient develops polycythaemia requires looking at primary gene changes and secondary environmental triggers.

  • JAK2 Mutation: A genetic mutation in the Janus kinase 2 gene is found in the vast majority of patients with primary polycythaemia vera.
  • Chronic Hypoxia: Conditions that lower blood oxygen over a long period, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, congenital heart disease, or living at very high altitudes, frequently cause secondary polycythaemia.
  • Tumor Production: Certain kidney or liver tumors can inappropriately secrete erythropoietin, driving red cell production upward regardless of oxygen needs.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

The clinical picture of polycythaemia is dominated by the effects of expanded blood volume and sluggish, thick blood flow.

  • Plethora: A reddish or ruddy complexion of the face, hands, and mucous membranes due to dilated blood vessels filled with excess red cells.
  • Neurological Symptoms: Headaches, dizziness, ringing in the ears, and blurred vision resulting from poor blood flow to the brain.
  • Pruritus: Severe itching, especially after taking a warm bath or shower, triggered by histamine release from increased white cells or basophils.
  • Thromboembolism: A major risk where sluggish blood clots inside blood vessels, potentially leading to stroke, deep vein thrombosis, or heart attack.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnosing polycythaemia involves a combination of routine blood tests and specific bone marrow or genetic evaluations to confirm the diagnosis and find the underlying cause.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management aims to reduce blood thickness, prevent dangerous blood clots, and control symptoms.

  • Therapeutic Phlebotomy: The cornerstone of treatment. Nurses remove a specific volume of blood at regular intervals to bring the hematocrit down to a safe level, replacing the volume with intravenous saline if needed.
  • Myelosuppressive Therapy: Medications like hydroxyurea may be prescribed to slow down the bone marrow's production of blood cells in high-risk patients.
  • Low-Dose Aspirin: Administered to reduce the risk of blood clots and microvascular pain, provided there are no contraindications.
  • Hydration and Activity: Nurses encourage high fluid intake to keep blood thinned out and promote early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

Without careful management, polycythaemia can lead to severe, life-threatening complications. The increased thickness of the blood and abnormal platelet function create a high risk for arterial and venous thrombosis, which can manifest as strokes, heart attacks, or pulmonary embolisms. Some patients experience bleeding complications because abnormal platelets do not function properly despite their high numbers. Over time, primary polycythaemia vera can transform into myelofibrosis, where scar tissue replaces normal bone marrow, or progress into acute leukemia.
