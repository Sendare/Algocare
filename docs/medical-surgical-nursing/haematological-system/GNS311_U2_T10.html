Bleeding Disorders

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Haemostasis

Haemostasis is the precise physiological mechanism the body employs to stop bleeding from injured blood vessels while maintaining fluid blood throughout the rest of the circulatory system. This protective response unfolds in three sequential phases: vascular spasm, primary haemostasis, and secondary haemostasis, followed eventually by fibrinolysis. - **Vascular spasm**: Immediate smooth muscle contraction in the wall of the damaged vessel reduces blood flow instantly. - **Primary haemostasis**: Platelets adhere to the exposed subendothelial collagen, activate, and aggregate to form a loose platelet plug. - **Secondary haemostasis**: The coagulation cascade is triggered, culminating in the conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands that stabilize the platelet plug. - **Fibrinolysis**: Plasmin gradually dissolves the clot once tissue repair is well underway, restoring normal vascular patency.

Classification and Etiology of Bleeding Disorders

Bleeding disorders arise when any component of the haemostatic mechanism fails, leading to either prolonged spontaneous bleeding or excessive haemorrhage following minor trauma. Clinically, these disorders are broadly divided into two major categories based on their origin: inherited and acquired. Inherited bleeding disorders are typically single-gene mutations passed down through families, manifesting early in childhood. They frequently involve deficiencies in specific clotting factors or structural proteins essential for platelet adhesion. Acquired bleeding disorders, conversely, develop later in life secondary to systemic conditions, nutritional deficiencies, liver disease, bone marrow suppression, or drug toxicities. Recognizing whether a bleeding diathesis is congenital or acquired dictates both diagnostic urgency and therapeutic intervention.

Common Inherited Bleeding Disorders: Haemophilia and Von Willebrand Disease

Haemophilia A and B are X-linked recessive conditions resulting from deficiencies in clotting factors VIII and IX, respectively. Because they are carried on the X chromosome, males are predominantly affected, while females generally serve as asymptomatic carriers. Patients with severe haemophilia often experience spontaneous deep tissue bleeding into joints and muscles, known as haemarthrosis, which can lead to chronic crippling arthropathy if left untreated. Von Willebrand disease is the most common inherited bleeding disorder overall, affecting males and females equally due to its autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. It is caused by a deficiency or structural dysfunction of von Willebrand factor, a protein required for normal platelet adhesion and the protection of factor VIII in the circulation. Clinical signs typically reflect primary haemostatic failure, such as frequent epistaxis, heavy menstrual bleeding, and excessive oozing from minor cuts.

Acquired Bleeding Disorders: DIC and Vitamin K Deficiency

Disseminated intravascular coagulation is a complex, life-threatening syndrome characterized by the systemic activation of coagulation pathways. This widespread clotting depletes platelets and clotting factors, paradoxically resulting in severe, simultaneous microvascular thrombosis and profuse haemorrhage from surgical sites, venipuncture sites, and mucous membranes. Common triggers include severe sepsis, obstetric emergencies, and major trauma. Vitamin K deficiency impairs the liver's ability to synthesize functional coagulation factors II, VII, IX, and X, along with anticoagulant proteins C and S. Because vitamin K is fat-soluble and largely dependent on dietary intake and normal intestinal flora absorption, deficiencies commonly arise in newborn infants before gut colonization, patients with prolonged antibiotic therapy, or individuals with fat malabsorption syndromes.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

Recognizing the external signs of a bleeding disorder requires a careful physical examination and a detailed health history. Primary haemostatic defects usually present with petechiae, purpura, ecchymosis, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, and menorrhagia. In contrast, secondary haemostatic defects—such as factor deficiencies—tend to produce deep-seated bleeding manifestations like large subcutaneous haematomas, retroperitoneal bleeding, and painful haemarthrosis. During assessment, the nurse must actively inquire about a family history of bleeding tendencies, dietary habits, recent infections, and the use of over-the-counter medications like aspirin or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs that interfere with platelet function. Careful inspection of the skin, mucous membranes, and joints provides vital clues regarding the severity and location of ongoing haemorrhage.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Tests

Laboratory evaluation of bleeding disorders involves a series of screening assays that assess different branches of the haemostatic system. - **Platelet count**: Measures the total number of circulating platelets to rule out thrombocytopenia. - **Prothrombin time (PT) / International Normalized Ratio (INR)**: Evaluates the extrinsic and common pathways of the coagulation cascade, commonly used to monitor warfarin therapy. - **Activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT)**: Evaluates the intrinsic and common pathways, prolonged in haemophilia and heparin therapy. - **Bleeding time or PFA-100**: Assesses primary platelet function and vascular response. Specific factor assays are subsequently ordered if screening tests return abnormal results, identifying the exact deficient protein.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management focuses on halting active bleeding and preventing future episodes by replacing missing components or modulating the clotting cascade. For inherited factor deficiencies, recombinant or plasma-derived factor concentrates are administered intravenously. In von Willebrand disease, desmopressin may be given to stimulate the release of stored von Willebrand factor from endothelial cells. For acquired disorders, treatment targets the underlying root cause. Vitamin K is administered orally or parenterally to reverse deficiency states, while fresh frozen plasma or cryoprecipitate replaces depleted coagulation factors in acute situations like DIC or severe liver failure. Platelet transfusions are reserved for significant thrombocytopenia or active bleeding unresponsive to other measures.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care centers on injury prevention, meticulous monitoring for signs of internal haemorrhage, and empowering patients to manage their condition safely at home. Nurses must avoid invasive procedures whenever possible, use small-gauge needles, apply prolonged pressure after venipuncture, and inspect stools and urine for occult blood. Education focuses on practical lifestyle adjustments. Patients taking anticoagulants or living with haemophilia should avoid contact sports, use soft-bristled toothbrushes, avoid razor blades in favor of electric shavers, and recognize red-flag symptoms such as sudden severe headache, dark tarry stools, or persistent epistaxis that warrant immediate medical evaluation.
