Bleeding Disorders

Pathophysiology of Haemostasis

Haemostasis is the body's precise, rapid response to stop bleeding from injured blood vessels without blocking the entire circulatory system. The process unfolds in overlapping stages designed to seal damaged tissue.

  • Vasoconstriction: Immediately after a blood vessel breaks, the smooth muscle in its wall contracts sharply to slow down blood flow to the area.
  • Primary haemostasis: Platelets rush to the site of injury, stick to the exposed subendothelial collagen, and clump together to form a soft, unstable platelet plug.
  • Secondary haemostasis: The coagulation cascade is triggered, activating clotting factors in a specific sequence that converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands, which firmly reinforce the platelet plug.
  • Fibrinolysis: Once tissue repair is well underway, the body breaks down the clot through the action of plasmin so normal blood flow is eventually restored.

Classification and Etiology

Bleeding disorders arise when any part of the haemostatic mechanism fails, whether due to a missing clotting protein, an abnormal platelet count, or fragile blood vessel walls. Clinicians divide these disorders into two major groups based on their origin: inherited and acquired.

Inherited disorders are passed down genetically and typically present early in childhood. They often involve single factor deficiencies, such as missing factor VIII in haemophilia A or a shortage of von Willebrand factor.

Acquired disorders develop later in life due to underlying medical conditions, nutritional deficits, or medications. Common causes include severe liver disease, where most clotting factors are manufactured, bone marrow suppression from cancer treatments, or anticoagulant drug overdoses. A frequent clinical misconception is that all bleeding problems are hereditary; nurses must remember that sudden onset bleeding in adults is usually acquired and points to an acute systemic trigger.

Inherited Disorders: Haemophilia and Von Willebrand Disease

Haemophilia A and B are X-linked recessive conditions, meaning they predominantly affect males who inherit the abnormal gene from their mothers. Haemophilia A involves a deficiency of factor VIII, while haemophilia B stems from a lack of factor IX. Patients with severe haemophilia experience spontaneous bleeding into large joints and muscles, leading to chronic pain and joint destruction if left untreated.

Von Willebrand disease is the most common inherited bleeding disorder, affecting males and females equally because it is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Von Willebrand factor acts like molecular glue, helping platelets stick to damaged vessel walls and carrying factor VIII in the blood. Consequently, patients with this condition usually experience mucosal bleeding, such as frequent nosebleeds, heavy menstrual periods, and prolonged bleeding after dental extractions.

Acquired Disorders: DIC and Vitamin K Deficiency

Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is a life-threatening condition triggered by severe systemic infections, trauma, or obstetric complications. It causes widespread, abnormal activation of clotting inside the blood vessels, which consumes all available platelets and clotting factors. Paradoxically, this massive internal clotting leads to severe, uncontrolled external hemorrhage from venipuncture sites and surgical wounds.

Vitamin K deficiency impairs the liver's ability to synthesize clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. Newborn infants are especially vulnerable to this deficiency because their sterile guts lack the bacteria needed to produce vitamin K, which is why prophylactic vitamin K injections are standard at birth. In adults, poor dietary intake, prolonged antibiotic therapy that kills gut flora, or fat malabsorption syndromes can similarly trigger bleeding tendencies.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

Recognizing a bleeding disorder requires careful observation of the skin, mucous membranes, and joints. The clinical presentation often differentiates primary haemostasis problems from secondary coagulation defects.

  • Petechiae: Tiny, pinpoint red or purple spots on the skin caused by minor capillary bleeding, typically seen in platelet disorders.
  • Purpura and Ecchymoses: Larger bruises resulting from blood leaking into subcutaneous tissue.
  • Epistaxis and Gingival Bleeding: Frequent nosebleeds and bleeding gums, common in platelet or von Willebrand deficiencies.
  • Hemarthrosis: Bleeding directly into joint cavities, causing severe pain, swelling, and restricted movement—a hallmark of severe haemophilia.

During assessment, nurses must ask targeted questions about the duration and volume of menstrual flow, easy bruising history, and any recent unusual bleeding after minor cuts or dental work.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Tests

Laboratory screening tests help pinpoint which part of the haemostatic pathway is failing.

  • Platelet count: Measures the number of circulating platelets; low counts indicate thrombocytopenia.
  • Prothrombin Time (PT) and INR: Evaluates the extrinsic and common pathways of coagulation, frequently used to monitor warfarin therapy and liver function.
  • Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT): Assesses the intrinsic and common pathways, prolonged in haemophilia and heparin therapy.
  • Fibrinogen level and D-dimer: Measures clotting protein reserves and breakdown products; elevated D-dimer strongly supports a diagnosis of DIC.

Nurses must ensure correct collection tube selection, gentle handling of samples, and immediate delivery to the laboratory to prevent artifactual clotting errors.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical treatment focuses on stopping active bleeding and preventing future episodes by replacing missing components or suppressing pathological clotting.

Replacement therapy involves infusing specific concentrated clotting factors, fresh frozen plasma, or platelet concentrates depending on the exact deficit. For von Willebrand disease or mild haemophilia A, desmopressin is often administered to stimulate the release of stored factor VIII and von Willebrand factor from endothelial cells.

Antifibrinolytic agents like tranexamic acid are frequently prescribed to prevent the premature breakdown of clots, especially during dental procedures or mucosal bleeding. In conditions like DIC, treating the underlying trigger—such as clearing a severe infection—remains the most critical step in saving the patient's life.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with bleeding disorders balances safety precautions with support for daily living. Nurses must minimize trauma by using the smallest possible needles for venipuncture, applying prolonged pressure to injection sites for at least five minutes, and avoiding rectal temperature measurements or IM injections.

Safety education is vital for patients and their families. Nurses should teach patients to use soft-bristled toothbrushes, avoid contact sports, wear medical alert jewelry, and recognize early warning signs of internal bleeding, such as sudden swelling, severe joint pain, or dark, tarry stools.
