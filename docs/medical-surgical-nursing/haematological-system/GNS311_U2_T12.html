Blood Transfusion and Complications

Introduction and Principles of Blood Transfusion

Blood transfusion is a life-saving medical intervention involving the intravenous delivery of whole blood or specific blood components to restore lost volume, improve oxygen-carrying capacity, or correct coagulation deficits. Every clinical nurse must understand that blood is practically treated as a liquid organ transplant. Components administered include packed red blood cells for severe anemia, platelets for bleeding disorders, fresh frozen plasma for clotting factor deficiencies, and cryoprecipitate for specific fibrinogen issues.

Ethical and safety principles demand strict adherence to patient consent, accurate identification, and evidence-based transfusion triggers. Transfusion should never be taken casually; it carries inherent immunological and infectious risks that require constant vigilance.

Blood Typing, Cross-Matching, and Compatibility

Safe transfusion relies heavily on understanding the ABO and Rh blood group systems.

  • Type A: Red blood cells carry A antigens; plasma contains anti-B antibodies.
  • Type B: Red blood cells carry B antigens; plasma contains anti-A antibodies.
  • Type AB: Red blood cells carry both A and B antigens; plasma contains neither antibody, making them universal recipients for red cells.
  • Type O: Red blood cells carry neither antigen; plasma contains both anti-A and anti-B antibodies, making them universal donors for red cells.

Cross-matching is the final laboratory check where donor red blood cells are mixed with recipient serum to detect any hidden antibodies that could cause agglutination and hemolysis. A minor mismatch can trigger a catastrophic hemolytic reaction.

Pre-Transfusion Assessment and Preparation

Before any blood product leaves the blood bank, the nurse must ensure the patient has a valid baseline assessment, signed consent, and secure intravenous access. A large-bore cannula, typically size 18-gauge or 20-gauge, is preferred in adults to prevent hemolysis of red blood cells during rapid infusion.

The nurse must obtain baseline vital signs including temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure immediately before starting. Any pre-existing fever should be reported to the physician, as it can mask or complicate the recognition of a transfusion reaction later. Finally, verify that the blood bag matches the cross-match report and hospital identification band through a mandatory two-person bedside check.

Administration Procedure and Patient Monitoring

Blood administration demands rigorous adherence to timing and tubing protocols. Always use a specialized blood administration set containing an integrated in-line filter to catch any micro-clots or cellular debris. Prime the Y-tubing exclusively with 0.9% Normal Saline; never use dextrose solutions, as they cause red blood cell hemolysis, or Ringer's lactate, because its calcium content can trigger clotting within the tubing.

Stay with the patient for the first 15 minutes of the infusion at a slow rate, because most severe acute reactions occur during this window. Recheck vital signs after 15 minutes, then periodically throughout the transfusion. Complete the infusion of a single unit of blood within a maximum of four hours to prevent bacterial proliferation in the nutrient-rich blood bag.

Acute Transfusion Complications and Nursing Management

When a transfusion reaction occurs, immediate action can save a patient's life.

  1. Stop the transfusion immediately: Discontinue the blood flow, but keep the IV line open by infusing fresh 0.9% Normal Saline through a new set of tubing.
  2. Assess the patient: Check vital signs, notify the physician and blood bank at once.
  3. Send samples: Return the blood bag, transfusion set, and fresh blood/urine samples to the laboratory for analysis.

Common acute reactions include acute hemolytic reaction (fever, flank pain, hypotension due to ABO incompatibility), febrile non-hemolytic reaction (fever and chills without hemolysis), allergic reaction (urticaria and itching), and fluid overload (TACO) resulting in dyspnea and hypertension from rapid volume expansion.

Delayed Transfusion Complications

Not all complications show up while the blood is running. Delayed reactions can appear days, weeks, or even months after the transfusion is complete.

  • Delayed hemolytic reaction: Occurs days to weeks later when previously undetectable antibodies form against donor antigens, leading to a gradual drop in hemoglobin and mild jaundice.
  • Transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease: A rare, fatal condition where donor white blood cells attack the recipient's tissues, common in immunocompromised patients unless blood is irradiated.
  • Transfusion-transmitted infections: Viral (HIV, Hepatitis B and C), bacterial, or parasitic agents transmitted despite rigorous donor screening.
  • Iron overload (Hemosiderosis): Occurs in patients receiving chronic, repeated transfusions (like those with sickle cell anemia) because the body has no effective mechanism to excrete excess iron.

Documentation and Post-Transfusion Care

Comprehensive documentation completes the safety cycle of any blood transfusion. The nurse must record the start and end times, total volume infused, product serial number, patient vital signs at every mandated interval, and any adverse reactions observed.

Post-transfusion monitoring continues for a brief window after completion. The intravenous line can be converted back to routine maintenance fluids or flushed and locked as per hospital policy. Any empty blood bags must be retained temporarily in the unit refrigerator according to institutional guidelines in case a delayed investigation is requested by the blood bank.
