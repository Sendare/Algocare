Blood Transfusion and Complications

Introduction and Principles of Blood Transfusion

Blood transfusion involves the intravenous transfer of whole blood or blood components from a donor to a recipient to restore circulatory volume, oxygen-carrying capacity, and hemostasis. Whole blood contains red blood cells, plasma, white blood cells, and platelets, but modern practice relies heavily on component therapy. Separating blood into packed red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitate allows clinicians to treat specific deficits while minimizing the risk of circulatory overload and allergen exposure.

Every transfusion carries inherent immunological and infectious risks. Strict physiological principles must guide the process. The primary goal is maintaining adequate tissue oxygenation by preserving hemoglobin levels and ensuring tissue perfusion without overworking the cardiovascular system. Safe transfusion practice rests on the bedrock of positive patient identification, rigorous laboratory cross-matching, and vigilant bedside monitoring.

Blood Typing, Cross-Matching, and Compatibility

Blood compatibility is determined by surface antigens on red blood cells and corresponding antibodies in the serum. The ABO system categorizes blood into types A, B, AB, and O based on A and B antigens. The Rhesus (Rh) system dictates whether blood is positive or negative based on the presence of the D antigen.

  • Type O negative: Considered the universal donor for red blood cells because its cells lack A, B, and Rh antigens, minimizing immune attack in a recipient.
  • Type AB positive: Considered the universal recipient for red blood cells because the serum contains no natural antibodies against A, B, or Rh antigens.
  • Type specific cross-match: Laboratory testing mixing donor red blood cells with recipient serum to detect active agglutination before release.

In emergency settings in Nigerian tertiary hospitals where O negative blood is scarce, uncrossed O positive blood may be issued to males or post-menopausal females to save time, avoiding rapid exsanguination.

Pre-Transfusion Assessment and Preparation

Preparation before starting a transfusion prevents fatal clerical and physiological errors. Baseline vital signs—temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure—must be recorded to establish a reference point against which potential reaction symptoms can be measured.

  1. Verify Medical Order: Ensure a written, signed doctor's prescription for the specific blood product exists.
  2. Obtain Informed Consent: Explain the risks, benefits, and alternatives to the patient or relatives, securing documented consent.
  3. Establish IV Access: Insert a large-bore intravenous cannula, typically 18-gauge or 16-gauge, to prevent mechanical hemolysis of red blood cells during rapid infusion.
  4. Prepare Normal Saline: Ensure only 0.9% Sodium Chloride is used as the priming and flush solution. Dextrose causes hemolysis and Ringer's lactate contains calcium which can trigger clotting.
  5. Bedside Two-Nurse Check: Two qualified healthcare professionals must independently verify patient identity using hospital identification bands against the blood bag label, compatibility form, and hospital number.

Administration Procedure and Patient Monitoring

Blood administration demands absolute vigilance from the nursing team. A specialized blood administration set featuring an inline filter must be used to trap micro-aggregates and debris. The transfusion must commence slowly, running at a rate of no more than 2 milliliters per minute for the first fifteen minutes. The nurse must remain at the bedside during this critical window, as most severe acute immunological reactions manifest within the first 50 milliliters of blood infused.

After fifteen minutes without adverse signs, the flow rate is adjusted to the prescribed level, ensuring the entire unit finishes within a maximum timeframe of four hours. Exceeding four hours invites bacterial proliferation within the nutrient-rich blood bag at room temperature. Vital signs are re-checked at fifteen minutes, then hourly until completion, and finally thirty minutes post-transfusion.

Classification of Blood Transfusion Reactions

Transfusion reactions are adverse events occurring during or after the administration of blood components. They are classified chronologically into acute and delayed reactions, and pathologically into immune-mediated and non-immune-mediated processes.

Acute Transfusion Complications and Nursing Management

Acute complications require lightning-fast clinical intervention. If any reaction sign appears—such as fever, chills, back pain, dyspnea, hypotension, or urticaria—the nurse must stop the transfusion immediately.

  1. Stop the Infusion: Disconnect the blood bag immediately while leaving the intravenous cannula open with fresh 0.9% normal saline using new tubing.
  2. Notify Physicians and Blood Bank: Inform the attending doctor and the blood bank laboratory immediately.
  3. Send Samples: Return the remaining blood bag, transfusion set, and freshly drawn blood and urine samples to the laboratory for clerical and hemolytic re-testing.
  4. Supportive Care: Administer emergency medications such as antihistamines, corticosteroids, or epinephrine as ordered, and monitor urinary output to detect early acute kidney injury resulting from hemoglobinuria.

Acute hemolytic reactions are catastrophic emergencies driven by ABO incompatibility, causing widespread intravascular hemolysis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and renal failure.

Delayed Transfusion Complications

Delayed complications emerge days, weeks, or even years after exposure to blood products. Delayed hemolytic reactions occur when a patient has a low-titer antibody that goes undetected during pre-transfusion testing, but flares up upon re-exposure, causing hemolysis 5 to 10 days later. Symptoms are typically milder than acute reactions, presenting as unexplained jaundice and falling hemoglobin levels.

Infectious transmission remains a significant concern in developing healthcare settings. Despite routine screening for Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and Syphilis, a window period exists where donors harbor infections without detectable antibodies. Transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease is another rare, nearly always fatal complication where donor lymphocytes attack the immunocompromised recipient's tissues.

Documentation and Post-Transfusion Care

Accurate documentation completes the transfusion process and ensures legal and clinical accountability. The nurse must record the exact time the transfusion started and ended, the total volume infused, the product identification numbers, baseline and post-transfusion vital signs, and any nursing observations or interventions.

The patient remains under observation for a designated period post-transfusion. Empty blood bags must not be discarded immediately; hospital policy usually dictates storing them in a refrigerator for 24 hours in case an investigation into a delayed reaction becomes necessary. Educating the patient to report late symptoms like fever, yellow eyes, or unusual fatigue ensures continuity of safe care after discharge.
