Blood Transfusion and Complications

Introduction and Principles of Blood Transfusion

Blood transfusion is a vital therapeutic intervention utilized in clinical practice to restore circulating blood volume, improve oxygen-carrying capacity, and correct coagulation defects. As nursing students in Nigeria, understanding the foundational principles of blood transfusion is essential for ensuring patient safety and preventing adverse outcomes. Blood products commonly administered include whole blood, packed red blood cells (PRBCs), fresh frozen plasma (FFP), and platelets. Each component has specific clinical indications; for instance, PRBCs are primarily used to treat severe anemia or acute blood loss without expanding plasma volume excessively, while FFP is indicated for patients with multiple coagulation factor deficiencies. The fundamental principle governing all transfusions is strict patient identification, ensuring that the right blood product is administered to the correct patient at the prescribed time. Because blood is considered a liquid organ transplant, it carries inherent immunological and infectious risks, necessitating meticulous adherence to institutional and national transfusion protocols. Nurses serve as the final safety barrier in the transfusion chain, bearing the legal and professional responsibility to verify orders, inspect blood products for integrity and expiration dates, and monitor the patient continuously throughout the procedure. Recognizing the physiological goals and potential hazards of blood components allows nurses to deliver competent, evidence-based care in various healthcare settings across Nigeria.

Blood Typing, Cross-Matching, and Compatibility

Blood typing and cross-matching are critical pre-transfusion processes designed to prevent severe, potentially fatal hemolytic transfusion reactions caused by immunological incompatibility. The ABO system categorizes blood into types A, B, AB, and O based on the presence or absence of specific antigens on the erythrocyte membrane, alongside naturally occurring antibodies in the plasma. Furthermore, the Rhesus (Rh) factor determines whether blood is positive or negative based on the D antigen. Type O negative blood is traditionally known as the universal red cell donor because it lacks A, B, and Rh antigens, making it less likely to trigger a severe recipient immune response in emergency situations. Conversely, Type AB individuals are considered universal plasma recipients. Cross-matching involves mixing the recipient's serum with the donor's red blood cells (major cross-match) to detect any unpredictable antibodies that could cause agglutination and hemolysis. Before any transfusion is initiated, rigorous bedside verification must take place, typically requiring two qualified healthcare professionals to match the patient's wristband details against the blood bag label, compatibility report, and hospital identification numbers. Any discrepancy in blood typing or patient identification mandates an immediate halt to the process and notification of the blood bank. Mastery of compatibility principles prevents catastrophic complement-mediated immune destruction of transfused cells.

Pre-Transfusion Assessment and Preparation

Pre-transfusion assessment and preparation set the foundation for a safe and successful blood administration process. The nurse must begin by evaluating the patient's baseline vital signs, including temperature, blood pressure, pulse rate, and respiratory rate, which serve as a reference point for detecting any future transfusion reactions. A thorough history should be taken to check for any previous transfusion reactions, multiple pregnancies, or allergies that could heighten immunological risks. The nurse must ensure that valid, written informed consent has been obtained from the patient or legal guardian, explaining the risks, benefits, and alternatives of the procedure. Additionally, the nurse must confirm that a patent intravenous (IV) access line is established, ideally using a large-bore cannula (18-gauge or 20-gauge) to prevent mechanical hemolysis of red blood cells during infusion. Only 0.9% Normal Saline (Sodium Chloride) solution must be hung alongside blood products, as other IV fluids like Lactated Ringer's contain calcium which can induce clotting, or Dextrose solutions which can cause hemolysis. The blood product should be collected from the blood bank only when the IV site is ready and the patient is fully prepared, ensuring it is checked against all documentation by two nurses at the bedside immediately before hanging. Proper preparation minimizes delays, maintains cold chain integrity until administration, and optimizes patient safety.

Administration Procedure and Patient Monitoring

The administration procedure and continuous patient monitoring are critical nursing responsibilities during a blood transfusion. The transfusion must be initiated slowly, typically at a rate of no more than 2 mL/min (or roughly 50 to 100 mL/hr) for the first 15 minutes, during which the nurse must remain with the patient. This initial window is crucial because the vast majority of acute, life-threatening immunological reactions manifest within the first 50 mL of blood infused. The nurse must monitor and record vital signs immediately before starting, 15 minutes into the transfusion, hourly until completion, and one hour post-transfusion. Blood must be infused using a specialized blood administration set that incorporates an inline filter to trap any clots or cellular debris. Standard transfusion protocols dictate that a unit of blood must be infused completely within a maximum of 4 hours to prevent bacterial proliferation, which increases significantly at room temperature. If the patient exhibits signs of a reaction, such as chills, fever, itching, dyspnea, or back pain, the nurse must immediately stop the transfusion, disconnect the blood tubing while leaving the IV line patent with normal saline, notify the physician and blood bank, and send the remaining blood bag and tubing for laboratory investigation. Regular assessment ensures prompt intervention and safeguards the patient against irreversible complications.

Classification of Blood Transfusion Reactions

Blood transfusion reactions are classified based on their underlying pathophysiology and the time frame in which they occur following the initiation of the transfusion. Broadly, they are categorized into acute reactions, which develop within 24 hours of administration, and delayed reactions, which manifest days, weeks, or even months later. Acute complications are further subdivided into immune-mediated and non-immune-mediated categories. Immune-mediated acute reactions include acute hemolytic reactions, febrile non-hemolytic reactions, allergic reactions ranging from mild urticaria to severe anaphylaxis, and transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI). Non-immune acute reactions involve circulatory overload (TACO), bacterial contamination resulting in septic shock, and physical or chemical hemolysis caused by improper warming or incompatible fluids. Delayed reactions encompass delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions, transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (TA-GVHD), post-transfusion purpura, and transfusion-transmitted infections such as HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C. Understanding this systematic classification helps nursing students quickly identify clinical presentations, differentiate between immune and non-immune etiologies, and implement appropriate life-saving emergency protocols in clinical practice.

Acute Transfusion Complications and Nursing Management

Acute transfusion complications require immediate, decisive nursing management to prevent morbidity and mortality. An acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, caused by ABO incompatibility, results in intravascular destruction of red blood cells presenting with fever, chills, chest or lower back pain, hemoglobinuria, and hypotension; the nurse's immediate action is to stop the transfusion, infuse normal saline, and maintain renal perfusion with diuretics and IV fluids as ordered. Febrile non-hemolytic reactions present with isolated temperature spikes and chills without hemolysis, managed by slowing the infusion and administering antipyretics like paracetamol. Allergic reactions characterized by urticaria and itching are managed with antihistamines, whereas severe anaphylaxis requires immediate epinephrine and airway support. Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO) occurs when blood is infused too rapidly, causing pulmonary edema, hypertension, and dyspnea; management includes stopping the transfusion, placing the patient in an upright position with feet dependent, and administering diuretics and oxygen. In suspected bacterial contamination leading to septic shock, broad-spectrum antibiotics, blood cultures, and aggressive hemodynamic support are vital. Prompt recognition and adherence to emergency response algorithms are fundamental nursing competencies in managing these critical events.

Delayed Transfusion Complications

Delayed transfusion complications manifest days to months following the administration of blood products and pose significant diagnostic challenges. Delayed hemolytic transfusion reactions typically occur 5 to 10 days post-transfusion due to anamnestic immune responses against minor red blood cell antigens that were undetectable during initial cross-matching, presenting clinically with unexplained drops in hemoglobin, mild jaundice, and low-grade fever. Transfusion-transmitted infections (TTIs) remain a critical public health concern in Nigeria; despite rigorous donor screening for HIV, Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, and Syphilis, a window period exists where infected donors may test negative. Post-transfusion purpura is a rare, severe condition characterized by profound thrombocytopenia developing about a week after transfusion, resulting from alloantibodies directed against platelet antigens. Transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease (TA-GVHD) occurs when viable donor T-lymphocytes engraft and attack the immunocompromised recipient’s tissues, carrying an exceptionally high mortality rate. Nurses must maintain vigilance during post-transfusion care, educating patients to report unusual symptoms such as yellowing of the skin, prolonged bleeding, unexplained fever, or persistent fatigue weeks after leaving the hospital, ensuring timely medical evaluation and intervention.

Documentation and Post-Transfusion Care

Accurate documentation and comprehensive post-transfusion care are essential components of legal and clinical nursing practice. Following the completion of a blood transfusion, the nurse must record all pertinent details in the patient's medical record, including the unique identification number of the blood product, the exact start and completion times, the volume and type of component infused, baseline and post-transfusion vital signs, and the patient's tolerance of the procedure. Any adverse reactions, the interventions implemented, and the physician's notification must be documented meticulously. The empty blood bag should be retained according to hospital policy—typically refrigerated for 24 hours in case laboratory investigation is subsequently required. Post-transfusion care involves monitoring the patient for delayed complications, assessing laboratory values such as post-transfusion hemoglobin and hematocrit levels to evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the intervention, and discontinuing the intravenous line or converting it to a standard maintenance solution if no further infusions are needed. Thorough documentation protects the healthcare team legally, ensures continuity of care, and provides a clear audit trail for quality assurance in clinical settings across Nigeria.
