Blood Transfusion and Complications

Introduction and Principles of Blood Transfusion

Blood transfusion is a life-saving therapy involving the intravenous delivery of whole blood or specific blood components, such as packed red blood cells, platelets, fresh frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate, to restore oxygen-carrying capacity, volume, or clotting factors.

  • Whole Blood: Used primarily for massive acute blood loss where volume and oxygen-carrying capacity must be restored simultaneously.
  • Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBCs): Concentrated red cells used to treat anemia and chronic blood loss without risking fluid overload.
  • Platelets: Administered to prevent or treat bleeding in patients with severe thrombocytopenia or platelet function disorders.
  • Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP): Contains clotting factors used to manage coagulopathies and reverse warfarin effects.

Clinical safety relies strictly on minimizing immunological risks and disease transmission through rigorous donor screening and standardized handling protocols.

Blood Typing, Cross-Matching, and Compatibility

Blood compatibility is determined primarily by the ABO system and the Rhesus (Rh) factor. Antigens present on red blood cell membranes dictate blood type, while corresponding antibodies circulate in the plasma.

  • Type A: A antigens on cells, anti-B antibodies in plasma.
  • Type B: B antigens on cells, anti-A antibodies in plasma.
  • Type AB: Both antigens on cells, no natural antibodies in plasma (Universal Recipient for PRBCs).
  • Type O: No antigens on cells, both anti-A and anti-B antibodies in plasma (Universal Donor for PRBCs).

The cross-match procedure mixes donor red cells with recipient serum in the laboratory to detect unexpected antibodies and guarantee serological safety before actual infusion occurs.

Pre-Transfusion Assessment and Preparation

Preparation before transfusion requires careful clinical checks to prevent fatal administrative errors. Nurses must secure valid informed consent, confirm baseline vital signs, and establish patent intravenous access using a large-bore cannula, typically 18 or 20 gauge, to prevent hemolysis.

Normal saline (0.9% Sodium Chloride) is the only intravenous fluid compatible with blood administration; dextrose or Ringer's lactate solutions cause cellular hemolysis or clotting.

Before hanging the unit, two qualified healthcare personnel must independently verify the patient's identity against the blood bag label, hospital identification number, blood group, and expiration date at the bedside.

Administration Procedure and Patient Monitoring

Blood administration demands strict vigilance. The infusion must begin slowly—typically no faster than 2mlich for the first fifteen minutes—while the nurse remains at the bedside to monitor for early signs of an acute reaction.

  • Check and record vital signs at baseline, after fifteen minutes, and hourly until completion.
  • Complete the transfusion of a single unit within a maximum of four hours to prevent bacterial proliferation in the blood bag at room temperature.
  • Use a specialized blood administration set containing a built-in mesh filter to trap clots and cellular debris.

If the patient remains stable after the initial slow phase, the flow rate can be increased to the prescribed therapeutic level.

Acute Transfusion Reactions and Nursing Actions

Acute transfusion reactions occur during or within 24 hours of blood administration. Recognizing symptoms early and intervening promptly prevents severe morbidity and mortality.

  • Acute Hemolytic Reaction: Caused by ABO incompatibility. Symptoms include fever, chills, lower back pain, hypotension, and hemoglobinuria.
  • Febrile Non-Hemolytic Reaction: Caused by donor white blood cell antibodies. Presents with isolated fever and chills without hemodynamic collapse.
  • Allergic/Anaphylactic Reaction: Triggered by plasma proteins. Manifests as urticaria, pruritus, wheezing, and potential cardiovascular collapse.
  • Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO): Caused by rapid infusion volume. Features dyspnea, orthopnea, hypertension, and pulmonary edema.

Immediate nursing action for any suspected reaction requires stopping the transfusion instantly, replacing the blood tubing with a fresh saline line, notifying the physician, and returning the blood bag to the laboratory.

Delayed Transfusion Complications

Delayed complications emerge days, weeks, or even years after a blood transfusion concludes. Although less dramatic than acute events, they carry significant long-term health risks for recipients.

  • Delayed Hemolytic Reactions: Occur days to weeks post-transfusion due to an anamnestic antibody response against minor red cell antigens.
  • Transfusion-Associated Graft-Versus-Host Disease (TA-GVHD): Rare, fatal condition where donor T-lymphocytes attack recipient tissues, common in immunocompromised hosts.
  • Iron Overload (Hemosiderosis): Develops in patients receiving chronic, repeated transfusions because the human body lacks an active mechanism to excrete excess iron.
  • Transfusion-Transmitted Infections: Transmission of blood-borne pathogens like Hepatitis B and C, HIV, syphilis, and malaria despite modern donor screening techniques.

Documentation and Post-Transfusion Care

Accurate documentation completes the transfusion process and ensures legal and clinical continuity of care. Nurses must record the start and completion times, total volume infused, specific blood product serial numbers, patient baseline and post-transfusion vital signs, and any nursing interventions required during the procedure.

Post-transfusion monitoring continues according to facility policy to detect any delayed reactions. Nurses evaluate the patient for clinical improvement, checking laboratory values like hemoglobin and hematocrit to assess therapeutic efficacy.
