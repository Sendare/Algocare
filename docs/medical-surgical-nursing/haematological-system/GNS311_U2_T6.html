Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Anaemia

Anaemia is defined as a reduction in the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, resulting from a decrease in the total circulating red blood cell mass, a drop in haemoglobin concentration, or both. This deficiency leaves peripheral tissues under-oxygenated. To compensate for tissue hypoxia, the cardiovascular system increases cardiac output and respiratory rate, while the kidneys release erythropoietin to stimulate bone marrow red cell production. Chronic hypoxia leads to generalised fatigue and reduced cellular metabolism across all major organ systems.

Classification and Etiology of Anaemia

Anaemias are classified based on red blood cell morphology using indices such as the mean corpuscular volume (MCV), or through underlying physiological mechanisms like decreased production, increased destruction, or blood loss. - **Microcytic Hypochromic Anaemia**: Characterised by small, pale cells with low MCV; typically caused by iron deficiency or thalassaemia. - **Normocytic Normochromic Anaemia**: Cells are normal in size and colour with normal MCV; often caused by acute blood loss, chronic disease, or haemolysis. - **Macrocytic Anaemia**: Characterised by enlarged cells with high MCV; commonly caused by vitamin B12 or folate deficiency.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

The clinical presentation of anaemia varies depending on the severity and the speed of onset. Common physical signs stem from cellular hypoxia and the cardiovascular system's attempt to compensate. - **General Appearance**: Pallor of the conjunctivae, nail beds, and palmar creases, alongside generalised fatigue. - **Cardiovascular Signs**: Tachycardia, palpitations, and bounding pulses due to increased cardiac output. - **Respiratory Signs**: Exertional dyspnoea and tachypnoea. - **Neurological Signs**: Dizziness, headache, and vertigo. - **Specific Signs**: Glossitis, angular stomatitis, and koilonychia in chronic iron deficiency; jaundice in haemolytic anaemias.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Evaluations

Laboratory testing confirms the presence of anaemia and identifies its specific root cause. - **Full Blood Count (FBC)**: Evaluates haemoglobin levels, haematocrit, total red blood cell count, and red cell indices like MCV. - **Serum Ferritin**: Measures stored iron; low levels confirm iron deficiency anaemia. - **Serum Iron and Total Iron-Binding Capacity (TIBC)**: Differentiates iron deficiency from anaemia of chronic disease. - **Vitamin B12 and Folate Assays**: Identifies megaloblastic causes. - **Reticulocyte Count**: Assesses the bone marrow's ability to produce new red blood cells, helping distinguish production failure from blood loss or destruction.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical treatment focuses on correcting the underlying cause, replacing deficient nutrients, or restoring blood volume when necessary. For iron deficiency, oral ferrous sulfate is standard, though intravenous iron may be required for malabsorption or severe depletion. Vitamin B12 injections are given for pernicious anaemia, while folic acid supplements address dietary deficiencies. In severe or life-threatening anaemia, packed red blood cell transfusions are administered cautiously to avoid circulatory overload, particularly in elderly patients.

Nursing Care Plan and Interventions

Nursing care for patients with anaemia centers around managing fatigue, preventing injury, and monitoring response to therapy. Nurses schedule care activities with adequate rest periods to conserve energy and reduce myocardial oxygen demand. For patients taking oral iron supplements, patient education includes advising that stools will turn dark green or black, which is a harmless side effect. Safety precautions are instituted for patients experiencing dizziness or orthostatic hypotension.

Complications and Patient Education in Anaemia

Unmanaged chronic anaemia can lead to severe complications, notably high-output heart failure, angina, and impaired cognitive function in older adults. Comprehensive patient education ensures long-term management success. Nurses instruct patients on dietary modifications, such as combining plant-based iron sources with vitamin C to enhance absorption, and avoiding tea or coffee with meals since tannins bind iron and inhibit uptake.
