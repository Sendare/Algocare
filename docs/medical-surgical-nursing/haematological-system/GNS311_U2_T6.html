Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Anaemia

Anaemia is a clinical condition characterized by a reduction in the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, resulting from a decrease in the number of circulating red blood cells, a lowered hemoglobin concentration, or both. Because oxygen is vital for cellular metabolism throughout the body, this deficit triggers compensatory mechanisms. The heart pumps faster and harder to circulate the remaining oxygen-rich blood more frequently, which can eventually lead to cardiac enlargement or failure if the anaemia is severe and prolonged. Meanwhile, the kidneys detect lower oxygen levels and release more erythropoietin to stimulate bone marrow production of new red blood cells. A common misconception among students is that anaemia is a standalone disease; rather, it is always a sign of an underlying medical problem that must be investigated.

Classification and Etiology of Anaemia

Anaemia is classified either by its underlying cause or by the microscopic appearance of the red blood cells, particularly their size and hemoglobin content.

  • Microcytic anaemia: Red blood cells are smaller than normal, frequently caused by iron deficiency or thalassemia.
  • Normocytic anaemia: Red blood cells are normal in size but reduced in number, often seen in acute blood loss or chronic disease.
  • Macrocytic anaemia: Red blood cells are larger than normal, typically resulting from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency.
  • Hemolytic anaemia: Red blood cells are destroyed prematurely due to inherited defects, infections, or immune reactions.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

The clinical picture of anaemia depends heavily on how rapidly it develops and the baseline health of the patient. When anaemia creeps on slowly, the body adapts remarkably well, and symptoms may remain mild until the condition is quite advanced.

  • Pallor: Noticeable paleness of the skin, nail beds, and conjunctiva due to reduced blood flow and low hemoglobin.
  • Fatigue and weakness: The most universal complaints, arising directly from inadequate cellular oxygenation.
  • Exertional dyspnea: Shortness of breath that occurs with mild physical activity as the lungs try to make up for low oxygen levels.
  • Tachycardia and palpitations: A fast or pounding heartbeat as the cardiovascular system works overtime.
  • Dizziness and headache: Reduced oxygen supply to the central nervous system.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Evaluations

Confirming anaemia and identifying its specific type requires a systematic battery of blood tests. The primary investigation is a complete blood count, which measures hemoglobin, hematocrit, and red blood cell counts.

Indices such as mean corpuscular volume tell the nurse and doctor whether cells are microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic. Further tests like serum ferritin, serum iron, total iron-binding capacity, and vitamin levels pinpoint the exact nutritional or systemic deficit. In cases where the bone marrow itself is suspected of failing to produce cells properly, a bone marrow aspiration and biopsy may be performed.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Treating anaemia requires correcting the root cause rather than just raising the hemoglobin number temporarily. If blood loss is the culprit, the source must be surgically or medically stopped. Pharmacological intervention matches the diagnosis: oral or intravenous iron preparations for iron deficiency, vitamin B12 injections for pernicious anaemia, and folic acid supplements for folate deficiency. In severe cases with acute cardiovascular instability, blood transfusions may be ordered to restore oxygen-carrying capacity quickly. Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents are also used for patients with chronic kidney disease whose bone marrow needs an extra chemical push.

Nursing Care Plan and Interventions

Nursing care focuses on conserving energy, monitoring for complications, and promoting comfort. Because fatigue is a major obstacle, the nurse should schedule nursing activities with adequate rest periods in between, grouping tasks to prevent exhausting the patient. Vital signs must be monitored regularly to catch early signs of worsening tissue perfusion or heart strain. For patients receiving blood transfusions, stringent safety checks and close observation during the first fifteen minutes are mandatory to catch transfusion reactions early. Dietary encouragement and collaborating with the dietary department ensure the patient receives iron- and vitamin-rich foods.

Complications and Patient Education

Unmanaged or chronic severe anaemia can lead to serious complications, most notably heart failure due to the long-term strain of pumping faster to supply oxygen. Other risks include increased susceptibility to infections, cognitive sluggishness, and severe fatigue that disrupts daily living. Patient education is a cornerstone of recovery. The nurse must teach patients how to recognize signs of returning anaemia, such as unusual shortness of breath or dizziness. When patients take oral iron, the nurse must explain that stools will turn dark green or black, which is a harmless normal side effect and not internal bleeding, preventing unnecessary panic and non-compliance.
