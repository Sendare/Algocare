Physical Examination in Haematological Disorders

Introduction and General Inspection

Assessing a patient with a suspected blood disorder requires a careful and systematic physical examination. Before touching the patient, take a moment just to observe. General inspection often provides the first vital clues about an underlying blood condition. Look at the patient's overall posture, facial expression, and breathing effort. Notice if the person appears weak, lethargic, or acutely distressed.

Vital signs must be checked thoroughly. Tachycardia and tachypnea frequently point toward severe anemia, as the heart and lungs work harder to deliver oxygen when red blood cell counts are low. A low-grade fever might signal an underlying malignancy like leukemia or an opportunistic infection due to neutropenia. Pay close attention to subtle changes, because systemic blood diseases rarely affect just one body system.

Integumentary System: Skin, Mucous Membranes, and Nails

The skin and mucous membranes act as an open window into the body's hematological health.

  • Pallor: Paleness of the skin, conjunctiva, and nail beds strongly suggests anemia. Always check the inner lower eyelid and the palmar creases for reliable color comparison.
  • Petechiae and Purpura: Tiny, non-blanching red or purple dots on the skin indicate thrombocytopenia, or a low platelet count. Purpura are slightly larger bruise-like patches caused by bleeding under the skin.
  • Jaundice: A yellow tint to the skin and sclera points toward excessive red blood cell destruction, known as hemolysis, which releases high levels of bilirubin.
  • Nail changes: Look closely for koilonychia, or spoon-shaped nails, which are classic signs of long-standing iron deficiency anemia.

Head, Eyes, Ears, Nose, and Throat (HEENT)

Evaluating the HEENT region reveals specific indicators of bleeding tendencies and chronic tissue starvation. Examine the oral cavity carefully. Glossitis, characterized by a smooth, red, and painful tongue, frequently accompanies iron, folate, or vitamin B12 deficiencies.

Inspect the gums for spontaneous bleeding, swelling, or hypertrophy, which are common findings in acute myeloid leukemia due to abnormal white blood cell infiltration. Check the conjunctivae for pallor as noted previously. Epistaxis, or frequent nosebleeds, can be an initial warning sign of clotting disorders, platelet dysfunctions, or severe thrombocytopenia. Always ask patients about recent episodes of bleeding from the gums when brushing their teeth.

Lymph Node Assessment

Systematic palpation of the lymph nodes is a cornerstone of the hematological physical exam. Lymph nodes filter lymphatic fluid and can become enlarged due to infection, immune reaction, or hematological malignancies such as lymphoma and leukemia.

Examine the nodes in a logical downward sequence: pre-auricular, post-auricular, occipital, tonsillar, submandibular, submental, anterior and posterior cervical, supraclavicular, axillary, and inguinal groups. Use the pads of your second and third fingers, moving them in gentle circular motions.

Note the size, shape, consistency, tenderness, and mobility of any palpable nodes. Lymphadenopathy that is hard, fixed, and painless raises high suspicion for malignancy, whereas tender, soft, and mobile nodes usually indicate a reactive inflammatory process.

Cardiovascular and Respiratory Evaluation

The cardiovascular and respiratory systems feel the immediate strain of blood disorders. When anemia reduces the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity, the heart beats faster and harder to maintain perfusion. Auscultation may reveal a systolic flow murmur, particularly at the apex or left sternal border, caused by turbulent blood flow through high-output states.

Assess the lungs for signs of increased work of breathing. Tachypnea and shallow breathing can occur secondary to severe anemia or pulmonary infections in immunocompromised patients. In fluid overload states, such as chronic kidney disease with secondary anemia or acute leukemia undergoing aggressive hydration, you might hear fine crackles at the lung bases.

Abdominal Examination: Hepatomegaly and Splenomegaly

The abdomen holds two major organs heavily involved in blood cell production and destruction: the liver and the spleen. Splenomegaly, or enlargement of the spleen, occurs frequently in hemolytic anemias, leukemias, lymphomas, and portal hypertension. The spleen works overtime destroying abnormal red blood cells or harboring malignant cells.

Begin light palpation in the right and left lower quadrants, working your way upward toward the costal margins. Remember that a normal spleen is often not palpable. If you feel it, note how many centimeters below the left costal margin it extends. Similarly, assess the liver size (hepatomegaly) by measuring its span and noting liver edge consistency. A firm, nodular liver or a massively enlarged spleen requires prompt diagnostic imaging.

Musculoskeletal and Neurological Manifestations

Hematological conditions frequently cause bone and nerve symptoms. Bone pain, particularly in the sternum, ribs, or spine, is a classic feature of multiple myeloma or acute leukemia due to marrow expansion and tumor infiltration.

Neurological assessments are equally critical. Vitamin B12 deficiency leads to subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, presenting as paresthesias (tingling in the hands and feet), loss of vibratory sense, and unsteady gait. Severe anemia can cause dizziness, headaches, and confusion due to cerebral hypoxia. Always test deep tendon reflexes and sensory function when evaluating patients with unexplained fatigue or neurological complaints.
