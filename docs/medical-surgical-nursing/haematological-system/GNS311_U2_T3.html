Physical Examination in Haematological Disorders

General Inspection and Vital Signs

A systematic physical examination for blood disorders begins the moment the patient walks into the consulting room or lies on the bed. General inspection provides immediate clues about tissue oxygenation, nutritional status, and chronic illness. Look closely at the patient's posture, energy levels, and facial expression. Pallor of the conjunctiva, nail beds, and palms often points toward anemia, while a yellowish skin tinge suggests hemolytic processes causing jaundice.

Vital signs fluctuate widely depending on the underlying hematological pathology. Tachycardia and tachypnea frequently develop as compensatory mechanisms when hemoglobin levels drop, forcing the heart and lungs to work harder to deliver oxygen. Fever may signal an underlying infection in patients with neutropenia or malignant conditions like leukemia.

Skin, Hair, and Nail Manifestations

The integumentary system frequently mirrors blood disorders.

  • Petechiae: Tiny, pinpoint, non-blanching red or purple spots caused by capillary bleeding, typically seen in thrombocytopenia.
  • Purpura: Larger purple spots resulting from small vessel bleeding beneath the skin.
  • Ecchymosis: Common bruises that appear spontaneously or from minimal trauma due to clotting factor deficiencies.
  • Koilonychia: Spoon-shaped nails associated with chronic iron deficiency anemia.
  • Alopecia: Hair thinning or loss sometimes linked to nutritional deficiencies or cytotoxic chemotherapy.

Head, Eyes, Ears, Nose, and Throat Examination

Inspection of the head and neck reveals critical indicators of systemic blood health. Examine the conjunctiva carefully; pale pink or white conjunctiva strongly suggests anemia, whereas injected or yellow sclera indicates hemolysis or liver involvement.

Inspect the oral cavity for mucosal bleeding, gingival hyperplasia, and glossitis. Atrophic glossitis, characterized by a smooth, red, and painful tongue, frequently accompanies iron, folate, or vitamin B12 deficiencies. Additionally, check for pharyngeal ulceration, which compromises host defense mechanisms in neutropenic states.

Lymph Node Assessment

Lymphadenopathy is a hallmark finding in numerous hematological malignancies, including lymphomas and leukemias. Palpate the lymph node chains systematically using the pads of your index and middle fingers in a gentle, circular motion.

  1. Submental and Submandibular: Check under the chin and jawline.
  2. Cervical Chains: Palpate anterior and posterior neck regions.
  3. Supraclavicular: Assess carefully, as left supraclavicular enlargement often signals internal malignancy.
  4. Axillary and Inguinal: Examine the armpits and groin for localized or generalized swelling.

Note the size, consistency, tenderness, and mobility of any palpable nodes. Hard, fixed, and painless nodes raise suspicion for malignancy, while tender, soft, and mobile nodes usually point toward an acute inflammatory or infectious process.

Cardiovascular and Respiratory Evaluation

The cardiovascular and respiratory systems bear the brunt of reduced oxygen-carrying capacity in conditions like severe anemia. Auscultation of the heart may reveal a flow murmur due to decreased blood viscosity and increased cardiac output. These systolic murmurs are typically heard best at the apex or left lower sternal border.

Pulmonary assessment focuses on detecting signs of hypoxia, pulmonary infarction, or secondary infection. Tachypnea, basilar crackles, or decreased breath sounds can indicate fluid overload, pulmonary edema, or chest wall complications related to sickle cell vaso-occlusive crises.

Abdominal Examination: Hepatomegaly and Splenomegaly

Abdominal palpation is critical for detecting extramedullary hematopoiesis and reticuloendothelial hyperactivity. The spleen and liver often enlarge when they resume blood cell production or destroy abnormal cells at an accelerated rate.

Begin light palpation in the right lower quadrant, moving upward toward the right costal margin to detect the liver edge. For the spleen, start palpation in the right iliac fossa and move diagonally toward the left upper quadrant, as a massively enlarged spleen grows downward and medially. Percussion of Traube's space helps corroborate splenomegaly when physical palpation remains inconclusive. A palpable spleen below the left costal margin in an adult is generally abnormal and warrants further investigation.

Musculoskeletal and Neurological Manifestations

Musculoskeletal and neurological assessments uncover systemic complications of hematological disorders. Bone pain, particularly over the sternum, ribs, and spine, is a hallmark of multiple myeloma and severe bone marrow expansion in hemolytic states like thalassemia.

Neurological exams are vital when evaluating vitamin B12 deficiency, which causes subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord. Assess for loss of vibration sense, impaired proprioception, unsteady gait, and peripheral neuropathies. Documenting baseline neurological function prevents missed complications during long-term treatment regimens.
