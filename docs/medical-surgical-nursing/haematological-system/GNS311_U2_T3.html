Physical Examination in Haematological Disorders

Introduction and General Inspection

Physical assessment in haematological disorders requires a systematic, head-to-toe approach to uncover subtle tissue changes caused by red cell deficiencies, white cell abnormalities, or bleeding diatheses. Clinicians begin with a general inspection, noting the patient's overall state of health, body habitus, and obvious distress. Vital signs often provide the first objective clue of systemic compromise; for instance, tachycardia and tachypnea frequently accompany severe anaemia as compensatory mechanisms for reduced oxygen-carrying capacity. Low-grade fevers may point toward underlying malignancies like lymphomas or active infections secondary to neutropenia, while orthostatic hypotension suggests significant acute blood loss or chronic volume depletion.

Integumentary System Assessment

The skin and its appendages frequently mirror haematological pathology. Pallor, best observed in the conjunctivae, nail beds, and palmar creases, serves as a classic indicator of anaemia, though skin tone variations can sometimes mask mild changes. Conversely, a ruddy, plethoric complexion points toward polycythaemia. Clinicians must meticulously inspect the integument for primary bleeding manifestations:

  • Petechiae: Tiny, non-blanching, pinpoint red or purple spots caused by capillary hemorrhages, typically signaling severe thrombocytopenia.
  • Purpura: Larger purple lesions resulting from blood extravasation into the skin, often associated with vascular disorders or clotting factor deficiencies.
  • Ecchymoses: Common bruises resulting from minor trauma in individuals with impaired hemostasis.
  • Koilonychia: Spoon-shaped nails classically linked to chronic iron deficiency anaemia.
  • Jaundice: A yellowing of the skin and sclera driven by accelerated red blood cell destruction in haemolytic anemias.

Head, Eyes, Ears, Nose, and Throat Findings

Examination of the head and mucosal surfaces provides critical visual evidence of systemic blood disorders. The conjunctivae should be routinely inspected for pallor, while scleral icterus points to unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia from active hemolysis. The oral cavity demands careful scrutiny: atrophic glossitis, characterized by a smooth, red, and tender tongue, frequently accompanies megaloblastic anemias like vitamin B12 or folate deficiency. Furthermore, spontaneous gingival bleeding, mucosal ulcerations, and petechiae on the hard palate suggest severe bone marrow suppression, such as that seen in acute leukemias or severe aplastic anaemia.

Lymph Node Evaluation

Palpating the superficial lymph node chains is a cornerstone of haematological assessment. Examiners systematically assess the head, neck, supraclavicular, axillary, and inguinal regions using gentle, circular motions with the pads of the index and middle fingers. When documenting lymphadenopathy, note the exact location, size, consistency, tenderness, and whether the nodes are matted or freely mobile. Tender, soft nodes usually point toward acute inflammatory or infectious processes, whereas firm, rubbery, discrete, and painless nodes strongly raise suspicion for lymphomas or chronic lymphocytic leukemia. Fixed, hard nodes may indicate metastatic malignancy, requiring urgent investigation.

Cardiovascular and Respiratory Manifestations

The cardiovascular and respiratory systems bear the heavy burden of compensating for altered blood components. Auscultation of the heart may reveal a hyperdynamic precordium and systolic flow murmurs, particularly over the apex and left sternal border, generated by decreased blood viscosity and increased cardiac output in severe anaemia. In the lungs, tachypnea and shallow breathing patterns may be observed. While the lungs are typically clear to auscultation in uncomplicated anaemia, adventitious sounds like crackles can emerge if the patient develops high-output heart failure or pulmonary infiltrates from infection or leukemic cell stasis.

Abdominal Examination: Hepatomegaly and Splenomegaly

Abdominal palpation is essential for evaluating organs involved in hematopoiesis, filtration, and destruction of blood elements. The spleen and liver often enlarge when called upon for extramedullary hematopoiesis or when clearing abnormal cells. Splenomegaly is commonly graded by how far the tip extends below the left costal margin along the midclavicular line. Causes range from portal hypertension and haemolytic anemias to myeloproliferative neoplasms and infiltrative disorders. Examiners must palpate gently starting in the right and left lower quadrants, moving superiorly to avoid missing a massively enlarged spleen that extends low into the pelvis.

Musculoskeletal and Neurological Signs

Musculoskeletal and neurological examinations help identify specific systemic complications of blood disorders. Bone tenderness, particularly over the sternum, ribs, or tibia, is a classic finding in conditions involving bone marrow expansion or infiltration, such as multiple myeloma or acute leukemias. Neurologically, vitamin B12 deficiency classically presents with subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, manifesting as impaired proprioception, vibratory sense loss, and symmetric paresthesias. In sickle cell disease, vaso-occlusive crises can cause localized bone infarction and severe joint pain without overt signs of acute inflammation.
