Health History in Haematological Disorders

Introduction to Health History in Haematological Nursing

Gathering a comprehensive health history forms the bedrock of clinical evaluation in haematology. Because blood disorders affect multiple organ systems simultaneously due to impaired oxygen delivery, defective clotting, or compromised immunity, a targeted interview reveals clues that objective laboratory tests might miss. The nursing interview requires patience, active listening, and an understanding of normal versus pathological physiology. In many Nigerian healthcare settings, patients may present late in the disease trajectory, making the history-taking process an urgent triage tool to identify life-threatening complications such as severe anaemia or acute vaso-occlusive crises before physical examination even begins.

Biographical Data and Demographic Factors

Demographic data provides foundational context by linking specific blood disorders to age, sex, occupation, and geographic ancestry. Certain conditions show stark epidemiological patterns: - **Age**: Congenital disorders like **haemophilia** and **sickle cell anaemia** present in infancy or early childhood, whereas malignancies like **multiple myeloma** or **chronic lymphocytic leukaemia** predominantly afflict older adults. - **Sex**: X-linked recessive conditions such as **haemophilia A** manifest almost exclusively in males, while females are typically asymptomatic carriers. - **Geography and Ethnicity**: **Sickle cell disease** has a high prevalence among populations of African descent, serving as an evolutionary adaptation against severe malaria, whereas **thalassaemia** is more common in Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and Southeast Asian populations.

Chief Complaint and History of Present Illness

The chief complaint captures the patient's primary reason for seeking care, usually translated into medical terminology during documentation. In blood disorders, patients frequently report overwhelming fatigue, shortness of breath, unexplained bruising, or recurrent infections. The **history of present illness (HPI)** dissects these symptoms using chronological questioning: When did the fatigue start? What triggers the bone pain? How quickly do bruises appear after minor trauma? A common clinical pitfall is dismissing chronic fatigue as simple stress or poor sleep without probing deeper into duration, functional impairment, and associated pallor.

Systemic Review of Symptoms in Blood Disorders

A systematic review of systems uncovers hidden manifestations across physiological boundaries: - **General**: Unexplained weight loss, night sweats, low-grade fevers pointing toward lymphoma or leukaemia. - **Integumentary**: Easy bruising (**ecchymosis**), tiny red pinpoint spots (**petechiae**), jaundice from haemolysis, or pale conjunctivae. - **Neurological**: Headaches, dizziness, tinnitus, or severe paresthesias due to vitamin B12 deficiency or hyperviscosity. - **Gastrointestinal**: Glossitis (smooth, red tongue), painful swallowing, or abdominal fullness reflecting **splenomegaly**. - **Musculoskeletal**: Deep bone pain typical of bone marrow expansion or infiltration.

Past Medical, Surgical, and Family History

Past medical history provides context regarding chronic illnesses that secondary affect blood production, such as chronic kidney disease reducing erythropoietin synthesis. Surgical history reveals risks of abnormal intraoperative or postoperative bleeding, or complications from previous blood transfusions. Family history is paramount in haematology. Many blood dyscrasias are inherited through autosomal recessive, autosomal dominant, or X-linked patterns. Constructing a pedigree chart helps identify carriers and predict risk for future generations. Nurses must inquire about unexplained childhood deaths, recurrent miscarriages in female relatives, or family members requiring frequent blood transfusions.

Medication History, Toxic Exposures, and Lifestyle

Exogenous substances can severely suppress bone marrow function or provoke immune-mediated cytopenias. A meticulous medication history must capture prescription drugs, over-the-counter analgesics, herbal preparations, and traditional remedies. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) can provoke gastrointestinal bleeding, while certain antibiotics and anticonvulsants are linked to **aplastic anaemia** or **agranulocytosis**. Environmental and occupational toxic exposures deserve equal scrutiny. Prolonged contact with industrial solvents like **benzene**, agricultural pesticides, or ionizing radiation significantly increases the risk of acute myeloid leukaemia and myelodysplastic syndromes. Lifestyle factors such as alcohol consumption can directly suppress bone marrow activity and cause macrocytosis independently of nutritional deficiencies.
