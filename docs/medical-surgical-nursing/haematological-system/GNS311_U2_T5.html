Diagnostic Evaluation of Haematological Disorders

Introduction to Haematological Diagnostics

Haematological diagnostics involves investigating blood and blood-forming tissues to diagnose disorders such as anaemia, leukopenia, bleeding disorders, and malignancies like leukemia. Because blood circulates through every organ system, haematological problems often present with wide-ranging, non-specific symptoms like chronic fatigue, bruising, or recurrent infections. A systematic diagnostic approach allows the healthcare team to pinpoint whether a problem originates from bone marrow production, peripheral destruction, or nutritional deficiencies.

Comprehensive Patient History and Physical Examination

A thorough clinical evaluation begins long before any blood is drawn. Nurses and doctors must gather a detailed patient history, paying close attention to dietary habits, occupational exposures to toxins like benzene, family history of inherited blood disorders, and medication use such as anticoagulants or long-term NSAIDs. The physical examination targets specific areas: pallor in the conjunctiva or nail beds suggests anaemia, petechiae or purpura point to platelet dysfunction, and lymphadenopathy or splenomegaly can indicate a lymphoproliferative disorder.

Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Peripheral Blood Smear

The Complete Blood Count (CBC) is the most common laboratory test ordered in clinical practice. It measures red blood cells, white blood cells, haemoglobin, hematocrit, and platelets. Red blood cell indices—such as MCV (mean corpuscular volume)—help classify anaemia as microcytic, normocytic, or macocytic. When a CBC shows abnormalities, a peripheral blood smear allows laboratory personnel to examine blood cells directly under a microscope for unusual shapes, sizes, or inclusion bodies, such as sickled cells or schistocytes.

Bone Marrow Aspiration and Biopsy

  • Bone Marrow Aspiration: Involves withdrawing a small amount of liquid bone marrow fluid using a specialized needle, typically from the posterior iliac crest, to evaluate cellular composition and maturation.
  • Bone Marrow Biopsy: Retrieves a small solid cylinder of bone marrow tissue to assess overall cellularity, architecture, and infiltration by abnormal cells such as lymphoma or metastatic cancer.

Nursing care during these procedures requires patient education, local anaesthetic administration support, strict sterile technique, and post-procedure monitoring of the site for bleeding and infection.

Coagulation and Haemostasis Tests

Assessing how well blood clots is vital for patients with bleeding tendencies or those starting anticoagulant therapy. Key tests include:

  • Prothrombin Time (PT) and INR: Evaluates the extrinsic clotting pathway and is used to monitor warfarin therapy.
  • Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT): Evaluates the intrinsic clotting pathway and monitors heparin therapy.
  • Fibrinogen and D-dimer: Fibrinogen measures clotting protein levels, while D-dimer detects the breakdown products of blood clots, helping rule out conditions like deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism.

Specialized Laboratory and Genetic Testing

When standard tests leave a diagnosis unclear, advanced diagnostics provide molecular clarity. Flow cytometry analyses cell surface markers to classify different types of leukemias and lymphomas. Cytogenetic analysis, including karyotyping and fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), detects chromosomal abnormalities like the Philadelphia chromosome in chronic myeloid leukemia. Molecular tests such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) identify specific gene mutations that drive haematological malignancies and guide targeted therapies.

Radiological and Imaging Studies

Imaging studies complement laboratory findings by showing structural involvement in lymph nodes, spleen, and bone. Computed tomography (CT) scans of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis stage lymphomas by identifying enlarged lymph nodes or organomegaly. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides detailed views of bone marrow infiltration in conditions like multiple myeloma. Positron emission tomography (PET) scans highlight metabolically active cancer tissues, helping clinicians differentiate between scar tissue and active tumor growth.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Care in Diagnostic Evaluations

Nurses play a vital role across all haematological diagnostic procedures. Beyond preparing patients physically and psychologically, nurses ensure informed consent is signed, monitor vital signs during invasive tests, and manage post-procedure recovery. Because diagnostic workups for blood disorders often involve frightening possibilities like cancer or chronic illness, providing empathetic communication and clear explanations helps reduce anxiety. Nurses must also promptly report critical lab values—such as severely low platelets or dangerously low neutrophils—to the primary provider.
