Diagnostic Evaluation of Haematological Disorders

Introduction to Haematological Diagnostics

Haematological diagnostics encompass a broad spectrum of investigations aimed at identifying disorders of the blood, bone marrow, and lymphatic systems. These evaluations range from simple automated cell counts to complex molecular assays. In clinical practice across Nigeria, where conditions like sickle cell anaemia and various haemoglobinopathies are prevalent, timely and accurate diagnostic workups are essential for directing appropriate management and improving patient outcomes.

Comprehensive Patient History and Physical Examination

A thorough clinical assessment serves as the foundation for uncovering blood disorders. Clinicians must actively probe for symptoms of chronic fatigue, unexplained bruising, recurrent infections, or bone pain. Physical examination requires systematic inspection: - **Pallor**: Evaluated in the conjunctivae, nail beds, and palms to suggest anaemia. - **Lymphadenopathy**: Palpation of cervical, axillary, and inguinal nodes points toward lymphoproliferative disorders. - **Splenomegaly**: Enlargement of the spleen indicates excessive red blood cell destruction or filtration demands. - **Jaundice**: Mild yellowing of the sclera signifies accelerated haemolysis.

Complete Blood Count (CBC) and Peripheral Blood Smear

The Complete Blood Count is the most frequently ordered screening tool in clinical haematology, measuring red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. Indices such as Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV) help classify anaemias into microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic categories. When automated CBC results flag abnormalities, a peripheral blood smear is examined under a microscope. This allows laboratory personnel to directly visualize cellular morphology. For instance, sickle cells, target cells, and schistocytes provide immediate clues regarding haemolytic processes or hemoglobinopathies that automated counters might miss.

Bone Marrow Aspiration and Biopsy Procedures

When peripheral blood tests fail to yield a definitive diagnosis or when malignancies like leukaemia or multiple myeloma are suspected, bone marrow examination becomes necessary. The posterior superior iliac spine is the preferred site in adults. 1. **Aspiration**: Extracts liquid marrow elements for cytological examination and cellular counting. 2. **Biopsy**: Obtains a small, intact core of solid bone marrow tissue to evaluate overall architecture and cellularity. Adequate local anaesthesia and clear communication are vital, as patients frequently experience significant anxiety regarding procedural pain.

Coagulation and Haemostasis Tests

Evaluating the blood's ability to clot properly prevents unexpected haemorrhage or thrombotic events. Key screening investigations include: | Test | Evaluates Pathway | Common Clinical Use | |---|---|---| | Prothrombin Time (PT / INR) | Extrinsic and common | Monitoring warfarin therapy, liver function | | Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT) | Intrinsic and common | Monitoring heparin therapy, haemophilia | | Bleeding Time / Platelet Function | Primary haemostasis | Assessing platelet plug formation | | Fibrinogen Assay | Terminal clotting phase | Detecting consumptive coagulopathies |

Specialized Laboratory and Genetic Testing in Haematology

Advanced diagnostics move beyond standard cell counts to pinpoint precise molecular defects. Haemoglobin electrophoresis separates various forms of haemoglobin, proving indispensable for confirming sickle cell disease, thalassaemias, and other structural haemoglobin variants. Flow cytometry analyses cell surface markers to classify leukemias and lymphomas with high precision. Cytogenetic studies, including fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR), detect specific chromosomal translocations and gene mutations that drive malignant haematopoietic clones.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Care in Diagnostic Evaluations

Nursing care during haematological diagnostics centers on patient preparation, procedural safety, and psychological support. Before invasive procedures like bone marrow biopsy, nurses verify informed consent, assess baseline vital signs, and administer prescribed mild sedatives. Post-procedural nursing actions focus on continuous site monitoring for bleeding or haematoma formation, particularly in patients with underlying coagulopathies. Additionally, nurses provide clear discharge instructions regarding wound care and warning signs that require immediate medical attention.
