Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Sickle Cell Anaemia

Sickle cell anaemia is a genetic blood disorder characterized by the inheritance of abnormal hemoglobin, known as hemoglobin S (HbS), from both parents. Under conditions of low oxygen tension, dehydration, or acidosis, this abnormal hemoglobin polymerizes and causes the normally pliable red blood cells to deform into a rigid, crescent or 'sickle' shape. These sickled cells have increased adhesiveness, leading to microvascular occlusion, tissue ischemia, and premature destruction of the red blood cells, which results in chronic hemolytic anaemia. Nursing students in Nigeria must understand that this autosomal recessive condition is highly prevalent in sub-Saharan Africa, making it a critical public health issue. The hallmark of the pathophysiology is vaso-occlusion, where abnormally shaped cells block small blood vessels, causing severe pain and multiorgan damage over time. Furthermore, the shortened lifespan of these red blood cells (typically 10 to 20 days compared to the normal 120 days) places immense strain on the bone marrow, triggering excessive erythropoiesis and clinical manifestations such as jaundice from increased bilirubin production. Recognizing these foundational mechanisms is vital for anticipating complications and providing targeted, evidence-based nursing care to affected individuals across various healthcare settings.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Presentation

The clinical manifestations of sickle cell anaemia are diverse and stem primarily from chronic haemolysis and recurring episodes of vaso-occlusion. Patients frequently present with chronic, mild to moderate fatigue and pallor due to baseline anaemia, alongside intermittent jaundice caused by elevated unconjugated bilirubin from red blood cell breakdown. One of the most characteristic presentations in infants is dactylitis, also known as hand-foot syndrome, which presents as painful symmetrical swelling of the hands and feet caused by vaso-occlusion in the metacarpal and metatarsal bones. As children grow older, they experience painful crises affecting the back, chest, abdomen, and extremities. Splenic sequestration crisis is another severe manifestation, characterized by sudden pooling of blood within the spleen, leading to acute hypovolemic shock, severe anaemia, and rapid enlargement of the spleen. Nurses must also be vigilant for signs of acute chest syndrome, characterized by chest pain, fever, and pulmonary infiltrates, which is a leading cause of mortality in these patients. Chronic organ damage can additionally manifest as leg ulcers, avascular necrosis of the femoral head, and susceptibility to encapsulated bacterial infections due to functional asplenia.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Accurate diagnostic evaluation and screening are crucial for early identification and management of sickle cell anaemia. In many endemic regions like Nigeria, newborn screening programs utilize high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), isoelectric focusing, or hemoglobin electrophoresis to detect abnormal hemoglobin variants shortly after birth. Hemoglobin electrophoresis remains a definitive diagnostic test, as it separates different types of hemoglobin based on their electrical charge, clearly identifying the presence of HbS, HbA, HbF, and HbC. Solubility testing, such as the dithionite solubility test, can quickly screen for the presence of hemoglobin S, though it cannot distinguish between sickle cell trait and sickle cell disease, requiring electrophoresis for confirmation. Prenatal diagnosis can also be performed using chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis for families with a known history of the condition. In clinical practice, routine complete blood counts (CBC) typically reveal low hemoglobin levels, elevated reticulocyte counts indicating active red blood cell production, and sometimes presence of target cells on peripheral blood smears. Early diagnosis allows healthcare providers to initiate prophylactic measures, such as penicillin prophylaxis and immunizations, significantly reducing childhood morbidity and mortality.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring in Sickle Cell Patients

Comprehensive nursing assessment and continuous monitoring are essential pillars in the inpatient and outpatient care of patients with sickle cell anaemia. Nurses must perform systematic head-to-toe assessments, paying close attention to pain scores, respiratory status, hydration levels, and neurological function. Baseline vital signs should be established and monitored frequently to detect early signs of complications such as infection, acute chest syndrome, or vaso-occlusive crises. Skin integrity must be evaluated regularly, particularly over bony prominences and the lower extremities, to identify early signs of chronic leg ulcers or ischemic injury. Nurses should monitor fluid balance meticulously, ensuring adequate hydration to prevent blood hyperviscosity and subsequent sickling. Neurological assessments are equally critical because silent or overt strokes are common neurological complications; nurses should screen for any acute focal deficits, changes in level of consciousness, or cognitive shifts. Additionally, psychosocial assessment is vital, as chronic pain and frequent hospitalizations significantly impact the mental health, educational attainment, and overall quality of life of the patient and their family. Documenting and communicating these assessment findings promptly to the interprofessional healthcare team ensures timely interventions and improves patient outcomes.

Management of Vaso-Occlusive Crises and Acute Pain

The management of vaso-occlusive crises (VOC) and acute pain is a primary responsibility for nurses caring for sickle cell patients. A vaso-occlusive crisis occurs when sickled red blood cells block capillary blood flow, causing severe localized ischemic pain. Nursing management begins with prompt and aggressive pain assessment using validated pain scales appropriate for the patient's developmental age. Analgesic administration should follow the WHO analgesic ladder, starting with non-opioids for mild pain and rapidly escalating to potent opioids (such as morphine, fentanyl, or pethidine) administered via patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) or scheduled IV doses for severe pain. Alongside pharmacotherapy, supportive measures such as intravenous fluid hydration (with hypotonic or isotonic solutions as prescribed) and supplemental oxygen therapy (if oxygen saturation drops below baseline) are critical to reverse hypoxia and reduce sickling. Nurses must also identify and treat precipitating factors, such as infections, exposure to extreme cold, physical exhaustion, or emotional stress. Monitoring for opioid-induced side effects, such as respiratory depression, sedation, and constipation, ensures patient safety during acute crisis management.

Pharmacological Interventions and Blood Transfusion Therapy

Pharmacological interventions and blood transfusion therapy play a central role in both the prevention and acute management of sickle cell anaemia complications. Hydroxyurea is a cornerstone disease-modifying medication; it works by increasing the production of fetal hemoglobin (HbF), which does not sickle easily, thereby reducing the frequency of painful crises and acute chest syndrome. Nurses must educate patients on hydroxyurea adherence while monitoring for potential bone marrow suppression, such as leukopenia and thrombocytopenia. Blood transfusion therapy, including simple transfusions and exchange transfusions, is utilized to rapidly increase hemoglobin levels, improve tissue oxygenation, and lower the proportion of circulating HbS cells. Transfusions are vital in emergencies such as severe acute anaemia, splenic sequestration, stroke prevention, and preparation for major surgeries. However, chronic blood transfusions carry significant risks, notably iron overload (hemosiderosis), which can damage organs such as the liver, heart, and endocrine glands. To mitigate iron overload, nurses must administer iron-chelating agents (such as deferasirox or desferrioxamine) as prescribed and monitor laboratory markers of iron storage, including serum ferritin levels.

Prevention and Recognition of Complications

Prevention and prompt recognition of complications are vital for improving the long-term survival and quality of life of individuals living with sickle cell anaemia. Due to functional asplenia caused by repetitive infarction and fibrosis of the spleen, patients are at extreme risk of severe infections from encapsulated organisms such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae. Consequently, prevention strategies include daily prophylactic penicillin therapy during childhood and strict adherence to recommended immunization schedules, including pneumococcal, meningococcal, and annual influenza vaccines. Nurses must educate patients and caregivers to recognize early signs of infection, such as fever, lethargy, or respiratory distress, and to seek immediate medical attention because infections can rapidly precipitate life-threatening crises. Other complications to monitor and prevent include acute chest syndrome, stroke, priapism, and chronic kidney disease. Routine transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography screening in children helps identify those at high risk for stroke, allowing for prophylactic chronic transfusion programs. Early intervention and vigilant preventative care drastically reduce morbidity and mortality rates associated with this chronic condition.

Patient Education, Lifestyle Modification, and Supportive Care

Patient education, lifestyle modification, and supportive care are indispensable components of comprehensive sickle cell disease management. Nurses play a frontline role in empowering patients and their families with knowledge to self-manage the condition and minimize trigger factors. Key lifestyle modifications include maintaining adequate oral hydration, avoiding extremes of temperature (such as very cold weather or overheated environments), preventing physical exhaustion, and avoiding tight clothing that could impede peripheral circulation. Nutritional counseling is also important, emphasizing a balanced diet rich in folic acid to support accelerated erythropoiesis, alongside adequate caloric intake. Genetic counseling should be provided to adolescents and adults with sickle cell trait or disease to help them make informed reproductive choices. Furthermore, comprehensive supportive care involves addressing psychosocial needs, connecting families with patient support groups, and advocating for accommodations in school and workplaces. By fostering strong therapeutic relationships and promoting self-efficacy, nurses help patients lead active, fulfilling lives while effectively managing their chronic condition.
