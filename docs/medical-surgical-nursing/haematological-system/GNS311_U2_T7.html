Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Sickle Cell Anaemia

Sickle cell anaemia is an inherited blood disorder where the body makes abnormal haemoglobin, known as haemoglobin S. Healthy red blood cells are round and flexible, moving easily through narrow blood vessels. When a person has sickle cell anaemia, deoxygenated haemoglobin S molecules stick together and form stiff rods. This changes the flexible disc into a crescent or sickle shape.

These rigid, sticky cells cause major problems in circulation:

  • Vaso-occlusion: Sickled cells clump together and block small blood vessels, stopping blood flow and oxygen delivery to tissues.
  • Haemolysis: Because these cells are fragile, the spleen destroys them much faster than normal, leading to chronic anaemia.

A common clinical misconception is that crisis pain only happens during physical exertion. In reality, blockages can be triggered by mild stress, dehydration, or temperature changes even at rest.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Presentation

The clinical picture of sickle cell anaemia varies widely, but chronic fatigue is almost always present due to baseline anaemia. When red blood cells break down rapidly, excess bilirubin enters the bloodstream, resulting in jaundice of the skin and sclera.

Children often present with dactylitis, also known as hand-foot syndrome, which is painful swelling of the hands and feet caused by blocked blood flow in the small bones. As patients grow older, repeated infarctions damage major organs. The spleen becomes scarred and stops working properly over time, leaving the individual vulnerable to severe bacterial infections. Leg ulcers, delayed growth, and chronic bone pain are also frequent features of long-term disease progression.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Detecting sickle cell anaemia early prevents life-threatening complications. In many healthcare systems, routine newborn screening identifies the condition within the first few days of life using a heel-stick blood sample.

The definitive test used to diagnose the specific type of sickle cell disorder is haemoglobin electrophoresis. This laboratory method separates different types of haemoglobin based on their electrical charge and movement through a gel medium. High-performance liquid chromatography is another modern technique used to measure exact percentages of haemoglobin variants. A complete blood count typically shows low haemoglobin and haematocrit levels, along with elevated reticulocyte counts as the bone marrow tries to replace destroyed red cells.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Nurses play a frontline role in tracking baseline changes and spotting early signs of trouble in sickle cell patients. Regular physical assessments must focus on pain levels, respiratory status, and neurological function.

  • Pain Assessment: Use age-appropriate pain scales frequently, noting the exact location and intensity of discomfort.
  • Respiratory Checks: Monitor oxygen saturation and listen to breath sounds. Sudden chest pain, fever, and a cough can indicate acute chest syndrome, a medical emergency.
  • Neurological Checks: Watch for sudden weakness, slurred speech, or confusion, which signal a potential stroke caused by narrowed cerebral vessels.

Management of Vaso-Occlusive Crises and Acute Pain

A vaso-occlusive crisis occurs when sickled cells jam small blood vessels, cutting off oxygen and causing intense, throbbing pain in the bones, joints, or abdomen. Immediate hospital management follows a clear protocol centered around hydration, oxygenation, and pain relief.

Intravenous or oral fluid therapy is critical to flush out the microvasculature and reduce blood thickness, but nurses must monitor carefully to prevent fluid overload. Supplemental oxygen is given only if the oxygen saturation drops below normal levels. Pain management relies on scheduled opioid analgesics rather than waiting for pain to peak, sometimes combined with non-opioid adjuncts. Heat packs can soothe tense muscles, but cold compresses must be avoided because cold temperatures promote further sickling.

Pharmacological Interventions and Blood Transfusion Therapy

Long-term medical management aims to reduce the frequency of painful crises and prevent organ damage. Hydroxyurea is a primary medication used for this purpose. It stimulates the production of foetal haemoglobin, which does not sickle easily and helps protect red blood cells from changing shape.

Blood transfusions are another cornerstone of therapy. Simple transfusions raise the total haemoglobin count during severe anaemia or acute chest syndrome. Exchange transfusions are used to remove blood containing high levels of haemoglobin S and replace it with healthy donor blood, lowering the risk of stroke in high-risk children. A major risk of repeated transfusions is iron overload, which requires medication called chelation therapy to pull excess iron out of organs.

Prevention and Recognition of Complications

Preventing complications is just as important as treating acute episodes. Because asplenia leaves patients vulnerable to encapsulated organisms like Streptococcus pneumoniae, routine immunisations and daily penicillin prophylaxis are mandatory throughout early childhood.

Patients and families must learn to recognize triggers that spark a crisis. Infection, physical or emotional stress, dehydration, and exposure to sudden cold weather are common culprits. Education on hand hygiene, avoiding extreme temperatures, and seeking prompt care for any fever helps avert life-threatening emergencies like overwhelming sepsis or splenic sequestration.

Patient Education, Lifestyle Modification, and Supportive Care

Empowering patients and their families with practical knowledge improves daily quality of life and reduces hospital readmissions. Lifestyle modifications focus on maintaining good hydration, wearing warm layers during cold weather, and avoiding high-altitude travel where oxygen levels are low.

Nurses should guide families on balancing rest with moderate activity, avoiding extreme physical exhaustion that can trigger a crisis. Psychosocial support is equally vital, as living with chronic pain and frequent hospital stays can lead to anxiety and depression. Connecting families with patient advocacy groups and counseling services ensures holistic, compassionate care.
