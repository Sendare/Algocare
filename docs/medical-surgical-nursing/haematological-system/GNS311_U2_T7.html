Sickle Cell Anaemia

Introduction and Pathophysiology

Sickle cell anaemia is a genetic blood disorder inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern. It arises from a single nucleotide substitution in the beta-globin gene, where valine replaces glutamic acid at the sixth position. This structural mutation creates abnormal haemoglobin, known as haemoglobin S (HbS).

Under conditions of low oxygen tension, dehydration, or acidosis, HbS molecules polymerize. This polymerization deforms the flexible, biconcave red blood cells into rigid, crescent or sickle shapes. These distorted cells cannot easily navigate narrow microvasculature, leading to two primary pathological consequences:

  • Vaso-occlusion: Sickled cells clump together, obstructing blood flow and causing localized tissue ischemia and infarction.
  • Chronic haemolysis: The rigid membranes of HbS cells make them fragile, resulting in premature destruction by the spleen and a shortened erythrocyte lifespan of roughly 10 to 20 days.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Presentation

The clinical picture of sickle cell anaemia varies widely, fluctuating between baseline chronic illness and acute, life-threatening episodes. Chronic haemolytic anaemia causes persistent baseline fatigue, pallor, and mild jaundice due to elevated unconjugated bilirubin from red cell breakdown. Growth retardation and delayed puberty are also common in adolescents due to chronic tissue hypoxia.

Acute presentations are dominated by painful crises, but systemic organ involvement creates a diverse array of symptoms:

  1. Dactylitis: Hand-foot syndrome characterized by painful symmetrical swelling of the hands and feet, frequently seen as an early sign in infants.
  2. Acute Chest Syndrome: A medical emergency presenting with fever, chest pain, tachypnoea, and new pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography.
  3. Splenic Sequestration: Sudden pooling of large volumes of blood within the spleen, causing rapid enlargement, profound hypovolaemic shock, and severe anaemia.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Early identification of sickle cell anaemia improves long-term survival and reduces childhood mortality. Universal newborn screening programs utilize high-performance liquid chromatography or isoelectric focusing to detect variant haemoglobins from cord blood samples before clinical symptoms emerge.

When evaluating older children or adults, or confirming a screening result, specific laboratory investigations are essential:

The solubility test cannot distinguish between sickle cell trait and sickle cell disease, making electrophoresis the definitive diagnostic standard.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing assessment forms the backbone of safe inpatient care for individuals living with sickle cell anaemia. Establishing a reliable baseline during pain-free intervals allows for prompt recognition of subtle deviations. Nurses must routinely evaluate pain intensity using age-appropriate scales, documenting exact physical locations, radiating patterns, and associated triggers.

Cardiorespiratory monitoring is equally critical. Baseline oxygen saturation may chronically hover between 92% and 95% due to the right shift of the oxygen-haemoglobin dissociation curve. However, any sudden drop warrants immediate investigation for acute chest syndrome or pulmonary infection. Palpation of the abdomen requires checking for left and right upper quadrant tenderness, monitoring liver span, and assessing splenic size to catch sequestration early. Neurological checks form part of routine shifts, scanning for subtle signs of silent cerebral infarcts or overt stroke.

Management of Vaso-Occlusive Crises and Acute Pain

The vaso-occlusive crisis is the hallmark acute complication of sickle cell anaemia, triggered by cold exposure, dehydration, infection, stress, or hypoxia. Management requires a rapid, protocol-driven approach centered on hydration, oxygenation, and aggressive analgesia.

Intravenous hydration with hypotonic or isotonic fluids helps reduce blood viscosity and encourages renal clearance of breakdown products, provided fluid overload is carefully avoided. Supplemental oxygen is administered strictly if pulse oximetry falls below the patient's baseline or drops under 92%. Analgesia follows the WHO pain relief ladder, utilizing scheduled NSAIDs for mild pain and potent opioids like morphine or pethidine for severe crises. Patient-controlled analgesia pumps are frequently employed for rapid dose titration. Warm compresses provide comforting local relief, but cold packs must be strictly avoided because chilling causes further vasoconstriction and sickling.

Pharmacological Interventions and Blood Transfusion

Long-term pharmacological management aims to reduce the frequency of painful crises and prevent cumulative end-organ damage. Hydroxyurea is the primary disease-modifying therapy. It works by stimulating production of foetal haemoglobin (HbF), which does not participate in sickling, thereby diluting the concentration of HbS and reducing red cell adhesion to vascular endothelium.

Blood transfusions play a dual role in managing sickle cell anaemia:

  • Simple transfusions: Used during acute anaemia, aplastic crises, or splenic sequestration to rapidly boost oxygen-carrying capacity.
  • Exchange transfusions: Used for severe complications like stroke or acute chest syndrome, removing HbS-laden cells while replacing them with normal donor red blood cells.

A major long-term risk of repeated transfusions is secondary haemochromatosis, requiring concurrent iron chelation therapy with agents like deferasirox to prevent cardiac and hepatic toxicity.

Prevention and Recognition of Complications

Preventing infections is paramount because fever and sepsis are major triggers for acute crises and remain leading causes of mortality in young children with sickle cell anaemia. Functional asplenism develops early in life due to repeated micro-infarctions, rendering patients highly susceptible to encapsulated organisms like *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Haemophilus influenzae*.

Prophylactic measures include daily oral penicillin V from infancy through at least age five, alongside strict adherence to routine childhood immunisations and annual influenza vaccines. Parents and patients must be taught to recognize early warning signs of complications, including fever above 38.5°C, sudden pallor, severe headache, visual disturbances, or sudden weakness. Prompt medical evaluation of a febrile child with sickle cell disease is mandatory to prevent overwhelming sepsis.

Patient Education, Lifestyle Modification, and Supportive Care

Empowering patients and families with practical self-management strategies improves quality of life and reduces hospital admissions. Education focuses on identifying and avoiding individual crisis triggers. Hydration targets must be individualized, emphasizing increased fluid intake during hot weather, physical exertion, or air travel.

Lifestyle guidance includes avoiding extreme temperature fluctuations, such as swimming in cold water or wearing inadequate clothing in cold air-conditioned rooms. Patients should also avoid high-altitude environments where atmospheric oxygen tension is low, unless supplemental oxygen is available. Folic acid supplementation is routinely prescribed to support the high rate of erythropoiesis. Psychosocial support is equally vital, as chronic pain and frequent hospitalisations place a heavy emotional burden on patients and their caregivers.
