Anatomy and Physiology of Blood and Blood Cells

Introduction to the Haematological System and Blood Composition

The haematological system is a vital transport network that permeates the entire human body, consisting of blood, blood-forming organs, and the lymphatic system. In a healthy adult, total blood volume averages approximately 5 to 6 liters in males and 4 to 5 liters in females, accounting for roughly 7 to 8 percent of total body weight. Blood is classified as a specialized fluid connective tissue composed of two main portions: a liquid extracellular matrix known as plasma, and formed elements, which are the cellular components. When a sample of blood is centrifuged, it separates into distinct layers based on density. The top layer comprises straw-colored plasma, making up about 55 percent of total blood volume. A thin, whitish middle layer called the buffy coat sits between the plasma and the packed red blood cells; this layer contains leukocytes (white blood cells) and thrombocytes (platelets), accounting for less than 1 percent of blood volume. The bottom layer consists of erythrocytes (red blood cells), which pack down to form roughly 45 percent of the blood volume, a measurement clinically referred to as the hematocrit. Understanding this basic composition is foundational for nursing students, as alterations in these proportions often signify pathological states such as anemia, polycythemia, infections, or hematological malignancies.

Physiological Functions of Blood in the Human Body

Blood performs multifaceted physiological functions essential for maintaining human homeostasis, broadly categorized into transport, regulation, and protection. As a transport medium, blood carries oxygen absorbed from the lungs to peripheral tissues and transports carbon dioxide waste away from cells back to the respiratory system for elimination. It also distributes nutrient molecules absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, metabolic waste products destined for renal excretion via the kidneys, and endocrine hormones secreted by ductless glands to their respective target organs. In its regulatory role, blood helps maintain optimal body temperature by absorbing and distributing heat generated by metabolically active skeletal muscles to the skin and lungs for dissipation. Furthermore, blood buffers maintain stable pH levels within body fluids by neutralizing metabolic acids and bases, and they regulate cellular fluid balance through osmotic and hydrostatic pressures. For protection, blood contains specialized defense mechanisms to guard against infection and hemorrhage. Leukocytes combat invading pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, and fungi through phagocytosis and immune responses, while circulating clotting factors and thrombocytes swiftly arrest bleeding through coagulation cascades whenever vascular integrity is compromised. For nursing practice, recognizing these vital roles aids in assessing clinical signs when a patient experiences hemorrhage, hypoxia, or systemic infection.

Plasma: Composition, Proteins, and Fluid Balance

Plasma is the complex, straw-colored liquid medium of blood, consisting of approximately 90 to 92 percent water, which serves as an effective solvent for a vast array of dissolved solutes. The remaining 8 to 10 percent of plasma is composed of plasma proteins, electrolytes, dissolved gases, nutrients, enzymes, hormones, and waste products. Plasma proteins are synthesized primarily by the liver (with the exception of gamma globulins, which are produced by plasma cells) and are categorized into three major groups: albumins, globulins, and fibrinogen. Albumins are the most abundant plasma proteins and play a critical role in maintaining colloid osmotic pressure, preventing excessive fluid leakage from capillaries into interstitial spaces. Globulins are further subdivided into alpha, beta, and gamma types; alpha and beta globulins transport lipids, iron, and fat-soluble vitamins, while gamma globulins function as antibodies (immunoglobulins) integral to adaptive immunity. Fibrinogen is an essential soluble clotting factor that is converted into insoluble fibrin strands during the coagulation cascade. Electrolytes such as sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride, and bicarbonate maintain proper pH, membrane polarization, and osmotic balance. Changes in plasma protein levels, particularly albumin, can lead to severe clinical manifestations such as generalized edema, highlighting the importance of understanding plasma composition for nursing assessments.

Erythrocytes (Red Blood Cells): Structure, Function, and Erythropoiesis

Erythrocytes, commonly known as red blood cells (RBCs), are specialized, highly differentiated cells whose primary function is the transport of oxygen from the respiratory surfaces to systemic tissues and the assistance of carbon dioxide transport. Structurally, mature human erythrocytes are biconcave discs, a shape that provides a high surface-area-to-volume ratio facilitating rapid gas exchange and enabling them to deform easily when squeezing through narrow capillaries. During their maturation in red bone marrow, mammalian erythrocytes extrude their nuclei and other organelles, leaving more internal space to pack millions of molecules of hemoglobin, the iron-containing protein responsible for binding oxygen. Erythropoiesis, the continuous process of red blood cell production, is regulated primarily by the hormone erythropoietin (EPO), which is synthesized and secreted by the kidneys in response to tissue hypoxia or low oxygen tension. The process begins with pluripotent hematopoietic stem cells differentiating through various progenitor stages, eventually forming reticulocytes before entering the bloodstream as mature erythrocytes. The normal lifespan of a circulating erythrocyte is approximately 120 days, after which aged and fragile cells are phagocytized and recycled by macrophages in the spleen and liver. Nurses must understand erythrocyte physiology because deficiencies in production, abnormal destruction, or hemoglobin abnormalities lead to clinical conditions such as iron-deficiency anemia or sickle cell disease.

Leukocytes (White Blood Cells): Types, Immunity, and Defense Mechanisms

Leukocytes, or white blood cells (WBCs), are nucleated cells that form a vital component of the body's immune system, defending against invading microorganisms, parasites, toxins, and abnormal cells. Unlike erythrocytes, leukocytes utilize the bloodstream primarily as a transport highway to reach sites of infection or tissue injury, actively migrating through capillary walls via a process called diapedesis and navigating tissues via amoeboid movement and chemotaxis. Leukocytes are broadly classified into two categories based on the presence or absence of visible cytoplasmic granules when stained: granulocytes and agranulocytes. Granulocytes include neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils. Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cells and serve as rapid-response phagocytes specialized in fighting bacterial infections. Eosinophils combat parasitic infections and play a major role in allergic inflammatory reactions. Basophils release histamine and heparin to promote inflammation and prevent inappropriate clotting in injured tissues. Agranulocytes consist of lymphocytes and monocytes. Lymphocytes, which include T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells, drive adaptive immunity by producing targeted antibodies and destroying virus-infected or cancerous cells. Monocytes migrate into tissues and transform into large phagocytic macrophages that engulf cellular debris and foreign antigens. Nurses monitor leukocyte counts closely through full blood count laboratory investigations to detect acute infections, inflammation, or immunosuppression.

Thrombocytes (Platelets) and the Haemostasis Process

Thrombocytes, commonly known as platelets, are not complete cells but rather small, anucleated cytoplasmic fragments shed from massive bone marrow precursor cells called megakaryocytes. Although small in size, platelets play a critical role in hemostasis, which is the physiological process that halts bleeding and repairs vascular injury while maintaining blood in a fluid state within undamaged vessels. Haemostasis occurs in three sequential, overlapping stages: vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and blood coagulation (clotting). When a blood vessel is severed or damaged, the immediate local response is vascular spasm, wherein smooth muscle in the vessel wall contracts strongly to instantly minimize blood loss. Next is platelet plug formation; exposed subendothelial collagen fibers cause circulating platelets to adhere, become activated, and release chemical mediators such as adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and thromboxane A2, which recruit additional platelets to form a temporary plug at the injury site. The third stage, blood coagulation, involves a complex cascade of enzymatic reactions involving multiple plasma clotting factors (numbered I through XIII), culminating in the conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads. These fibrin threads reinforce the platelet plug to form a stable, durable blood clot. Following tissue repair, the clot undergoes fibrinolysis, a process where plasmin breaks down the fibrin meshwork to restore normal blood flow. Nurses must master these mechanisms to manage patients receiving anticoagulant therapy or those suffering from bleeding disorders.

Blood Grouping Systems: ABO and Rhesus Compatibility

Blood grouping systems are vital classifications based on the presence or absence of specific inherited antigenic glycoproteins and glycolipids on the surface of erythrocyte membranes. The two most clinically significant systems used in transfusion medicine and nursing practice are the ABO system and the Rhesus (Rh) system. The ABO system categorizes blood into four main types—A, B, AB, and O—determined by the presence of antigen A, antigen B, both, or neither on red blood cells, respectively. Plasma naturally contains pre-formed antibodies (agglutinins) against the antigens absent from an individual's own red blood cells (e.g., Type A blood possesses anti-B antibodies). Mixing incompatible blood types causes agglutination (clumping) and subsequent hemolysis, which can lead to fatal transfusion reactions. The Rhesus system is based primarily on the presence or absence of the D antigen (Rh factor). Individuals with the D antigen are designated Rh-positive, while those lacking it are Rh-negative. Unlike ABO antibodies, anti-Rh antibodies are not naturally present in the plasma of Rh-negative individuals; instead, they develop only after prior sensitization through exposure to Rh-positive blood, such as during an incompatible transfusion or across pregnancies involving an Rh-negative mother and an Rh-positive fetus (which can cause hemolytic disease of the newborn). Nurses play a pivotal role in verifying patient blood types, checking crossmatching results, and preventing transfusion errors.
