Anatomy and Physiology of Blood and Blood Cells

Introduction to Blood Composition and General Properties

Blood is a specialized fluid connective tissue that circulates continuously through the heart, arteries, capillaries, and veins, transporting vital substances to every living cell and removing metabolic waste products. An average healthy adult human body contains roughly 5 liters of blood, which accounts for about 7 to 8 percent of total body weight. Whole blood consists of two main portions: a fluid extracellular matrix called plasma, which makes up about 55 percent of blood volume, and formed elements, which comprise the remaining 45 percent.

  • Erythrocytes: Red blood cells that form the vast majority of formed elements and carry oxygen.
  • Leukocytes: White blood cells responsible for immune defense against infections.
  • Thrombocytes: Platelets that play a vital role in blood clotting and preventing excessive bleeding.

The ratio of red blood cells to total blood volume is known as the hematocrit. When blood is spun down in a centrifuge, the heavier red blood cells pack down at the bottom, a thin whitish layer containing white blood cells and platelets called the buffy coat sits in the middle, and the straw-colored plasma remains at the top.

Core Physiological Functions of Blood

Blood performs three broad categories of physiological duties essential for human survival: transportation, regulation, and protection.

Transportation involves moving oxygen from the lungs and nutrients from the digestive tract to body tissues, while simultaneously carrying metabolic wastes like carbon dioxide to the lungs and urea to the kidneys for elimination. Hormones synthesized by endocrine glands also travel through the bloodstream to their target organs.

Regulation duties keep the internal environment stable. Blood helps maintain optimal body temperature by absorbing and distributing heat throughout the body. It also regulates tissue fluid pH through chemical buffer systems and maintains normal fluid volume within the cardiovascular system.

Protection mechanisms guard the body against blood loss and disease. Hemostasis prevents excessive bleeding through clotting mechanisms when vessels are injured. Meanwhile, white blood cells, antibodies, and complement proteins circulating in the blood actively defend against foreign invaders, toxins, and pathogens.

Plasma Composition and Fluid Balance

Plasma is a complex aqueous solution composed of about 90 to 92 percent water, which acts as a versatile solvent for carrying various solutes. Dissolved within plasma are electrolytes, nutrients, respiratory gases, hormones, waste products, and an abundance of plasma proteins. Electrolytes such as sodium, potassium, calcium, and chloride help maintain osmotic balance, membrane potentials, and acid-base homeostasis.

Plasma proteins make up about 7 percent of the plasma volume and are mostly produced by the liver:

  • Albumin: The most abundant plasma protein, responsible for creating colloidal osmotic pressure that keeps water inside the blood vessels.
  • Globulins: Divided into alpha, beta, and gamma types; alpha and beta transport lipids and fat-soluble vitamins, while gamma globulins act as antibodies.
  • Fibrinogen: An essential soluble precursor protein that converts into insoluble strands of fibrin during blood clot formation.

When a blood sample clots and the clotting proteins are removed, the remaining clear fluid is called serum rather than plasma.

Erythrocytes and Oxygen Transport

Erythrocytes, or red blood cells, are specialized discs designed for efficient gas transport. A mature human red blood cell is a biconcave disc, a shape that provides a large surface area relative to its volume for gas exchange and allows the cell to bend and squeeze through narrow capillaries. During their maturation process in the red bone marrow, mammalian erythrocytes expel their nucleus and other organelles to create more internal space for hemoglobin.

Hemoglobin is a large, iron-rich protein molecule made up of four polypeptide chains, each bound to a pigmented ring called a heme group. Each iron atom at the center of a heme group can reversibly bind one molecule of oxygen, allowing a single red blood cell to carry millions of oxygen molecules. When oxygen binds to iron in the lungs, the blood becomes bright red, forming oxyhemoglobin. As blood unloads oxygen to active tissues, it turns a darker red as deoxyhemoglobin.

The production of new red blood cells is called erythropoiesis. When kidneys detect low blood oxygen levels, they release a hormone called erythropoietin (EPO), which stimulates the bone marrow to accelerate red blood cell production. Mature erythrocytes have a lifespan of about 120 days before old or damaged cells are broken down by macrophages in the spleen and liver.

Leukocytes and Immune Defense Mechanisms

Leukocytes, or white blood cells, are complete cells containing nuclei and normal organelles. Unlike red blood cells, they are not confined strictly to the bloodstream and can slip through capillary walls into tissue spaces through a process called diapedesis, guided by chemical trails in a movement known as chemotaxis. Leukocytes are divided into two distinct groups based on the presence or absence of visible granules in their cytoplasm:

  • Granulocytes: Include neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils. Neutrophils are rapid-response phagocytes that devour bacteria; eosinophils combat parasitic worms and moderate allergic reactions; basophils release histamine during inflammatory and allergic responses.
  • Agranulocytes: Include lymphocytes and monocytes. Lymphocytes (T cells and B cells) orchestrate adaptive immunity and produce antibodies; monocytes transform into large macrophages that phagocytize debris and pathogens in tissues.

An abnormal elevation in total white blood cell count is called leukocytosis, which typically indicates an active infection, inflammation, or physical stress on the body.

Thrombocytes and the Haemostasis Process

Hemostasis is the precise physiological response that stops bleeding when a blood vessel is injured, preventing catastrophic blood loss. The process occurs in three sequential steps:

  1. Vascular Spasm: The damaged blood vessel wall immediately constricts to reduce blood flow through the break.
  2. Platelet Plug Formation: Platelets (thrombocytes), which are small cell fragments derived from giant bone marrow cells called megakaryocytes, stick to the exposed collagen fibers of the torn vessel and clump together to form a temporary plug.
  3. Coagulation (Blood Clotting): A complex cascade of plasma clotting factors culminates in the conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads. These threads weave through the platelet plug, trapping blood cells to form a solid, durable mesh clot.

Once the vessel wall heals, the clot gradually dissolves through a process called fibrinolysis, mediated by the enzyme plasmin.

ABO and Rhesus Blood Grouping Systems

Blood typing is based on the presence or absence of specific inherited glycoproteins and glycolipids called antigens on the surface of red blood cells. Plasma contains naturally occurring antibodies called agglutinins that react against foreign blood antigens not found on the person's own red blood cells.

  • ABO System: Individuals are classified based on A and B antigens. Type A blood has A antigens and anti-B antibodies; Type B has B antigens and anti-A antibodies; Type AB has both antigens and no antibodies (universal recipient); Type O has neither antigen and both anti-A and anti-B antibodies (universal donor).
  • Rhesus (Rh) System: Classified as either positive or negative based on the presence of the D antigen. People who are Rh-positive have the antigen, while Rh-negative individuals do not.

If incompatible blood types are mixed during a transfusion, the recipient's antibodies bind to the donor red blood cells, causing them to clump together in a reaction called agglutination, which is followed by cell destruction and life-threatening complications.
