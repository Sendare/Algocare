Anatomy and Physiology of Blood and Blood Cells

Introduction to the Haematological System and Blood Composition

Blood is a specialized connective tissue circulating continuously through the cardiovascular system. In a healthy adult, total blood volume is approximately 5 to 6 liters in males and 4 to 5 liters in females, accounting for roughly eight percent of total body weight. When whole blood is collected and spun in a centrifuge with an anticoagulant, it separates into distinct layers based on density. The upper layer is a pale yellow fluid called plasma, making up about 55 percent of total volume. A thin, whitish middle layer known as the buffy coat contains white blood cells and platelets, representing less than one percent. The bottom layer consists of erythrocytes, packed tightly and accounting for the remaining 45 percent, a metric known as the hematocrit or packed cell volume.

Physiological Functions of Blood in the Human Body

Blood acts as the primary transport vehicle for the human body, maintaining homeostasis across three broad physiological categories: distribution, regulation, and protection.

  • Transport: Carries oxygen from the lungs and nutrients from the digestive tract to body tissues, while simultaneously removing metabolic waste products like carbon dioxide and urea to elimination organs.
  • Regulation: Maintains optimal systemic pH through chemical buffers, regulates core body temperature by absorbing and distributing heat, and preserves adequate fluid volume in the cardiovascular system.
  • Protection: Guards against blood loss via the clotting mechanism and defends the body against pathogens through circulating white blood cells and antibodies.

Plasma Composition and Fluid Balance

Plasma is approximately 91.5 percent water, providing the fluid medium necessary for substance suspension and transport. The remaining 8.5 percent consists of solutes, primarily plasma proteins synthesized mainly by the liver. These proteins are divided into three main groups:

  1. Albumins: The most abundant plasma proteins; they exert colloid osmotic pressure, pulling water back into capillaries to maintain blood volume and pressure.
  2. Globulins: Include alpha and beta globulins that transport iron, lipids, and fat-soluble vitamins, as well as gamma globulins (antibodies) crucial for immune defense.
  3. Fibrinogen: An essential soluble precursor protein that converts into insoluble fibrin threads during blood clot formation.

Erythrocytes: Structure, Function, and Erythropoiesis

Erythrocytes are specialized for gas transport. Lacking nuclei and mitochondria, mature red blood cells have a unique biconcave disc shape that maximizes surface area-to-volume ratio and allows flexibility as they squeeze through narrow capillaries. The cytoplasm is packed with hemoglobin, an iron-containing protein that reversibly binds oxygen molecules.

Production of red blood cells is called erythropoiesis, stimulated by the hormone erythropoietin (EPO) released by the kidneys in response to hypoxia. In adults, this process occurs within the red bone marrow of flat bones and the epiphyses of long bones. The lifespan of a normal erythrocyte is roughly 120 days, after which aged cells are phagocytized by macrophages in the spleen and liver.

Leukocytes: Types, Immunity, and Defense Mechanisms

White blood cells contain nuclei and full cellular machinery, serving to protect the body against invading pathogens, toxins, and damaged cells. They are divided into two main categories based on cytoplasmic appearance under a microscope:

  • Granulocytes: Possess prominent cytoplasmic granules and lobed nuclei. This group includes neutrophils (primary phagocytes during acute bacterial infection), eosinophils (active during parasitic infections and allergic reactions), and basophils (release histamine and heparin during inflammatory responses).
  • Agranulocytes: Lack visible staining granules. This group includes lymphocytes (T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells central to adaptive immunity) and monocytes (which differentiate into wandering and fixed macrophages in tissues).

Thrombocytes and the Haemostasis Process

Thrombocytes, commonly called platelets, are not complete cells but rather anuclear cytoplasmic fragments pinched off from large bone marrow precursor cells known as megakaryocytes. Their primary role is haemostasis, the physiological process that stops bleeding when a blood vessel is injured. Haemostasis occurs in three sequential steps:

  1. Vascular spasm: Smooth muscle in the damaged vessel wall contracts instantly to reduce blood flow.
  2. Platelet plug formation: Exposed collagen fibers cause circulating platelets to swell, become sticky, and clump together to seal the break.
  3. Coagulation (Blood clotting): A complex cascade of clotting factors converts liquid plasma fibrinogen into solid fibrin meshwork, stabilizing the platelet plug.

Blood Grouping Systems: ABO and Rhesus Compatibility

Blood typing relies on identifying specific surface glycoproteins and glycolipids called antigens present on the plasma membrane of erythrocytes. The two most clinically significant classification systems are ABO and Rhesus.

  • ABO System: Based on the presence or absence of antigen A and antigen B. Blood types include Type A (A antigens, anti-B antibodies), Type B (B antigens, anti-A antibodies), Type AB (both antigens, no antibodies), and Type O (no antigens, both anti-A and anti-B antibodies).
  • Rhesus (Rh) System: Based on the presence of the D antigen. Individuals with the antigen are Rh-positive, while those lacking it are Rh-negative. Matching these systems correctly during blood transfusion prevents severe hemolytic transfusion reactions.
