Complications of Haematological Disorders

Introduction to Haematological Disorders and Vulnerability to Complications

Haematological disorders encompass a broad spectrum of conditions affecting blood components, including red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and plasma proteins. Because blood circulates through every organ system, primary defects in cellular production, function, or destruction rarely remain isolated. Instead, they trigger widespread systemic consequences. When oxygen-carrying capacity drops, clotting factors fail, or immune cells malfunction, the body's baseline physiological equilibrium destabilizes rapidly. In resource-constrained settings across Nigeria, where malaria, nutritional deficiencies, and inherited hemoglobinopathies intersect, these complications frequently present as acute medical emergencies in emergency departments and primary healthcare clinics alike.

Vaso-Occlusive and Ischaemic Crises in Sickle Cell Disease

Under conditions of hypoxia, dehydration, or infection, mutant hemoglobin S polymerizes, distorting flexible erythrocytes into rigid, crescent shapes. These abnormal cells increase blood viscosity and adhere to vascular endothelium, obstruct microvascular blood flow, and precipitate severe tissue ischemia.

  • Acute Chest Syndrome: Characterized by chest pain, fever, and new pulmonary infiltrates, representing a leading cause of mortality.
  • Splenic Sequestration: Sudden pooling of large volumes of blood within the spleen, causing rapid enlargement, profound hypovolemic shock, and severe anemia.
  • Priapism: Painful, prolonged penile erection resulting from trapped outflow, risking permanent erectile tissue fibrosis if untreated.
  • Avascular Necrosis: Ischemic destruction of the femoral or humeral head due to chronic microvascular occlusion of bone trabeculae.

Bleeding Disorders and Haemorrhagic Complications

Hemostasis relies on a delicate balance between procoagulant factors, platelets, and fibrinolytic mechanisms. When this system fails through quantitative or qualitative platelet deficits, or inherited coagulation factor deficiencies such as hemophilia, spontaneous or prolonged bleeding ensues.

Superficial microvascular bleeding typically manifests as petechiae, purpura, or mucosal oozing from the gums and epistaxis. Conversely, deep-seated defects in the clotting cascade present as hemarthrosis, intramuscular hematomas, and retroperitoneal bleeding. In acute settings like Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC), widespread microvascular clotting consumes available platelets and clotting factors paradoxically, culminating in simultaneous thrombosis and severe, life-threatening hemorrhage from venipuncture sites and surgical wounds.

Infectious Vulnerabilities Linked to Leukopenia and Immunosuppression

White blood cells, particularly neutrophils and lymphocytes, form the vanguard of the human immune response against invading pathogens. When malignant infiltration of the bone marrow, chemotherapy, or autoimmune destruction causes profound leukopenia, the body loses its primary defense mechanism. Minor breaches in the skin or mucosal barriers rapidly escalate into overwhelming, fulminant sepsis without the localizing inflammatory signs typically seen in healthy individuals.

Fever may be the sole clinical indicator of a life-threatening bacterial or fungal invasion in a neutropenic patient. Nursing assessment must prioritize immediate thermal monitoring and prompt collection of blood cultures before starting broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics.

Cardiovascular and Respiratory Manifestations of Severe Anemia

When the hemoglobin concentration falls precipitously, the blood's capacity to transport oxygen to metabolically active tissues declines. To compensate for this oxygen deficit, the cardiovascular system undergoes significant hemodynamic alterations. Cardiac output increases through both elevated heart rate and enhanced stroke volume, aiming to maintain tissue oxygenation. Over time, this chronic hyperdynamic state leads to myocardial workload exhaustion, cardiomegaly, and ultimately high-output heart failure.

Concurrently, patients experience dyspnea on exertion, generalized fatigue, and tissue hypoxia that stimulates tachypnea. In clinical practice, elderly patients or those with underlying coronary artery disease tolerate these compensatory shifts poorly, frequently precipitating acute anginal chest pain or myocardial infarction even in the absence of obstructive coronary plaque.

Organ Damage Resulting from Iron Overload and Repeated Transfusions

The human body possesses no active physiological mechanism to excrete excess iron. Consequently, patients who undergo chronic, repeated blood transfusions—such as those with thalassemia major or severe aplastic anemia—inevitably accumulate massive tissue iron stores. As the reticuloendothelial system and transferrin binding capacities saturate, free non-transferrin-bound iron catalyzes the formation of highly toxic reactive oxygen species via Fenton reactions.

This oxidative stress causes progressive lipid peroxidation and cellular damage across multiple endocrine and visceral organs. The heart, liver, and pancreas are primary targets: iron cardiomyopathy leads to fatal arrhythmias or heart failure, hepatic siderosis progresses to cirrhosis, and pancreatic infiltration results in secondary diabetes mellitus ("bronze diabetes"). Chelation therapy is essential to bind and remove excess iron, protecting vital organs.

Thromboembolic Complications in Hypercoagulable States

Certain haematological disorders tip the coagulation balance heavily toward thrombosis. Myeloproliferative neoplasms, such as polycythemia vera and essential thrombocythemia, produce excessive numbers of abnormal blood cells that increase blood viscosity and platelet reactivity. Similarly, inherited deficiencies of natural anticoagulants like protein C, protein S, or antithrombin III predispose patients to inappropriate clot formation.

Thromboembolic events manifest as deep vein thrombosis (DVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), or unusual site thrombosis such as hepatic vein thrombosis (Budd-Chiari syndrome) or cerebral sinus thrombosis. Nurses must monitor for unilateral limb swelling, sudden pleuritic chest pain, or unexplained neurological deficits.

Nursing Assessment, Monitoring, and Emergency Interventions

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with haematological disorders requires vigilant, systematic assessment and rapid recognition of deteriorating clinical parameters. Monitoring protocols must track vital sign trends, peripheral perfusion, fluid balance, and laboratory values including hemoglobin, platelet counts, and absolute neutrophil counts.

Emergency interventions require prompt execution: administering supplemental oxygen during ischemic crises, maintaining large-bore intravenous access for fluid resuscitation or blood product administration, and adhering strictly to transfusion safety protocols. Furthermore, nurses play a vital role in educating patients and their families on recognizing early warning signs—such as rising fever, abnormal bruising, or severe localized pain—to ensure timely presentation before life-threatening complications occur.
