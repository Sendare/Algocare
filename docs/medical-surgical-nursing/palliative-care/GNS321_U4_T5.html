Pain Assessment

Introduction and Nature of Pain

Pain is widely recognized as the fifth vital sign, demanding the same clinical attention as temperature, pulse, respiration, and blood pressure. It is deeply personal, defined simply as whatever the experiencing person says it is, and existing whenever they say it does. This subjective nature makes patient self-report the gold standard of assessment. Nurses must acknowledge that pain is not merely a physical sensation; it carries emotional, psychological, and spiritual dimensions that heavily influence how a patient copes. Failing to treat pain adequately can lead to delayed healing, increased hospital stays, and chronic physical complications.

Pathophysiology and Types of Pain

Pain signals travel through specific pathways from the site of tissue injury up to the cerebral cortex.

  • Nociceptive pain: Caused by damage to body tissues, further divided into somatic (sharp, localized skin or muscle pain) and visceral (deep, cramping organ pain).
  • Neuropathic pain: Arises from direct damage or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system itself, often described as burning, shooting, or tingling.
  • Acute pain: Sudden onset, tied directly to tissue trauma, and typically resolves as healing occurs.
  • Chronic pain: Lasting beyond the normal healing time, usually defined as three to six months or longer, and often serving no protective biological purpose.

Principles of Comprehensive Assessment (OPQRSTUV)

A thorough pain history requires a systematic approach. Nurses commonly use the mnemonic OPQRSTUV to gather complete details without overwhelming the patient.

  1. OnsetData: When did the pain start, and how did it begin?
  2. Provoking/Palliating factors: What makes the pain worse or better?
  3. Quality: What does the pain feel like (sharp, dull, aching)?
  4. Region/Radiation: Where is the pain located, and does it spread anywhere else?
  5. Severity: How intense is the pain on a standardized rating scale?
  6. Treatment: What medications or remedies have already been tried?
  7. Understanding/Values: What does the patient believe is causing the pain, and what are their personal goals?

Pain Assessment Tools and Scales

Different patients require different measurement tools based on their age, cognitive status, and communication abilities.

  • Numeric Rating Scale (NRS): A 0 to 10 scale where 0 means no pain and 10 means the worst possible pain; highly effective for alert adults.
  • Wong-Baker FACES Scale: Uses cartoon faces showing smiling to crying expressions, ideal for young children or adults with mild language barriers.
  • Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS) or FLACC: Designed for non-verbal patients, assessing indicators like facial expression, leg movement, and cry.

A common clinical mistake is assuming a patient who is resting quietly or sleeping is free of pain; severe exhaustion often masks outward distress.

Evaluating Pain in Special Populations

Assessing pain becomes more complex when patients cannot verbally communicate. In neonates, infants, and cognitively impaired adults, nurses must rely heavily on physiological changes and behavioral cues. Elevated heart rate, elevated blood pressure, sweating, grimacing, and rigid body postures are strong indicators of distress. For older adults, nurses must avoid assuming that persistent pain is a normal part of aging. Cultural beliefs also shape how pain is expressed or suppressed, requiring nurses to provide culturally sensitive care without personal bias.

Documenting and Reassessing Pain Outcomes

Assessment does not end when an intervention is given; closing the loop requires prompt reassessment. Nurses must document the baseline pain score, the specific nursing or medical intervention applied (such as medication administration or repositioning), and the patient's response within an appropriate timeframe—usually 30 to 60 minutes after oral medication. Clear, objective documentation ensures continuity of care across shifts and helps the healthcare team evaluate whether the current pain management plan is working effectively.
