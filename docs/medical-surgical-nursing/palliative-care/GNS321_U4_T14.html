Pain Management

Definition and Nature of Pain in Palliative Care

Pain in palliative care is rarely just a physical sensation. It is a complex experience that encompasses physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions, often referred to as total pain. When caring for patients facing life-limiting illnesses in Nigerian hospitals, nurses must recognize that unresolved anxiety, fear of death, or financial worries can directly worsen a patient's physical suffering.

Pain is entirely subjective; the gold standard for assessment is simply whatever the patient says it is, regardless of vital signs or outward appearance. A patient with normal blood pressure and a calm face can still be experiencing severe agony. Dismissing a patient's report because they do not look distressed compromises effective care and erodes trust.

Pathophysiology and Classifications of Pain

Pain transmission involves specialized sensory fibers called nociceptors that detect tissue injury and send signals up the spinal cord to the brain. Understanding the underlying mechanism helps nurses anticipate which drugs will work best.

  • Nociceptive pain: Arises from actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue and is divided into somatic (aching, localized in bones or muscles) and visceral (cramping, poorly localized in internal organs).
  • Neuropathic pain: Caused by a primary lesion or dysfunction in the nervous system itself, typically described as burning, shooting, or electric-shock-like sensations.
  • Inflammatory pain: Triggered by the body's immune response to tissue damage, involving chemical mediators that lower the activation threshold of nociceptors.

Comprehensive Pain Assessment and Nursing Tools

A thorough pain assessment guides all subsequent therapeutic decisions. Nurses use standardized mnemonic tools to capture the full clinical picture of a patient's discomfort. The PQRSTU framework is widely utilized in clinical practice:

  • Provocation/Palliation: What brings it on? What makes it better or worse?
  • Quality/Quantity: What does it feel like? (sharp, dull, throbbing, burning)
  • Region/Radiation: Where is it located? Does it spread anywhere?
  • Severity: How intense is it on a rating scale?
  • Timing: When did it start? How long does it last? Is it constant or intermittent?
  • Understanding: What does the patient believe is causing this pain?

For quantification, visual analogue scales, numerical rating scales (0 to 10), or the Wong-Baker FACES scale are selected based on the patient's age, cognitive status, and cultural familiarity.

Pharmacological Management and the WHO Analgesic Ladder

The World Health Organization (WHO) analgesic ladder provides a systematic framework for prescribing pain medication based on intensity. The core principle is to match the drug potency to the patient's reported pain score.

StepPain IntensityRecommended MedicationsExamples
Step 1Mild pain (1-3)Non-opioids +/- adjuvantsParacetamol, Ibuprofen
Step 2Moderate pain (4-6)Weak opioids +/- non-opioids and adjuvantsTramadol, Codeine
Step 3Severe pain (7-10)Strong opioids +/- non-opioids and adjuvantsMorphine, Fentanyl

Medications should be given by the clock rather than strictly on an as-needed (PRN) basis for persistent cancer or chronic pain, ensuring a steady therapeutic blood level prevents pain recurrence.

Non-Pharmacological Pain Interventions

Medications alone are rarely sufficient for comprehensive pain relief. Non-pharmacological nursing interventions work alongside analgesics by altering cognitive focus, reducing muscle tension, or stimulating sensory pathways to block pain signals at the spinal cord level (gate control theory).

  • Physical methods: Heat and cold therapy, gentle massage, physical positioning, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS).
  • Cognitive-behavioral methods: Deep breathing exercises, guided imagery, distraction techniques, and music therapy.

Integrating these strategies empowers patients, reduces reliance on high opioid doses, and addresses the psychological stress that amplifies physical discomfort.

Managing Breakthrough Pain and Adjuvant Medications

Breakthrough pain is a transient flare of severe pain that occurs against a background of otherwise controlled persistent pain. Patients require a rescue dose of a short-acting opioid, typically calculated as 10 to 15 percent of the total daily baseline opioid dose, available for immediate administration.

Adjuvant medications are drugs originally developed for other conditions that enhance analgesia or target specific pain types:

  • Anticonvulsants (e.g., gabapentin) and antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline) for neuropathic pain.
  • Corticosteroids for reducing inflammation around tumors or nerve compression.
  • Muscle relaxants for painful spasms.

Nursing Care, Monitoring, and Managing Side Effects

Administering powerful analgesics requires vigilant nursing oversight. Opioids carry predictable side effects that do not diminish over time with the same ease as sedation, meaning nurses must proactively manage them rather than waiting for complaints.

  • Constipation: Opioids slow bowel motility significantly. A stimulant laxative must be prescribed concurrently with regular opioid therapy; dietary fiber alone is insufficient.
  • Sedation and Respiratory Depression: Assess sedation scores regularly. Respiratory depression always follows excessive sedation; monitor respiratory rate before and after administration.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Often occurs during initiation or dose titration and usually resolves within a week; short-term antiemetics provide relief.

Neglecting prophylactic laxative administration is a common clinical oversight that causes severe preventable distress for patients.

Multidisciplinary Approach and Patient Education

Effective pain management requires collaborative teamwork involving doctors, nurses, pharmacists, physiotherapists, and spiritual care providers. Each discipline contributes distinct expertise to address the multifaceted nature of suffering.

Patient and family education is equally critical. Many patients harbor unfounded fears regarding addiction, tolerance, and side effects when prescribed opioids for chronic or terminal illness. Nurses must dispel these myths, explaining the difference between physical dependence (a normal physiological adaptation) and psychological addiction (addictive behavior). Clear instructions on medication schedules, rescue doses, and when to seek help ensure safety and adherence at home.
