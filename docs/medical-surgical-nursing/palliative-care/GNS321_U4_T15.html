Management of Symptoms Other Than Pain

Introduction to Non-Pain Symptom Management in Palliative Care

Palliative care extends far beyond pain relief, focusing heavily on the comprehensive management of various distressing physical and psychological symptoms that impact the quality of life of terminal patients in Nigerian healthcare settings. While pain is frequently the most feared symptom, conditions such as breathlessness, severe fatigue, nausea, and existential distress can be equally debilitating. Effective nursing management requires a shift from curative goals to comfort-oriented, holistic care that honors the dignity of the patient and supports their family. Nurses serve as the primary advocates and frontline caregivers, recognizing that untreated non-pain symptoms can drastically accelerate physical decline and emotional suffering. Understanding the pathophysiology and patient experience of these diverse symptoms is vital for student nurses preparing for clinical practice, ensuring they can deliver compassionate, evidence-based interventions in both hospital wards and home-based palliative care programs.

Assessment and Holistic Evaluation of Palliative Patients

Assessment is the foundational step in managing non-pain symptoms effectively. In palliative nursing, assessment must be holistic, covering physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions. Nigerian nursing students must master systematic symptom evaluation tools, such as the Palliative Performance Scale or numeric rating scales adapted for non-pain distress. Nurses should actively listen to patients and their families, acknowledging that cultural beliefs and socioeconomic factors significantly influence how symptoms are perceived and reported. A thorough physical assessment helps differentiate whether fatigue or nausea is a direct result of disease progression, medication side effects, or secondary complications. Furthermore, reassessment is crucial; symptoms fluctuate rapidly in advanced illness, and interventions must be continually evaluated for their efficacy and adjusted accordingly to maintain optimal patient comfort and dignity.

Management of Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Nausea, Vomiting, and Constipation

Gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, and constipation are exceptionally common and distressing in advanced diseases like cancer and end-stage organ failure. Nausea and vomiting can stem from diverse etiologies, including vestibular stimulation, biochemical imbalances, or gastrointestinal stasis, requiring targeted antiemetics rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. Nurses must collaborate with physicians to select appropriate pharmacological agents, while also employing non-pharmacological measures like small, frequent meals and pleasant room ventilation. Conversely, constipation is frequently precipitated by opioid analgesics, immobility, and poor fluid intake. A proactive bowel management protocol—incorporating adequate hydration, high-fiber diets where appropriate, and prescribed stimulant or osmotic laxatives—is vital to prevent severe complications such as bowel obstruction or fecal impaction. Patient and family education regarding regular bowel charting is an essential nursing responsibility in these clinical scenarios.

Respiratory Support and Management of Dyspnea and Cough

Dyspnea (breathlessness) and persistent cough are terrifying symptoms that provoke profound anxiety in both the patient and their caregivers. In palliative care, dyspnea is subjective and must be evaluated based on the patient's report rather than objective measures alone. Nursing interventions include positioning the patient in high Fowler's, utilizing bedside fans to stimulate trigeminal facial receptors which helps reduce the sensation of breathlessness, and administering prescribed low-dose opioids to reduce the respiratory drive and air hunger. Oxygen therapy should be reserved for hypoxic patients, as it may not relieve dyspnea in normoxic individuals. For a persistent cough, humidification, gentle suctioning if appropriate, and antitussive medications can provide significant relief. Nurses must also offer continuous emotional reassurance, as panic worsens dyspnea in a vicious cycle.

Addressing Constitutional Symptoms: Fatigue, Anorexia, and Cachexia

Constitutional symptoms—including profound fatigue, anorexia, and cachexia—are hallmarks of advanced systemic disease and often signal disease progression. Fatigue in palliative care is multifactorial, encompassing physical weakness, emotional exhaustion, and sleep disturbances; it cannot be resolved simply by resting. Nurses should help patients prioritize daily activities, conserving energy for things that matter most to them. Anorexia and cachexia (muscle wasting) can cause severe distress to families who equate feeding with love and care. Nursing education is crucial here: nurses must reassure families that the loss of appetite is a natural part of the dying process and that forcing food can cause aspiration, vomiting, or abdominal discomfort. Instead, focus should shift to comfort measures like oral hygiene, offering small sips of favored liquids, and managing secondary issues like dry mouth.

Interventions for Neuropsychiatric Symptoms: Delirium, Anxiety, and Depression

Neuropsychiatric symptoms such as delirium, anxiety, and depression are prevalent and frequently under-diagnosed in terminal illness. Delirium, characterized by acute confusion and fluctuating consciousness, can be hyperactive, hypoactive, or mixed. Nurses must investigate underlying reversible causes such as infection, dehydration, or medication toxicity, while maintaining a safe, calm, and well-lit environment. Anxiety and depression stem from the existential weight of a terminal diagnosis and require empathetic listening, counseling, and appropriate pharmacological interventions such as anxiolytics or antidepressants. Family education is vital so loved ones understand that sudden behavioral changes or withdrawal are often manifestations of the underlying disease process rather than personal rejection. Collaborative mental health support ensures that both the psychological and spiritual needs of the patient are met comprehensively.

Skin Integrity and Management of Pruritus and Pressure Injuries

Maintaining skin integrity is a critical aspect of comfort care, as compromised skin leads to immense physical suffering, pain, and increased risk of infection. Pruritus (itching) can be generalized and deeply frustrating, resulting from uremia, jaundice, or medication side effects; it is managed through skin emollients, antihistamines, or treating the underlying metabolic disturbance. Simultaneously, pressure injuries are a major risk in immobile, cachectic palliative patients. Nurses must implement rigorous preventative protocols, including regular repositioning, use of pressure-relieving support surfaces, meticulous skin hygiene, and maintaining adequate nutritional status where feasible. However, in the very final stages of life, the skin may fail despite best efforts due to poor perfusion. In such cases, nursing goals shift toward meticulous wound care, odor management, and ensuring patient comfort.

Nursing Interventions and Multidisciplinary Collaborative Care

The effective management of non-pain symptoms cannot be achieved in isolation; it requires robust multidisciplinary collaborative care. Nurses act as the central coordinators, communicating patient needs across a team that includes physicians, pharmacists, physiotherapists, social workers, and spiritual leaders. Pharmacists assist in adjusting complex medication regimens and routes of administration, such as sublingual or subcutaneous routes, when swallowing becomes impossible. Physiotherapists and occupational therapists contribute valuable strategies for safe positioning and energy conservation. Social workers and spiritual counselors address the psychosocial and existential burdens weighing on the patient and their relatives. For Nigerian nursing students, mastering this collaborative approach ensures comprehensive, seamless care that upholds holistic nursing values and optimizes the quality of life for patients and their families until the end of life.
