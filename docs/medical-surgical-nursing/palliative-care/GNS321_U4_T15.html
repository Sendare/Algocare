Management of Symptoms Other Than Pain

Introduction to Non-Pain Symptom Management in Palliative Care

Palliative care extends far beyond pain relief. While analgesia remains a cornerstone of comfortable end-of-life care, distressing physical and psychological symptoms can erode a patient's dignity and quality of life just as severely. Non-pain symptoms—ranging from unremitting nausea to terminal restlessness—require the same rigorous clinical attention as nociceptive pain. The overarching philosophy shifts here from curative treatment to comfort optimization, focusing heavily on relief of suffering rather than disease reversal.

Assessment and Holistic Evaluation

Effective symptom management begins with thorough, ongoing assessment. Patients with advanced illnesses often experience multiple concurrent symptoms, creating a complex clinical picture where one issue masks another.

  • Comprehensive History: Evaluate onset, duration, pattern, and exacerbating factors for each reported symptom.
  • Validated Tools: Utilize targeted scales like the Edmonton Symptom Assessment System (ESAS) to track symptom intensity over time.
  • Psychosocial Context: Explore the patient's emotional landscape, spiritual beliefs, and family dynamics, as psychological distress frequently amplifies physical discomfort.

Management of Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Nausea, Vomiting, and Constipation

Gastrointestinal disturbances are among the most debilitating complications in advanced disease. Nausea and vomiting can stem from diverse pathways—chemoreceptor trigger zone activation, vestibular input, or delayed gastric emptying—meaning antiemetic selection must target the specific underlying mechanism rather than relying on a single broad-spectrum drug.

Constipation is equally prevalent, frequently driven by opioid analgesics, immobility, and poor oral intake. Proactive bowel regimens combining a stimulant laxative (such as senna) with a stool softener (such as docusate) should be initiated concurrently whenever opioid therapy starts, rather than waiting for impaction to occur.

Respiratory Support: Dyspnea and Cough

Dyspnea is a subjective experience of breathing discomfort that terrifies both patients and families. Non-pharmacological measures provide rapid relief: positioning the patient upright, directing a cool fan breeze across the trigeminal nerve area of the face, and teaching pursed-lip breathing techniques.

When pharmacological intervention is required, low-dose systemic opioids (such as oral morphine) remain the gold standard. Opioids reduce the sensation of breathlessness by lowering the brainstem's sensitivity to hypercapnia and hypoxia, without necessarily depressing respiratory rate when titrated carefully. For intractable cough, humidified air and antitussives offer supplementary relief.

Addressing Constitutional Symptoms: Fatigue, Anorexia, and Cachexia

Constitutional symptoms erode physical reserves and alter family dynamics around eating and caregiving.

  • Fatigue: Differs from normal tiredness; it is unrelenting and unimproved by rest. Management focuses on energy conservation and setting realistic daily goals.
  • Anorexia and Cachexia: Marked by profound muscle wasting, metabolic changes, and loss of appetite. Forcing food often causes distress. Education must reframe mealtimes away from nutrition and toward social connection, emphasizing small, attractive portions of favorite foods.

Interventions for Neuropsychiatric Symptoms

Cognitive and mood disturbances require urgent evaluation because they impair communication and decision-making.

Delirium is frequently reversible when underlying triggers—such as urinary retention, fecal impaction, hypoxia, or medication toxicity—are identified and treated. Environmental modifications, including familiar objects and gentle reorientation, should accompany pharmacological agents like haloperidol when agitation threatens safety.

Meanwhile, anxiety and depression require active listening, validated psychological support, and judicious use of fast-acting anxiolytics or antidepressants when emotional distress compromises daily comfort.

Skin Integrity, Pruritus, and Pressure Injuries

Maintaining skin integrity in compromised patients demands constant vigilance. Pruritus (itching) can stem from uremia, jaundice, or opioid side effects, often responding poorly to standard antihistamines; targeted treatments include specific binders, moisturizers, or low-dose specific neuropathic agents.

Pressure injuries represent a major threat to comfort. Preventive care centers on regular position changes, moisture management, and pressure-relieving surfaces. When wounds are unavoidable at the end of life, the goal shifts from aggressive healing to infection prevention, odor control, and pain minimization during dressing changes.

Multidisciplinary Collaborative Care

No single discipline can manage the multifaceted symptom burden of advanced illness alone. Comprehensive care requires seamless coordination among physicians, nurses, pharmacists, social workers, dietitians, and spiritual care providers.

Nurses act as the central communication hub, observing subtle changes, translating complex medical regimens for families, and advocating for timely symptom re-evaluations across the entire care team.
