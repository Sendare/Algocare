Concepts of Palliative Care

Introduction and Definition of Palliative Care

Palliative care is an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing the problems associated with life-threatening illness. It achieves this through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual.

Contrary to popular belief, palliative care is not just for the final days of life. It can be introduced alongside disease-modifying treatments early in the course of a serious illness like cancer, heart failure, or kidney disease.

Core Principles and Philosophy

The philosophy of palliative care rests on valuing life while recognizing dying as a normal process. It neither hastens nor postpones death. Instead, it affirms life and regards dying as a normal human experience.

  • Patient Autonomy: Respecting the choices and values of the individual.
  • Holistic Approach: Addressing body, mind, and spirit simultaneously.
  • Support Systems: Helping families cope during the illness and through bereavement.

Palliative Care Versus Curative Treatment

A common misconception is that patients must choose between palliative care and curative care. In modern healthcare, these two approaches overlap significantly.

FeatureCurative CarePalliative Care
Primary GoalEradicate disease and cureRelieve suffering and improve comfort
TimingUsually early in diagnosisAny time during serious illness
FocusDisease pathology and organsThe whole person and family

When a disease stops responding to curative therapies, palliative care takes center stage, ensuring dignity and comfort until the end.

The Total Pain Concept

Pain in advanced illness is rarely just physical. Dame Cicely Saunders introduced the concept of total pain, which acknowledges that physical suffering is invariably compounded by psychological, social, and spiritual distress.

  • Physical: Tissue damage, fatigue, nausea, and breathlessness.
  • Psychological: Anxiety, depression, fear of the unknown, and loss of control.
  • Social: Financial worries, family burden, and isolation from community.
  • Spiritual: Loss of meaning in life, guilt, and existential questions about faith.

The Multidisciplinary Palliative Care Team

Meeting the complex needs of a palliative patient requires a team of various professionals working closely together. Nurses often act as the central coordinators, bridging communication between the patient, family, and other specialists.

  • Doctors: Manage complex symptom control and medical treatments.
  • Nurses: Provide daily bedside care, wound management, and patient advocacy.
  • Social Workers: Assist with financial resources, family counseling, and discharge planning.
  • Chaplains or Imams: Offer spiritual guidance and existential support tailored to local beliefs.

Patient and Family-Centered Communication

Effective communication is a cornerstone of good palliative care. Nurses must practice active listening, allowing patients and their families to express fears and preferences openly without judgment.

Honest discussions about prognosis help families prepare and make informed decisions. Cultural beliefs regarding death and dying must be respected, especially in diverse settings like Nigeria where family elders often play a major role in healthcare decision-making.

End-of-Life Care and Ethical Considerations

Caring for patients at the very end of life involves navigating sensitive ethical issues such as pain management, withdrawal of burdensome treatments, and respecting the right to die with dignity.

In many Nigerian communities, death is a communal event, and home care is often preferred over hospital death. Nurses must balance professional medical standards with cultural expectations, ensuring that pain relief is never withheld due to unfounded fears of addiction when a patient is dying.
