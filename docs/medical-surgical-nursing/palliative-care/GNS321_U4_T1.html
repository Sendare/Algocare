Overview of Palliative Care

Introduction and Definition of Palliative Care

Palliative care is an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing the problems associated with life-threatening illness. It achieves this through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification, flawless assessment, and impeccable treatment of pain and other physical, psychosocial, and spiritual problems. Unlike traditional medical care that focuses strictly on curing a disease, palliative care centers on the whole human being. It can be introduced at any stage of a serious illness, often running concurrently alongside life-prolonging therapies. Nurses encounter palliative care across many settings, from large teaching hospitals in urban centers to rural primary health clinics, making a solid understanding of this approach essential for holistic nursing practice.

Core Philosophy and Principles

The philosophy of palliative care rests on valuing life while regarding dying as a normal process. It neither hastens nor postpones death. Instead, it affirms life and regards dying as a natural part of human existence. Key principles include:

  • Patient-Centeredness: Respecting the individual goals, values, and choices of the patient.
  • Symptom Relief: Providing aggressive, careful management of pain and distressing physical symptoms.
  • Family Support: Including the family in care planning and offering support throughout the illness and bereavement.
  • Team Approach: Utilizing various healthcare professionals to address the multifaceted needs of patients.

Palliative Care Versus Curative Treatment

A common misconception is that patients must choose between palliative care and curative treatment. In modern healthcare, these two approaches are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.

FeatureCurative TreatmentPalliative Care
Primary GoalEradicate disease or prolong lifeOptimize comfort and quality of life
TimingOften early in the disease trajectoryAny time, from diagnosis onward
MeasuresChemotherapy, surgery, radiationPain control, psychological counseling, comfort measures

When a disease becomes refractory to curative interventions, palliative care gently shifts from prolonging survival to ensuring peace, dignity, and symptom relief.

The Multidisciplinary Palliative Care Team

Meeting the complex needs of a palliative patient requires a diverse team of professionals. No single discipline can address every dimension of human suffering.

  1. Doctors: Provide medical evaluation, prescribe disease-directed therapy, and manage complex symptom protocols.
  2. Nurses: Coordinate daily care, monitor symptoms, administer medications, and serve as the main bridge between the patient and the medical team.
  3. Social Workers: Help families navigate financial strain, access community resources, and handle discharge planning.
  4. Chaplains or Spiritual Leaders: Offer spiritual guidance, prayer, and existential support tailored to the patient's faith.
  5. Pharmacists: Optimize drug regimens, prevent adverse interactions, and prepare specialized formulations.

Holistic Assessment of Symptoms and Pain Management

Pain in palliative care is rarely just physical; it often intertwines with anxiety, depression, and existential distress. Nurses must assess pain thoroughly using appropriate scales, remembering that pain is whatever the patient says it is. Beyond pain, patients frequently experience fatigue, nausea, dyspnea, and delirium. Management relies on both pharmacological interventions, such as the WHO analgesic ladder, and non-pharmacological methods like repositioning, massage, and relaxation techniques. Regular reassessment ensures that comfort measures remain effective as the disease progresses.

Psychosocial, Cultural, and Spiritual Support

Human suffering extends far beyond the physical body. Patients facing the end of life often grapple with fear of the unknown, loss of dignity, and worries about their dependents. Culturally competent care is vital, especially in diverse societies like Nigeria, where cultural beliefs shape dying rituals, truth-telling preferences, and family decision-making structures. Spiritual care involves listening deeply to patients as they search for meaning, reconciliation, and peace. Nurses do not need to share a patient's exact religious beliefs to provide supportive spiritual presence; they simply need to respect and facilitate what brings the patient comfort.

End-of-Life Care and Bereavement Support

As death approaches, the focus narrows intensely to comfort, dignity, and presence. Physical signs of active dying include cool extremities, changes in breathing patterns such as Cheyne-Stokes respirations, and reduced responsiveness. Nurses support families by explaining these normal transitions, reducing fear, and ensuring hygiene and comfort. After death, bereavement support begins. Grief is a natural response to loss, and nurses can help families by offering a compassionate ear, encouraging healthy coping mechanisms, and connecting them with community or religious grief support groups.

The Role of the Nurse in Palliative Care in Nigeria

In Nigeria, palliative care is growing but still faces challenges such as limited access to strong opioids, cultural reluctance to discuss terminal diagnoses openly, and scarce specialized facilities. Nigerian nurses serve as vital advocates, educators, and direct care providers. They bridge the gap between medical teams and families, often translating complex medical jargon into understandable local languages. Despite infrastructural hurdles, nurses provide compassionate bedside care, manage basic symptom relief, and champion the integration of palliative care principles into standard nursing education and clinical practice across the nation.
