Overview of Palliative Care

Introduction and Definition of Palliative Care

Palliative care is an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing the problems associated with life-threatening illness. It achieves this through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other physical, psychosocial, and spiritual issues. Far from being restricted to the final hours of life, this care model can be integrated alongside disease-modifying therapies early in a chronic illness trajectory. In busy Nigerian tertiary hospitals, patients often present with advanced malignancies or end-stage organ failures late in their disease course, making timely introduction of palliative principles essential for reducing unnecessary hospital admissions and distress.

Core Philosophy and Principles

The framework of palliative care rests on several non-negotiable tenets designed to honor human dignity. - **Patient-Centeredness**: Care plans align with the personal goals, values, and preferences of the individual. - **Affirmation of Life**: It regards dying as a normal process and neither hastens nor postpones death. - **Integration**: Psychological and spiritual aspects are integrated into clinical patient management. - **Support System**: A robust network helps patients live as actively as possible until death, while supporting families during illness and bereavement.

Palliative Care Versus Curative Treatment

A common misconception views palliative care and curative treatment as mutually exclusive paths. In clinical practice, they often overlap. | Attribute | Curative Treatment | Palliative Care | | --- | --- | --- | | **Primary Goal** | Eradicate disease and prolong life | Maximize comfort and quality of life | | **Timing** | Typically initiated at diagnosis | Can begin at diagnosis alongside therapy | | **Focus** | Organ pathology and disease markers | Total personhood, symptoms, and family | Understanding this distinction prevents the premature withdrawal of disease-directed therapies when patients can still benefit from them, while ensuring comfort measures are never neglected.

The Multidisciplinary Palliative Care Team

Complex suffering cannot be managed by a single discipline. Effective palliative delivery requires a coordinated team where each member contributes specialized expertise. Nurses coordinate daily care and act as communication hubs; physicians manage complex medical regimens and diagnosis; social workers navigate financial burdens, family dynamics, and discharge planning; chaplains or spiritual leaders address existential distress; and pharmacists optimize complex analgesic dosing schedules. In resource-constrained Nigerian settings, team members often wear multiple hats, but collaborative communication remains vital to prevent gaps in patient safety and emotional support.

Holistic Assessment and Pain Management

Pain in palliative care is rarely purely physical; it is often compounded by anxiety, fatigue, and fear, a concept known as **total pain**. Assessment must be systematic, using validated tools such as numerical rating scales or visual analogue scales. Pharmacological management follows the World Health Organization analgesic ladder, moving from non-opioids to weak opioids, and finally to strong opioids like morphine. Nurses must actively dispel myths surrounding opioid addiction in terminal illness, ensuring that fear of dependence does not lead to undertreatment of severe pain.

Psychosocial, Cultural, and Spiritual Support

Addressing psychological distress requires active listening and validation of feelings such as grief, anger, and anticipatory loss. Culturally, Nigerian societies place immense value on community, family consensus, and spiritual faith during illness. Healthcare providers must respect cultural rituals, traditional beliefs, and religious practices without imposing personal biases. Spiritual assessment explores sources of hope, meaning, and forgiveness, recognizing that existential suffering can be just as debilitating as physical symptoms.

End-of-Life Care and Bereavement Support

As death approaches, clinical focus shifts to comfort measures, hygiene, and supporting family presence. Signs of imminent death include decreased level of consciousness, irregular breathing patterns such as Cheyne-Stokes respirations, cool extremities, and diminished urinary output. Invasive monitoring is discontinued. Bereavement support begins before death through anticipatory guidance and continues after loss, helping surviving family members navigate grief reactions and adjust to life without their loved one.

The Role of the Nurse in Palliative Care in Nigeria

Nigerian nurses operate within unique sociocultural and infrastructural environments. Challenges include limited availability of oral morphine, institutional skepticism regarding palliative approaches, and heavy nurse-to-patient ratios. Despite these hurdles, nurses serve as patient advocates, educators, and direct caregivers. They educate families on home care techniques, advocate for equitable pain relief policy reform, and provide compassionate presence. Empowering local nursing leadership in palliative care remains critical to bridging the gap between urban specialist centers and rural community healthcare delivery.
