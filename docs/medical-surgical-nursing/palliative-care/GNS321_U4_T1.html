Overview of Palliative Care

Introduction and Definition of Palliative Care

Palliative care is an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing problems associated with life-threatening illness. It prevents and relieves suffering through the early identification, impeccable assessment, and treatment of pain and other physical, psychosocial, and spiritual issues. Contrary to popular belief, this care is not reserved solely for the final days of life. It can be introduced at the time of diagnosis alongside disease-modifying therapies to manage distressing symptoms effectively.

Core Philosophy and Principles

The philosophy of palliative care rests on viewing the patient as a whole person rather than just a disease entity. Key principles guide clinical practice daily:

  • Patient-centred care: Respecting individual preferences, values, and goals in medical decision-making.
  • Quality of life focus: Maximizing comfort and functional ability while minimizing distress.
  • Family involvement: Treating the patient and family as a single unit of care.
  • Interdisciplinary teamwork: Combining various professional skills to address complex needs.

Palliative Care Versus Curative Treatment

Many patients and healthcare providers mistakenly view palliative care and curative treatment as mutually exclusive. Curative treatment aims to eradicate disease and restore health, often involving aggressive interventions like chemotherapy or surgery. Palliative care focuses on comfort and symptom relief, regardless of whether the disease can be cured. As a disease advances, the balance of care shifts, but palliative approaches can and should coexist with disease-directed therapies from day one.

The Multidisciplinary Palliative Care Team

Complex suffering requires a diverse team of professionals working in close coordination. A standard team includes doctors, nurses, medical social workers, pharmacists, physiotherapists, and spiritual care providers. Each discipline brings specialized skills to address physical symptoms, emotional distress, financial worries, and existential questions. Regular team meetings ensure that communication remains clear and care plans adapt as the patient's condition evolves.

Holistic Assessment and Pain Management

Pain in palliative care is rarely just physical; it encompasses total pain, which includes emotional, social, and spiritual dimensions. Nurses must conduct thorough, regular assessments using validated tools. Pharmacological management follows the WHO analgesic ladder, moving from non-opioids to weak opioids, and finally to strong opioids like morphine when necessary. Healthcare providers must address common fears regarding opioid addiction, tolerance, and respiratory depression through patient education.

Psychosocial, Cultural, and Spiritual Support

Addressing psychological distress involves helping patients and families cope with anxiety, depression, and anticipatory grief. Cultural norms heavily influence how illness and death are perceived, discussed, and managed within Nigerian communities. Respecting cultural practices and involving traditional or religious leaders when appropriate fosters trust. Spiritual support helps patients find meaning, resolve existential distress, and maintain hope, regardless of specific religious affiliations.

End-of-Life Care and Bereavement Support

As death approaches, care shifts toward ensuring comfort, dignity, and peacefulness. Signs of the active dying phase include changes in breathing patterns, coolness in extremities, and decreased responsiveness. Nurses manage symptoms meticulously, support family presence, and facilitate peaceful rituals. Bereavement support begins before death and continues afterward, helping families navigate grief through counseling, follow-up calls, and community support groups.

The Role of the Nurse in Palliative Care in Nigeria

Nurses form the backbone of palliative care delivery in Nigerian healthcare institutions. They coordinate patient care, administer medications, monitor symptom control, and serve as the primary communication link between the family and the medical team. In resource-limited settings, Nigerian nurses often advocate for essential medications like oral morphine, provide home-based care education, and combat cultural stigmas surrounding terminal illness through community outreach.
