Ethical and Human Rights Issues in End-of-Life Care

Introduction to End-of-Life Care and Nursing Ethics

End-of-life care centers on relieving suffering and maintaining the dignity of patients facing life-limiting illnesses. When curative treatments are no longer effective, the clinical focus shifts entirely to palliative comfort, symptom management, and psychological support. Nursing ethics provides the foundational framework guiding clinicians through these emotionally charged and morally complex scenarios. Ethical nursing practice requires balancing technological capabilities with humanistic values, ensuring that the preservation of life is never pursued at the cruel expense of prolonging agony.

Core Ethical Principles in Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Clinical decision-making at the end of life relies heavily on four bioethical pillars:

  • Autonomy: Respecting the patient's right to make independent choices about their healthcare, even when those choices conflict with medical advice.
  • Beneficence: Acting in the best interest of the patient to promote well-being and alleviate distress.
  • Non-maleficence: The obligation to 'do no harm,' which often translates to avoiding burdensome, futile interventions that merely prolong the dying process.
  • Justice: Ensuring fair and equitable distribution of healthcare resources and palliative support regardless of socioeconomic status.

Patient Human Rights and Self-Determination

Every individual retains fundamental human rights until the moment of death, including the right to bodily integrity, privacy, and freedom from cruel or degrading treatment. Self-determination empowers patients to dictate the terms of their final days. When healthcare providers impose unwanted medical interventions out of paternalism, they violate basic human rights. Respecting self-determination means accepting that a patient may prioritize comfort and personal closure over an extended quantity of life.

Informed Consent, Refusal of Treatment, and Advance Directives

Informed consent is not merely a signature on a paper; it is an ongoing communication process ensuring patients understand their diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment options. Just as patients have the right to consent to care, they hold an absolute legal and ethical right to refuse any medical intervention, even if that refusal will result in death. Advance directives—such as living wills and durable powers of attorney for healthcare—allow individuals to document their wishes beforehand, protecting their autonomy if they eventually lose the capacity to speak for themselves.

Navigating Euthanasia, Physician-Assisted Suicide, and Terminal Sedation

Clear distinctions must be maintained between different end-of-life practices. Euthanasia involves a clinician directly administering a lethal substance to end a patient's life, whereas physician-assisted suicide requires the clinician to provide the means while the patient self-administers it. Both remain illegal and culturally contentious in many jurisdictions, including Nigeria. Conversely, terminal sedation (palliative sedation) is an ethically accepted practice where medication is titrated to relieve intractable, unbearable suffering by rendering the patient unconscious, with the primary intent to comfort rather than hasten death.

Withholding Versus Withdrawing Life-Sustaining Treatment

A frequent ethical dilemma involves stopping or choosing not to start life support. Withholding treatment means deciding not to initiate a medical intervention, while withdrawing means stopping an intervention already in progress, such as turning off a mechanical ventilator. Ethically and legally, there is no meaningful distinction between the two; both are permissible if a treatment is deemed futile or against the expressed wishes of the patient or surrogate. Nurses often struggle emotionally with withdrawal, mistakenly perceiving it as an active act of killing rather than allowing the underlying disease to take its natural course.

Role of the Family, Surrogate Decision-Makers, and Conflicts

When a patient loses decision-making capacity, family members or designated surrogate decision-makers step in to guide care. Surrogates are ethically bound to use substituted judgment—making the choice the patient would have wanted—or act in the patient's best interests if the prior wishes are unknown. Conflict frequently arises when family members disagree among themselves, or when a surrogate demands aggressive, futile interventions that cause unnecessary suffering to the patient. Nurses must facilitate compassionate communication, mediate disputes, and advocate for the patient's welfare.

Cultural, Religious, and Legal Contexts in Nigerian Healthcare

End-of-life care in Nigeria is profoundly shaped by diverse cultural beliefs, Islamic and Christian traditions, and a pluralistic legal system comprising statutory, customary, and sharia laws. Many Nigerian communities view dying at home surrounded by extended family as the ideal, contrasting with Western hospital-centric models. Religious beliefs heavily influence pain perception, views on suffering, and the belief in miraculous recovery, which can sometimes delay acceptance of terminal prognoses. Furthermore, Nigerian statutory law does not formally recognize advance directives or legal frameworks for withdrawing life support in the same manner as some Western jurisdictions, creating unique medico-legal gray areas for practicing nurses.

Moral Distress and Support Strategies for Nurses

Nurses frequently experience moral distress when they know the ethically correct action to take but are constrained by institutional policies, physician refusal, or legal barriers. Witnessing patients endure painful, futile treatments causes emotional exhaustion, burnout, and compassion fatigue. Combating moral distress requires intentional support strategies: participating in ethics committees, engaging in reflective debriefing sessions with colleagues, establishing institutional channels for open dialogue, and practicing robust self-care. Acknowledging emotional pain is not a sign of weakness, but a vital step toward sustaining compassionate nursing practice.
