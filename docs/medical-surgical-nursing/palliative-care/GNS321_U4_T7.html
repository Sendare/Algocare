Ethical and Human Rights Issues in End-of-Life Care

Introduction to End-of-Life Care and Nursing Ethics

End-of-life care marks a critical phase where medical intervention shifts from curative treatment to comfort and dignity. Nurses often stand at the center of this transition, bridging the gap between medical technology, suffering patients, and anxious families. Nursing ethics provides the moral compass needed to navigate these intensely emotional spaces. When science offers countless ways to prolong biological life, the fundamental question shifts from what we can do to what we ought to do.

Every day, clinical decisions at the bedside carry profound moral weight. Ethical nursing practice requires more than following hospital protocols or doctor's orders; it demands deep reflection on human dignity, suffering, and the limits of modern medicine. As patient advocates, nurses ensure that the voices of the dying are not lost beneath the roar of hospital machinery.

Core Ethical Principles in Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Clinical decision-making relies on established ethical principles. Understanding these concepts helps nurses unpack tangled clinical dilemmas:

  • Autonomy: Respecting the patient's right to make their own choices about their body and healthcare, even when others disagree.
  • Beneficence: Acting in the best interest of the patient to promote wellbeing and alleviate suffering.
  • Non-maleficence: The duty to do no harm, avoiding unnecessary painful interventions that offer no real benefit.
  • Justice: Fair and equitable distribution of healthcare resources and care delivery without discrimination.

Informed Consent, Refusal of Treatment, and Advance Directives

Patients retain the right to direct their medical treatment until they lose the capacity to do so. Informed consent requires that patients receive clear, understandable information about risks, benefits, and alternatives before agreeing to any procedure. Just as patients can consent, competent adults also possess the legal and ethical right to refuse any treatment, including life-saving therapies.

When a terminal illness robs a person of their decision-making capacity, advance directives speak in their place. These legal documents include living wills, which outline specific medical treatments a person wants or rejects, and durable powers of attorney for healthcare, which designate a trusted proxy to make decisions. Nurses play a vital role in identifying, filing, and honoring these documents before emergencies strike.

Withholding Versus Withdrawing Life-Sustaining Treatment

A common source of moral anxiety among junior nurses is the distinction between withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment.

  • Withholding treatment: Deciding not to start a medical intervention, such as declining to initiate dialysis or mechanical ventilation.
  • Withdrawing treatment: Stopping a medical intervention that has already been started, such as turning off a ventilator or removing a feeding tube.

Ethically and legally, there is no difference between the two. Both actions recognize that the burden of treatment outweighs the benefit, and both allow the underlying disease to take its natural course. However, withdrawing treatment feels psychologically heavier for healthcare providers because it requires a deliberate, active physical step.

Navigating Euthanasia, Physician-Assisted Suicide, and Terminal Sedation

End-of-life debates often touch on active measures to end suffering. Euthanasia occurs when a clinician or another person directly administers a lethal dose of medication to end a patient's life, usually at the patient's request. Physician-assisted suicide involves the clinician providing the means—such as prescribing a lethal dose of medication—for the patient to self-administer. Both practices remain illegal in most countries, including Nigeria, and clash directly with traditional nursing codes of ethics that prohibit intentionally ending life.

By contrast, terminal sedation (palliative sedation) is ethically and legally distinct. It involves administering sedatives to relieve refractory, unbearable symptoms by rendering the patient unconscious until natural death occurs. The primary intent is comfort, not death, making it an accepted clinical practice.

Role of the Family, Surrogate Decision-Makers, and Conflicts

When a dying patient loses capacity, families step in as surrogate decision-makers. The goal of surrogate decision-making is substituted judgment—making the choice the patient would have made for themselves—or deciding based on the patient's best interests if their wishes are unknown.

Complications arise when family members disagree with each other or when the family's demands conflict with the clinical team or the patient's prior stated wishes. Nurses must mediate these tensions with empathy and cultural sensitivity. Protecting the patient remains the primary duty, which means a surrogate cannot demand treatments that are medically futile or harmful.

Cultural, Religious, and Legal Contexts in Nigerian Healthcare

End-of-life care in Nigeria is deeply shaped by cultural, communal, and religious values. Unlike Western models that prioritize individual autonomy above all else, Nigerian societies often view decision-making through a communal lens. Extended families frequently participate in discussions regarding prognosis, treatment limits, and funeral arrangements. Islam and Christianity, the two dominant religions, heavily influence perspectives on suffering, pain, and the sanctity of life, often leading families to favor aggressive interventions based on faith in divine healing.

Legally, formal advance directives and living wills are uncommon and lack robust statutory frameworks in Nigerian jurisprudence. Criminal codes explicitly prohibit assisted suicide and euthanasia. Consequently, Nigerian nurses must balance Western bioethical principles with deep respect for family-centered dynamics and spiritual beliefs.

Moral Distress and Support Strategies for Nurses

Caring for dying patients while managing conflicting demands takes a heavy psychological toll. Moral distress occurs when a nurse knows the ethically correct action to take but is constrained by institutional rules, physician orders, or legal barriers from performing it. Repeated exposure to futile treatments, unmanaged pain, and distressed families can lead to burnout, emotional exhaustion, and leaving the profession.

Combating moral distress requires intentional support strategies. Hospitals must foster open communication through ethics rounds and debriefing sessions. Nurses themselves must build resilience by recognizing their emotional limits, leaning on supportive colleagues, and engaging in reflective practice.
