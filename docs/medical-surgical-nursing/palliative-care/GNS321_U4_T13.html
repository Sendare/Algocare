Care and Support in Palliative Care

Introduction to Palliative Care and Holistic Support

Palliative care is an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing problems associated with life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial and spiritual. Unlike curative treatment, which aims to reverse or halt disease progression, palliative care focuses entirely on comfort and dignity, and it can be delivered alongside disease-modifying therapies from the time of diagnosis. Holistic nursing in this context means viewing the client as an integrated whole rather than a collection of failing organ systems. In Nigerian clinical settings, where family structures and cultural beliefs heavily influence health decisions, integrating holistic support requires working closely with relatives and respecting community values. The goal remains maintaining optimal functional capacity and reducing distress at every stage of the illness trajectory.

Assessment of Physical Symptoms and Pain Management

Pain is among the most feared symptoms for individuals with advanced illness, yet it is entirely manageable when assessed systematically. The WHO analgesic ladder guides pharmacological intervention, starting with non-opioids for mild discomfort, progressing to weak opioids for moderate pain, and utilizing strong opioids like morphine for severe pain. Regular re-evaluation using validated tools, such as the numerical rating scale or behavioral observation charts for non-verbal clients, ensures dosing is adequate. Beyond pain, nurses must actively screen for other distressing physical signs:

  • Fatigue: Profound exhaustion that does not resolve with rest, requiring energy conservation education.
  • Dyspnea: A subjective sensation of breathlessness managed with low-dose opioids, fans, and positioning.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Managed through targeted antiemetics based on the underlying physiological trigger.
  • Cachexia: Severe weight loss and muscle wasting managed with nutritional support tailored to comfort rather than strict dietary requirements.

Psychological and Emotional Support for Patients

A serious, life-limiting diagnosis triggers a complex psychological cascade, frequently moving through stages of shock, denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and eventual acceptance. Mental distress often manifests somatically, mimicking or worsening physical complaints. Active listening, validating emotional responses, and offering a calm, non-judgmental presence form the core of nursing intervention. Avoid offering false reassurances like 'everything will be fine,' which isolates the suffering individual. Instead, encourage verbalization of fears regarding loss of autonomy, body image alterations, and the impending end of life. Referral to psychiatric or counseling specialists is warranted when sadness deepens into clinical depression or unmanageable anxiety.

Addressing Spiritual and Existential Needs

Existential distress surfaces when individuals confront the finitude of life, questioning their worth, legacy, and the meaning of their suffering. Spiritual needs extend far beyond formal religious practices, encompassing the search for peace, forgiveness, and connection with a higher power or humanity. Nurses must perform a basic spiritual screen, identifying sources of hope as well as spiritual pain, such as feelings of abandonment or guilt. Facilitating visits from spiritual leaders, traditional healers, or pastoral care workers according to the client's preference bridges a vital gap in holistic healing. Creating space for life review allows individuals to articulate their life's achievements and reconcile broken relationships, fostering a peaceful transition.

Family and Caregiver Support Strategies

Illness never happens in isolation; the family unit shares the burden of diagnosis, physical caregiving, and eventual loss. Caregiver burnout is a pervasive risk, characterized by physical exhaustion, chronic anxiety, and social withdrawal. Assessing the readiness and capacity of family caregivers prevents catastrophic breakdown of the support system. Education on safe patient transfer techniques, medication administration, and recognizing signs of deterioration empowers relatives and reduces anxiety. Respite care options, where available, offer essential relief intervals for exhausted family members. In many Nigerian communities, caregiving is distributed across extended family networks, requiring nurses to coordinate communication with key family elders and primary decision-makers.

Communication and Advance Care Planning

Transparent, empathetic communication builds trust and ensures client autonomy is respected throughout the trajectory of serious illness. Advance care planning involves discussing and documenting future medical preferences while the client retains decision-making capacity. Nurses often act as facilitators, helping clients articulate their wishes regarding resuscitation, artificial nutrition, and preferred places of care. In environments where discussing death is culturally taboo or viewed as inviting misfortune, communication must be gentle, honoring family dynamics while ensuring the client's voice is heard. Clear documentation prevents unwanted, aggressive interventions at the end of life and spares grieving families from making agonizing guesses during crises.

End-of-Life Care and Comfort Measures

Active dying presents distinct physiological signs that signal the final hours or days of life. Recognizing these changes—such as Cheyne-Stokes respirations, the 'death rattle' caused by pooled upper airway secretions, mottling of the extremities, and diminishing consciousness—prepares the family and directs nursing interventions toward strict comfort. Invasive monitoring, routine blood draws, and burdensome diagnostic tests are discontinued. Oral care with swabs keeps moist mucosa comfortable, while repositioning prevents pressure injuries. Family members should be encouraged to touch, talk to, and sit with the dying person, as hearing is often the last sense to fade.

Bereavement and Grief Support in Nursing Practice

Bereavement is the period of grief and mourning after a death, a natural response to loss that varies immensely across individuals and cultures. Normal grief involves waves of sadness, longing, and sleep disruption, whereas complicated grief is characterized by prolonged, debilitating stagnation that impairs daily functioning. Nursing care does not terminate at the moment of death; supporting the surviving family through condolence visits, facilitating appropriate mortuary or funeral rites, and providing referral information for support groups are vital concluding duties. Nurses must also process their own grief, acknowledging the emotional toll of repeated exposure to loss to prevent compassion fatigue and burnout.
