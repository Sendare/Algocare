Nutritional Care in Palliative Care

Introduction to Nutritional Care in Palliative Settings

Nutritional care in palliative settings shifts its primary focus significantly compared to curative healthcare environments. While standard nutrition aims at growth, prevention of deficiencies, and tissue repair, palliative nutrition prioritizes patient comfort, symptom reduction, and overall quality of life. For nursing students practicing in Nigeria, understanding this paradigm shift is crucial. As patients approach the advanced stages of terminal illnesses such as cancer, heart failure, or end-stage renal disease, their metabolic demands and natural desires regarding food change drastically. Forcing a terminally ill patient to eat or drink can induce unnecessary physical distress, nausea, vomiting, and psychological anxiety for both the patient and their grieving family members. Instead, nursing interventions must center on holistic care, honoring the social and emotional significance of food while respecting the physiological reality that the dying body no longer processes nutrients the way it used to. Nurses act as educators and advocates, guiding families to understand that decreased oral intake is a normal part of the dying trajectory rather than a form of starvation. By reframing nutrition as a source of pleasure and comfort rather than a strict medical requirement, nurses can alleviate guilt and foster a more peaceful end-of-life experience.

Metabolic and Physiological Changes in Advanced Illness

Advanced illnesses induce profound metabolic and physiological alterations that fundamentally change how the human body processes energy. Conditions like advanced cancer and organ failure frequently trigger cachexia and anorexia-cachexia syndrome, which is characterized by systemic inflammation, hypermetabolism, and muscle wasting that cannot be reversed simply by increasing caloric intake. Unlike simple starvation where the body conserves lean muscle mass by burning fat reserves, illness-related cachexia actively breaks down skeletal muscle and adipose tissue due to pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukins. Furthermore, the gastrointestinal tract often experiences delayed gastric emptying, reduced enzyme secretion, and decreased absorptive surface area, which leads to early satiety, bloating, and discomfort even after consuming small amounts of food. Organs of elimination and filtration also slow down, meaning that fluid retention, edema, and uremia can develop if hydration is aggressively forced. Nigerian nursing students must recognize these intrinsic physiological changes so they can differentiate between normal end-of-life metabolic decline and correctable nutritional deficiencies. Understanding these shifts helps nurses provide realistic expectations to multidisciplinary teams and families, ensuring that care plans align with what the patient's body can safely and comfortably handle.

Nutritional Assessment and Screening Tools

Nutritional assessment and screening in palliative care require a specialized approach that balances clinical thoroughness with patient burden. Standard anthropometric measurements and rigorous biochemical panels used in acute care may cause unnecessary pain, fatigue, and distress to a frail, terminally ill patient. Therefore, palliative nutritional assessments rely heavily on validated clinical tools such as the Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) or the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), modified to suit the patient's fatigue levels and prognosis. Nurses evaluate functional status, changes in weight over time, physical symptoms affecting eating (such as pain, dry mouth, or nausea), and the patient's personal goals regarding food. Rather than focusing solely on numerical weight loss or body mass index, the assessment explores the psychological and emotional impact of eating alterations. It is vital to determine whether the patient still finds pleasure in eating or if food has become a source of dread and physical suffering. In Nigerian clinical settings, incorporating family insights through careful history-taking is also essential, as family members often observe subtle daily changes in appetite and swallowing. Ultimately, the nutritional assessment guides the healthcare team in formulating a compassionate, individualized care plan that prioritizes dignity and comfort above strict dietary compliance.

Symptom Management Affecting Nutrition

Symptom management is the cornerstone of preserving whatever nutritional intake a palliative patient desires and can physically handle. Common symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, pain, fatigue, dyspnea, mucositis, constipation, and xerostomia (dry mouth) frequently impair a patient's willingness and ability to eat. For instance, uncontrolled chronic pain can completely suppress appetite, while severe dyspnea makes the coordination of chewing, swallowing, and breathing exhausting. Nurses play a pivotal frontline role in identifying these barriers and implementing timely pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. Administering antiemetics prior to mealtimes, ensuring adequate analgesia, and providing meticulous oral hygiene to manage xerostomia and fungal infections can drastically improve a patient's readiness to take small sips or bites of food. Dietary adjustments should also match the patient's symptoms; offering smaller, frequent meals rather than large plates, serving foods at room temperature to minimize strong odors that trigger nausea, and utilizing flavor enhancers when taste perception is altered can all make a significant difference. Educating the patient and family on these comfort measures empowers them to manage day-to-day eating challenges. By successfully mitigating distressing symptoms, nurses help restore a sense of normalcy and dignity around mealtimes, allowing patients to enjoy food purely for pleasure without suffering adverse physical consequences.

Comfort Feeding and Modified Diets

Comfort feeding and modified diets represent essential components of holistic palliative care nursing. When patients can no longer manage standard textures due to dysphagia, fatigue, or cognitive decline, transitioning to modified diets—such as pureed, minced, or soft foods—can maintain safe oral intake and reduce the risk of aspiration pneumonia. However, modifications must also look appetizing and retain distinct flavors to stimulate the patient's waning appetite. When even modified foods become too burdensome, the philosophy shifts entirely to 'comfort feeding' or 'hand-feeding for pleasure'. In this approach, restrictions are completely lifted, and patients are offered tiny tastes of their favorite culturally familiar foods, ice chips, or sips of juice purely for sensory satisfaction, hydration of the oral mucosa, and social connection. The emphasis is removed from caloric counts or nutritional adequacy; instead, the focus is on the human interaction, love, and care conveyed during the feeding process. Nurses must educate families that comfort feeding is a profound act of love and care, replacing the anxiety of forcing nutrition with the peacefulness of savoring a favorite flavor. Safety remains a priority, requiring appropriate patient positioning at a 45 to 90-degree angle, careful observation for signs of fatigue or choking, and stopping immediately if the patient shows disinterest or distress.

Ethical and Cultural Considerations in Artificial Nutrition and Hydration

The initiation or withdrawal of artificial nutrition and hydration (ANH), such as nasogastric tubes or percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tubes, raises complex ethical and cultural dilemmas in palliative care. Nigerian nurses frequently navigate these challenges within diverse cultural and religious frameworks where food and water carry deep symbolic meanings of hospitality, care, and life preservation. Ethically, the principle of beneficence requires healthcare providers to act in the patient's best interest, while autonomy respects the patient's prior wishes or the decisions of authorized surrogate decision-makers. In advanced terminal illness, clinical evidence demonstrates that ANH does not improve survival, reduce the risk of aspiration, or enhance quality of life; rather, it can cause fluid overload, tube site infections, gastrointestinal distress, and the need for physical restraints in confused patients. Nurses must facilitate empathetic, transparent discussions with families, helping them understand that foregoing or withdrawing ANH is not synonymous with abandonment or starvation, but rather a compassionate choice to allow natural death without mechanical prolongation. Cultural sensitivities must be handled with utmost respect, ensuring that family concerns are heard, religious leaders are involved when desired, and all decisions are thoroughly documented in alignment with local legal and professional standards of practice.

Supporting Patients and Families Through Changing Nutritional Needs

Supporting patients and families through changing nutritional needs requires deep emotional intelligence, therapeutic communication, and continuous education from the nursing team. In many cultures across Nigeria, preparing and sharing food is a primary expression of love, care, and communal bonding. When a dying loved one stops eating, families often experience profound feelings of helplessness, guilt, and grief, interpreting the refusal of food as a sign that they are failing as caregivers. Nurses play a transformative role by providing anticipatory guidance, preparing families well in advance for the natural decline in appetite and metabolic needs. By validating their emotional distress and teaching alternative ways to show love—such as reading aloud, gentle massage, moistening lips, holding hands, or sharing memories—nurses help families redirect their nurturing energy away from the kitchen and toward meaningful emotional presence. Furthermore, nurses support the patient by validating their loss of appetite, ensuring they feel no pressure or guilt for eating very little. Creating a peaceful, unhurried environment during mealtimes and honoring personal preferences fosters dignity and emotional comfort. Ultimately, navigating this transition with compassion strengthens the therapeutic alliance, helping families find peace and acceptance during the end-of-life journey.
