Clinical Assessment in Palliative Care

Introduction to Clinical Assessment in Palliative Care

Clinical assessment in palliative care is a continuous, dynamic process focused on improving the quality of life for patients facing life-limiting illnesses and their families. Unlike curative medicine, which primarily aims at disease eradication or arrest, palliative assessment shifts its priority toward comfort, symptom control, and the preservation of dignity. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding this paradigm shift is crucial, as it prepares you to look beyond vital signs and laboratory results to address the multi-dimensional suffering experienced by patients. Assessment in this context begins at the time of diagnosis and extends through the disease trajectory into bereavement care for the family. It involves identifying physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs early to prevent crises and reduce unnecessary hospital admissions. Nurses serve as the primary coordinators of care, utilizing regular, systematic evaluations to detect subtle changes in the patient's condition. By establishing open communication channels and trusting relationships, nurses can elicit the true goals of care from patients and their families, ensuring that care plans remain aligned with their personal values and cultural beliefs, especially within the diverse socio-cultural landscape of Nigerian healthcare settings.

Holistic Patient and Family-Centred Evaluation

A holistic patient and family-centred evaluation recognizes that an illness affects not just the individual patient, but the entire family unit. In many Nigerian communities, family members play a central role in caregiving, decision-making, and financial support. Therefore, nurses must assess the family's coping mechanisms, resilience, and potential caregiver burnout. Holistic evaluation integrates the physical, psychological, social, and spiritual dimensions of care, viewing the patient as a whole person rather than a collection of symptoms. During the assessment, nurses should actively listen to the patient's narrative, acknowledging their unique experiences, fears, and cultural background. Family members should be included in discussions as appropriate, while respecting the patient's autonomy and confidentiality. Evaluating the family dynamics helps identify who the primary caregiver is, what support systems are available, and whether cultural or religious obligations might impact the acceptance of certain palliative interventions. By engaging both patient and family in the assessment process, nurses can design individualised care plans that respect family values, foster therapeutic alliances, and alleviate the collective burden of chronic or terminal illness.

Comprehensive Pain Assessment and Management

Pain is one of the most feared and prevalent symptoms among palliative care patients, making comprehensive pain assessment critical. Nurses must evaluate pain using validated assessment tools appropriate for the patient's cognitive and developmental status, such as the Numeric Rating Scale, Visual Analogue Scale, or behavioral observation scales for non-verbal patients. A thorough assessment must explore the multidimensional nature of pain, categorized by the mnemonic OPQRST: Onset, Provoking/palliating factors, Quality, Region/radiation, Severity, and Temporal characteristics. Furthermore, nurses need to distinguish between different types of pain, including somatic, visceral, and neuropathic pain, as each requires specific pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. Total pain is another vital concept in palliative care, acknowledging that physical pain is frequently intensified by psychological anxiety, social isolation, and spiritual distress. Regular reassessment is essential, especially after initiating or modifying analgesic therapy, following the WHO analgesic ladder principles. Nigerian nursing students must also be aware of cultural expressions of pain, ensuring that stoicism or vocal emotional displays are neither dismissed nor misinterpreted, but rather understood within the patient's unique cultural framework.

Evaluating Non-Pain Physical Symptoms

Beyond pain, palliative care patients frequently suffer from a constellation of distressing physical symptoms that severely impact their daily functioning and quality of life. Common symptoms include dyspnoea, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, constipation, delirium, and cachexia. Evaluating these non-pain symptoms requires systematic screening using targeted assessment tools and careful clinical observation. For instance, dyspnoea should be assessed not only by measuring respiratory rate and oxygen saturation, but also by exploring the subjective feeling of breathlessness experienced by the patient. Fatigue must be differentiated from normal tiredness, assessing its impact on the patient's ability to perform activities of daily living. Gastrointestinal symptoms like nausea and constipation must be evaluated proactively, especially since opioids commonly prescribed for pain can induce constipation. Nurses should inquire about bowel habits, dietary intake, and fluid balance regularly. Identifying the underlying cause of each symptom—whether disease progression, medication side effect, or metabolic imbalance—guides effective intervention. In resource-limited settings, nurses must rely heavily on thorough clinical history-taking, physical examination, and basic symptom assessment scales to prioritize which physical discomforts require immediate palliative relief.

Psychological and Emotional Status Assessment

Psychological and emotional distress is highly prevalent among individuals facing life-limiting illnesses, manifesting as anxiety, depression, grief, anger, and loss of control. Evaluating psychological status requires empathetic communication, active listening, and the use of validated screening tools such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale or the distress thermometer. Nurses must create a safe, non-judgmental environment where patients feel comfortable expressing difficult emotions without fear of burdening their caregivers or healthcare providers. It is essential to differentiate between normal sadness or grief associated with terminal illness and clinical depression that requires intervention. Nurses should explore the patient's understanding of their diagnosis, their coping strategies, and their fears regarding the future, dying, or the well-being of their dependents. In many Nigerian communities, mental health issues may carry social stigma, making patients reluctant to voice emotional pain. Therefore, nurses must approach psychological assessment with cultural sensitivity, framing emotional well-being as an integral component of overall health. Early identification of psychological distress allows for timely multidisciplinary referrals to counselors, social workers, or mental health professionals, significantly improving the patient's emotional resilience and coping capacity.

Spiritual and Existential Distress Evaluation

Spiritual and existential distress evaluation is a core component of comprehensive palliative care. Spirituality in this context extends beyond formal religion to encompass a person's search for meaning, purpose, connection, and peace in life. As patients approach the end of life, they frequently grapple with existential questions such as 'Why is this happening to me?', 'What is the meaning of my life?', and 'What awaits me after death?' Nurses can assess spiritual well-being using simple screening tools like the FICA spiritual history tool, which explores Faith, Importance/influence, Community, and Address in care. It is imperative that nurses approach spiritual assessments with neutrality, empathy, and deep respect for the patient's belief system, whether Christian, Islamic, traditional, or secular. In Nigeria, religion and spiritual beliefs heavily influence how individuals make sense of illness, suffering, and mortality. Unresolved spiritual distress can significantly exacerbate physical and emotional suffering. When spiritual needs are identified, nurses should facilitate access to appropriate spiritual care providers, such as pastoral counselors, imams, or tribal spiritual leaders, ensuring the patient's holistic needs are fully met.

Social and Practical Support Needs Assessment

Social and practical support needs assessment is essential for ensuring that patients and their families can navigate terminal illness with dignity and adequate resources. Life-limiting illnesses often impose severe financial strain, loss of income, housing instability, and disruption of family roles. In Nigeria, where social security systems are limited, out-of-pocket healthcare expenses can impoverish entire families. Nurses must evaluate the patient's living situation, financial resources, insurance or out-of-pocket capacity, and availability of informal caregivers. Practical support assessments should also identify legal needs, such as drafting a will, appointing a healthcare proxy, or organizing guardianship for dependent children. Evaluating social needs involves examining the patient's support network, identifying potential sources of isolation, and assessing caregiver strain. Social workers and community health officers are vital partners in addressing these challenges. By conducting a thorough social and practical assessment, nurses can mobilize community resources, connect families with charitable organizations or governmental support where available, and advocate for interventions that reduce the socio-economic burdens associated with prolonged terminal illness.

Prognostication and Recognizing the Dying Phase

Prognostication and recognizing the dying phase are critical nursing competencies that guide timely and appropriate end-of-life care. While predicting the exact trajectory of life-limiting illnesses is notoriously difficult, nurses must be skilled in identifying indicators of disease progression and the transition into the active dying phase (the last days or hours of life). Recognizing physical signs of the dying phase—such as reduced oral intake, excessive somnolence, altered breathing patterns (like Cheyne-Stokes respiration), mottling of the skin, cool extremities, and a decreased level of consciousness—allows nurses to prepare the family and adjust the care plan. Accurate recognition prevents inappropriate, burdensome interventions and shifts the focus entirely to comfort measures, such as mouth care, positioning, and psychological reassurance. Nurses must communicate these clinical changes honestly, compassionately, and sensitively to the family, allowing them time to gather, say their goodbyes, and participate in cultural or religious death rituals. Documenting and observing these terminal signs ensures that the patient passes with dignity and that the family receives the guidance and support they need during this profound transition.

Documentation and Interdisciplinary Care Planning

Documentation and interdisciplinary care planning are essential for maintaining continuity of care, ensuring patient safety, and honoring patient and family wishes throughout the palliative care journey. Palliative care involves a diverse team of professionals, including physicians, nurses, social workers, pharmacists, and spiritual care providers. Clear, objective, and timely documentation ensures that all team members are informed of the patient's evolving symptom status, care goals, and preferences. Interdisciplinary care planning meetings allow the team to review assessment findings, coordinate interventions, and anticipate potential clinical or social crises. Nurses play a pivotal role in recording advance care planning discussions, Do-Not-Resuscitate (DNR) orders, and preferred place of care or death, while ensuring these documents comply with institutional and legal standards. In Nigerian clinical settings, comprehensive documentation also serves as a legal record and improves communication across different shifts and departments. By keeping meticulous notes and fostering active collaboration among multidisciplinary team members, nurses help safeguard the patient's dignity, minimize communication gaps, and ensure seamless delivery of compassionate, holistic care.
