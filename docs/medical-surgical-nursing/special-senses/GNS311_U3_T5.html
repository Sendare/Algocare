Conditions Affecting Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT)

Anatomy and Physiology of the ENT System

The ear, nose, and throat form an interconnected anatomical network responsible for hearing, balance, olfaction, respiration, and phonation. The ear is divided into three regions: the **external ear** (collects sound waves), the **middle ear** (contains the malleus, incus, and stapes which amplify vibrations), and the **inner ear** (houses the cochlea for hearing and the semicircular canals for equilibrium via the vestibulocochlear nerve). Air enters through the nasal cavity, which is lined with a highly vascular mucous membrane that warms, moistens, and filters inspired air before it reaches the nasopharynx. The paranasal sinuses—frontal, ethmoid, sphenoid, and maxillary—act as resonance chambers for the voice and reduce skull weight. The pharynx connects the nasal and oral cavities to the larynx and esophagus, housing the tonsils and adenoids which play a primary role in immune defense. Finally, the larynx protects the lower airway during swallowing and contains the vocal cords for speech production.

Comprehensive ENT Assessment and Physical Examination

Clinical evaluation of the ENT system requires a systematic approach using specialized instruments and observational skills. Assessment begins with a thorough health history focusing on hearing loss, vertigo, pain, tinnitus, nasal obstruction, or dysphagia. - **Otoscopic Examination**: Used to inspect the external auditory canal and tympanic membrane. In adults, pull the pinna upward and backward; in young children, pull downward and straight back to straighten the canal. - **Nasal Inspection**: Utilizes a nasal speculum or penlight to evaluate mucosal color, septal deviation, polyps, and turbinate hypertrophy. - **Throat and Oral Cavity Assessment**: Involves a tongue depressor and adequate light source to inspect the tonsils, posterior pharynx, and oral mucosa for exudate, asymmetry, or lesions. - **Voice and Hearing Screening**: Simple bedside tests include the **whisper test** for hearing acuity and the **Rinne and Weber tests** utilizing a tuning fork to differentiate conductive from sensorineural hearing loss.

Common Ear Disorders and Nursing Management

Ear pathologies frequently cause pain, impaired communication, and balance disturbances. **Otitis externa** (swimmer's ear) involves inflammation of the external auditory canal, presenting with severe pain upon manipulation of the pinna. **Otitis media** is an infection of the middle ear, common in pediatric populations due to shorter, more horizontal Eustachian tubes, often managed with analgesics and appropriate antibiotic therapy. **Meniere's disease** is a disorder of the inner ear characterized by the classic triad of episodic vertigo, tinnitus, and fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss resulting from endolymphatic hydrops. Nursing care focuses on safety precautions during vertigo attacks, low-sodium dietary adjustments, and administering prescribed diuretics or antiemetics. **Presbycusis** represents age-related sensorineural hearing loss, requiring environmental adaptations such as facing the patient when speaking and minimizing background noise.

Nasal and Sinus Pathologies

Disorders of the nose and sinuses disrupt respiration, olfaction, and sinus drainage, frequently leading to secondary infections. **Allergic rhinitis** triggers mucosal edema, sneezing, and clear rhinorrhea upon exposure to airborne allergens. **Epistaxis** (nosebleed) is common, often originating from Kiesselbach's plexus on the anterior nasal septum. First-line nursing management for epistaxis involves having the patient sit upright, lean forward, and apply direct lateral nasal pressure for 10 to 15 minutes. **Sinusitis** involves inflammation of the sinus mucous membranes, causing facial pain, purulent nasal discharge, and maxillary toothache. Chronic nasal obstruction can also stem from a deviated nasal septum or nasal polyps, which may require surgical correction if conservative medical management fails.

Throat and Upper Airway Conditions

Infections and inflammatory processes in the throat can rapidly compromise the upper airway, making rapid assessment critical. **Pharyngitis** (sore throat) can be viral or bacterial, with streptococcal infections requiring antibiotic therapy to prevent severe systemic sequelae such as rheumatic fever. **Tonsillitis** involves inflammation of the palatine tonsils, which can progress to a **peritonsillar abscess**, characterized by severe throat pain, trismus (difficulty opening the mouth), and a muffled "hot potato" voice. **Epiglottitis** is a life-threatening medical emergency caused by rapid bacterial inflammation of the epiglottis, leading to acute airway obstruction. Cardinal signs include drooling, dysphagia, distress, and stridor. The nurse must never attempt to examine the throat with a tongue depressor in a patient suspected of epiglottitis, as this can trigger sudden, fatal laryngospasm.

Surgical Interventions and Perioperative ENT Care

Surgical procedures in the ENT specialty range from minor outpatient procedures to extensive reconstructive surgeries. Common interventions include **tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy (T&A)**, **tympanoplasty** (reconstruction of the eardrum), and **rhinoplasty**. Postoperative nursing care centers heavily on maintaining a patent airway and monitoring for hemorrhage. Following a tonsillectomy, the nurse must monitor for frequent swallowing, which is an early and reliable indicator of bleeding. The patient should be kept in a semi-Fowler position to reduce edema and instructed to avoid coughing, clearing the throat, or using straws. Pain control is essential to encourage oral fluid intake, utilizing prescribed analgesics regularly rather than on an as-needed basis.

Potential Complications and Emergency ENT Care

ENT conditions and surgeries carry unique risks that can escalate rapidly into life-threatening airway emergencies. **Airway obstruction** can occur secondary to severe edema, foreign body aspiration, hematoma formation, or laryngospasm. Emergency management requires immediate availability of suction equipment, bag-valve-mask devices, and emergency tracheostomy trays at the bedside. Following ear surgeries, damage to the facial nerve (Cranial Nerve VII) is a serious potential complication, assessed by checking for facial symmetry, smiling, and eye closure. Deep neck space infections can also arise from untreated dental or pharyngeal sources, leading to mediastinitis or airway compromise if not aggressively treated with intravenous antibiotics and surgical drainage.
