Conditions Affecting Ear, Nose and Throat (ENT)

Anatomy and Physiology of the ENT System

The ear, nose, and throat structures form a tightly interconnected system responsible for vital senses and protective functions. The ear is divided into the external ear, middle ear, and inner ear. The external ear channels sound waves toward the tympanic membrane. Within the middle ear, three small bones called the ossicles—the malleus, incus, and stapes—amplify these vibrations and transmit them to the inner ear. The inner ear contains the cochlea for hearing and the vestibular system for balance.

The nose and paranasal sinuses warm, filter, and humidify inspired air while housing olfactory receptors. The nasal cavity connects directly to the nasopharynx, which flows into the oropharynx and laryngopharynx. The throat contains the tonsils and adenoids, which are masses of lymphoid tissue acting as a first line of immune defense against inhaled pathogens. Below lies the larynx, protecting the lower airway during swallowing and housing the vocal cords for speech.

Comprehensive ENT Assessment Techniques

A thorough ENT physical examination requires specific tools and a systematic approach. The nurse inspects the external ear for symmetry, lesions, and discharge, then uses an otoscope to visualize the tympanic membrane. Normal tympanic membranes appear pearly grey, translucent, and intact with a visible light reflex.

Nasal assessment involves inspecting the external contour and using a nasal speculum or penlight to evaluate the nasal mucosa and septum for swelling, bleeding, or deviation. The nurse inspects the oral cavity and throat using a tongue depressor and light source, noting the size of the tonsils, color of the pharyngeal mucosa, and presence of exudate.

  • Otoscopy: Pull the pinna up and back in adults, or down and back in children, to straighten the ear canal for safe viewing.
  • Palpation: Gently press over the frontal and maxillary sinuses to check for tenderness indicating inflammation.
  • Voice testing: Assess crude hearing using the whispered voice test from a distance of two feet.

Common Ear Disorders and Nursing Care

Ear disorders frequently involve either conductive or sensorineural hearing loss, infections, or balance disturbances. Otitis media is an infection of the middle ear often seen in children due to a short, horizontal Eustachian tube. Symptoms include otalgia, fever, and a bulging, erythematous eardrum. Otitis externa, commonly called swimmer's ear, involves inflammation of the external ear canal characterized by pain when manipulating the auricle.

Meniere's disease is an inner ear disorder marked by episodic vertigo, tinnitus, fluctuating hearing loss, and a feeling of fullness in the ear. Nursing care focuses on safety during vertigo attacks, administering anti-vertigo medications, and educating patients on low-sodium diets to reduce endolymphatic fluid pressure.

Nasal and Sinus Pathologies

Nasal and sinus disorders disrupt normal breathing, olfaction, and drainage. Allergic rhinitis causes sneezing, nasal congestion, and clear rhinorrhea triggered by environmental allergens. Sinusitis involves inflammation of the paranasal sinuses, which can be acute or chronic, leading to facial pain, purulent nasal discharge, and headache.

Epistaxis, or nosebleeds, can arise from local trauma or systemic conditions like hypertension. Most nosebleeds originate from Kiesselbach's plexus on the anterior nasal septum. Nursing management for an anterior nosebleed includes having the patient sit upright and lean forward, applying direct lateral pressure to the soft nose for 10 to 15 minutes, and advising against blowing the nose afterward.

Throat and Upper Airway Conditions

Throat conditions range from mild pharyngitis to life-threatening airway obstruction. Acute pharyngitis, commonly known as a sore throat, can be viral or bacterial (such as Group A streptococcus). Streptococcal infections require antibiotic therapy to prevent serious complications like rheumatic fever.

Tonsillitis involves inflammation of the palatine tonsils. When chronic or recurrent, it may necessitate a tonsillectomy. Epiglottitis is a medical emergency characterized by rapid inflammation of the epiglottis, often caused by *Haemophilus influenzae*. It leads to severe drooling, stridor, and respiratory distress. Nurses must never use a tongue depressor to inspect the throat if epiglottitis is suspected, as this can trigger sudden and complete airway closure.

Surgical Interventions and Perioperative Care

Surgical procedures in ENT nursing require meticulous preoperative preparation and vigilant postoperative monitoring. Common surgeries include tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy (T&A), myringotomy with tympanostomy tube placement, and rhinoplasty.

Postoperative nursing care centers heavily on maintaining a patent airway and monitoring for hemorrhage. Following a tonsillectomy, persistent frequent swallowing is an early sign of bleeding. The nurse monitors vital signs, provides ice collars to reduce swelling and pain, and ensures the patient is positioned on their side or semi-Fowler's until fully awake. Diet progression starts with clear liquids, avoiding red-colored fluids, citrus juices, and rough foods that can irritate the surgical site.

Potential Complications and Emergency Care

ENT emergencies demand rapid clinical assessment and decisive intervention to preserve life and sensory function. Airway obstruction can occur secondary to trauma, foreign bodies, severe angioedema, or epiglottitis. Signs of impending airway closure include stridor, use of accessory muscles, cyanosis, and severe restlessness.

If upper airway obstruction cannot be relieved, emergency procedures such as a tracheostomy or cricothyroidotomy become necessary. Nurses must keep emergency equipment, including suction apparatus, bag-valve-mask devices, and a tracheostomy insertion tray, readily available in high-risk settings. For foreign body aspirations in the nose or ear, nurses must avoid blind probing, which can push the object deeper into the tissue.

Patient Education and Discharge Planning

Effective discharge planning empowers patients to manage their recovery and recognize warning signs that require medical attention. Education following ear surgery includes keeping water out of the ear canal, avoiding forceful nose blowing, and refraining from flying or heavy lifting until cleared by the surgeon.

For nasal and throat procedures, patients learn to avoid hot, spicy foods, maintain adequate hydration, and take prescribed analgesics or antibiotics as directed. The nurse provides clear instructions on red-flag symptoms such as bright red bleeding, high fever, increasing pain, or difficulty breathing, ensuring the patient knows when to return for urgent evaluation.
