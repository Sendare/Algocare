Nursing Management of Eye, Ear, Nose and Throat Disorders

Comprehensive EENT Assessment

Evaluating the eyes, ears, nose, and throat requires a systematic approach using basic clinical tools. The nurse inspects the external structures for symmetry, inflammation, lesions, and discharge before moving to specialized examinations.

  • Eye assessment: Involves testing visual acuity using the Snellen chart, checking pupillary reaction to light, and inspecting the conjunctiva and sclera.
  • Ear assessment: Uses an otoscope to view the tympanic membrane, noting color, landmarks, and presence of fluid or perforation. The nurse pulls the pinna upward and backward in adults, but downward and straight back in infants.
  • Nose assessment: Requires checking nasal patency, inspecting the mucosa for swelling or bleeding, and observing the septum for deviation.
  • Throat assessment: Involves examining the oral cavity, tonsils, and pharynx using a tongue depressor and penlight while noting the gag reflex.

Nursing Care in Common Eye Disorders

Eye conditions like glaucoma, cataracts, and conjunctivitis demand specific nursing interventions to preserve vision and prevent complications.

Glaucoma management centers on lowering intraocular pressure through prescribed eye drops and teaching patients about lifelong adherence. A common misconception is that patients will feel pain when pressure rises, but chronic open-angle glaucoma is typically painless until significant vision loss occurs.

For cataracts, nursing care focuses on preoperative counseling and strict postoperative instructions to avoid intraocular pressure spikes. Nurses instruct patients to avoid bending below the waist, heavy lifting, or straining during bowel movements after surgery.

In infectious conjunctivitis, rigorous hand hygiene and preventing cross-contamination are top priorities. Nurses ensure patients understand how to apply eye ointments without touching the tip of the tube to the conjunctiva.

Management of Ear and Hearing Disorders

Patients experiencing otitis media, otitis externa, or sensorineural hearing loss require targeted nursing support. Pain management is central during acute ear infections, along with monitoring for drainage or fever. When administering ear drops, the nurse warms the solution to room temperature to prevent severe vertigo or dizziness caused by caloric stimulation of the inner ear.

For patients with permanent hearing loss, communication strategies must adapt. The nurse faces the patient directly, speaks in a normal tone without shouting, and minimizes background noise to improve comprehension. Irrigation of the ear canal for cerumen impaction is contraindicated if a tympanic perforation is suspected.

Care of Upper Respiratory and Nasal Conditions

Nasal trauma, epistaxis, and severe rhinitis require prompt nursing evaluation to maintain airway patency and control bleeding.

During an episode of epistaxis, the nurse positions the patient sitting upright and leaning forward slightly to prevent blood from draining down the pharynx, which causes nausea and aspiration. Direct lateral pressure is applied to the soft lower portion of the nose for at least ten continuous minutes.

For patients with severe nasal congestion or post-operative sinus surgery, humidification and adequate hydration thin mucous secretions. Nurses monitor for frequent swallowing, which often serves as a subtle sign of ongoing posterior nasal bleeding.

Throat Disorders and Airway Maintenance

Conditions such as acute tonsillitis, peritonsillar abscesses, and post-adenotonsillectomy recovery put the upper airway at immediate risk. The primary nursing responsibility following throat surgery is continuous airway surveillance.

  • Immediate post-op position: Place the patient in a side-lying or semi-prone position to facilitate drainage of saliva and blood until fully conscious.
  • Hemorrhage monitoring: Watch for restlessness, tachycardia, pallor, and frequent throat clearing or swallowing.
  • Dietary progression: Start with clear fluids once the gag reflex returns, avoiding red or brown liquids which mimic blood if vomited.

Pharmacological Therapy in EENT Care

Medications for the eyes, ears, and nose require precise administration techniques to ensure local therapeutic effects while minimizing systemic absorption. Eye drops require pulling down the lower conjunctival sac to form a small pocket where the drop lands. Afterward, applying gentle pressure to the nasolacrimal duct for one to two minutes prevents systemic drainage through the tear ducts.

Nasal sprays demand proper positioning: the tip is directed away from the nasal septum to prevent mucosal irritation and epistaxis. For ear drops, straightening the canal ensures the liquid reaches the tympanic membrane. Nurses must verify that ophthalmic and otic medications are never interchanged, as putting ear drops into an eye can cause severe chemical injury and permanent damage.

Patient Education and Sensory Rehabilitation

Long-term success in EENT management relies heavily on patient education and adapting to sensory deficits. Nurses help patients with low vision organize their home environment by removing tripping hazards, utilizing high-contrast labels, and ensuring adequate lighting.

For individuals with hearing impairments, rehabilitation includes training in hearing aid maintenance, lip-reading techniques, and environmental modifications like amplified telephone ringers. Nurses also emphasize compliance with chronic conditions like glaucoma, explaining that missing daily eye drops can lead to irreversible optic nerve damage.
