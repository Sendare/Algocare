Conditions Affecting the Eye

Anatomy and Physiology Review of the Eye

The eye is a complex sensory organ housed within the bony orbit, designed to capture light and convert it into neural impulses. Light first passes through the transparent **cornea**, the primary refractive surface, then through the **pupil**, an opening regulated by the pigmented **iris**. Behind the iris sits the **lens**, which changes shape via ciliary muscles to focus images onto the **retina**. The retina contains photoreceptors known as **rods** (responsible for dim-light and peripheral vision) and **cones** (responsible for color and sharp central vision). Nerve signals from these photoreceptors converge at the **optic disc**—creating the physiological blind spot—and travel via the **optic nerve** to the brain. Internal eye pressure is maintained by the continuous production and drainage of **aqueous humor** in the anterior chamber, while the posterior cavity is filled with viscous **vitreous humor** that helps maintain globe shape.

Comprehensive Ophthalmic Assessment and Diagnostics

Assessing ocular health requires a systematic approach combining subjective history and objective physical tests. Start by evaluating visual acuity using the **Snellen chart** for distance vision, noting the ratio where the numerator is 20 feet and the denominator is the distance at which a person with normal vision can read the same line. External structures, extraocular muscle movements, and pupillary light reflexes (assessed for direct and consensual response) give vital clues about neurological and local integrity. Diagnostic procedures quantify specific variables: - **Tonometry**: Measures intraocular pressure (IOP) to screen for glaucoma; normal range typically spans 10 to 21 mmHg. - **Ophthalmoscopy**: Allows direct visualization of the internal structures, including the optic disc, macula, and retinal blood vessels. - **Slit-lamp examination**: Provides magnified three-dimensional view of anterior segment structures to detect subtle corneal abrasions or inflammatory cells.

Refractive Errors and Common Visual Impairments

Refractive errors occur when the eye fails to bend light correctly, preventing it from focusing precisely on the retina. These common optical variations are easily corrected with spectacles, contact lenses, or refractive surgery. - **Myopia (Nearsightedness)**: Light focuses in front of the retina because the eyeball is too long or the refractive power is too strong, making distant objects blurry. - **Hyperopia (Farsightedness)**: Light focuses behind the retina because the eyeball is too short, causing difficulty focusing on close items. - **Astigmatism**: Irregular curvature of the cornea or lens causes light rays to scatter, resulting in distorted or blurred vision at all distances. - **Presbyopia**: Age-related hardening of the natural lens reduces accommodation, typically forcing middle-aged adults to hold reading material farther away.

Inflammatory and Infectious Eye Conditions

Inflammatory and infectious processes can involve the superficial membranes or the internal structures of the eye. **Conjunctivitis**, commonly known as pink eye, can stem from bacterial, viral, or allergic agents. Bacterial conjunctivitis presents with purulent discharge and matting of the eyelashes, whereas viral variants often produce watery discharge and are highly contagious in community settings. Keratitis involves inflammation of the cornea, frequently linked to improper contact lens hygiene or herpes simplex virus infections, carrying a severe risk of permanent corneal scarring if untreated. Uveitis affects the middle vascular layer (iris, ciliary body, and choroid) and manifests with deep ocular ache, photophobia, and a constricted, irregular pupil. Nursing care focuses on strict hand hygiene, administering prescribed antibiotic or anti-inflammatory drops, and teaching patients never to share washcloths or eye makeup.

Glaucoma: Pathophysiology, Types, and Nursing Care

Glaucoma is characterized by progressive optic neuropathy associated with elevated intraocular pressure, leading to peripheral visual field loss. - **Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma (POAG)**: The most common form. Aqueous humor drainage is slowly blocked at the trabecular meshwork. It develops silently with no early pain, gradually constricting the visual field. - **Angle-Closure Glaucoma**: A medical emergency where the iris shifts forward, completely blocking the drainage angle. Patients experience sudden excruciating eye pain, blurred vision, colored halos around lights, and nausea. Management involves lifelong pharmacological therapy using beta-blockers, prostaglandin analogs, or cholinergic agents to lower intraocular pressure. Nurses must stress adherence to eye drop regimens, as untreated glaucoma results in irreversible blindness.

Cataracts and Age-Related Macular Degeneration

Both cataracts and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) are major causes of visual decline in older adults, though they affect different parts of the optical system. A **cataract** is the opacification of the crystalline lens caused by protein clumping from aging, trauma, or prolonged steroid use. Patients report blurred vision, faded colors, and glaring headlights at night. Treatment is surgical removal when vision loss interferes with daily tasks. **Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)** targets the macula, destroying sharp central vision while sparing peripheral vision. It exists in two forms: **dry AMD** (slow breakdown of light-sensitive cells with drusen deposits) and **wet AMD** (abnormal fragile blood vessels leak fluid beneath the macula). A simple screening tool is the **Amsler grid**, where patients check for wavy or distorted lines.

Ocular Trauma and Emergency Management

Ocular injuries range from superficial foreign bodies to penetrating glob injuries that threaten total vision loss. Chemical burns require immediate, prolonged irrigation with sterile saline or water for at least 15 to 30 minutes before evaluating visual acuity. For penetrating trauma or foreign body lodgment, never attempt to remove the embedded object, apply pressure, or manipulate the eye. Instead, apply a rigid protective shield over the eye to prevent further accidental displacement of intraocular contents. Prompt referral to an ophthalmologist is mandatory. Nurses must also assess tetanus immunization status in any traumatic open wound affecting ocular structures.

Surgical Interventions and Postoperative Nursing Management

Ophthalmic surgeries, such as phacoemulsification for cataracts or trabeculectomy for glaucoma, require careful postoperative management to protect the healing eye and prevent infection or elevated intraocular pressure. Nursing care centers on strict activity restrictions. Patients must avoid bending at the waist, lifting heavy objects, straining during bowel movements, or rubbing the eye, as these actions spike venous pressure in the head. Protective eye shields should be worn during sleep. Antibiotic and corticosteroid eye drops are prescribed to prevent infection and control inflammation. Nurses must teach proper aseptic drop instillation techniques, ensuring the dropper tip never touches the eyelashes or conjunctiva.

Rehabilitation and Patient Education for Low Vision

When medical and surgical treatments cannot fully restore functional sight, low-vision rehabilitation helps patients maximize their remaining vision and maintain independence. Low vision is defined as visual impairment that cannot be corrected by standard glasses, contact lenses, or medical intervention, yet still allows some usable sight. Nurses collaborate with multidisciplinary specialists to introduce adaptive devices, including magnifying lenses, large-print reading materials, high-contrast household items, and auditory screen readers. Home safety modifications—such as removing loose throw rugs, improving lighting in stairwells, and marking appliance dials with tactile bumps—reduce fall risks. Emphasizing emotional support helps patients navigate the grief and loss associated with declining vision.
