Conditions Affecting the Eye

Anatomy and Physiology Review of the Eye

The human eye is a specialized sensory organ structured to capture light and convert it into neural signals for the brain. Light first passes through the cornea, the clear, dome-shaped front surface responsible for the majority of the eye's focusing power. Directly behind the cornea lies the anterior chamber, a small space filled with aqueous humor, a watery fluid that nourishes surrounding tissues and maintains intraocular pressure.

Next, light travels through the pupil, an opening whose size is regulated by the pigmented iris. Behind the pupil sits the lens, a flexible structure that fine-tunes focus for near and distant objects through a process called accommodation. Once light passes through the clear vitreous humor filling the back cavity of the eye, it strikes the retina.

The retina contains photoreceptor cells known as rods (which function in dim light and detect motion) and cones (which are responsible for sharp color vision in bright light). These cells transform light energy into electrical impulses, which travel via the optic nerve to the occipital lobe of the brain for visual interpretation.

Refractive Errors and Common Visual Impairments

Refractive errors occur when the shape of the eye prevents light from focusing directly on the retina, resulting in blurred vision. These are among the most common reasons individuals seek eye care.

  • Myopia (Nearsightedness): The eyeball is too long or the cornea is too steeply curved, causing light rays to focus in front of the retina. Distant objects appear blurry.
  • Hyperopia (Farsightedness): The eyeball is too short or the cornea is too flat, causing light rays to focus behind the retina. Close-up tasks cause eye strain and blurred near vision.
  • Astigmatism: The cornea or lens has an irregular, asymmetric curve, splitting light into multiple focal points and causing distorted or smeared vision at all distances.
  • Presbyopia: A natural, age-related loss of lens flexibility that reduces the eye's ability to focus on close objects, typically becoming noticeable around age 40.

Inflammatory and Infectious Eye Conditions

Infections and inflammatory processes can affect various structures of the eye, causing pain, redness, and potential vision loss if left untreated.

Conjunctivitis, commonly known as pink eye, is an inflammation or infection of the clear membrane lining the eyelid and sclera. It can stem from viral, bacterial, or allergic triggers. Bacterial conjunctivitis typically presents with thick purulent discharge, whereas viral cases feature watery discharge and a gritty sensation.

Keratitis involves inflammation of the cornea, frequently linked to contact lens misuse, trauma, or herpes simplex virus infections. It causes severe eye pain, photophobia, and a feeling that something is stuck in the eye. A corneal ulcer can rapidly develop, presenting an ocular emergency.

Uveitis is inflammation of the middle layer of the eye (the uvea), which includes the iris, ciliary body, and choroid. It is often associated with systemic autoimmune disorders and requires prompt corticosteroid therapy to prevent permanent structural damage.

Glaucoma: Pathophysiology, Types, and Nursing Care

Glaucoma is a group of eye conditions characterized by progressive optic nerve damage, frequently linked to elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) caused by impaired drainage of aqueous humor.

Open-angle glaucoma is the most prevalent form. The drainage angle remains open, but fluid flows out too slowly, leading to a gradual, painless pressure buildup that slowly steals peripheral vision.

Closed-angle glaucoma is a medical emergency. The iris is pushed forward, suddenly blocking the drainage angle. Patients experience severe eye pain, headache, nausea, colored halos around lights, and blurred vision.

Nurses must monitor IOP, reinforce strict adherence to lifelong eye drops (such as beta-blockers or prostaglandin analogs), and educate patients never to stop medications abruptly. For closed-angle episodes, immediate medical intervention is required to lower pressure and save the optic nerve.

Cataracts and Age-Related Macular Degeneration

Both cataracts and age-related macular degeneration (AMD) are major causes of visual impairment in older adults, though they affect entirely different parts of the eye and produce distinct symptoms.

FeatureCataractsMacular Degeneration (AMD)
Primary Affected StructureCrystalline lensRetina (macula)
Main Visual SymptomBlurry, hazy vision; faded colorsLoss of central vision; straight lines look wavy
Underlying MechanismProtein clumping and lens opacityBreakdown of light-sensitive cells in the macula

Cataracts develop slowly, making night driving difficult and creating glare from oncoming headlights. Surgical removal and lens replacement restore sight successfully. AMD comes in dry and wet forms; dry AMD involves cellular debris accumulation, while wet AMD involves abnormal, leaky blood vessels under the macula. While treatment can slow wet AMD, neither form is fully curable.

Ocular Trauma and Emergency Management

Eye injuries require swift, careful assessment to prevent permanent vision loss. Trauma can be blunt (from fists or balls) or penetrating (from sharp objects or flying metal shards).

When a foreign body is present, healthcare providers flush the eye with sterile saline unless it is embedded. If an object is embedded or a penetrating globe injury is suspected, never attempt to remove the object. Doing so can cause intraocular contents to spill out, destroying the eye.

Chemical burns are true ocular emergencies requiring immediate, continuous irrigation with water or normal saline for at least 15 to 30 minutes before any further assessment takes place. Nurses should protect the injured eye with a rigid shield, avoid applying any pressure, and arrange immediate transfer to an ophthalmologist.

Surgical Interventions and Postoperative Nursing Management

Eye surgeries, such as cataract extraction with intraocular lens implantation or trabeculectomy for glaucoma, require meticulous postoperative care to protect the surgical site and promote healing.

Because increased intraocular pressure can rupture fresh sutures or cause bleeding, nurses must actively prevent activities that raise pressure. Patients must avoid bending at the waist, lifting heavy objects, straining during bowel movements, or coughing aggressively.

Postoperative care also involves administering prescribed antibiotic and anti-inflammatory eye drops using clean technique, ensuring the tip of the dropper never touches the eye. Nurses teach patients to wear an eye shield at night to prevent accidental rubbing or trauma while sleeping, and to report severe pain, flashing lights, or sudden vision loss immediately.

Rehabilitation and Patient Education for Low Vision

Low vision refers to significant visual impairment that cannot be fully corrected with glasses, contact lenses, medicine, or surgery, yet leaves some functional sight.

Nurses play a key role in helping patients maximize their remaining vision and maintain independence. Rehabilitation strategies focus on environmental modifications and adaptive tools. Improving household lighting, installing contrasting colored tape on stair edges, and decluttering walkways drastically reduce fall risks.

Assistive devices include magnifying glasses, large-print reading materials, talking clocks, and electronic screen readers. Beyond practical tools, nurses provide emotional support, acknowledging the grief and frustration that often accompany vision loss while connecting patients with community support groups and low-vision specialists.
