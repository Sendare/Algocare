Diagnostic Evaluation of Eye, Ear, Nose and Throat Disorders

Introduction to EENT Diagnostic Evaluations

Specialized assessment of the eyes, ears, nose, and throat requires distinct instrumentation and systematic observation. Because these structures are compact and sensitive, clinical evaluations often combine direct visualization, functional testing, and imaging. The physical layout of a typical Nigerian clinical setting requires nurses to master both specialized diagnostic tools, such as the otoscope and ophthalmoscope, and the ability to triage patients reporting overlapping symptoms like headache and facial pain.

Ophthalmological Assessments and Diagnostic Tests

Evaluating visual function and ocular structures begins with basic visual acuity testing using the Snellen chart. Advanced diagnostic procedures include **tonometry** to measure intraocular pressure for glaucoma screening, and **ophthalmoscopy** to examine the internal structures of the retina and optic disc. - **Visual Acuity**: Measures sharpness of vision at a standardized distance. - **Tonometry**: Detects elevated intraocular pressure, a major risk factor for glaucoma. - **Slit-Lamp Examination**: Provides magnified 3D stereoscopic view of the anterior eye segment. - **Fluorescein Angiography**: Highlights retinal blood vessel abnormalities using injected dye.

Otological Evaluations and Hearing Tests

Assessment of auditory and vestibular function relies on both physical inspection and specialized testing. An otoscopic examination reveals the condition of the external auditory canal and the tympanic membrane. Functional hearing evaluations include the **Rinne and Weber tests** using a tuning fork to differentiate between conductive and sensorineural hearing loss. For precise measurements, **audiometry** graphs hearing thresholds across various frequencies, while **tympanometry** assesses middle ear compliance and mobility of the eardrum.

Rhinological and Nasal Endoscopic Examinations

Nasal and sinus evaluations extend beyond external inspection. Anterior rhinoscopy permits visualization of the nasal mucosa, septum, and inferior turbinates using a nasal speculum and penlight. When deeper structures must be evaluated, **nasal endoscopy** allows the clinician to navigate narrow passages, visualize the ostiomeatal complex, and identify polyps, chronic inflammation, or anatomical obstructions.

Throat, Laryngeal, and Pharyngeal Diagnostics

Visualizing the pharynx and larynx requires overcoming the gag reflex and anatomical obstructions. Indirect laryngoscopy utilizes a small angled mirror and a light source to view the vocal cords during phonation. For superior detail and biopsy capabilities, flexible or rigid **laryngoscopy** is performed. Clinicians also routinely obtain throat swabs for microbiological culture and sensitivity testing when bacterial infections like streptococcal pharyngitis are suspected.

Radiological and Laboratory Investigations in EENT

When physical examination and endoscopy provide insufficient data, imaging and laboratory tests complete the diagnostic picture. **Computed tomography (CT)** scans are the gold standard for evaluating complex bone structures in sinusitis, facial fractures, and mastoiditis. **Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** offers superior soft tissue resolution for soft tissue masses, optic nerve pathology, and inner ear tumors. Blood tests, including complete blood counts and inflammatory markers, help evaluate systemic infections and autoimmune conditions affecting EENT structures.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Nurses play a critical role before, during, and after EENT diagnostic evaluations. Preparation involves explaining procedures to alleviate anxiety, positioning patients correctly, and ensuring necessary equipment is functional and sterile. Post-procedure care often includes monitoring for complications such as bleeding after nasal biopsies or managing localized anesthesia wear-off. In clinical environments across Nigeria, clear communication regarding potential post-test restrictions—such as temporary vision blurring from dilating drops—ensures patient safety before discharge.
