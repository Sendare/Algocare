Oncologic Emergencies

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Oncologic Emergencies

Oncologic emergencies are acute, life-threatening events directly or indirectly related to a patient's malignancy or its treatment. These crises can be the initial presentation of cancer or a complication during advanced stages. Pathophysiologically, they generally arise from three mechanisms: direct tumor mass effects obstructing vital structures, metabolic derangements driven by rapid cell turnover or ectopic hormone production, or severe treatment-induced cytopenias. In Nigerian clinical practice, where many patients present with advanced, bulky disease due to delayed healthcare access, recognizing these rapid physiological shifts early is a core nursing responsibility. Prompt identification halts multi-organ failure before aggressive anticancer therapy even begins.

Metabolic Emergencies: Tumor Lysis Syndrome and Hypercalcemia

Metabolic oncologic emergencies alter body chemistry rapidly. - **Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)**: Triggered by massive, rapid lysis of malignant cells—often following cytotoxic chemotherapy or radiation—releasing intracellular contents into the bloodstream. This causes hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, secondary hypocalcemia, and hyperuricemia, leading to acute kidney injury. - **Hypercalcemia of Malignancy (HCM)**: Most commonly caused by tumor secretion of parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP) or direct bone lysis. It depresses neuromuscular activity, presenting as profound lethargy, muscle weakness, nausea, and constipation ("bones, stones, groans, and psychiatric overtones"). Aggressive hydration with normal saline and loop diuretics is standard initial management for both conditions to restore renal clearance.

Structural Emergencies: Spinal Cord Compression and Superior Vena Cava Syndrome

Structural emergencies occur when expanding tumor masses mechanically compromise critical anatomical conduits. - **Spinal Cord Compression (MSCC)**: Caused by tumor growth in the epidural space, commonly from breast, lung, or prostate metastases. Back pain is usually the earliest symptom, followed by motor weakness, sensory loss, and late autonomic dysfunction like urinary retention. Corticosteroids are administered immediately to reduce edema. - **Superior Vena Cava Syndrome (SVCS)**: Results from external vascular compression of the SVC by mediastinal tumors or thrombosis. Manifestations include facial edema, distended neck veins, and upper extremity swelling, worsened by bending forward.

Hematologic Emergencies: Febrile Neutropenia and Hyperleukocytosis

Hematologic crises involve severe quantitative imbalances in blood components, predisposing patients to infection or circulatory stasis. - **Febrile Neutropenia**: Defined as an oral temperature greater than 38.3°C (or 38.0°C sustained for an hour) in a patient with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) below 500 cells/mm³. Because classical signs of inflammation may be absent due to a lack of neutrophils, fever is often the sole indicator of a potentially lethal systemic infection. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics must be initiated within the golden hour. - **Hyperleukocytosis**: Characterized by an extremely elevated white blood cell count (usually > 100,000/µL), common in acute leukemias, causing blood hyperviscosity and microcirculation sludging, particularly in the pulmonary and cerebral beds.

Nursing Assessment and Early Recognition

Early recognition relies on baseline risk stratification and diligent baseline neurological, respiratory, and circulatory assessments. Nurses must screen patients receiving high-risk chemotherapy regimens for subtle changes in mentation, motor function, urinary output, and skin integrity. For instance, testing for sensory deficits or mild gait changes can catch spinal compression before irreversible paralysis occurs. Monitoring fluid balance charts meticulously helps detect early renal impairment in potential tumor lysis syndrome. Quick escalation to the primary oncology team alters the trajectory of care dramatically.

Acute Management and Supportive Care Interventions

Acute management requires coordinated interprofessional teamwork. Medical interventions target the underlying physiologic derangement: allopurinol or rasburicase for hyperuricemia, intravenous bisphosphonates or calcitonin for hypercalcemia, and high-dose dexamethasone for spinal cord compression or superior vena cava syndrome. Supportive nursing care focuses on hemodynamic stabilization, strict intake and output recording, pain management, and infection control precautions. Creating a calm, safe environment minimizes distress for patients experiencing acute dyspnea, neurological deficits, or severe metabolic delirium.

Patient Education and Supportive Care

Empowering patients and their families bridges the gap between hospital discharge and community self-management. Education must cover the precise warning signs that necessitate immediate hospital return, such as unexpected fevers, sudden numbness in limbs, facial swelling, or severe muscle cramps. In resource-constrained settings, financial and logistical barriers can delay return; hence, teaching family caregivers simple daily symptom checklists is vital. Supportive care also addresses psychosocial distress, ensuring patients and relatives feel supported throughout a frightening oncologic crisis.
