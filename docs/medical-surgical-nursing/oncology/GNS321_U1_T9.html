Medical Intervention During Cancer Treatment

Introduction to Cancer Medical Interventions

Medical interventions for cancer treatment encompass a multimodal approach designed to cure, control, or palliate the disease. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding these interventions is vital as the burden of cancer rises in sub-Saharan Africa. The primary medical modalities include chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, hormone therapy, and radiation therapy. Each of these interventions aims to disrupt cellular growth, division, or signaling pathways, but they often affect both malignant and healthy rapidly dividing tissues. As a nurse, your role extends beyond drug administration to include comprehensive patient assessment, education, and monitoring for adverse effects. Because cancer treatments can severely impair a patient's physiological reserves and immune system, holistic care is paramount. You must coordinate closely with the multidisciplinary healthcare team, including oncologists, pharmacists, and dietitians, to ensure optimal therapeutic outcomes while safeguarding the patient's quality of life. Furthermore, cultural considerations and socioeconomic factors influencing treatment adherence in the Nigerian context must be factored into care planning.

Principles of Chemotherapy and Administration Safety

Chemotherapy involves the use of cytotoxic medications to systemic eradicate cancer cells by interfering with cellular replication and DNA synthesis. When administering these high-alert medications, strict safety protocols must be observed by nursing personnel to prevent accidental exposure and catastrophic dosing errors. Cytotoxic agents are often classified by their mechanism of action, such as alkylating agents, antimetabolites, and mitotic inhibitors. Because these drugs cannot differentiate between healthy and malignant cells, tissues with high turnover rates—such as the bone marrow, gastrointestinal tract, and hair follicles—are frequently damaged, leading to side effects like myelosuppression, alopecia, and mucositis. Safe administration begins with double-checking calculations with another qualified nurse, verifying body surface area, and inspecting patency of the intravenous access to prevent extravasation, which can cause severe tissue necrosis. Nurses must wear appropriate personal protective equipment, including powder-free chemotherapy gloves, gowns, and eye protection when preparing and administering these agents. Proper disposal of all equipment in designated cytotoxic waste containers is mandatory to protect both healthcare workers and the environment from hazardous contamination.

Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy Mechanisms

Targeted therapy and immunotherapy represent a paradigm shift from traditional cytotoxic chemotherapy by focusing on specific molecular characteristics and immune pathways of cancer cells. Targeted therapies use drugs or small molecules to block specific enzymes, proteins, or other molecules involved in the growth and survival of tumor cells, minimizing damage to normal host cells. Examples include tyrosine kinase inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies that interrupt specific signaling cascades. Immunotherapy, on the other hand, stimulates or reshapes the patient's own immune system to recognize and destroy cancer cells more effectively. Checkpoint inhibitors, CAR-T cell therapy, and cytokines are common immunotherapeutic approaches. As a nurse, monitoring for unique toxicities associated with these agents is essential. Targeted therapies can cause dermatological toxicities like rash, hypertension, and gastrointestinal perforation. Immunotherapies often trigger immune-related adverse events (irAEs) due to heightened immune activity, presenting as pneumonitis, colitis, hepatitis, or endocrinopathies. Patient education must emphasize prompt reporting of subtle symptoms, such as a persistent dry cough or changes in bowel habits, because early intervention with corticosteroids or immunosuppressants can prevent life-threatening organ damage.

Hormone Therapy in Cancer Treatment

Hormone therapy, or endocrine therapy, is utilized to treat cancers that are sensitive to hormones, such as certain types of breast and prostate cancer. These interventions work by either blocking the body's natural hormones or lowering their production to slow or stop the growth of hormone-receptor-positive tumors. Common modalities include selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) like tamoxifen, aromatase inhibitors, and luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone (LHRH) agonists or antagonists. Nurses must educate patients on the expected side effects, which often mimic menopause or androgen deprivation. These include hot flashes, mood swings, weight gain, decreased libido, and bone mineral density loss leading to osteoporosis. For patients taking aromatase inhibitors or receiving long-term endocrine blockade, baseline and periodic bone density scans are recommended, alongside encouraging adequate calcium and vitamin D intake and weight-bearing exercises. Furthermore, nurses should assess for thromboembolic events, such as deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, particularly in patients taking SERMs. Providing empathetic counseling helps patients manage lifestyle adjustments and enhances adherence to long-term oral regimens, which are crucial for reducing recurrence rates.

Radiation Therapy and Nursing Care

Radiation therapy uses high-energy ionizing radiation to damage the DNA of cancer cells, causing cell death or preventing cellular reproduction. It can be delivered externally via teletherapy or internally via brachytherapy, where radioactive sources are placed directly into or near the tumor. Nursing care for patients undergoing radiation requires a focus on skin management, nutrition, and fatigue mitigation. Radiotherapy causes localized skin reactions in the treatment field, ranging from mild erythema to dry or moist desquamation. Nurses must instruct patients to keep the irradiated skin clean and dry, use mild soaps, avoid abrasive rubbing, and refrain from applying lotions, perfumes, or ice packs to the area without medical approval. Fatigue is a nearly universal side effect; nurses should help patients prioritize activities and balance rest with light exercise. For internal radiation (brachytherapy), strict safety protocols regarding time, distance, and shielding must be followed by nursing staff to minimize radiation exposure, and visitors may be restricted. Emotional support is equally vital, as patients often experience anxiety regarding the treatment machinery and perceived bodily burns.

Management of Treatment-Related Toxicities and Side Effects

Management of treatment-related toxicities is a cornerstone of oncology nursing, as severe side effects can cause dose reductions or treatment cessation. Common systemic toxicities include myelosuppression, which manifests as neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia. Nurses must monitor complete blood counts closely. A neutropenic patient is at high risk for life-threatening infections; education must cover hand hygiene, avoiding crowds, and reporting fevers immediately. Thrombocytopenia requires bleeding precautions, such as using soft toothbrushes and avoiding invasive procedures when possible, while anemia may require blood transfusions or erythropoiesis-stimulating agents. Gastrointestinal toxicities like nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and stomatitis are managed proactively with antiemetics, antidiarrheals, and meticulous oral care using saline rinses. Nutritional support is critical, encouraging small, frequent, nutrient-dense meals. Alopecia, though non-life-threatening, carries significant psychological distress; nurses should discuss coping strategies, such as wigs, scarves, or hats, before hair loss occurs. Assessing the patient's pain level and utilizing the WHO analgesic ladder ensures that cancer-related pain is managed effectively, contributing to an overall better quality of life during therapy.

Oncological Emergencies and Acute Medical Interventions

Oncological emergencies are acute, life-threatening medical conditions caused directly or indirectly by the cancer or its treatment, requiring prompt recognition and intervention by nurses. Key emergencies include superior vena cava obstruction (SVCO), spinal cord compression, tumor lysis syndrome (TLS), hypercalcemia of malignancy, and febrile neutropenia. For instance, SVCO occurs when a tumor compresses the superior vena cava, leading to facial edema, jugular venous distention, and dyspnea; nursing interventions involve elevating the head of the bed and administering corticosteroids or preparing for radiation. Spinal cord compression is characterized by new-onset back pain, motor weakness, and sensory loss; immediate administration of high-dose corticosteroids and emergent imaging are required to prevent permanent paralysis. Tumor lysis syndrome results from the rapid destruction of a large number of tumor cells, releasing intracellular potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids into the bloodstream, leading to hyperkalemia, hyperuricemia, and acute renal failure. Nursing care for TLS includes aggressive intravenous hydration, alkalinization of urine, and administration of allopurinol or rasburicase. Recognizing these signs early allows for life-saving interventions in acute care settings.

Palliative Care and Symptom Management in Advanced Cancer

Palliative care and symptom management are integral components of advanced cancer care, focusing on improving the quality of life for patients and their families facing life-limiting illness. Unlike hospice care, which is reserved for the final months of life, palliative care can and should be initiated concurrently with disease-directed cancer therapies. The nurse plays a pivotal role in assessing and relieving distressing symptoms such as pain, dyspnea, fatigue, nausea, anorexia, and existential distress. Comprehensive pain management requires regular assessment using validated tools and adherence to the WHO analgesic ladder, combining non-opioid and opioid medications with adjuvant therapies. Managing cachexia and anorexia involves nutritional counseling, small palatable meals, and sometimes appetite stimulants, rather than forced enteral feeding which can cause distress. Furthermore, palliative care encompasses psychosocial and spiritual support for both the patient and their caregivers, helping them navigate difficult treatment decisions, advance care planning, and end-of-life preferences. As a nurse, providing compassionate, culturally sensitive communication ensures dignity, comfort, and peace during the terminal stages of cancer.
