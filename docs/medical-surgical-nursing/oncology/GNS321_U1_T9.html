Medical Intervention During Cancer Treatment

Introduction to Cancer Medical Interventions

Cancer medical interventions encompass a diverse array of systemic and local therapies designed to eradicate malignant cells, control disease progression, or alleviate symptoms. Unlike surgical resection, which targets localized solid tumors, medical treatments circulate throughout the body to reach micrometastases. The primary modalities include **chemotherapy**, **targeted therapy**, **immunotherapy**, **hormone therapy**, and **radiation therapy**. Treatment selection depends on histological tumor type, staging, molecular profiling, and the patient's physiological reserve. In clinical practice across Nigeria, patients often present with advanced-stage disease due to delayed diagnosis, making systemic medical interventions the cornerstone of management alongside palliative support.

Principles of Chemotherapy and Administration Safety

Chemotherapy utilizes cytotoxic drugs to disrupt the cell cycle, targeting rapidly dividing cells. Because malignant cells divide uncontrollably, they are more vulnerable to these agents than normal tissue. However, healthy rapidly dividing tissues—such as bone marrow, hair follicles, and the gastrointestinal epithelium—are inevitably affected, causing predictable side effects. Safe administration requires strict adherence to institutional protocols: - **Dosage Calculation**: Usually based on body surface area using height and weight. - **Vascular Access**: Many vesicant drugs require central venous access devices to prevent severe tissue necrosis from extravasation. - **Personal Protective Equipment**: Handling cytotoxic agents demands specialized chemotherapy-rated gloves, gowns, and eye protection to protect healthcare personnel from occupational exposure.

Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy Mechanisms

Unlike traditional chemotherapy, which attacks all rapidly dividing cells, targeted therapy and immunotherapy focus on specific molecular characteristics of cancer cells. - **Targeted Therapy**: Utilizes small molecules or monoclonal antibodies to block specific genes, proteins, or the tissue environment that contributes to cancer growth and survival. For instance, tyrosine kinase inhibitors block specific signaling pathways driving tumor proliferation. - **Immunotherapy**: Harnesses the patient's own immune system to recognize and destroy cancer cells. Immune checkpoint inhibitors block proteins made by immune system cells, which prevents checkpoints from signaling the immune system to stop attacking cancer cells.

Hormone Therapy in Cancer Treatment

Hormone therapy is utilized for cancers that are sensitive to hormonal stimulation, most notably breast and prostate cancers. The underlying premise involves either lowering the body's natural hormone levels or blocking hormones from attaching to cancer cell receptors. For example, estrogen receptor-positive breast cancers may be treated with selective estrogen receptor modulators or aromatase inhibitors. In prostate cancer, androgen deprivation therapy is achieved through surgical castration or luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone agonists. Clinical monitoring must address the long-term metabolic and structural consequences of induced hormonal deprivation, such as osteoporosis, cardiovascular changes, and vasomotor symptoms.

Radiation Therapy and Nursing Care

Radiation therapy uses high-energy ionizing radiation to damage the DNA of cancer cells, causing cell death or preventing replication. It can be delivered externally via a machine outside the body (external beam radiation therapy) or internally by placing radioactive material directly inside or near the tumor (brachytherapy). Nursing care focuses on managing localized skin reactions, fatigue, and organ-specific toxicities. Skin in the radiation field requires meticulous care: gentle cleansing, avoiding harsh soaps, refraining from applying lotions or adhesive tape prior to treatment sessions, and protecting the area from sun exposure. Patients undergoing internal radiation require specific safety precautions regarding time, distance, and shielding to minimize healthcare worker and visitor radiation exposure.

Management of Treatment-Related Toxicities and Side Effects

Oncological treatments frequently induce multisystem toxicities that require proactive nursing intervention. Bone marrow suppression results in myelosuppression, manifesting as neutropenia (infection risk), thrombocytopenia (bleeding risk), and anemia (fatigue). Gastrointestinal toxicities include mucositis, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea, which compromise nutritional status and hydration. Proactive antiemetic therapy, meticulous oral hygiene, and dietary modifications are essential. Additionally, peripheral neuropathy caused by neurotoxic chemotherapeutic agents requires careful sensory assessment and dose adjustments to prevent permanent neurological deficits.

Oncological Emergencies and Acute Medical Interventions

Cancer patients are vulnerable to acute, life-threatening complications either from the tumor itself or as a consequence of treatment. Recognizing these emergencies early saves lives. Common oncological emergencies include: - **Superior Vena Cava Syndrome**: Compression of the superior vena cava leading to facial edema, jugular venous distension, and respiratory distress. - **Tumor Lysis Syndrome**: Rapid destruction of malignant cells releasing intracellular potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids into the bloodstream, causing hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, and acute kidney injury. - **Spinal Cord Compression**: Tumor pressure on the spinal cord causing back pain, motor weakness, and sensory loss. - **Febrile Neutropenia**: Fever in the setting of low absolute neutrophil count, treated as an immediate medical emergency requiring broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Palliative Care and Symptom Management in Advanced Cancer

Palliative care focuses on improving the quality of life for patients and families facing life-threatening illness. Rather than focusing on curative intent, medical interventions in this phase prioritize comfort, pain control, and psychological support. Comprehensive symptom management addresses chronic pain, dyspnea, cachexia, and depression. Opioid analgesics are the mainstay for cancer pain management, and nurses must dispel myths regarding addiction to ensure adequate dosing. Integrating palliative care early alongside disease-directed therapies reduces physical and emotional distress, supporting patients and their families throughout the disease trajectory.
