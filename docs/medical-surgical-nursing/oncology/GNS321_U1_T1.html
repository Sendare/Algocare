Cancer Epidemiology

Introduction to Cancer Epidemiology

Cancer epidemiology is the study of how often cancer occurs in different populations and why. Instead of looking at a single sick patient in a hospital bed, epidemiologists look at whole communities, regions, and countries. They investigate patterns related to age, gender, occupation, and geographical location to understand how diseases spread and develop. This branch of science helps public health planners figure out where to direct resources, build specialized hospitals, and run awareness campaigns. By tracking these trends over decades, researchers can tell if a certain type of cancer is becoming more common or if prevention programs are actually working.

Global and Nigerian Cancer Burden

Cancer is a massive public health challenge worldwide, and Nigeria is no exception. Globally, millions of new cases are diagnosed every year, with low- and middle-income countries bearing a heavy share of late-stage diagnoses and deaths. In Nigeria, the most common cancers differ slightly from Western nations due to a mix of infectious agents, lifestyle shifts, and environmental exposures.

  • Breast Cancer: The leading malignancy among Nigerian women, often presenting at a much younger age compared to women in high-income countries.
  • Cervical Cancer: The second most common cancer in Nigerian women, strongly linked to persistent Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection and limited screening access.
  • Prostate Cancer: The most prevalent cancer among Nigerian men, carrying a high mortality rate due to late presentation for medical care.
  • Liver Cancer: Highly prevalent across sub-Saharan Africa, frequently driven by chronic hepatitis B and C infections and exposure to dietary aflatoxins.

Key Epidemiological Terminology and Measures

To read research and understand public health reports, nurses must master basic statistical terms.

  • Incidence: The number of NEW cases of a disease diagnosed in a specific population during a given time period. It tells us how fast a disease is spreading.
  • Prevalence: The total number of existing cases (both old and new) in a population at a given point in time. It shows how widespread a condition is.
  • Mortality Rate: The frequency of death from a specific disease within a defined population over a set period.
  • Survival Rate: The percentage of people who survive a specific type of cancer for a certain number of years, usually five years, after diagnosis.

Risk Factors and Determinants

A risk factor is any attribute, characteristic, or exposure of an individual that increases the likelihood of developing cancer. Some risk factors are fixed, while others can be modified through personal choice or public policy.

Genetics, Environment, and Lifestyle in Etiology

Cancer does not happen overnight. It is usually the result of a multi-step process where genetic material inside a cell becomes damaged. While inherited gene mutations, such as BRCA1 or BRCA2, place certain families at very high risk, the vast majority of cancers are caused by acquired mutations. These acquired changes accumulate over a lifetime due to environmental exposures and personal lifestyle choices.

Tobacco smoke contains dozens of known carcinogens that directly attack DNA. Poor diet, obesity, and physical inactivity contribute significantly to colorectal and breast cancers. When these external triggers combine with an individual's unique genetic makeup, normal cell division goes out of control, leading to tumor formation. Understanding this interplay helps nurses explain to patients why healthy lifestyle habits matter even if cancer does not run in their family.

Cancer Screening and Early Detection Strategies

Screening means testing people for cancer before they show any symptoms. The goal is to catch malignant changes at an early, highly treatable stage, or even to find precancerous lesions and remove them before they turn into invasive cancer.

Common population-wide screening methods include:

  • Mammography: X-ray imaging of the breast to detect early lumps or microcalcifications.
  • Pap Smear and HPV Testing: Procedures to collect cervical cells to find abnormal changes or high-risk viral strains.
  • Fecal Occult Blood Test or Colonoscopy: Screening tools used to check for polyps or bleeding in the large intestine.

For a screening program to succeed in a community, tests must be affordable, safe, and culturally acceptable, and clear pathways for follow-up treatment must be available.

Role of Nurses in Cancer Prevention and Data Collection

Nurses sit on the front lines of cancer prevention, early detection, and epidemiological data gathering. Because nurses interact directly with patients and communities daily, they are uniquely positioned to educate the public about modifiable risk factors like smoking cessation, healthy dieting, and vaccine uptake against HPV and Hepatitis B.

In clinical and public health settings, nurses also assist with cancer registries. Accurate data collection by nurses ensures that local cancer burdens are reported correctly to government health ministries. These records allow epidemiologists to track disease trends, evaluate the success of treatment centers, and advocate for better healthcare policies. Every time a nurse takes a detailed family history or logs a new diagnosis accurately, they contribute directly to the global fight against cancer.
