Neoplastic Diseases

Introduction to Neoplastic Diseases

The word neoplasm means new growth. In clinical practice, we refer to this abnormal mass of tissue simply as a tumor. When normal cells undergo genetic mutation, their regulatory mechanisms for growth and division fail. Instead of resting or dying when old or damaged, these altered cells multiply uncontrollably, forming a distinct mass that serves no physiological purpose for the body. Understanding this cellular rebellion is the foundation of all oncology nursing.

Characteristics of Benign Cells

Benign tumors are localized growths that do not invade surrounding healthy tissues or spread to distant body parts. Under the microscope, benign cells look very similar to the normal tissue of origin, a property we call high differentiation. They grow slowly and are usually surrounded by a fibrous capsule that makes them easy to surgical remove. However, a benign label does not mean entirely harmless; a brain tumor classified as benign can still cause fatal pressure inside the skull.

Characteristics of Malignant Cells

Malignant diseases are characterized by aggressive growth, lack of differentiation, and the ability to spread. These cancer cells disregard normal tissue boundaries and invade local structures. The hallmark of malignancy is metastasis, where cells break away from the primary tumor and travel through the blood or lymphatics to seed new tumors in distant organs. Malignant cells also stimulate the growth of new blood vessels, a process called angiogenesis, to feed their rapid expansion.

Classification and Types of Neoplastic Disease

  • Carcinomas: Tumors arising from epithelial tissue, which lines body surfaces, cavities, and organs.
  • Sarcomas: Cancers originating from supportive and connective tissues such as bone, muscle, fat, and blood vessels.
  • Leukemias: Malignancies of the blood-forming tissues, primarily the bone marrow, resulting in high numbers of abnormal white blood cells.
  • Lymphomas and Myelomas: Cancers affecting the immune system, specifically involving lymphocytes and plasma cells.

Staging and Grading of Neoplasms

Healthcare providers use staging and grading to plan treatment and predict outcomes.

A high stage means widespread disease, while a high grade indicates poorly differentiated, aggressive cells.

Nursing Management and Care of Oncology Patients

Caring for patients with neoplastic diseases requires a holistic approach that manages physical symptoms alongside emotional distress. Nurses monitor for severe treatment complications such as bone marrow suppression, infection risks from neutropenia, bleeding from thrombocytopenia, and extreme fatigue. Nutritional support, pain management using the WHO ladder, and emotional reassurance for patients and families form the core of daily nursing interventions.
