Nursing Management During Cancer Treatment and Surgery

Overview of Cancer Treatment Modalities

Cancer therapy combines various modalities tailored to the tumor type, stage, and overall health status of the client. Understanding these approaches helps nursing professionals anticipate specific care needs and adverse events. - **Surgery**: Often the primary treatment for solid tumors, aiming for complete removal (curative) or debulking to ease symptoms. - **Chemotherapy**: Systemic cytotoxic medications designed to destroy rapidly dividing cells, affecting both malignant and normal tissues. - **Radiation therapy**: High-energy ionizing rays targeted locally to damage cancer cell DNA while sparing surrounding healthy structures. - **Immunotherapy**: Biologic agents that stimulate the body's own immune system to recognize and attack malignant cells. - **Hormone therapy**: Medications or surgical interventions that block or lower hormone levels to slow the growth of hormone-receptor-positive cancers.

Preoperative and Postoperative Care for Oncological Surgery

Oncological surgeries range from localized excisions to extensive resections like radical neck dissections or pelvic exenterations. Preoperative nursing focuses on baseline nutritional assessment, psychological preparation, and deep-breathing instruction to prevent postoperative pulmonary complications. Postoperative care requires vigilant monitoring for surgical complications alongside cancer-specific risks. Nurses must frequently assess surgical drains, monitor for hemorrhage, and enforce early ambulation to prevent venous thromboembolism. Pain management must be aggressive and proactive, as uncontrolled pain delays recovery and impairs immune function. Additionally, body image alterations resulting from disfiguring surgeries require sensitive, empathetic nursing communication and early referral to support services.

Administration and Safety Protocols for Chemotherapy

Handling and administering cytotoxic medications demand strict adherence to safety protocols to protect both the client and the healthcare provider. Chemotherapy drugs are typically administered via specialized central venous access devices or peripheral lines, depending on the vesicant potential of the agent. A **vesicant** is a drug that can cause severe tissue necrosis and blistering if it infiltrates surrounding subcutaneous tissue. Nurses must verify blood return before administration, monitor the infusion site continuously, and immediately stop the infusion if extravasation is suspected. Standard personal protective equipment—including chemotherapy-rated gloves, gowns, and eye protection—must be worn during preparation and administration to minimize occupational exposure.

Radiation Therapy: Nursing Interventions and Skin Care

Radiation therapy utilizes ionizing radiation to destroy cancer cells within a precise anatomical field. Nurses play a crucial role in managing the side effects of radiation, particularly skin integrity alterations within the treatment portal. Radiation dermatitis can range from mild erythema to severe moist desquamation. Clients must receive explicit skin care education. The treatment markings placed by the radiation oncologist must never be washed off or altered. Nurses should instruct clients to clean the area gently with lukewarm water and mild soap, pat dry rather than rub, and avoid applying lotions, perfumes, or deodorants near the site unless specifically approved by the radiation team. Additionally, clients should avoid exposing the radiated skin to extreme temperatures, direct sunlight, and tight-fitting clothing.

Managing Treatment Side Effects and Toxicities

Cancer treatments disrupt multiple organ systems, leading to predictable acute and chronic toxicities. Bone marrow suppression is among the most frequent, resulting in neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia. - **Neutropenia**: Decreased white blood cells increase infection risk; clients need strict hand hygiene, avoidance of fresh fruits or flowers in severe cases, and prompt reporting of low-grade fevers. - **Thrombocytopenia**: Low platelet counts impair clotting; nurses must monitor for petechiae, purpura, and bleeding gums while teaching clients to use soft-bristled toothbrushes and electric razors. - **Mucositis**: Inflammation of the gastrointestinal mucosa causes painful ulcerations; frequent saline mouth rinses and avoidance of spicy or acidic foods provide relief. - **Alopecia**: Temporary hair loss causes significant emotional distress, requiring supportive counseling and practical head covering options.

Oncological Emergencies: Recognition and Acute Nursing Response

Oncological emergencies are acute, life-threatening complications that can occur as a direct result of the cancer or its treatment. Prompt recognition and swift nursing interventions are essential for survival. - **Superior Vena Cava Syndrome (SVCS)**: Compression of the superior vena cava by tumor mass leads to facial edema, jugular vein distention, and upper extremity swelling. Treatment involves corticosteroids and radiation. - **Spinal Cord Compression**: Tumor expansion pressing on the spinal cord causes severe back pain, motor weakness, sensory loss, and bowel or bladder dysfunction. - **Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS)**: Rapid destruction of malignant cells releases intracellular contents into the bloodstream, causing hyperkalemia, hyperuricemia, hyperphosphatemia, and hypocalcemia, which can lead to acute kidney injury and cardiac arrhythmias. - **Hypercalcemia**: Commonly caused by bone metastasis or ectopic hormone production, resulting in fatigue, confusion, nausea, and muscle weakness.

Nutritional Support and Symptom Management

Cancer cachexia—a complex syndrome characterized by progressive weight loss, muscle atrophy, fatigue, and anorexia—significantly impairs treatment tolerance and survival outcomes. Managing nutritional decline requires aggressive symptom control, including pharmacological interventions for nausea, pain, and early satiety. Nurses should encourage small, frequent meals rich in protein and calories rather than three large meals. Nutritional supplements and enteral or parenteral nutrition may be implemented when oral intake is inadequate. Addressing taste alterations, managing stomatitis, and creating a pleasant, odor-free dining environment further encourage caloric intake and improve the client's quality of life during difficult therapeutic regimens.

Psychosocial Support and Palliative Care Integration

A cancer diagnosis impacts every dimension of a client's life, creating profound emotional distress, anxiety, depression, and spiritual questioning. Integrating palliative care early in the disease trajectory improves symptom management, enhances psychological well-being, and supports family caregivers. Nurses provide essential emotional support through active listening, facilitating open communication, and validating the client's feelings without false reassurance. Interdisciplinary collaboration with social workers, chaplains, and psycho-oncology specialists ensures holistic care. Ultimately, palliative care focuses on optimizing comfort and dignity, ensuring that treatment goals align with the client's personal values and preferences.
