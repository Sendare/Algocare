Nursing Management During Cancer Treatment and Surgery

Introduction to Cancer Treatment Modalities

Cancer treatment requires a comprehensive, multidisciplinary approach tailored to the type, stage, and overall health status of the patient. The primary modalities include surgery, chemotherapy, radiation therapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. As nursing students in Nigeria, understanding these modalities is crucial because you will often care for patients transitioning through different phases of their therapeutic journey. Surgery is frequently used for localized tumors, offering a chance for cure or debulking to enhance the efficacy of other treatments. Chemotherapy utilizes cytotoxic drugs to destroy rapidly dividing cancer cells systemically, though it also impacts healthy rapidly dividing cells like bone marrow and gastrointestinal mucosa. Radiation therapy employs high-energy ionizing radiation to damage the DNA of cancer cells within a specific localized field. Immunotherapy and targeted therapies represent newer, precision-based approaches that either harness the patient's immune system or target specific molecular pathways essential for tumor growth. Nurses serve as the vital link in coordinating these therapies, monitoring patient compliance, educating patients and their families about expected outcomes, and managing adverse events. In resource-constrained settings, recognizing the unique physiological and psychological burdens these treatments impose allows nurses to deliver holistic, patient-centered care. Effective management begins with a thorough baseline assessment, including nutritional status, baseline laboratory values, and psychological readiness, ensuring that any deviation from normal parameters is promptly reported to the multidisciplinary oncological team.

Preoperative and Postoperative Nursing Care for Cancer Surgery

Preoperative and postoperative nursing care for oncological surgical patients requires rigorous clinical vigilance and specialized knowledge. In the preoperative phase, the nurse conducts a comprehensive baseline assessment, ensuring that vital organ functions, nutritional status, and coagulation profiles are evaluated. Psychological support is equally paramount, as patients often experience profound anxiety regarding the cancer diagnosis and the impending surgical procedure. Informed consent must be verified, and education regarding postoperative expectations, such as pain management, deep breathing exercises, and early ambulation, should be thoroughly provided. In the postoperative phase, nursing priorities shift rapidly toward monitoring for complications such as hemorrhage, infection, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and pulmonary embolism. Pain management is critical; nurses must utilize multimodal analgesia and assess pain regularly using validated scales. Wound care requires strict aseptic technique to prevent surgical site infections, which are particularly dangerous in immunocompromised cancer patients. Furthermore, nurses must monitor fluid and electrolyte balance, maintain patency of drainage tubes, and assess gastrointestinal recovery before advancing diets. Early mobilization is encouraged to prevent venous stasis and promote respiratory expansion. By anticipating potential complications and providing meticulous post-surgical care, nurses significantly improve patient recovery rates and overall clinical outcomes in oncological settings.

Administration and Safety Protocols for Chemotherapy

The administration of chemotherapy demands strict adherence to safety protocols due to the high toxicity and hazardous nature of these antineoplastic agents. Nurses handling chemotherapy must be specially certified and utilize appropriate Personal Protective Equipment (PPE), including powder-free chemotherapy gloves, gowns, and eye protection, to minimize occupational exposure through inhalation, absorption, or ingestion. Before administration, double-checking dosages with a second registered nurse is a mandatory safety check to prevent fatal medication errors. Nurses must meticulously verify patient identity, the correct drug, dosage, route, and timing against the prescribed protocol and current laboratory results, particularly absolute neutrophil counts (ANC) and platelet counts. Vascular access management is critical; many chemotherapy drugs are vesicants that can cause severe local tissue necrosis and sloughing if extravasation occurs. Nurses must verify blood return prior to administration and continuously monitor the infusion site for signs of pain, swelling, or redness. In the event of extravasation, the infusion must be stopped immediately, and specific institutional protocols involving antidotes or hot/cold compresses must be initiated. Proper disposal of all materials contaminated with cytotoxic agents, including tubing, syringes, and excreta, must follow hazardous waste guidelines to protect healthcare personnel, patients, and the environment from toxic contamination.

Radiation Therapy: Nursing Interventions and Skin Care

Radiation therapy is a localized cancer treatment modality that uses high-energy rays to destroy cancer cells, but it also affects surrounding healthy tissues, particularly the skin within the treatment field. Nurses play a crucial role in managing radiation dermatitis and supporting patients throughout their radiation schedule. Skin care education is paramount: patients must be instructed to gently wash the irradiated skin with mild soap and lukewarm water, patting it dry rather than rubbing. It is vital to preserve the treatment markings (tattoos or ink) placed by the radiation therapist, meaning patients should avoid scrubbing these areas or using harsh adhesive tapes. Nurses must advise patients to avoid applying lotions, perfumes, powders, or deodorants to the treatment field immediately before a radiation session, as these can alter the dosage delivery by acting as a bolus. Loose-fitting, soft cotton clothing should be worn over the treatment area to minimize friction and mechanical irritation. Furthermore, patients must protect the irradiated skin from extreme temperatures, including ice packs and heating pads, as well as direct sun exposure both during and long after treatment completion. Nurses should regularly assess the skin for erythema, dry desquamation, or moist desquamation, promptly reporting any breakdown to the radiation oncologist so that treatment breaks or specialized dressings can be coordinated to prevent severe infection.

Managing Common Treatment Side Effects and Toxicities

Cancer treatments frequently induce a wide range of side effects and toxicities due to their impact on both malignant and healthy rapidly proliferating cells. Bone marrow suppression, or myelosuppression, leads to neutropenia, anemia, and thrombocytopenia, placing patients at high risk for life-threatening infections, profound fatigue, and spontaneous bleeding. Nurses must monitor complete blood counts (CBC) meticulously and educate patients on infection-prevention strategies, such as frequent hand hygiene, avoiding crowds, and reporting fevers immediately. Gastrointestinal toxicities, including nausea, vomiting, mucositis, and diarrhea, are also prevalent. Prophylactic antiemetics should be administered before chemotherapy infusions, and patients should be encouraged to eat small, frequent, bland meals. Oral hygiene is critical for managing mucositis; nurses should recommend soft-bristled toothbrushes, non-alcoholic mouthwashes, and regular saline rinses to prevent painful oral ulcerations. Alopecia (hair loss) is another distressing psychological side effect; preparing patients beforehand and discussing options like wigs, scarves, or caps can help mitigate emotional distress. Peripheral neuropathy, characterized by numbness, tingling, and pain in the hands and feet, requires careful neurological assessment and patient safety education to prevent falls and burns. By proactively identifying and managing these toxicities, nurses enhance treatment tolerance, promote patient comfort, and prevent avoidable complications.

Oncological Emergencies: Recognition and Acute Nursing Response

Oncological emergencies are acute, life-threatening complications that can arise either as a direct result of the cancer itself or as a side effect of its treatment. Recognizing these emergencies early and initiating a rapid nursing response is critical for survival. Superior Vena Cava (SVC) Obstruction occurs when a tumor compresses the superior vena cava, leading to facial and neck edema, jugular venous distension, and respiratory distress; nursing intervention includes elevating the head of the bed and preparing for emergency imaging or radiation. Spinal Cord Compression is caused by tumor invasion into the spinal epidural space, presenting with progressive back pain, motor weakness, sensory loss, and bowel/bladder dysfunction; nurses must immediately report neurological deficits and maintain spinal precautions. Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS) results from the rapid destruction of large numbers of tumor cells, releasing intracellular potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids into the bloodstream, leading to hyperkalemia, hyperuricemia, hyperphosphatemia, and hypocalcemia, which can precipitate acute kidney injury and cardiac arrhythmias. Nursing management for TLS includes aggressive intravenous hydration, administration of allopurinol or rasburicase, and continuous cardiac monitoring. Hypercalcemia of malignancy, most commonly seen in breast, lung, and multiple myeloma cancers, causes fatigue, confusion, nausea, and constipation; treatment involves aggressive hydration with normal saline and loop diuretics. Quick identification and prompt interprofessional intervention can save the patient's life and stabilize them for ongoing oncological care.

Nutritional Support and Symptom Management

Nutritional support and symptom management are foundational pillars in the holistic care of cancer patients, directly influencing treatment tolerance, immune function, and overall survival. Many cancer patients experience cancer-related anorexia and cachexia—a complex metabolic syndrome characterized by severe muscle wasting, weight loss, and physical fatigue that cannot be entirely reversed by conventional nutritional support. Nurses play an instrumental role in conducting nutritional screenings and assessments to identify patients at risk for malnutrition early. Symptom management must be aggressive; nausea, vomiting, pain, and taste alterations (dysgeusia) should be controlled pharmaceutically and non-pharmaceutically to encourage oral intake. Dietary modifications should include offering small, frequent, nutrient-dense, and high-protein meals rather than large, overwhelming portions. Incorporating favorite foods, utilizing nutritional supplement drinks, and creating a pleasant, calm dining environment can also stimulate appetite. When oral intake remains inadequate despite aggressive symptom management and dietary counseling, artificial nutrition via enteral tube feeding or parenteral nutrition may be indicated. Nurses are responsible for monitoring feeding tolerance, maintaining secure tube or central line placements, preventing infection, and educating the patient and family on home management. Addressing nutritional deficits not only preserves physical strength and organ function but also profoundly improves the patient's quality of life throughout their oncological journey.

Psychosocial Support and Palliative Care Integration

Psychosocial support and the integration of palliative care are essential components of comprehensive cancer management, focusing on the quality of life of both the patient and their family. A cancer diagnosis frequently triggers profound psychological distress, including anxiety, depression, fear of recurrence, and existential crisis. Nurses provide essential emotional support through active listening, compassionate communication, offering realistic hope, and validating the patient's feelings. Referral to professional psychological counseling, support groups, or psychiatric services should be facilitated when distress becomes clinically significant. Palliative care is often misunderstood as solely end-of-life care, but current guidelines recommend its early integration alongside curative treatments to manage distressing symptoms like pain, dyspnea, fatigue, and depression. By addressing physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs concurrently, palliative care enhances comfort and resilience. As patients approach the end of life, nurses coordinate hospice care, ensuring pain is rigorously controlled, and provide bereavement support to grieving family members. In Nigerian and broader cultural contexts, nurses must sensitively navigate family dynamics, spiritual beliefs, and cultural practices surrounding illness and dying, ensuring that care remains respectful, dignified, and centered on the patient's expressed wishes and values.
