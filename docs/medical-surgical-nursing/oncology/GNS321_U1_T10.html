Nursing Management During Cancer Treatment and Surgery

Overview of Cancer Treatment Modalities

Cancer treatment plans vary widely depending on the type, location, and stage of the disease. Often, patients receive a combination of therapies to achieve the best possible outcome. Understanding these main approaches helps nurses anticipate specific care needs and prepare patients for what lies ahead.

  • Surgery: Used to remove solid tumors, obtain a tissue biopsy, or relieve pressure on surrounding organs in palliative cases.
  • Chemotherapy: Systemic medications that travel throughout the body to target and destroy rapidly dividing cells, affecting both cancerous and healthy cells.
  • Radiation therapy: The use of high-energy rays directed locally to destroy cancer cells and shrink tumors within a specific treatment field.
  • Biotherapy and targeted therapy: Newer treatments that use the body's immune system or focus on specific genetic changes unique to the cancer cells.

Preoperative and Postoperative Nursing Care for Cancer Surgery

Surgical intervention remains a cornerstone for diagnosing, staging, and treating many solid tumors. Preoperative nursing care focuses on baseline physical assessments, screening for nutritional deficits, and providing psychological reassurance to an anxious patient and their family. Nurses must thoroughly review lab results, especially coagulation profiles and complete blood counts, since cancer and prior therapies can compromise healing.

Postoperative care shifts toward preventing common surgical complications while managing cancer-specific risks. Nurses monitor incision sites closely for signs of delayed healing, infection, or dehiscence. Pain control is paramount, as unmanaged pain shallowly alters breathing patterns and increases the risk of atelectasis. Early ambulation, deep breathing exercises, and meticulous DVT prophylaxis are standard nursing interventions to promote recovery after major oncological resections.

Administration and Safety Protocols for Chemotherapy

Administering cytotoxic medications demands strict adherence to safety protocols to protect both the patient and the healthcare team. Nurses handling these agents must wear personal protective equipment, including powder-free chemotherapy gloves, gowns, and eye protection when risk of splashing exists. Specialized disposal containers for hazardous waste are required in every clinical setting where these drugs are prepared or administered.

Before starting an infusion, nurses verify the patient's identity, review baseline laboratory values—particularly absolute neutrophil counts and platelet counts—and check the patency of the intravenous access. Many chemotherapy drugs are vesicants, meaning they cause severe tissue necrosis if they leak out of the vein. Regular checks for blood return and patient feedback regarding stinging or burning at the site are mandatory throughout the infusion to catch extravasation early.

Radiation Therapy and Skin Management

Radiation therapy targets local malignancies using ionizing radiation, damaging the DNA of cancer cells. Because the rays pass through healthy tissue to reach the tumor, regional skin reactions are common. Nurses play a vital role in educating patients on how to care for irradiated skin during the weeks of treatment.

Skin care protocols emphasize gentleness and minimizing irritation. Patients should wash the area using mild soap and warm water, patting dry gently rather than rubbing. Applying harsh commercial lotions, perfumes, or powders over the treatment field is strictly discouraged, as are heating pads, ice packs, and tight clothing that creates friction. Maintaining skin integrity prevents painful desquamation and reduces the risk of secondary infections.

Managing Treatment Side Effects and Toxicities

Systemic and local cancer treatments bring a wide array of side effects that challenge both patients and nursing staff. Myelosuppression remains one of the most frequent and dangerous toxicities, leaving patients vulnerable to anemia, bleeding, and life-threatening infections. Gastrointestinal toxicities like nausea, vomiting, mucositis, and diarrhea severely impact nutrition and comfort.

Proactive symptom management makes a tremendous difference in treatment tolerance. Antiemetics work best when given before chemotherapy rather than waiting until nausea starts. For patients experiencing mucositis, nurses encourage frequent gentle mouth rinses with saline, avoiding alcohol-based mouthwashes that burn and dry the oral mucosa. Monitoring blood counts regularly allows early intervention with growth factors or blood products before severe complications develop.

Oncological Emergencies

Oncological emergencies are acute, life-threatening events caused either by the cancer itself or as a direct complication of its treatment. Recognizing early warning signs is vital because rapid medical intervention can save a patient's life.

  • Superior Vena Cava Syndrome (SVCS): Compression of the great vein by a tumor, causing facial edema, neck vein distension, and respiratory distress.
  • Spinal Cord Compression: Tumor invasion into the spinal column, presenting with new back pain, motor weakness, and bowel or bladder changes.
  • Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS): Massive destruction of cancer cells releasing cellular contents into the blood, causing severe electrolyte imbalances like hyperkalemia and hyperuricemia that threaten kidney function.
  • Febrile Neutropenia: A medical emergency defined by a fever in a patient with a very low neutrophil count, requiring immediate blood cultures and broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Nutritional Support and Symptom Management

Cancer and its aggressive treatments often trigger profound nutritional decline, manifesting as cachexia, anorexia, taste alterations, and severe weight loss. Nurses must assess nutritional status continuously, tracking weight changes, dietary intake, and lab markers like serum albumin. Adequate nutrition supports tissue healing, immune function, and overall treatment tolerance.

Managing barriers to eating requires practical, individualized nursing strategies. Offering small, frequent meals rather than three large plates can prevent overwhelming a nauseated patient. Foods should be nutrient-dense, high in protein, and served at room temperature to minimize strong odors that trigger aversive responses. When oral intake remains inadequate despite aggressive symptom control, enteral tube feeding or parenteral nutrition may be necessary to sustain the patient.

Psychosocial Support and Palliative Care Integration

A cancer diagnosis brings immense emotional distress, impacting not only the patient but their entire support network. Feelings of anxiety, depression, grief, and existential fear are entirely normal responses to a life-altering illness. Nurses provide foundational emotional support by offering a compassionate presence, actively listening to fears, and connecting patients with professional counseling or support groups when needed.

Palliative care should never be reserved solely for the end of life; it integrates early alongside active disease treatment to enhance quality of life. By focusing aggressively on symptom relief, spiritual support, and clear communication regarding goals of care, palliative teams help patients navigate complex treatment decisions. This holistic approach ensures that dignity, comfort, and personal values remain at the center of the nursing care plan.
