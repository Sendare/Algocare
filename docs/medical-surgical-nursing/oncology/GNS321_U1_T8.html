Management of Patients with Cancer

Introduction to Cancer Care and Treatment Goals

Cancer care requires a shift in perspective from acute disease intervention to long-term chronic management and supportive care. Treatment goals generally fall into three distinct categories: curative, control, and palliative. Curative therapy aims for complete eradication of malignant cells, often utilizing aggressive regimens when the cancer is localized. Control therapy halts the progression of the disease and manages the tumor burden when a cure is unattainable, allowing patients to live extended, comfortable lives. Palliative goals focus exclusively on symptom relief and optimizing the quality of life for individuals with advanced, incurable malignancies. Nigerian nursing practice often encounters patients presenting at late clinical stages, making palliative goals a dominant focus in local oncology wards. Establishing clear goals with the patient and multidisciplinary care team prevents futile interventions and directs clinical decision-making toward what truly benefits the individual.

Surgical Management in Oncology

Surgical intervention remains one of the oldest and most effective modalities for treating solid tumors. Procedures serve multiple purposes across the cancer trajectory:

  • Diagnostic Surgery: Biopsy procedures to obtain tissue samples for definitive histological confirmation and staging.
  • Curative Surgery: Complete resection of the primary tumor along with a margin of healthy surrounding tissue to ensure no microscopic disease remains.
  • Prophylactic Surgery: Removal of non-vital organs or tissues that are at a significantly high risk of developing malignancy based on genetic markers.
  • Palliative Surgery: Debulking procedures performed to relieve pain, obstruction, or hemorrhage, improving immediate comfort rather than extending survival.
  • Reconstructive Surgery: Restoring form and function following disfiguring tumor resections, commonly seen in breast or head and neck cancers.

Principles and Nursing Care of Patients Undergoing Radiation Therapy

Radiation therapy utilizes high-energy ionizing radiation to damage the DNA of malignant cells, leading to cellular death or reproductive failure. Because radiation affects rapidly dividing cells indiscriminately, fast-growing normal tissues like the skin, mucous membranes, and bone marrow sustain collateral damage. Nurses play a vital role in managing localized skin reactions, which can range from mild erythema to moist desquamation. Patients must be taught to keep the irradiated skin dry, avoid applying harsh soaps or lotions before treatment sessions, and wear loose-fitting cotton clothing to minimize friction. Fatigue is another universal side effect that accumulates over the course of treatment, requiring balanced pacing of daily activities. Dietary modifications are often necessary for individuals receiving pelvic or abdominal radiation, as mucosal irritation along the gastrointestinal tract frequently triggers nausea, cramping, and diarrhea.

Chemotherapy Administration and Safety Precautions

Systemic chemotherapy medications circulate throughout the entire body to target dividing cells, making them effective against metastatic disease. Safe administration requires specialized certification, rigorous double-checking protocols, and personal protective equipment due to the hazardous, cytotoxic nature of these drugs. Extravasation—the inadvertent leakage of vesicant chemotherapy into surrounding subcutaneous tissues—poses a severe nursing emergency that can cause extensive tissue necrosis. Nurses must constantly monitor infusion sites for signs of pain, swelling, or the absence of blood return. Common systemic toxicities arise from bone marrow suppression, leading to neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and anemia. Managing these vulnerabilities demands strict infection control practices, bleeding precautions, and patient education regarding fever reporting, which remains a medical emergency in immunocompromised individuals.

Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy

Unlike traditional chemotherapy that attacks all rapidly dividing cells, targeted therapies focus on specific molecular mechanisms, proteins, or signaling pathways that drive cancer growth. This precision approach often results in a different side effect profile, frequently featuring dermatologic toxicities such as acneiform rashes, hypertension, and gastrointestinal disturbances. Immunotherapy harnesses the patient's own immune system to recognize and destroy malignant cells by blocking immune checkpoint inhibitors that cancers use to hide from T-cells. While innovative, immunotherapy introduces unique immune-related adverse events, characterized by inflammatory conditions in healthy organs like colitis, pneumonitis, thyroiditis, and hepatitis. Nurses monitor closely for subtle changes in respiratory status or bowel habits, as unchecked immune-mediated inflammation can rapidly escalate into life-threatening organ failure if corticosteroids are not promptly administered.

Oncological Emergencies and Acute Complications

Oncological emergencies require rapid recognition and immediate intervention to prevent irreversible organ damage or death. Tumor lysis syndrome occurs when massive numbers of cancer cells are destroyed rapidly, releasing intracellular potassium, phosphate, and nucleic acids into the bloodstream, which causes severe hyperkalemia, hyperuricemia, and acute kidney injury. Spinal cord compression presents with progressive back pain, motor weakness, sensory loss, and bowel or bladder dysfunction, demanding urgent corticosteroids and imaging. Superior vena cava syndrome results from tumor compression of the great vessels, causing facial edema, jugular venous distension, and respiratory distress. Hypercalcemia of malignancy, frequently driven by bone metastases or parathyroid-like hormone secretion, causes profound fatigue, confusion, and constipation. Prompt identification of these acute shifts allows nurses to initiate life-saving protocols before permanent physiological compromise occurs.

Pain and Symptom Management in Cancer

Symptom management is foundational to comprehensive oncology care, as chronic pain and debilitating distress compromise every dimension of a patient's well-being. The World Health Organization analgesic ladder guides pharmacological intervention, moving from non-opioids for mild pain to potent opioids like morphine for moderate-to-severe pain. Nurses must dispel common myths regarding opioid addiction and tolerance when dealing with cancer pain, ensuring patients receive adequate, around-the-clock dosing combined with breakthrough medications. Beyond pain, cancer cachexia, nausea, and chronic fatigue severely diminish quality of life. Nutritional support, antiemetics administered proactively before emetogenic treatments, and energy conservation strategies help mitigate these pervasive physical burdens. Effective symptom control requires regular, systematic reassessment using validated rating scales rather than relying solely on spontaneous patient complaints.

Psychosocial Support and Palliative Care

A cancer diagnosis triggers profound emotional distress, anxiety, depression, and existential questioning for both the patient and their family. Psychosocial support involves active listening, facilitating open communication, and connecting individuals with counseling services and support groups. Palliative care runs parallel to disease-directed treatment, focusing explicitly on relief from the symptoms, pain, and stress of serious illness. Integrating palliative principles early in the disease trajectory improves quality of life and helps align medical interventions with the patient's personal values and preferences. In many Nigerian communities, terminal illness carries heavy cultural and spiritual significance, requiring nurses to engage family members sensitively while honoring the patient's autonomy and dignity through the end-of-life journey.
