Management of Patients with Cancer

Introduction to Cancer Care and Treatment Goals

Cancer care begins the moment a patient receives a definitive diagnosis. Treatment planning requires a clear understanding of the disease stage, tumor biology, and the overall health status of the patient. Nurses play a pivotal role in guiding patients and their families through this overwhelming initial phase by clarifying medical jargon and helping them adjust to the diagnosis.

Treatment goals generally fall into three distinct categories:

  1. Cure: The primary aim is complete eradication of the malignant disease, restoring the patient to their pre-illness state of health.
  2. Control: When a complete cure is unattainable, treatment focuses on arresting tumor growth, preventing further spread, and prolonging life.
  3. Palliative: When neither cure nor control is possible, the goal shifts entirely to relieving symptoms, managing pain, and maximizing the remaining quality of life.

Surgical Management in Oncology

Surgery remains one of the oldest and most effective interventions for solid tumors. Depending on the clinical intent, oncology surgery serves several distinct purposes:

  • Diagnostic Biopsy: Obtaining a tissue sample to confirm the exact cell type and grade of the malignancy.
  • Prophylactic Surgery: Removing non-vital tissues or organs that are at a significantly high genetic risk of developing cancer.
  • Curative Surgery: Excision of the local tumor along with a margin of healthy surrounding tissue to ensure complete removal.
  • Palliative Surgery: Performed not to cure, but to relieve distressing symptoms such as bowel obstruction or severe pain caused by tumor pressure.

Postoperative nursing care focuses on monitoring for typical surgical complications like hemorrhage and infection, while also watching closely for organ dysfunction specific to the operative site. Nurses must also address the psychological impact of altered body image resulting from radical resections, such as a mastectomy or colostomy.

Principles and Nursing Care in Radiation Therapy

Radiation therapy uses high-energy ionizing rays to destroy cancer cells by damaging their internal DNA, halting their ability to divide and multiply. It can be delivered externally via a machine outside the body (teletherapy) or internally through radioactive implants placed directly into or near the tumor site (brachytherapy).

Patients receiving external beam radiation require careful skin care because the rays pass through healthy skin to reach the deep tumor. Nurses instruct patients to keep the marked skin area clean and dry, avoid scrubbing the markings, and protect the site from extreme temperatures and direct sunlight.

For patients undergoing internal unsealed or sealed brachytherapy, safety protocols center on minimizing staff and visitor radiation exposure. Nurses caring for brachytherapy patients strictly manage time, distance, and shielding principles, often limiting bedside exposure and wearing specialized dosimeter badges.

Chemotherapy Administration and Safety Precautions

Chemotherapy involves systemic medications designed to travel throughout the body and destroy rapidly dividing cells. Because these drugs cannot easily distinguish between malignant cells and normal rapidly dividing healthy cells—such as bone marrow, hair follicles, and the gastrointestinal lining—patients frequently experience widespread side effects like alopecia, myelosuppression, and mucositis.

Administering these potent drugs requires rigorous safety standards. Cytotoxic agents are hazardous substances capable of causing reproductive toxicity or secondary malignancies in healthcare workers with chronic exposure. Nurses must wear personal protective equipment, including powder-free chemotherapy gloves, gowns, and eye protection when handling drugs or bodily fluids containing remnants of chemotherapy.

Extravasation represents a major acute risk during intravenous administration. If a vesicant chemotherapy drug leaks out of the vein and into the surrounding subcutaneous tissue, it can cause severe tissue necrosis. Nurses must immediately stop the infusion, notify the provider, and follow institutional protocols regarding warm or cold compresses and specific antidote medications.

Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy

Unlike traditional chemotherapy that broadly attacks all fast-dividing cells, targeted therapies focus on specific molecular differences and proteins unique to cancer cells. By inhibiting specific enzymes or receptors involved in tumor growth, these medications interrupt signaling pathways while sparing many healthy tissues.

Immunotherapy takes a different approach by harnessing the patient's own immune system to recognize and destroy malignant cells. Immune checkpoint inhibitors are a common class of immunotherapy that release natural brakes on T-cells, allowing them to mount an aggressive attack against the tumor.

Nurses managing patients on immunotherapy must monitor closely for immune-related adverse events. Because the immune system becomes hyperactive, it can mistakenly attack healthy organs, requiring prompt corticosteroid intervention.

Oncological Emergencies and Acute Complications

Cancer and its aggressive treatments can trigger sudden, life-threatening clinical crises that require immediate nursing and medical intervention. Recognizing early warning signs is vital to preventing patient mortality.

  • Superior Vena Cava Syndrome (SVCS): Compression of the superior vena cava by mediastinal tumors, leading to facial edema, jugular vein distension, and upper body plethora.
  • Spinal Cord Compression: Tumor invasion into the epidural space causing severe back pain, motor weakness, sensory loss, and potential bowel or bladder dysfunction.
  • Tumor Lysis Syndrome (TLS): Rapid destruction of a massive number of cancer cells releases intracellular contents into the bloodstream, resulting in severe hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperuricemia, and hypocalcemia that can precipitate acute kidney injury.
  • Febrile Neutropenia: A medical emergency defined as a single oral temperature of 38.3°C or higher in a patient with an absolute neutrophil count below 1,000 cells/mm³, requiring immediate broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics.

Pain and Symptom Management in Cancer

Pain is one of the most feared symptoms experienced by cancer patients. Effective pain management follows the WHO analgesic ladder, moving from non-opioids for mild pain to strong opioids for moderate to severe pain. Nurses must assess pain regularly, believing the patient's self-report, and administer analgesics around the clock rather than strictly on an as-needed basis for chronic cancer pain.

Beyond pain, cancer patients frequently battle cancer-related fatigue, nausea, and cachexia. Fatigue differs from normal tiredness and does not resolve with rest; nurses help patients manage energy conservation techniques. Antiemetics should be given proactively before emetogenic chemotherapy treatments rather than waiting for nausea to begin. Proper nutritional support and honest, gentle conversations about changes in appetite help address wasting syndromes.

Psychosocial Support and Palliative Care

A cancer diagnosis triggers profound emotional distress, anxiety, and depression for both the patient and their loved ones. Nurses provide holistic care by listening actively, offering a reassuring presence, and connecting families with social workers, spiritual counselors, and community support groups.

Palliative care is not restricted to end-of-life care; it can and should be integrated alongside active disease-modifying treatments from the time of diagnosis. The core objective is to improve the quality of life by addressing physical, psychological, social, and spiritual needs. When curative options are exhausted, hospice care steps in to ensure a dignified, comfortable passing, supporting family members through their grief and bereavement process.
