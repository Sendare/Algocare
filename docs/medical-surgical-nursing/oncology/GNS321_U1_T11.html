Cancer Rehabilitation

Introduction and Objectives of Cancer Rehabilitation

Cancer rehabilitation helps individuals affected by malignancy maintain or restore physical, psychological, social, and vocational functioning throughout their disease trajectory. Historically viewed as palliative or limited to end-of-life care, modern oncology recognizes rehabilitation as an essential continuum starting at the time of diagnosis. Malignancies and their treatments—including surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy—frequently induce profound physiological impairments. The overarching goal is not merely survival, but the preservation of independence and dignity. By addressing functional deficits early, healthcare providers mitigate long-term disability, reduce hospital readmission rates, and enhance health-related quality of life for patients navigating complex therapeutic regimens.

The Multidisciplinary Team Approach

Comprehensive cancer care requires coordinated input from diverse professional disciplines to address multifaceted patient needs. No single specialty possesses the breadth of skill required to manage the systemic impact of oncological treatments.

  • Oncology Nurses: Monitor clinical status, coordinate care transitions, and manage acute symptom flare-ups.
  • Physical and Occupational Therapists: Restore musculoskeletal strength, gait stability, and independent performance of activities of daily living.
  • Dietitians: Assess nutritional status, calculate metabolic requirements, and combat cachexia.
  • Social Workers and Psychologists: Address financial toxicity, coping mechanisms, and emotional distress for both patients and their families.
  • Physicians: Direct medical therapies, manage pharmacotherapy, and oversee oncological disease progression.

Comprehensive Functional Assessment

A thorough baseline assessment establishes the foundation for individualized rehabilitation planning. Clinicians evaluate physical strength, joint range of motion, cardiovascular endurance, and cognitive processing speed. Standardized tools such as the Karnofsky Performance Scale or the ECOG performance status quantify a patient's ability to self-care and perform physical activity. In busy Nigerian clinical settings where formal electronic records may be limited, structured paper-based screening questions regarding baseline mobility and self-care deficits remain vital. Beyond physical metrics, assessments must capture psychological readiness, social support structures, and home environmental barriers to ensure safe discharge planning.

Management of Cancer-Related Fatigue and Pain

Cancer-related fatigue and pain represent two of the most debilitating symptoms reported by individuals undergoing oncological therapy. Unlike normal exhaustion, cancer-related fatigue does not resolve with rest and severely limits daily functioning. Interventions combine non-pharmacological approaches, such as structured aerobic exercise and conservation of energy techniques, with targeted pharmacological management. Pain management follows the World Health Organization analgesic ladder, balancing opioids and adjuvant medications while monitoring for side effects like constipation and sedation. Clinicians must routinely screen for both symptoms using validated visual analog scales, as underreporting remains common due to patient fears of distracting the medical team from primary cancer treatment.

Physical Therapy and Mobility Interventions

Physical therapy in oncology aims to prevent deconditioning, maintain joint flexibility, and manage treatment-induced toxicities such as chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy or radiation fibrosis. Prescribed exercise programs are highly individualized, taking into account platelet counts, hemoglobin levels, and metastatic bone involvement to prevent pathological fractures. Resistance training combats muscle wasting, while balance exercises reduce fall risks among frail survivors. Clinicians educate patients on proper body mechanics and adaptive equipment usage, empowering them to navigate their environments safely despite physical limitations.

Nutritional Support and Metabolic Care

Malignancy and intensive therapies frequently induce metabolic alterations, anorexia, and cancer cachexia—a complex wasting syndrome characterized by progressive weight loss and skeletal muscle depletion. Adequate nutritional support is vital to maintain immune function, improve treatment tolerance, and enhance wound healing after surgical resections. Clinical dietitians perform detailed nutritional screenings to identify deficiencies early. Interventions range from dietary counseling and high-calorie medical supplements to enteral tube feeding or parenteral nutrition when the gastrointestinal tract cannot safely absorb nutrients. Managing treatment side effects like nausea, mucositis, and dysgeusia remains essential to encourage adequate oral intake.

Psychosocial Support and Survivorship Care

The psychological burden of a cancer diagnosis extends far beyond physical healing, frequently triggering anxiety, depression, body image distress, and fear of disease recurrence. Survivorship care plans provide structured roadmaps for post-treatment follow-up, outlining surveillance schedules for recurrence, potential late-occurring toxicities, and lifestyle recommendations. Psychosocial support encompasses individual counseling, peer support groups, and psychiatric referrals when necessary. Empowering survivors with clear education regarding healthy lifestyle modifications—such as smoking cessation, balanced nutrition, and regular physical activity—fosters long-term wellness and helps patients reclaim control over their lives.
