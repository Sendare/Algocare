Pathophysiology of Respiratory Disorders

Normal Pulmonary Mechanics and Gas Exchange

Respiration depends on a seamless coordination between ventilation, diffusion, and perfusion. Ventilation moves air into and out of the lungs, driven by pressure gradients. When the diaphragm contracts, the thoracic cavity expands, lowering intrapleural pressure and drawing air inward.

Gas exchange occurs across the alveolocapillary membrane. Oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the pulmonary capillaries, binding to hemoglobin, while carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction to be expelled.

  • Compliance: The ease with which the lungs and chest wall stretch during inspiration.
  • Elastic Recoil: The tendency of the lungs to return to their original size after stretching during expiration.
  • Surfactant: A lipoprotein complex that lowers surface tension within the alveoli, preventing them from collapsing.

Mechanisms of Airway Obstruction and Restriction

Pulmonary disorders generally fall into two major categories: obstructive and restrictive defects. Understanding the mechanics of each helps clarify why patients struggle to breathe.

Obstructive disorders make it difficult to exhale air quickly. Air gets trapped inside the lungs due to increased resistance in the airways. Common causes include mucosal swelling, bronchospasm, and excessive mucus production.

Restrictive disorders prevent the lungs from fully expanding. The problem lies in reduced compliance, meaning the lung tissue or chest wall is stiff. This stiffness limits the total volume of air the lungs can hold.

Ventilation-Perfusion Mismatch and Shunting

For optimal gas exchange, ventilation (airflow) and perfusion (blood flow) must match. In a healthy lung, this ventilation-perfusion ratio is well balanced. When this balance is disrupted, hypoxemia develops.

  • High V/Q Mismatch: Ventilation exceeds perfusion. Alveolar dead space occurs when blood flow is blocked, such as in a pulmonary embolism, meaning air reaches the alveoli but no blood is there to pick it up.
  • Low V/Q Mismatch: Perfusion exceeds ventilation. Blood passes through alveoli that are poorly ventilated, such as in pneumonia or atelectasis, meaning the blood gets no fresh oxygen.
  • Shunting: An extreme low V/Q state where blood moves from the right side of the heart to the left side without ever touching functioning alveoli.

Pathophysiology of Obstructive Pulmonary Diseases

Conditions like asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) are characterized by chronic airflow limitation.

In asthma, exposure to triggers causes acute bronchoconstriction, airway inflammation, and thick mucus production. This process is largely reversible with treatment.

COPD, which includes chronic bronchitis and emphysema, is usually progressive and irreversible. Chronic bronchitis involves inflammation of the large and small airways with hypersecretion of mucus, producing a chronic productive cough. Emphysema involves the permanent destruction of alveolar walls and loss of elastic recoil. As alveoli merge into large, floppy air spaces, air trapping worsens, flattening the diaphragm and increasing the work of breathing.

Pathophysiology of Restrictive Pulmonary Diseases

Restrictive diseases limit lung volume because the chest wall, pleura, or lung parenchyma cannot expand normally. These conditions are categorized as either intrinsic (affecting the lung tissue itself) or extrinsic (affecting the chest wall, pleura, or neuromuscular system).

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis is a common intrinsic restrictive disease. Chronic inflammation leads to the destruction of lung architecture and replacement with fibrotic scar tissue. This stiff scar tissue thickens the alveolocapillary membrane, severely impairing gas diffusion.

Extrinsic causes include conditions like severe scoliosis, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, and neuromuscular disorders like myasthenia gravis, where weak breathing muscles prevent full lung expansion.

Pulmonary Vascular Disorders and Hypertension

The pulmonary circulation is a low-pressure system designed to handle the entire cardiac output. When pulmonary vessels become constricted, blocked, or destroyed, resistance rises, leading to pulmonary hypertension.

Pulmonary arterial hypertension increases the workload of the right ventricle. The right side of the heart must pump against unusually high pressure in the lungs. Over time, this chronic strain causes right ventricular hypertrophy and eventual failure, a condition known as cor pulmonale.

A classic acute vascular disorder is a pulmonary embolus, where a blood clot from the deep veins of the legs travels through the right side of the heart and lodges in the pulmonary arterial bed, abruptly cutting off blood flow to a section of the lung.

Clinical Manifestations and Compensatory Mechanisms

When respiratory function falters, the body activates several compensatory mechanisms to maintain adequate oxygen delivery.

Dyspnea, or shortness of breath, is the hallmark subjective symptom of respiratory distress, driven by chemoreceptors detecting low oxygen, high carbon dioxide, or acidemia. Tachypnea and the use of accessory muscles develop to increase ventilation.

Chronically low blood oxygen triggers the kidneys to release erythropoietin, stimulating the bone marrow to produce more red blood cells, a condition called secondary polycythemia. While this increases oxygen-carrying capacity, it also thickens the blood, worsening the workload on the heart. Cyanosis, a bluish discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes, appears late when deoxygenated hemoglobin rises.
