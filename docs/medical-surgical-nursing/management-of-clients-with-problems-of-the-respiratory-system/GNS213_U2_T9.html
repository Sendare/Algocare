Pathophysiology of Respiratory Disorders

Introduction to Respiratory Pathophysiology

Respiratory pathophysiology is a core foundational topic for nursing students in Nigeria preparing for professional examinations and clinical practice. It involves the study of functional changes that occur within the respiratory system during disease states. Understanding how diseases alter normal respiratory function allows nurses to anticipate clinical deterioration, interpret diagnostic investigations accurately, and deliver targeted, evidence-based nursing interventions. Respiratory disorders disrupt the primary function of the respiratory system, which is to deliver oxygen to the tissues and remove carbon dioxide. When this system fails, either due to airway obstruction, alveolar destruction, pulmonary vascular disease, or chest wall abnormalities, systemic homeostasis is severely threatened. In the Nigerian clinical context, where nurses frequently encounter patients with communicable and non-communicable respiratory conditions such as tuberculosis, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and pneumonia, mastering pathophysiology is vital. It bridges the gap between basic medical sciences and bedside nursing care, enabling the nurse to assess breath sounds, monitor oxygen saturation, administer oxygen therapy safely, and manage respiratory emergencies effectively. A strong grasp of these mechanisms ensures that nursing students can critically analyze patient scenarios and provide holistic, safe care.

Normal Pulmonary Mechanics and Gas Exchange

Normal pulmonary mechanics involve the physical forces and processes that govern the movement of air into and out of the lungs, known as ventilation. This process relies on pressure gradients created by the contraction of respiratory muscles, primarily the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, which alter thoracic cavity volume. According to Boyle's law, as thoracic volume increases during inspiration, intrapulmonary pressure drops below atmospheric pressure, causing air to flow into the lungs. Expiration is typically a passive process driven by elastic recoil of the lung tissue and chest wall as the respiratory muscles relax. Gas exchange, the second critical component, occurs across the extremely thin alveolar-capillary membrane via passive diffusion. Oxygen moves from the alveoli, where its partial pressure is high, into the pulmonary capillaries where partial pressure is low, binding primarily to hemoglobin. Simultaneously, carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction, passing from the venous blood of the pulmonary capillaries into the alveoli to be exhaled. Factors influencing this diffusion include the surface area of the membrane, the thickness of the membrane, and the diffusion coefficient of the gases, as described by Fick's law of diffusion. Any alterations in compliance, airway resistance, or alveolar membrane integrity can impair these mechanical and diffusion processes, resulting in respiratory compromise.

Mechanisms of Airway Obstruction and Restriction

Airway obstruction and restriction are two major pathophysiological categories of pulmonary dysfunction that impede effective breathing. Airway obstruction is characterized by an increased resistance to airflow, particularly during expiration. This is frequently caused by conditions that narrow or destroy the airways, such as chronic bronchitis, asthma, and emphysema. In obstructive disorders, air is easily inspired, but trapping occurs because the narrowed airways collapse during forced exhalation, leading to prolonged expiratory times and hyperinflation of the lungs. Conversely, restriction involves a reduction in total lung capacity and compliance, meaning the lungs cannot fully expand. Restrictive disorders can be intrinsic, affecting the lung parenchyma itself (such as pulmonary fibrosis, interstitial lung disease, and infant respiratory distress syndrome), or extrinsic, involving extrapulmonary structures like the chest wall, pleura, or neuromuscular system (such as severe scoliosis, myasthenia gravis, or obesity). In restrictive pathologies, the work of breathing increases significantly because the patient must exert more effort to expand stiff, non-compliant lungs or chest walls. Nurses must differentiate between these two patterns during assessment, as they present with distinct clinical features, spirometric findings, and management protocols in clinical settings.

Ventilation-Perfusion Mismatch and Shunting

Optimal gas exchange requires a matched relationship between ventilation (airflow to the alveoli, denoted as V) and perfusion (blood flow through the pulmonary capillaries, denoted as Q). In a healthy lung, the overall ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) ratio is approximately 0.8. However, regional variations exist due to gravity, with upper lung zones receiving more ventilation relative to perfusion, and lower zones receiving more perfusion relative to ventilation. A V/Q mismatch occurs when this delicate balance is disrupted. A high V/Q ratio (wasted ventilation) happens when alveoli are well-ventilated but receive inadequate blood supply, such as in a pulmonary embolism where blood flow is obstructed. Conversely, a low V/Q ratio occurs when alveoli receive adequate blood flow but insufficient ventilation, seen in conditions like atelectasis, asthma, or pneumonia. The extreme manifestation of a low V/Q ratio is a shunt, where deoxygenated blood bypasses the alveoli entirely without participating in gas exchange at all (a V/Q ratio of zero). Conditions like severe pulmonary edema or congenital heart defects can cause shunting, leading to profound hypoxemia that is characteristically refractory to simple supplemental oxygen therapy. Nurses caring for patients with V/Q mismatches must closely monitor oxygen saturation trends and administer prescribed oxygen or respiratory therapies to mitigate tissue hypoxia.

Pathophysiology of Obstructive Pulmonary Diseases

Obstructive pulmonary diseases encompass chronic conditions characterized by persistent airflow limitation. The two most common clinical entities are chronic bronchitis and emphysema, frequently grouped under chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), as well as bronchial asthma. Chronic bronchitis involves chronic inflammation of the large and small airways, characterized by hypertrophy of mucous glands, excessive mucus hypersecretion, and chronic productive cough. This leads to airway lumen narrowing and frequent bacterial infections. Emphysema, on the other hand, involves the abnormal and permanent enlargement of air spaces distal to the terminal bronchioles, accompanied by the destruction of alveolar walls and loss of elastic recoil. This loss of elasticity causes alveolar collapse during expiration, resulting in severe air trapping, hyperinflation, and increased residual volume. Asthma is an inflammatory disorder characterized by hyper-reactive airways, reversible bronchoconstriction, mucosal edema, and thick mucus production, often triggered by allergens, exercise, or infections. Pathophysiologically, these obstructive diseases increase airway resistance, reduce forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), and create significant work of breathing. Patients often present with dyspnea, wheezing, and pursed-lip breathing as they try to keep their airways open during exhalation.

Pathophysiology of Restrictive Pulmonary Diseases

Restrictive pulmonary diseases are characterized by a reduction in lung volumes and impaired lung expansion. Pathophysiologically, they are divided into intrinsic conditions (affecting the lung parenchyma) and extrinsic conditions (affecting the chest wall, pleura, or neuromuscular apparatus). Intrinsic restrictive diseases, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, cause chronic inflammation and fibrosis of the alveolar interstitium. This deposition of collagen and scar tissue thickens the alveolar-capillary membrane, severely impairing gas diffusion and decreasing lung compliance. Extrinsic restrictive disorders do not directly harm lung tissue initially, but instead limit chest wall movement or neural drive. Examples include severe kyphoscoliosis, ankylosing spondylitis, pleural effusion, morbid obesity, and neuromuscular disorders like myasthenia gravis or Guillain-Barré syndrome, which weaken the diaphragm and intercostal muscles. Regardless of the specific cause, the hallmark of all restrictive disorders is a decreased total lung capacity (TLC), forced vital capacity (FVC), and reduced compliance. Patients typically present with rapid, shallow breathing (tachypnea), dry cough, and progressive dyspnea on exertion because stiff lungs or restricted chest walls require excessive energy to inflate.

Pulmonary Vascular Disorders and Hypertension

Pulmonary vascular disorders directly affect the circulation of blood through the lungs, disrupting the normal low-pressure, high-capacity pulmonary vascular bed. A major pathology in this category is pulmonary hypertension (PH), defined as an elevated mean pulmonary arterial pressure. Pulmonary hypertension can be primary (idiopathic) or secondary to chronic hypoxemia, left-sided heart failure, recurrent pulmonary thromboembolism, or chronic lung diseases like COPD. Hypoxemia acts as a potent vasoconstrictor in the pulmonary vasculature (hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction), which over time causes structural remodeling of the pulmonary vessel walls, including smooth muscle hypertrophy and intimal fibrosis. This progressive narrowing and stiffening of the pulmonary arteries drastically increases pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). As PVR rises, the right ventricle must work significantly harder to pump blood through the lungs against this high resistance. This sustained pressure overload eventually leads to right ventricular hypertrophy and subsequent right-sided heart failure, a condition clinically known as cor pulmonale. Another critical vascular disorder is pulmonary embolism (PE), where a thrombus originating typically from the deep veins of the lower extremities travels and occludes the pulmonary arterial tree, resulting in acute V/Q mismatch, sudden dyspnea, chest pain, and hemodynamic collapse.

Clinical Manifestations and Compensatory Mechanisms

When respiratory disorders disrupt gas exchange and pulmonary mechanics, the body activates various clinical manifestations and compensatory mechanisms to maintain homeostasis. Common clinical manifestations include dyspnea (the subjective sensation of breathlessness), tachypnea, cyanosis (bluish discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes indicating severe hypoxemia), cough, and abnormal breath sounds such as wheezes, crackles, or diminished air entry. To compensate for hypoxemia and hypercapnia, the respiratory and cardiovascular systems initiate rapid adjustments. The central and peripheral chemoreceptors detect changes in arterial blood gases and pH, stimulating the respiratory center in the medulla to increase respiratory rate and depth (hyperventilation). Simultaneously, the sympathetic nervous system is activated, increasing heart rate and cardiac output to enhance tissue oxygen delivery. In chronic hypoxemia, such as in long-standing COPD or high-altitude adaptation, the kidneys respond by releasing increased amounts of erythropoietin (EPO), stimulating the bone marrow to produce more red blood cells (secondary polycythemia) to maximize oxygen-carrying capacity. However, while these compensatory mechanisms temporarily sustain life, prolonged strain can exhaust the cardiopulmonary system, culminating in respiratory failure if medical and nursing interventions are not promptly implemented.
