Pathophysiology of Respiratory Disorders

Normal Pulmonary Mechanics and Gas Exchange

Pulmonary mechanics involve the physical forces governing airflow into and out of the lungs. Air moves down a pressure gradient, flowing from an area of higher pressure in the atmosphere to an area of lower pressure inside the alveoli. During inspiration, the contraction of the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles expands the thoracic cavity, decreasing intrapleural pressure and drawing air inward. Expiration is typically a passive process driven by elastic recoil of the lung tissue and chest wall. Gas exchange relies on the diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide across the alveolar-capillary membrane. This delicate barrier consists of alveolar epithelium, an interstitial space, and capillary endothelium. According to Fick's law of diffusion, the rate of gas transfer is directly proportional to the surface area available and the concentration gradient, while inversely proportional to the thickness of the membrane. Any pathological thickening of this membrane impairs arterial oxygenation.

Mechanisms of Airway Obstruction and Restriction

Pulmonary dysfunctions are broadly categorized into obstructive and restrictive ventilatory defects based on airflow limitation or volume reduction. - **Obstructive defects**: Characterized by difficulty expelling air rapidly, leading to significantly reduced forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1). Resistance increases due to airway lumen narrowing from bronchospasm, mucosal edema, excessive mucus production, or loss of elastic recoil. - **Restrictive defects**: Characterized by a reduction in total lung capacity and vital capacity. The lung parenchyma becomes stiff, or chest wall expansion is impeded, preventing the lungs from fully inflating during inspiration.

Ventilation-Perfusion Mismatch and Shunting

Efficient gas exchange requires a balanced matching of ventilation (airflow, V) to perfusion (blood flow, Q) across all regions of the lungs. In a healthy lung, the ideal V/Q ratio is approximately 0.8. Pathological states disrupt this balance. A **V/Q mismatch** occurs when alveoli are ventilated but poorly perfused (high V/Q, dead space) or when alveoli are perfused but receive inadequate ventilation (low V/Q). A **pulmonary shunt** represents the extreme low end of the V/Q spectrum, where deoxygenated blood passes from the right side of the heart to the left side without ever coming into contact with ventilated alveoli. True shunts, such as those caused by alveolar collapse or filling with fluid, cannot be corrected by administering supplemental oxygen alone because the blood never reaches functional gas exchange surfaces.

Pathophysiology of Obstructive Pulmonary Diseases

Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease are the most prevalent obstructive disorders, each possessing distinct yet overlapping cellular mechanisms. Asthma is characterized by chronic airway inflammation driven by mast cells, eosinophils, and T-helper lymphocytes. Exposure to triggers induces acute bronchoconstriction, airway edema, and mucus plugging, resulting in episodic wheezing and dyspnea. Airway remodeling can occur with chronic disease duration, causing permanent subepithelial fibrosis. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease combines chronic bronchitis and emphysema. Chronic bronchitis involves hypertrophy of bronchial mucous glands and copious sputum production. Emphysema involves the permanent destruction of alveolar walls and loss of elastic recoil due to an imbalance between proteases and antiproteases, typically triggered by cigarette smoke inhalation. This destruction leads to air trapping and hyperinflation of the chest.

Pathophysiology of Restrictive Pulmonary Diseases

Restrictive disorders limit lung expansion, categorized into intrinsic parenchymal diseases and extrinsic chest wall disorders. Intrinsic disorders, such as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, involve injury to the alveolar epithelium followed by an abnormal, exuberant fibroproliferative response. Fibroblasts proliferate and secrete excessive collagen, thickening the alveolar walls and transforming the normal delicate interstitium into stiff, scarred tissue. This reduces compliance, meaning greater pressure is required to expand the lungs. Extrinsic disorders originate outside the lung parenchyma. Conditions such as severe scoliosis, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, or neuromuscular weakness impair the mechanical movement of the thoracic cage. While the lung tissue itself may initially be healthy, the external restriction prevents adequate tidal volume generation.

Pulmonary Vascular Disorders and Hypertension

The pulmonary circulation is a low-pressure, high-capacity vascular bed. When resistance within these vessels rises significantly, pulmonary hypertension develops. Pulmonary arterial hypertension can stem from primary endothelial dysfunction leading to vasoconstriction and vascular remodeling, or secondary causes such as chronic hypoxia. Alveolar hypoxia triggers potent localized vasoconstriction in the pulmonary arterioles—a mechanism that redirects blood away from poorly ventilated areas to better-ventilated alveoli. However, when widespread chronic hypoxia occurs, generalized pulmonary vasoconstriction ensues, increasing right ventricular afterload. Over time, this elevated workload causes right ventricular hypertrophy and eventual right heart failure, a clinical syndrome known as cor pulmonale.

Clinical Manifestations and Compensatory Mechanisms

When respiratory pathology compromises gas exchange or mechanics, the body deploys rapid compensatory responses while clinical symptoms emerge. Dyspnea, or the subjective sensation of difficult breathing, arises from increased work of breathing, chemoreceptor stimulation by hypercapnia or hypoxia, and signals from proprioceptors in the respiratory muscles. Hypoxemia stimulates peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies, triggering tachypnea to increase alveolar ventilation. Chronic hypoxemia prompts renal compensation, releasing erythropoietin to stimulate bone marrow production of red blood cells, resulting in secondary polycythemia. Chronic hypercapnia leads to renal retention of bicarbonate to buffer serum pH, maintaining acid-base balance at the expense of chronic respiratory acidosis.
