Complications of Respiratory Disorders

Understanding Respiratory Complications

Respiratory disorders rarely stay confined to a single airway or lung lobe. When a primary condition like chronic bronchitis, pneumonia, or asthma is left unmanaged, it triggers a cascade of secondary problems throughout the body. The respiratory system's main job is simple: bring in oxygen and blow off carbon dioxide. When disease damages this process, the whole body suffers. Nurses must look beyond the primary diagnosis and anticipate how compromised gas exchange strains the heart, the brain, and the blood itself. Catching these shifts early prevents minor chest infections from turning into life-threatening multi-system crises.

Hypoxemia and Acute Respiratory Failure

When oxygen levels in the blood drop too low, the patient develops hypoxemia. Left unchecked, this plunges the patient into acute respiratory failure, a medical emergency where the respiratory system can no longer perform its basic duties.

  • Type 1 (Hypoxemic) Failure: The patient cannot get enough oxygen into the blood. Common in severe pneumonia or pulmonary edema.
  • Type 2 (Hypercapnic) Failure: The patient cannot get rid of carbon dioxide, leading to a dangerous buildup of acid in the blood. Common in severe COPD flare-ups or drug overdoses that suppress breathing.

Patients in respiratory failure often show restlessness, confusion, and rapid breathing as their bodies fight desperately for air.

Cor Pulmonale and Pulmonary Hypertension

The heart and lungs work as a tight team. When lung diseases cause chronic low oxygen, the small blood vessels inside the lungs tighten up and constrict. This increases pressure in the pulmonary circulation, leading to pulmonary hypertension.

Over time, the right side of the heart has to pump much harder to push blood through these narrowed lung vessels. This constant strain makes the right ventricle thick and weak, a condition known as cor pulmonale or right-sided heart failure. Nurses caring for patients with severe long-term lung disease must watch closely for signs of heart strain, such as swollen ankles, a bulging neck vein, and an enlarged liver.

Atelectasis and Pleural Effusion

A lung cannot exchange gas properly if the tiny air sacs, or alveoli, are squashed or collapsed. Atelectasis is the collapse of part or all of a lung, often happening after surgery when a patient takes shallow breaths and fails to cough. Secretions plug the small airways, trapping air that eventually gets absorbed by the blood, causing the alveoli to deflate.

Similarly, pleural effusion happens when abnormal fluid collects in the pleural space between the lung and the chest wall. This extra fluid presses against the lung tissue, preventing it from fully expanding during inhalation. Both conditions rob the patient of vital lung capacity, leading to shortness of breath and diminished breath sounds over the affected area.

Pneumothorax and Hemothorax

The chest cavity is normally a closed, negative-pressure space that keeps the lungs inflated. When air or blood breaches this space, the delicate pressure balance is ruined.

ConditionWhat AccumulatesMajor Clinical Danger
PneumothoraxAir in the pleural spaceLung collapse; tension pneumothorax can press on the heart and cause cardiac arrest.
HemothoraxBlood in the pleural spaceLung collapse plus risk of severe blood loss and shock.

A patient with a severe pneumothorax will have absent breath sounds on the affected side and sudden, sharp chest pain. A tension pneumothorax is a true emergency that requires immediate needle decompression before a chest tube can be inserted.

Acid-Base Imbalances: Respiratory Acidosis and Alkalosis

The lungs play a massive role in keeping the blood's pH within a safe, narrow range by controlling carbon dioxide levels. Carbon dioxide acts like an acid in the body.

When a patient hypoventilates—meaning they breathe too slowly or shallowly—they trap carbon dioxide. This leads to respiratory acidosis, where blood pH drops below normal. Conditions like severe asthma, COPD, or opioid overdose commonly cause this.

Conversely, when a patient hyperventilates—breathing too fast or deeply due to anxiety, pain, or fever—they blow off too much carbon dioxide. This causes respiratory alkalosis, where blood pH rises. Nurses monitor arterial blood gases (ABGs) to track these dangerous shifts.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring for Complications

Spotting a respiratory complication early requires continuous, hands-on clinical observation. The nurse must never rely on monitors alone.

Check the respiratory rate, depth, and rhythm. Look for the use of accessory muscles in the neck and chest, which tells you the patient is working too hard to breathe. Assess skin color for cyanosis around the lips and nail beds, indicating low oxygen. Listen with a stethoscope for unequal breath sounds, crackles, or wheezes. Keep a close eye on pulse oximetry readings, but always verify with the patient's physical appearance and mental state. Confusion or restlessness is often the very first clue that the brain is lacking oxygen.

Collaborative Management and Patient Education

Managing respiratory complications requires a true team effort involving doctors, respiratory therapists, nurses, and the patient. Medical interventions often include oxygen therapy, bronchodilators, chest tubes for fluid or air drainage, and sometimes mechanical ventilation.

Education is just as vital as medication. Nurses must teach patients how to perform deep breathing and coughing exercises to keep alveoli open and clear out mucus. Show chronic respiratory patients how to do pursed-lip breathing, which helps keep airways open longer during exhalation and eases shortness of breath. When patients understand their condition and learn how to conserve energy, they prevent repeated hospital admissions.
