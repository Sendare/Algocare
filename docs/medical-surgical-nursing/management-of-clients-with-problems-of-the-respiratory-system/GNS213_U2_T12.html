Complications of Respiratory Disorders

Introduction to Respiratory Complications

Respiratory complications arise when primary pulmonary disorders disrupt normal gas exchange, ventilation, or perfusion. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, severe pneumonia, and asthma attacks can rapidly cascade into systemic instability if compensatory mechanisms fail. In busy Nigerian hospital wards, where oxygen supply infrastructure can occasionally face logistical hurdles, recognizing early subtle signs of respiratory deterioration prevents catastrophic decompensation. Complications are broadly categorized into acute life-threatening events, such as acute respiratory failure and tension pneumothorax, and chronic progressive states, including pulmonary hypertension and chronic cor pulmonale.

Hypoxemia and Acute Respiratory Failure

Hypoxemia represents a deficient amount of oxygen in arterial blood, whereas acute respiratory failure occurs when the respiratory system fails in one or both of its gas exchange functions: oxygenation and carbon dioxide elimination. Type 1 respiratory failure involves hypoxemia with normal or low carbon dioxide, typically seen in conditions like pulmonary edema and pneumonia. Type 2 involves hypercapnia paired with hypoxemia, characteristic of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations or central nervous system depression. Clinical manifestations include restlessness, confusion, tachypnea, and cyanosis. Pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas analysis provide definitive diagnostic data.

Pulmonary Hypertension and Cor Pulmonale

Chronic hypoxia leads to pulmonary vasoconstriction, increasing vascular resistance within the pulmonary bed. Over time, sustained high pressure in the pulmonary circulation forces the right ventricle to work harder against increased afterload, culminating in right ventricular hypertrophy and eventual right-sided heart failure, known as cor pulmonale.

  • Symptoms: Exertional dyspnea, fatigue, peripheral edema, and distended neck veins.
  • Signs: A prominent parasternal lift, loud pulmonic heart sound, and hepatomegaly due to systemic venous congestion.
  • Management Focus: Reducing pulmonary vascular resistance and managing fluid overload.

Atelectasis and Pleural Effusion

Atelectasis describes the collapse of alveoli, preventing respiratory exchange of carbon dioxide and oxygen in a portion of the lungs. It frequently occurs postoperatively due to shallow breathing, retained secretions, and immobility. Conversely, pleural effusion is the abnormal accumulation of fluid within the pleural space, compressing the underlying lung tissue. Effusions can be transudative, resulting from systemic factors like heart failure, or exudative, caused by local inflammation, infection, or malignancy. Diminished breath sounds and dullness to percussion over the affected lung zone are hallmark physical examination findings for both conditions.

Pneumothorax and Hemothorax

The entry of air or blood into the pleural cavity disrupts the negative intrapleural pressure necessary for lung expansion. A pneumothorax can be spontaneous, traumatic, or tension-based. A tension pneumothorax is a true medical emergency where air enters the pleural space during inspiration but cannot escape, shifting the mediastinum and compressing the heart and contralateral lung. Hemothorax involves blood accumulation in the pleural space, often secondary to chest trauma or surgical procedures. Both conditions produce absent breath sounds on the affected side, severe respiratory distress, and tracheal deviation in advanced tension pneumothorax.

Acid-Base Imbalances: Respiratory Acidosis and Alkalosis

The respiratory system plays a rapid role in regulating blood pH by controlling carbon dioxide elimination. Respiratory acidosis develops when hypoventilation causes carbon dioxide retention, driving up carbonic acid and lowering blood pH below 7.35. Conditions such as severe asthma, opioid overdose, and chronic obstructive disease flare-ups frequently precipitate this. Conversely, respiratory alkalosis occurs during hyperventilation, where excessive carbon dioxide is blown off, reducing carbonic acid and elevating pH above 7.45. Anxiety, pain, and early sepsis commonly trigger hyperventilation.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring for Complications

Continuous surveillance is vital for detecting subtle shifts in respiratory status before catastrophic failure occurs. Nurses assess breathing patterns, use of accessory muscles, skin color, and respiratory rate every shift or more frequently for unstable clients. Arterial blood gas results and pulse oximetry trends offer quantitative backing to physical observations. Mental status changes must never be dismissed as mere fatigue; they often signal early cerebral hypoxia or hypercapnia. Documenting baseline parameters ensures any acute deviation is identified instantly.

Collaborative Management and Patient Education

Managing respiratory complications requires coordinated teamwork among physicians, respiratory therapists, and nursing staff. Interventions range from administering supplemental oxygen and bronchodilators to setting up mechanical ventilators and chest tube drainage systems. Patient education centers on teaching controlled breathing techniques, smoking cessation, and recognizing early warning signs of infection or respiratory distress. Empowering clients with clear action plans promotes adherence and reduces preventable hospital readmissions.
