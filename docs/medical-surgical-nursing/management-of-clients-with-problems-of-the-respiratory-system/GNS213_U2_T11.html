Nursing Management of Respiratory Disorders

Comprehensive Respiratory Assessment and Monitoring

Evaluating the respiratory system requires a systematic approach combining observation, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. Begin by observing the patient's general appearance, noting skin color, chest symmetry, and work of breathing. Use of accessory muscles during inspiration signals significant respiratory distress and increased work of breathing. Count the respiratory rate quietly without alerting the patient, as conscious awareness alters breathing patterns.

Auscultation demands a quiet environment and a stethoscope diaphragm placed firmly against bare skin. Listen through a complete respiratory cycle from apex to base, comparing corresponding lung fields side to side. Normal breath sounds include vesicular, bronchovesicular, and bronchial sounds, each heard over specific anatomical landmarks. Adventitious sounds—such as crackles, wheezes, rhonchi, and pleural friction rubs—indicate underlying pathology like fluid overload, bronchospasm, or inflammation.

Pulse oximetry provides continuous, non-invasive monitoring of arterial oxygen saturation. However, cold peripheral extremities, nail polish, or severe hypotension can disrupt sensor readings. Always correlate digital saturation values with the patient's clinical presentation and color.

Maintaining a Patent Airway and Clearance Techniques

Airway clearance is paramount in preventing atelectasis and infection in patients unable to clear secretions independently.

  • Effective Coughing: Instruct the patient to take a deep diaphragmatic breath, hold it for a few seconds, and perform a series of huffs or sharp coughs to mobilize mucus upward.
  • Chest Physiotherapy (CPT): Combines postural drainage, percussion, and vibration to dislodge thick mucus from bronchial walls, directing secretions toward larger airways for expulsion.
  • Suctioning: Indicated only when audible secretions, coughing, or sawtooth patterns on a capnograph suggest retained mucus. Maintain sterile technique during endotracheal or tracheostomy suctioning, limiting passes to ten to fifteen seconds to prevent severe hypoxia.

Adequate systemic hydration thins viscous respiratory secretions naturally, making clearance techniques significantly more effective than mechanical interventions alone.

Oxygen Therapy and Respiratory Support Devices

Oxygen is prescribed as a medication and must be administered with careful attention to flow rates, delivery devices, and patient stability.

  • Nasal Cannula: Delivers low-flow oxygen from 1 to 6 liters per minute, suitable for patients requiring mild support while allowing them to eat and speak freely.
  • Venturi Mask: Utilizes high-flow Bernoullis principles to deliver a precise, fixed concentration of oxygen, making it ideal for clients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease at risk for carbon dioxide retention.
  • Non-Rebreather Mask: Equipped with a reservoir bag and one-way valves, capable of delivering near 100 percent oxygen concentrations in emergency stabilization scenarios.

Monitoring for oxygen toxicity involves watching for substernal chest pain, dry cough, and progressive dyspnea during prolonged high-concentration therapy. Humidification is necessary for flow rates exceeding 4 liters per minute to prevent drying and irritation of delicate nasal and bronchial mucosa.

Pharmacological Management in Respiratory Disorders

Pharmacotherapy for respiratory conditions targets bronchoconstriction, inflammation, and excessive mucus production. Bronchodilators relax smooth muscles around airways. Short-acting beta-agonists serve as rescue medications for acute bronchospasm, whereas long-acting agents provide daily control. Inhaled corticosteroids reduce chronic airway inflammation and mucosal edema, forming the bedrock of asthma management.

A critical nursing responsibility is teaching correct inhaler and spacer technique. Many patients fail to coordinate actuation with inhalation, depositing medication in the mouth rather than the lungs. After administering inhaled corticosteroids, instruct the patient to rinse their mouth thoroughly with water to prevent oral candidiasis and hoarseness.

Mucolytics and expectorants help break down thick secretions, while systemic antibiotics are reserved for documented bacterial respiratory infections rather than viral exacerbations.

Nursing Interventions for Acute and Chronic Respiratory Conditions

Managing acute respiratory distress requires rapid, calm prioritization. Position the patient in high Fowler's or an upright forward-leaning posture to maximize thoracic expansion and diaphragm movement. Administer prescribed oxygen, establish intravenous access, and stay with the client to alleviate anxiety, which otherwise worsens dyspnea.

For chronic conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, teach pursed-lip breathing. This technique prolongs exhalation, prevents premature airway collapse, and helps trapped air escape from damaged alveoli. Encourage energy conservation strategies, breaking daily activities into smaller tasks interspersed with rest periods to prevent severe oxygen desaturation. Monitor intake and output carefully, as systemic steroids and respiratory infections frequently alter fluid balance.

Patient Education and Pulmonary Rehabilitation

Empowering patients with chronic respiratory illnesses requires structured education and supervised physical conditioning. Pulmonary rehabilitation programs combine exercise training, nutritional counseling, and psychosocial support to break the debilitating cycle of breathlessness and physical deconditioning.

Teach patients to identify early triggers and symptom flare-ups, such as changes in sputum color, increased fatigue, or sudden weight gain indicating fluid retention. Provide a written action plan detailing when to adjust medications or seek emergency medical help. Encourage smoking cessation through non-judgmental counseling and referral to support groups, as ongoing tobacco exposure remains the primary driver of preventable lung disease progression.
