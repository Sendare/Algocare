Nursing Management of Respiratory Disorders

Comprehensive Respiratory Assessment and Monitoring

Assessing the respiratory system requires a systematic approach using inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. Nurses must observe the patient's breathing rate, depth, and effort. Noticeable use of accessory muscles in the neck and abdomen usually signals increased work of breathing and potential distress.

  • Rate and Rhythm: Normal adult breathing is 12 to 20 breaths per minute, regular and unlabored.
  • Breath Sounds: Listen for normal vesicular sounds over lung fields, alongside abnormal sounds like crackles, wheezes, or pleural friction rubs.
  • Oxygen Saturation: Continuous pulse oximetry tracks peripheral oxygenation levels, though nurses should always correlate numbers with the patient's actual clinical appearance.

Cyanosis is a late sign of hypoxia. Waiting for a blue tint on the skin or lips before providing oxygen is a dangerous clinical mistake; early indicators like restlessness and confusion appear long before color changes.

Maintaining a Patent Airway and Clearance Techniques

A clear airway is vital for adequate gas exchange. When patients cannot clear thick mucus or secretions independently, nurses intervene with specific techniques.

  1. Hydration: Adequate systemic fluid intake thinned secretions naturally, making them much easier to cough out.
  2. Effective Coughing: Teach patients the huff coughing technique, which uses open vocal cords to move mucus upward without collapsing airways.
  3. Chest Physiotherapy: Postural drainage, percussion, and vibration help mobilize trapped secretions from specific lung segments.
  4. Suctioning: Sterile pharyngeal or tracheal suctioning removes mucus when coughing fails, but it must be done carefully to avoid causing hypoxia or mucosal trauma.

Oxygen Therapy and Respiratory Support Devices

Oxygen is treated as a medical drug; it requires a proper prescription, careful monitoring, and correct delivery devices based on clinical needs.

  • Nasal Cannula: Delivers low-flow oxygen from 1 to 6 liters per minute, suitable for stable patients who need modest support.
  • Venturi Mask: Delivers precise, controlled oxygen concentrations, making it ideal for patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease who risk retaining carbon dioxide.
  • Non-Rebreather Mask: Provides high concentrations of oxygen using a reservoir bag and one-way valves for emergency stabilization.

Oxygen supports combustion. Nurses must strictly enforce safety rules, prohibiting smoking or open flames near any oxygen source in homes and hospital wards.

Pharmacological Management and Inhaler Technique

Medications delivered directly to the respiratory tract act quickly with fewer systemic side effects, but correct delivery technique is essential.

  • Bronchodilators: Medications like albuterol relax airway smooth muscles to open narrowed passages during acute wheezing or bronchospasm.
  • Anti-inflammatory Agents: Inhaled corticosteroids reduce swelling and mucus production in chronic conditions like asthma.
  • Spacer Devices: Using a spacer with a metered-dose inhaler helps medicine reach the lungs rather than sticking uselessly to the back of the throat.

When a patient has a prescription for both a bronchodilator and an inhaled corticosteroid, the bronchodilator must always be given first. Opening the airways with the bronchodilator ensures the steroid can travel deep into the lung tissue.

Nursing Care for Acute and Chronic Conditions

Managing acute respiratory distress requires rapid stabilization, while chronic conditions require steady lifestyle management and symptom tracking.

In acute asthma attacks or pneumonia, nurses prioritize maintaining an open airway, administering prescribed oxygen, and positioning the patient upright in a high Fowler's position to ease lung expansion. Continuous monitoring of respiratory rate, heart rate, and oxygen saturation guides the care team's next steps.

For chronic conditions like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, care centers on conserving energy, preventing respiratory infections through vaccinations, and teaching pursed-lip breathing. Pursed-lip breathing slows down expiration, prevents small airway collapse, and helps the patient feel more in control during shortness of breath.
