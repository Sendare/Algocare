Endotracheal Intubation

Introduction and Clinical Indications

Endotracheal intubation involves passing a flexible plastic tube through the nose or mouth into the trachea. This invasive procedure secures an open airway when the body cannot maintain one independently. In emergency settings across Nigerian teaching hospitals, rapid establishment of a definitive airway saves lives during severe trauma, cardiac arrest, or respiratory failure.

  • Failure of airway maintenance: Unconsciousness from head injuries, drug overdoses, or profound shock.
  • Failure of ventilation or oxygenation: Severe asthma, acute respiratory distress syndrome, or pulmonary edema causing exhaustion.
  • Anticipated clinical deterioration: Burns involving the face and neck or progressive neurological decline where swelling will soon obstruct breathing.

Anatomical Landmarks and Considerations

Successful intubation relies heavily on a firm grasp of upper airway anatomy. The mouth, pharynx, larynx, and trachea form a continuous passage. During direct laryngoscopy, the practitioner visualizes the tongue, epiglottis, and vocal cords.

The glottis is the targeted opening between the vocal cords. Anatomical variations, such as a receding mandible, a short muscular neck, or protruding front teeth, make visualization difficult. These anatomical challenges are assessed prior to the procedure using scoring systems like the Mallampati classification to anticipate a difficult airway.

Essential Equipment and Preparation

Preparation reduces procedural risks. Clinicians gather and check all equipment before touching the patient. The acronym SATA or similar mnemonics help organize the setup.

  • Suction: A functioning suction source with a rigid catheter to clear secretions or blood.
  • Bag-valve-mask (BVM): Connected to an oxygen source for manual ventilation.
  • Airways: Oropharyngeal and nasopharyngeal airways as backups.
  • Laryngoscope: Handle and blades (Macintosh curved or Miller straight) with tested working lights.
  • Tube: Endotracheal tube of the correct size, checked for cuff integrity, with a stylet inserted inside.
  • Assessment tools: A stethoscope and a carbon dioxide detector.

Pre-oxygenation and Pharmacology

Before passing the tube, the patient undergoes pre-oxygenation using a tight-fitting face mask with 100% oxygen to replace nitrogen in the functional residual capacity of the lungs. This creates an oxygen reservoir, buying crucial time during attempts when breathing stops.

Simultaneously, rapid sequence induction uses a combination of an induction agent to cause unconsciousness and a neuromuscular blocking agent to paralyze the skeletal muscles, including the jaw and vocal cords. Common induction agents include ketamine or propofol, paired with a paralytic like succinylcholine or rocuronium.

The Intubation Procedure

The operator positions the patient's head in the sniffing position to align the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes. Holding the laryngoscope in the left hand, the blade enters the right side of the mouth, sweeping the tongue to the left.

The blade advances until it rests in the vallecula or lifts the epiglottis directly. Once the vocal cords come into view, the right hand introduces the endotracheal tube through the cords until the cuff disappears past them. The stylet is gently removed, and the cuff is inflated with air. The entire procedure should ideally take under thirty seconds to prevent severe hypoxia.

Confirming Tube Placement

Accurate placement in the trachea must be verified immediately to prevent accidental esophageal intubation, which is rapidly fatal if uncorrected. Multiple checks provide the safest confirmation.

Nursing Care and Tube Maintenance

Once placement is confirmed, the tube is securely anchored with tape or commercial holders, and the depth at the lip or teeth is documented. Nurses monitor tube position continuously because accidental extubation or movement can occur with minor head turns.

Airway hygiene requires regular suctioning to clear secretions, performed using sterile technique for limited durations to prevent hypoxia. Humidified oxygen keeps secretions thin. Oral care is performed frequently using chlorhexidine mouthwash to prevent ventilator-associated pneumonia, and cuff pressures are monitored to protect the tracheal mucosa from ischemic necrosis.

Complications and Emergency Troubleshooting

Despite careful technique, intubation carries inherent risks. Immediate complications include dental damage, soft tissue lacerations, vocal cord injury, and esophageal misplacement. Long-term risks involve tracheal stenosis or infection.

When a ventilated patient suddenly deteriorates with dropping oxygen levels, nurses use the DOPE mnemonic to troubleshoot:

  • Displacement: The tube has slipped out or into a mainstem bronchus.
  • Obstruction: Secretions, a mucous plug, or a kinked tube blocks airflow.
  • Pneumothorax: A collapsed lung caused by high pressure or trauma.
  • Equipment failure: Disconnected oxygen tubing, empty oxygen tanks, or ventilator malfunction.

Extubation and Post-Extubation Care

Extubation occurs when the condition requiring intubation resolves and the patient can breathe independently and protect their airway. The team performs a spontaneous breathing trial to assess readiness.

The procedure requires suctioning the pharynx and endotracheal tube, deflating the cuff, and withdrawing the tube in one smooth motion while encouraging the patient to cough. Supplemental oxygen is applied immediately. Nurses monitor closely for stridor, hoarseness, or respiratory fatigue, which signal upper airway edema or failure.
