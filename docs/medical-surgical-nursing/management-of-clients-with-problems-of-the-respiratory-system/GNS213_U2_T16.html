Endotracheal Intubation

Indications and Goals of Endotracheal Intubation

Endotracheal intubation is an advanced airway management technique where a flexible plastic tube is inserted through the mouth or nose into the trachea. The primary goals are to maintain a patent airway, prevent aspiration of gastric contents, deliver adequate oxygen and mechanical ventilation, and facilitate pulmonary hygiene through suctioning.

Common clinical scenarios requiring intubation include:

  • Respiratory failure: Inability to maintain adequate gas exchange, leading to severe hypoxia or hypercapnia.
  • Airway protection: Loss of protective airway reflexes due to head injury, stroke, or drug overdose.
  • Hemodynamic instability or shock: Preserving oxygen delivery to vital organs by reducing the work of breathing.
  • General anesthesia: Securing the airway during major surgical procedures requiring muscle relaxation.

Anatomical Considerations and Equipment Preparation

Successful intubation relies on a solid understanding of upper airway anatomy and meticulous preparation. The goal is to align the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes—often achieved by placing a small pillow under the patient's head and extending the neck, known as the 'sniffing position.' Visualizing the vocal cords requires passing the blade past the tongue, routing it into the vallecula or directly lifting the epiglottis.

Before starting, the nurse must gather and check the equipment using the 'STAT' or 'SOPES' mnemonic:

  • Suction: Working suction catheter and canister.
  • Oxygen: Bag-valve-mask (BVM) connected to a high-flow oxygen source.
  • Position and Pharmacology: Proper bed height, emergency drugs, and sedatives.
  • Equipment: Laryngoscope handle, working blades of various sizes, endotracheal tubes with inflated cuffs, stylet, and a 10ml syringe.
  • Securement: Tape or commercial tube holder, plus a stethoscope and carbon dioxide detector.

Pre-oxygenation and Pharmacological Agents

Before any attempt to pass the tube, the patient must be thoroughly pre-oxygenated. Replacing the nitrogen in the patient's functional residual capacity with 100% oxygen creates an oxygen reservoir, buying crucial time and preventing dangerous drops in blood oxygen levels during airway manipulation. This is usually done using a non-rebreather mask or a bag-valve-mask device for three to five minutes.

Rapid sequence intubation (RSI) involves administering a sedative or induction agent to cause unconsciousness, immediately followed by a neuromuscular blocking agent to induce total paralysis. Common induction agents include etomidate or propofol, while succinylcholine or rocuronium are standard paralytics. Nurses must monitor for sudden drops in blood pressure after sedation is given, especially in patients who are already volume-depleted.

Step-by-Step Intubation Procedure

During the actual intubation attempt, timing and teamwork are everything. The operator holds the laryngoscope in the left hand and opens the patient's mouth with the right hand. The blade is inserted into the right side of the mouth, sweeping the tongue to the left.

The blade is advanced until the tip sits in the vallecula (for a curved Macintosh blade) or directly lifts the epiglottis (for a straight Miller blade). Once the vocal cords come into view, the operator gently passes the endotracheal tube through the cords until the cuff disappears past them. The stylet is removed, and the cuff is inflated with air using a syringe. Each intubation attempt should ideally last no longer than 30 seconds to prevent severe oxygen deprivation; if unsuccessful, the team must stop, re-oxygenate the patient using a bag-valve-mask, and try again.

Confirmation of Tube Placement

Confirming correct endotracheal tube placement is non-negotiable and must be done immediately after insertion. Accidental esophageal intubation is a life-threatening emergency that leads to rapid brain death if missed.

Clinicians use a combination of primary and secondary assessment methods:

  • End-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2) detector: A colorimetric device or capnograph confirms the presence of exhaled carbon dioxide; a color change or numerical waveform proves the tube is in the trachea.
  • Auscultation: Listening with a stethoscope over both lung apices and bases, as well as the epigastrium. Breath sounds should be equal and bilateral, with no gurgling sounds heard over the stomach.
  • Chest rise and symmetry: Observing equal movement of both sides of the chest during ventilation.
  • Chest X-ray: The gold standard for final confirmation, showing the tip of the tube positioned about 2 to 6 centimeters above the carina.

Ongoing Nursing Care and Tube Maintenance

Once placement is confirmed, securing the tube properly prevents accidental extubation or movement. The nurse marks the depth at the lip or teeth line, documenting it clearly in the patient chart. Routine care involves regular monitoring of the tube fixation tape or holder to check for skin breakdown around the lips and nares.

Because the artificial bypasses the upper airway's natural humidification and filtration, warmed humidified oxygen must be delivered continuously. Suctioning is performed only when clinical signs indicate secretions—such as visible mucus in the tube, coughing, or sawtooth patterns on the ventilator flow waveform—rather than on a rigid schedule. Nurses must also monitor cuff pressures, maintaining them between 20 and 30 cmH2O to prevent tracheal ischemia while ensuring a tight seal for mechanical ventilation.

Complications and Emergency Troubleshooting

Endotracheal intubation carries inherent risks ranging from minor dental trauma during insertion to life-threatening mechanical failures. Early complications include chipped teeth, mucosal lacerations, vocal cord damage, and mainstem bronchus intubation (usually the right mainstem, causing collapse of the left lung).

When a patient suddenly deteriorates while intubated, nurses use the DOPE mnemonic to troubleshoot quickly:

  • Displacement: The tube has slipped into the esophagus or a mainstem bronchus.
  • Obstruction: The tube is blocked by thick secretions, a mucous plug, or the patient biting it.
  • Pneumothorax: A collapsed lung caused by high ventilation pressures.
  • Equipment failure: Detached ventilator tubing, empty oxygen tanks, or power loss.

Extubation and Post-Extubation Care

Extubation is the removal of the endotracheal tube once the patient can breathe independently and no longer requires mechanical ventilation. Before attempting extubation, the patient must meet specific criteria: adequate spontaneous breathing trials, stable hemodynamics, intact protective airway reflexes, and the ability to follow commands and manage their own secretions.

During the procedure, the nurse suctions the pharynx above the cuff, deflates the cuff completely, and pulls the tube out in one smooth motion while encouraging the patient to cough. Supplemental oxygen is applied immediately via a face mask or nasal cannula. Nurses must monitor closely for stridor—a high-pitched, musical breathing sound indicating laryngeal edema—which requires prompt notification of the physician and potential administration of racemic epinephrine or re-intubation.
