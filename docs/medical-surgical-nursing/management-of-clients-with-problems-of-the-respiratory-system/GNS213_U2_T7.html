Chest Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Thoracic Cavity

The thoracic cavity is an airtight, bony-cartilaginous cage enclosing vital organs responsible for respiration and circulation. Its boundaries include the sternum anteriorly, thoracic vertebrae posteriorly, and twelve pairs of ribs laterally, all interlinked by intercostal muscles. The floor of the cavity is formed by the dome-shaped diaphragm, the primary muscle of ventilation. Inside, the cavity is partitioned into the mediastinum and two pleural cavities, housing the lungs. Each lung is enveloped by a double-layered serous membrane known as the **pleura**. The visceral pleura closely adheres to the lung surface, while the parietal pleura lines the inner chest wall and mediastinum. Between these two layers lies the **pleural space**, which normally contains only a few milliliters of lubricating fluid. This thin fluid film reduces friction during respiratory movements and maintains a negative pressure gradient, pulling the lungs outward against the chest wall to keep them expanded.

Pathophysiology of Pleural and Chest Wall Conditions

Normal pulmonary function relies heavily on intrapleural pressure remaining lower than atmospheric pressure. When air, blood, or excess fluid enters the pleural space, this delicate negative pressure is disrupted. The accumulating substance exerts mechanical pressure on lung tissue, leading to partial or complete lung collapse, clinically termed **atelectasis**. Chest wall injuries, such as multiple rib fractures, disrupt the structural rigidity required for normal mechanics. In a condition like **flail chest**, a segment of the rib cage detaches and moves paradoxically during breathing—moving inward during inspiration and outward during expiration. This impairs effective ventilation, increases dead space, and severely compromises gas exchange, quickly leading to hypoxemia and respiratory fatigue if left unaddressed.

Classification of Common Chest Disorders

Chest disorders frequently alter the intrapleural environment. They are classified based on the specific substance accumulating within the pleural cavity or the structural failure of the chest wall. - **Pneumothorax**: The presence of air in the pleural space. A **tension pneumothorax** is a life-threatening variant where air enters but cannot escape, shifting the mediastinum and compressing the vena cava. - **Hemothorax**: The accumulation of blood in the pleural cavity, typically resulting from blunt or penetrating chest trauma, or surgical complications. - **Pleural Effusion**: An abnormal collection of fluid in the pleural space, which can be categorized as transudative (due to systemic fluid shifts) or exudative (due to inflammation, infection, or malignancy).

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

Patients experiencing thoracic disorders present with distinct clinical signs reflecting impaired oxygenation and increased work of breathing. Cardinal manifestations include acute dyspnea, tachypnea, and pleuritic chest pain that worsens with deep inspiration or coughing. Physical assessment requires a systematic approach: - **Inspection**: Observe for asymmetric chest expansion, use of accessory muscles, and tracheal deviation. - **Palpation**: Check for areas of tenderness, subcutaneous emphysema (crepitus), and reduced tactile fremitus over fluid or air pockets. - **Percussion**: Yields a hyperresonant sound over areas of trapped air (pneumothorax) and a dull sound over areas of fluid accumulation (hemothorax or effusion). - **Auscultation**: Reveals diminished or absent breath sounds on the affected side of the thorax.

Diagnostic Procedures and Interpretation

Accurate identification of chest disorders relies on imaging and laboratory investigations. A standard **chest radiograph (CXR)** remains the primary diagnostic tool, clearly displaying mediastinal shift, fluid lines, or areas of absent lung markings in a pneumothorax. When a pleural effusion requires further analysis, a **thoracentesis** is performed to aspirate fluid for laboratory testing. The fluid is examined for protein content, cell counts, and microbiological culture to differentiate transudates from exudates. Additionally, arterial blood gases (ABGs) are frequently monitored to assess the degree of gas exchange impairment, typically revealing hypoxemia and respiratory alkalosis in early acute stages.

Medical and Surgical Management: Chest Tube Therapy

Re-establishing negative intrapleural pressure is the core objective when managing significant pneumothoraces or hemothoraces. This is achieved through the insertion of a **chest tube** connected to a drainage system. The tube is typically inserted into the second to fourth intercostal space for air removal, or lower down for fluid drainage. Water-seal and modern dry suction systems operate on principles of gravity and suction control to allow air and fluid to exit the chest while preventing backflow into the pleural cavity. Nurses must continuously monitor the system for bubbling in the water-seal chamber—which can indicate an air leak—and ensure the drainage unit remains upright and below the level of the patient's chest at all times.

Nursing Care Plan and Complication Prevention

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with chest disorders focuses on promoting optimal respiratory function and preventing secondary complications. Interventions include encouraging deep breathing exercises, utilizing incentive spirometry, and assisting with early ambulation once stabilized. Pain management is critical, as incisional or pleuritic chest pain inhibits effective coughing and deep inspiration. Nurses must remain vigilant for complications such as re-expansion pulmonary edema, infection at the insertion site, or accidental chest tube dislodgement. Emergency bedside supplies—including sterile petroleum gauze, padded clamps, and sterile water—must be readily available whenever a patient has an active chest tube in place.
