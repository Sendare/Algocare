Chest Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Thoracic Cavity

The thoracic cavity is a well-protected bony cage formed by the sternum, ribs, and thoracic vertebrae. Inside this space sit the lungs and the heart, enclosed by two thin layers of tissue called the pleura. The visceral pleura hugs the outer surface of the lungs, while the parietal pleura lines the inside of the chest wall. Between these two layers lies the pleural space, which normally holds only a tiny amount of lubricating fluid. This fluid allows the lungs to slide smoothly against the chest wall during breathing without friction.

Breathing relies entirely on pressure differences. When the diaphragm and intercostal muscles contract, the chest cavity expands. This expansion creates negative pressure inside the thorax, pulling air rapidly down the trachea into the lungs. When these muscles relax, the chest shrinks, pressure rises, and air is pushed out. Any disruption to this delicate pressure system, whether from trauma, fluid buildup, or air leakage, directly compromises ventilation and gas exchange.

Pneumothorax, Hemothorax, and Pleural Effusion

Chest disorders often disrupt the negative pressure of the pleural space. A pneumothorax occurs when air enters the pleural space, causing the lung to collapse partially or completely. This can happen spontaneously in tall, thin individuals or result directly from blunt or penetrating chest trauma. When air enters on inspiration but cannot escape, a life-threatening tension pneumothorax develops, shifting the mediastinum and compressing the heart and opposite lung.

A hemothorax is the accumulation of blood in the pleural cavity, usually resulting from chest injury, surgery, or ruptured blood vessels. It not only compresses lung tissue but can lead to severe hypovolemic shock.

  • Pleural Effusion: An abnormal buildup of fluid within the pleural space, separate from blood or air. It is categorized as transudate, caused by systemic issues like heart failure, or exudate, caused by local inflammation, infection, or malignancy.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

Assessing a patient with a chest disorder requires a systematic approach focused on the respiratory and cardiovascular systems. Patients frequently present with sudden, sharp pleuritic chest pain that worsens on deep breathing or coughing, alongside dyspnea and tachypnea.

Physical examination reveals distinct findings depending on the underlying pathology:

  • Inspection: Asymmetrical chest wall movement, use of accessory muscles, and possible tracheal deviation in severe cases.
  • Palpation: Decreased or absent tactile fremitus over areas of fluid or air, and tenderness along fractured ribs.
  • Percussion: Hyperresonance heard over a pneumothorax due to trapped air, and dullness heard over a pleural effusion or hemothorax due to fluid density.
  • Auscultation: Diminished or completely absent breath sounds on the affected side of the chest.

Diagnostic Procedures and Interpretation

Accurate diagnosis of thoracic conditions combines imaging studies with laboratory and bedside evaluations. A standard chest X-ray remains the primary imaging tool to visualize lung collapse, fluid levels, and mediastinal shifts. For finer details, a computed tomography (CT) scan provides cross-sectional views of the lung parenchyma and pleural surfaces.

Arterial blood gases (ABGs) are routinely drawn to evaluate gas exchange, frequently revealing hypoxemia and respiratory alkalosis or acidosis depending on the severity of distress. When a pleural effusion requires identification, a thoracentesis is performed. This procedure involves inserting a needle through the chest wall into the pleural space to withdraw fluid for laboratory analysis, distinguishing between transudative and exudative causes.

Medical and Surgical Management: Chest Tube Therapy

Restoring negative pressure and re-expanding the lung is the primary goal in managing significant chest disorders. When air or fluid collects in the pleural space, a chest tube (thoracostomy tube) is inserted into the thoracic cavity and connected to a water-seal drainage system.

The drainage system typically has three chambers:

  1. Collection chamber: Collects blood, pus, or fluid draining from the patient.
  2. Water-seal chamber: Acts as a one-way valve allowing air to exit the chest while preventing outside air from entering.
  3. Suction control chamber: Regulates the amount of negative suction applied to help pull out stubborn air or fluid.

Normal functioning of a chest tube includes intermittent bubbling in the water-seal chamber when the patient coughs or exhales (indicating active air escape) and tidaling, which is the rise and fall of water with respiration. Continuous bubbling usually signals an air leak in the system or insertion site.

Nursing Care Plan, Monitoring, and Complication Prevention

Managing a patient with a chest tube requires vigilant nursing care to prevent complications like tension pneumothorax, infection, or tube dislodgement. The nurse must keep the drainage system below the level of the patient's chest at all times to prevent backflow of fluid into the pleural cavity. Tubing should be kept free of dependent loops or kinks that could trap fluid and block drainage.

Emergency equipment must be kept readily available at the bedside, including sterile petrolatum gauze, two hemostats, and sterile water. If the chest tube accidentally disconnects from the system, the nurse immediately immerses the end of the tube in sterile water to maintain the water seal. If the tube is pulled completely out of the chest, the nurse applies a sterile occlusive dressing taped on three sides to allow air to escape while preventing a tension pneumothorax.
