Anatomy and Physiology of the Respiratory System

Introduction and Functional Division of the Respiratory System

Respiration is the primary physiological process that supplies oxygen to body tissues while removing carbon dioxide. This system works in tandem with the cardiovascular system to maintain internal homeostasis. Functionally, respiration spans four distinct processes: pulmonary ventilation, external respiration, transport of respiratory gases, and internal respiration.

Anatomically, the respiratory tract is divided into two primary zones:

  • Conducting Zone: Structures that filter, warm, and moisten incoming air without participating in gas exchange.
  • Respiratory Zone: Terminal structures containing alveoli where gas exchange actually occurs.

Anatomy of the Upper Respiratory Tract

The upper respiratory tract consists of structures located outside the thoracic cavity: the nose, nasal cavity, sinuses, and pharynx. Air enters through the nares, passing into the nasal cavity which is lined with pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium and richly vascularized mucous membranes. This rich blood supply helps warm cold air, while mucus traps particulate matter.

The pharynx serves as a shared muscular pathway for both the respiratory and digestive tracts, subdivided into the nasopharynx, oropharynx, and laryngopharynx. The uppermost section, the nasopharynx, contains the pharyngeal tonsils and the openings of the auditory tubes, linking respiratory health directly to middle ear function.

Anatomy of the Lower Respiratory Tract

The lower respiratory tract begins below the larynx, encompassing the trachea, bronchial tree, and lungs housed within the thoracic cavity.

  • Trachea: A flexible tube reinforced by C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage that prevent collapse during pressure changes.
  • Bronchial Tree: Branching airways starting at the primary bronchi, dividing into secondary and tertiary bronchi, and eventually forming bronchioles.
  • Lungs: Paired spongy organs enclosed by double-layered serous membranes called pleura. The right lung has three lobes while the left lung has two to accommodate the cardiac notch.

Microscopic Structure of the Lungs and Alveoli

Gas exchange occurs across the delicate walls of millions of alveoli clustered at the ends of respiratory bronchioles. The respiratory membrane is exceptionally thin, consisting of alveolar epithelium, a fused basement membrane, and capillary endothelium.

Two primary cell types populate the alveolar walls:

  • Type I alveolar cells: Simple squamous epithelial cells that form the ultra-thin structure ideal for rapid gas diffusion.
  • Type II alveolar cells: Cuboidal cells that secrete pulmonary surfactant, a detergent-like substance reducing surface tension and preventing alveolar collapse.

Alveolar macrophages patrol the inner surfaces, phagocytizing airborne debris that escaped the upper airway defense mechanisms.

Mechanics of Pulmonary Ventilation

Pulmonary ventilation relies on pressure gradients created by changes in thoracic volume, governed by Boyle's Law: pressure and volume are inversely related.

  • Inhalation: An active process where the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles contract, expanding the thoracic cavity. Intra-alveolar pressure drops below atmospheric pressure, drawing air inward.
  • Exhalation: Typically a passive process driven by the elastic recoil of lung tissue and relaxation of inspiratory muscles, raising intra-alveolar pressure above atmospheric levels.

Intrapleural pressure remains constantly negative relative to intra-alveolar pressure, acting like a suction mechanism that keeps the lungs pinned against the thoracic wall.

Gas Exchange and Transport

Gas exchange in the lungs and tissues operates entirely by simple diffusion, driven by partial pressure gradients. Oxygen moves from high alveolar partial pressure ($PO_2$) into deoxygenated pulmonary capillary blood. Carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction down its partial pressure gradient.

Once in the blood, oxygen is primarily bound to hemoglobin within red blood cells, with only a small fraction dissolved in plasma. Carbon dioxide is transported in three ways: dissolved in plasma, bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin, and mainly converted into bicarbonate ions within red blood cells, buffering systemic pH.

Neural and Chemical Regulation of Respiration

Respiration is an autonomic rhythmic process coordinated by respiratory centers located in the brainstem—specifically the medullary respiratory center and the pontine respiratory group. While neural centers set the basic breathing pace, chemical stimuli fine-tune the rate and depth of ventilation.

Central chemoreceptors located on the ventral surface of the medulla oblongata monitor cerebrospinal fluid pH, which drops when excess carbon dioxide diffuses across the blood-brain barrier. Peripheral chemoreceptors in the carotid and aortic bodies primarily detect significant drops in arterial oxygen levels, alongside arterial carbon dioxide and hydrogen ion concentrations.

Clinical Implications for Nursing Assessment

Understanding respiratory anatomy and physiology directly informs clinical nursing assessments. Nurses evaluate respiratory status by observing breathing rate, rhythm, depth, and effort. The use of accessory muscles in the neck and shoulders signals increased work of breathing, pointing to airway obstruction or poor lung compliance.

Auscultation reveals breath sounds generated by turbulent airflow through the respiratory tree. Normal vesicular sounds heard over peripheral lung fields contrast with abnormal adventitious sounds such as crackles, wheezes, or pleural friction rubs, each corresponding to specific pathophysiological alterations in alveolar or bronchial structures.
