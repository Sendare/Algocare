Anatomy and Physiology of the Respiratory System

Introduction and Upper Respiratory Tract Anatomy

The human body requires a continuous supply of oxygen to sustain cellular metabolism and produce energy, while simultaneously eliminating metabolic waste products like carbon dioxide. The respiratory system achieves this vital exchange through two main functional divisions: the upper and lower respiratory tracts. The upper respiratory tract consists of structures outside the thoracic cavity, primarily the nose, nasal cavity, sinuses, and pharynx. Air normally enters through the nasal cavity, where it encounters a rich network of blood vessels and ciliated mucous membranes. This setup warms, humidifies, and filters incoming air, trapping dust and pathogens before they reach delicate lung tissues. The pharynx, commonly known as the throat, serves as a shared muscular pathway for both the respiratory and digestive systems. It directs air downward toward the larynx while routing food and liquids safely toward the esophagus. Within this region, the epiglottis acts as a crucial trapdoor, snapping shut over the laryngeal opening during swallowing to prevent aspiration.

Anatomy of the Lower Respiratory Tract

Passing downward past the larynx, air enters the lower respiratory tract, beginning with the trachea or windpipe. The trachea is a rigid tube reinforced by C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage. These rings keep the airway open continuously, preventing it from collapsing during pressure changes in the chest. The open part of the C-shape faces backward toward the esophagus, allowing the esophagus to expand slightly when swallowing food. At its lower end, the trachea branches into the left and right primary bronchi, which plunge directly into the lungs. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter, and more vertical than the left, making it the most common path for aspirated foreign objects to enter. Inside the lung tissue, these primary bronchi branch repeatedly into smaller secondary bronchi, tertiary bronchi, and eventually tiny bronchioles. Unlike the trachea, bronchioles lack cartilage support and rely instead on smooth muscle to regulate airflow diameter.

Microscopic Structure of the Lungs and Alveoli

The ultimate destination of inspired air is the alveoli, the microscopic air sacs where gas exchange takes place. Millions of these tiny sacs cluster together like bunches of grapes at the ends of respiratory bronchioles, creating a massive surface area for gas transfer. The walls of the alveoli are extremely thin, composed primarily of simple squamous epithelium known as Type I alveolar cells. Interspersed among these are Type II alveolar cells, which secrete pulmonary surfactant. This detergent-like substance reduces surface tension in the fluid lining the alveoli, preventing them from collapsing inward during exhalation. A dense network of pulmonary capillaries wraps tightly around each alveolus, forming the respiratory membrane. Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse rapidly across this ultra-thin membrane because the distance between alveolar air and capillary blood is microscopic.

Mechanics of Pulmonary Ventilation

Pulmonary ventilation is the physical movement of air into and out of the lungs, governed by simple gas laws and pressure gradients. Air flows from an area of higher pressure to an area of lower pressure. During inhalation, the diaphragm contracts and moves downward while the external intercostal muscles pull the ribs upward and outward. This action increases the volume of the thoracic cavity. According to Boyle's law, as container volume increases, internal pressure decreases. When intrapulmonary pressure drops below atmospheric pressure, air rushes into the lungs. Exhalation is normally a passive process driven by the elastic recoil of lung tissue and chest muscles. As the diaphragm and intercostal muscles relax, thoracic volume decreases, raising intrapulmonary pressure above atmospheric pressure and pushing air out.

Gas Exchange and Transport

Once air reaches the alveoli, external respiration occurs, driven by differences in partial pressures. Oxygen diffuses from the alveoli, where its partial pressure is high, into the pulmonary capillaries, where its partial pressure is low. Simultaneously, carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction, moving from capillary blood into the alveoli to be exhaled. Once inside the bloodstream, about 98.5% of oxygen binds reversibly to hemoglobin molecules inside red blood cells, forming oxyhemoglobin. The remaining small fraction dissolves directly into the plasma. Carbon dioxide is transported back to the lungs through three main mechanisms: dissolved in plasma, bound to hemoglobin as carbaminohemoglobin, and primarily as bicarbonate ions dissolved in the plasma following chemical buffering by red blood cells.

Neural and Chemical Regulation of Respiration

Breathing is an automatic process controlled by respiratory centers located in the brainstem, specifically the medulla oblongata and pons. The medullary respiratory center sets the basic rhythm and pace of ventilation. While this rhythm is automatic, it adjusts continuously based on chemical feedback from the body. Central chemoreceptors located on the surface of the medulla monitor cerebrospinal fluid pH and carbon dioxide levels. Because carbon dioxide easily crosses the blood-brain barrier, rising blood carbon dioxide levels lead to an increase in hydrogen ions, signaling the respiratory center to increase the rate and depth of breathing. Peripheral chemoreceptors located in the carotid and aortic bodies monitor arterial oxygen levels, carbon dioxide, and blood pH, providing a secondary backup drive to breathe when blood oxygen drops severely.

Clinical Implications for Nursing Assessment

A thorough respiratory assessment provides vital clues about a patient's oxygenation and overall health status. Nurses evaluate respiratory rate, depth, rhythm, and effort by observing chest movement and listening with a stethoscope. Abnormal breath sounds, such as wheezes, crackles, or diminished air entry, often point to specific underlying pathologies like airway narrowing, fluid accumulation, or alveolar collapse. Additionally, non-invasive monitoring tools like pulse oximetry allow nurses to track arterial oxygen saturation continuously. Recognizing early signs of respiratory distress—such as nasal flaring, use of accessory muscles, or tachypnea—ensures timely clinical intervention before tissue hypoxia leads to cellular damage.
