Mechanical Ventilation

Introduction and Indications for Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving supportive therapy used when a patient's respiratory system is unable to maintain adequate gas exchange independently. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding when a patient requires this intervention is crucial for timely clinical decision-making in emergency and critical care settings. The primary indications for mechanical ventilation can be broadly categorized into acute respiratory failure, severe hypoxemia, acute hypercapnia, and increased work of breathing that leads to impending respiratory muscle exhaustion. Conditions such as severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbations, status asthmaticus, and drug overdoses frequently precipitate the need for invasive or non-invasive mechanical support. Furthermore, patients with depressed conscious levels resulting from traumatic brain injury or cerebrovascular accidents often require mechanical ventilation to protect their airway and prevent aspiration. The overarching goal of initiating ventilation is to maintain adequate alveolar ventilation, ensure optimal oxygenation, reduce the metabolic workload of the respiratory muscles, and allow time for the underlying disease process to resolve. Nurses must remain vigilant in identifying subtle signs of respiratory deterioration, such as tachypnea, use of accessory muscles, paradoxical breathing, and altered mental status, which often signal the immediate need for mechanical ventilatory support.

Anatomy and Physiology of Ventilation vs. Mechanical Support

To fully grasp mechanical ventilation, nursing students must first understand normal pulmonary physiology and how mechanical support alters it. Spontaneous ventilation is a negative-pressure process. During inhalation, the diaphragm and intercostal muscles contract, expanding the thoracic cavity. This creates a negative intrapleural pressure that draws air into the lungs. Exhalation is normally a passive process driven by the elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall. In contrast, mechanical ventilators operate on positive pressure. They push air into the lungs by generating a positive pressure gradient, forcing gas through the endotracheal tube and into the alveoli. While this effectively ensures oxygen delivery and carbon dioxide removal, it fundamentally alters normal cardiopulmonary physiology. Positive pressure ventilation increases mean intrathoracic pressure, which can impede venous return to the heart, reduce preload, and potentially lead to a decrease in cardiac output and systemic blood pressure. Additionally, positive pressure can cause uneven ventilation, preferentially pushing air into areas of least resistance rather than dependent lung regions, predisposing the patient to atelectasis or overdistension. Nurses must monitor hemodynamic stability closely in mechanically ventilated patients, recognizing that changes in ventilator settings can rapidly impact cardiac function.

Modes of Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilators offer various modes of support tailored to the patient's specific respiratory needs, level of consciousness, and recovery stage. The two foundational control categories are Volume Control (VC) and Pressure Control (PC). In Volume Control, a predetermined tidal volume is delivered with each breath, guaranteeing minute ventilation but allowing airway pressures to fluctuate based on lung compliance and resistance. In Pressure Control, a set inspiratory pressure is delivered, ensuring that airway pressures do not exceed a safe threshold, though tidal volume may vary. Furthermore, modes can be categorized by how breaths are initiated and assisted. Continuous Mandatory Ventilation (CMV) or Controlled Mechanical Ventilation delivers a set number of mandatory breaths regardless of patient effort, which is useful for heavily sedated or paralyzed patients. Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV) allows the patient to breathe spontaneously between ventilator-delivered mandatory breaths, synchronizing the machine breath with the patient's inspiratory effort to prevent breath stacking. Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV) is a spontaneous mode where the ventilator assists each patient-initiated breath with a preset level of pressure, greatly reducing the work of breathing during weaning. As a critical care nurse, recognizing these modes helps in evaluating patient-ventilator synchrony and participating in interprofessional care planning.

Ventilator Settings and Parameters

Configuring and monitoring ventilator settings is a core competency for nurses caring for critically ill patients. Key parameters include the Fraction of Inspired Oxygen (FiO2), Tidal Volume (TV), Respiratory Rate (RR), Positive End-Expiratory Pressure (PEEP), and I:E ratio. FiO2 represents the concentration of oxygen delivered, ranging from 21% (room air) to 100%, and should be kept at the lowest effective level to prevent oxygen toxicity. Tidal volume is typically calculated based on the patient's ideal body weight (IBW), usually 6 to 8 mL/kg, to prevent volutrauma from over-inflation. The respiratory rate is set to achieve adequate minute ventilation and normal carbon dioxide elimination. PEEP applies positive pressure at the end of expiration to keep alveoli open, prevent alveolar collapse, and improve oxygenation, especially in conditions like ARDS. High PEEP, however, can compromise blood pressure. The inspiratory-to-expiratory (I:E) ratio dictates the duration of inhalation versus exhalation, normally set at 1:2 to allow sufficient time for complete exhalation and prevent breath stacking (auto-PEEP). Nurses must cross-check ventilator settings against the medical prescription during every shift handoff and document any changes promptly, ensuring alarms are always active and set to safe parameters.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing assessment and continuous monitoring are essential for detecting complications and ensuring patient safety during mechanical ventilation. The nurse must perform frequent respiratory assessments, including observing chest symmetry, auscultating bilateral breath sounds to confirm equal air entry and rule out mainstem intubation, and evaluating SpO2 via pulse oximetry. Hemodynamic parameters such as heart rate, blood pressure, and urine output must be tracked regularly, as positive pressure ventilation can alter cardiac output and renal perfusion. Ventilator alarms—such as high-pressure, low-pressure, and low-minute ventilation alarms—must never be silenced or ignored; they serve as critical early warning systems for tube displacement, secretions, circuit disconnection, or patient distress. The nurse should also evaluate sedation levels using standardized tools like the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) to prevent patient agitation, accidental extubation, or prolonged unconsciousness. Furthermore, monitoring arterial blood gas (ABG) results allows the healthcare team to evaluate the effectiveness of ventilation and oxygenation, guiding adjustments in settings. Regular checks of the endotracheal tube position at the lip line, documented every shift, prevent unplanned extubations.

Airway Management and Securing the Endotracheal Tube

Proper airway management and secure tube fixation are vital nursing responsibilities for patients on mechanical ventilation. Endotracheal tubes (ETTs) provide a direct conduit to the lower airways but bypass natural defense mechanisms, increasing the risk of infection and displacement. The nurse must ensure the ETT is securely anchored using specialized adhesive tape or commercial tube holders, with the centimeter mark at the lip or teeth documented consistently across shifts. Two healthcare providers should participate when repositioning the ETT or changing the tube holder to prevent accidental extubation. Oral care is a critical component of airway management; utilizing chlorhexidine mouthwash and gentle suctioning helps reduce the bacterial load in oral secretions, significantly lowering the incidence of Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP). Endotracheal suctioning should be performed only when clinically indicated—such as when coarse breath sounds, coughing, or saw-tooth patterns on the ventilator flow waveform are observed—rather than routinely. Hyperoxygenation and hyperinflation before and after suctioning help minimize hypoxia and atelectasis during the procedure. Nurses must also monitor cuff pressure regularly, maintaining it between 20 to 30 cmH2O to prevent tracheal mucosal ischemia while avoiding air leaks.

Complications of Mechanical Ventilation and Preventive Care

Mechanical ventilation carries significant risks of complications that require proactive nursing care and preventive strategies. Key complications include Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP), barotrauma (such as pneumothorax), volutrauma, oxygen toxicity, and ventilator-induced diaphragm dysfunction (VIDD). To prevent VAP, nurses implement the ventilator bundle, which includes elevating the head of the bed between 30 and 45 degrees, performing daily sedative interruption and 'awakening trials,' assessing readiness for extubation daily, practicing meticulous hand hygiene, and providing regular oral hygiene with chlorhexidine. Barotrauma results from high airway pressures causing alveolar rupture, which can lead to subcutaneous emphysema, pneumomediastinum, or a tension pneumothorax—a life-threatening emergency marked by absent breath sounds on the affected side, tracheal deviation, and hemodynamic collapse. Nurses must be prepared to assist with needle decompression or chest tube insertion if tension pneumothorax occurs. Oxygen toxicity, caused by prolonged exposure to high FiO2 levels, can damage lung tissue through reactive oxygen species; therefore, nurses should titrate oxygen downward as soon as patient status permits. Early mobilization and physical therapy also mitigate general deconditioning and muscle wasting associated with prolonged mechanical support.

Weaning Protocols and Extubation

Weaning is the gradual process of transitioning a patient from mechanical ventilatory support back to spontaneous breathing. The weaning process begins as soon as the underlying condition that necessitated ventilation improves. Nurses play a pivotal role in assessing weaning readiness by monitoring criteria such as stable hemodynamics, adequate oxygenation (e.g., PaO2/FiO2 ratio > 200 on low FiO2), acceptable hemoglobin levels, spontaneous breathing capability, and resolution of acute infection or metabolic derangements. A Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT) is commonly conducted using T-piece trials or low levels of pressure support (e.g., PSV 5-8 cmH2O with PEEP 5) for 30 to 120 minutes. During the SBT, the nurse closely monitors the patient for signs of weaning failure, including tachypnea, tachycardia, hypertension or hypotension, diaphoresis, agitation, falling oxygen saturation, and use of accessory muscles. If the patient tolerates the SBT successfully, the interprofessional team proceeds with extubation. Extubation involves explaining the procedure to the patient, suctioning the ETT and oropharynx, defusing the cuff, removing the tube at peak inspiration, and encouraging coughing and deep breathing. Post-extubation care requires monitoring for stridor (which may indicate upper airway edema), hoarseness, respiratory distress, and providing supplemental oxygen via nasal cannula or face mask as prescribed.
