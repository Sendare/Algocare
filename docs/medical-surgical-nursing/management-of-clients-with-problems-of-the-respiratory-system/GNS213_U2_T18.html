Mechanical Ventilation

Indications for Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation is a life-support intervention used when a patient cannot maintain adequate gas exchange on their own. The primary goals are to assist or replace spontaneous breathing, deliver adequate oxygen, and remove carbon dioxide. Indications generally fall into two main categories: acute respiratory failure and the need to protect the airway.

  • Hypoxemic Respiratory Failure: Occurs when the lungs cannot oxygenate blood properly, commonly seen in pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), or pulmonary edema.
  • Hypercapnic Respiratory Failure: Happens when the body cannot eliminate carbon dioxide effectively, often due to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) flare-ups, drug overdoses, or severe neuromuscular weakness.
  • Airway Protection: Needed when a patient has a depressed level of consciousness from head trauma, stroke, or severe shock, placing them at high risk for aspiration.

Modes of Mechanical Ventilation

Ventilator modes describe how the machine interacts with the patient's respiratory drive. Choosing the right mode depends on how much work of breathing the patient can safely handle.

  • Controlled Mandatory Ventilation (CMV): The ventilator delivers breaths at a set rate and volume regardless of patient effort. Rarely used today except in completely paralyzed or heavily sedated patients.
  • Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV): Delivers a set number of mandatory breaths synchronized with any patient breathing efforts, while allowing the patient to breathe spontaneously between machine breaths.
  • Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV): Assists spontaneous breathing by giving a pre-set amount of pressure during inspiration to help overcome the resistance of the endotracheal tube.
  • Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP): Applies constant positive pressure throughout the breathing cycle to keep alveoli open in spontaneously breathing patients.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Monitoring

Caring for a mechanically ventilated patient requires continuous vigilance. The nurse must assess both the patient's clinical response and the mechanical function of the ventilator system.

  • Tube Placement Verification: Confirm the endotracheal tube position using auscultation of bilateral breath sounds, observing chest symmetry, and checking end-tidal carbon dioxide (ETCO2) detectors.
  • Vital Signs and Oxygenation: Continuously monitor pulse oximetry and capnography. Sudden drops in oxygen saturation require immediate assessment.
  • Sedation and Comfort: Use standardized sedation scales (such as the RASS) to prevent patient distress, accidental extubation, or fighting the ventilator.
  • Ventilator Alarms: Never ignore or silence an alarm without troubleshooting. High-pressure alarms often indicate secretions, biting the tube, or coughing; low-pressure alarms usually signal a disconnection or leak.

Airway Management and Tube Security

Maintaining a secure artificial airway is vital to prevent accidental extubation or airway trauma. The nurse plays a central role in daily airway care.

  • Tube Securement: Use commercial tube holders or secure adhesive tape methods, repositioning the tape or holder periodically to prevent pressure ulcers on the lips and corners of the mouth.
  • Cuff Management: Maintain endotracheal cuff pressures between 20 to 30 cmH2O. Over-inflation causes tracheal ischemia and necrosis, while under-inflation increases the risk of aspiration pneumonia.
  • Suctioning: Suction the airway only when clinically indicated (such as visible secretions, coughing, or coarse breath sounds), not on a routine schedule. Hyperoxygenate the patient before suctioning to prevent hypoxia.

Complications of Mechanical Ventilation and Preventive Care

While life-saving, mechanical ventilation carries significant risks that require proactive nursing care to prevent.

  • Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP): Bacteria enter the lower respiratory tract around the endotracheal cuff. Prevent VAP by elevating the head of the bed 30 to 45 degrees, performing meticulous oral care with chlorhexidine, and conducting daily sedation vacations.
  • Barotrauma and Volutrauma: High pressures or excessive volumes can rupture alveoli, leading to pneumothorax. Use low tidal volumes and monitor pressure limits.
  • Stress Ulcers: Critical illness and mechanical stress increase gastric acid production. Prophylactic proton pump inhibitors or H2 blockers are commonly prescribed.
  • Diaphragm Atrophy: Inactivity of respiratory muscles leads to rapid muscle wasting. Minimizing deep sedation helps maintain diaphragm function.

Weaning Protocols and Extubation

Weaning is the gradual transition from mechanical ventilatory support back to spontaneous breathing. The process begins as soon as the underlying condition that led to intubation starts to improve.

  • Weaning Criteria: The patient must be hemodynamically stable, have adequate oxygenation (e.g., acceptable PaO2 on low FiO2), be able to initiate spontaneous breaths, and show resolved acute disease.
  • Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT): A trial period (usually 30 to 120 minutes) where the patient breathes with minimal or no ventilator support (using T-piece or low CPAP/pressure support) to test their endurance.
  • Extubation Readiness: If the patient passes the SBT, maintains good vital signs, and has an intact cough and gag reflex, the endotracheal tube is removed.
  • Post-Extubation Care: Monitor closely for stridor, hoarseness, respiratory distress, or inability to clear secretions, as these signal upper airway swelling or failure.
