Mechanical Ventilation

Indications and Physiological Goals of Mechanical Ventilation

Mechanical ventilation is a life-saving supportive therapy utilized when spontaneous breathing is inadequate to sustain life. The primary goals are to manipulate gas exchange, relieve the work of breathing, and reverse life-threatening hypoxemia or acute respiratory acidosis. Clinical scenarios demanding this intervention fall into two broad categories: acute respiratory failure and severe physiological exhaustion. In Nigerian intensive care units, late presentation of conditions such as severe acute asthma, tetanus-induced spasms, eclamptic pulmonary edema, and advanced tropical infections frequently precipitate this critical requirement. - **Acute Hypoxemic Respiratory Failure**: Characterized by a failure of oxygenation, commonly seen in severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and pulmonary contusions. - **Acute Hypercapnic Respiratory Failure**: Inability to eliminate carbon dioxide effectively, resulting from central nervous system depression, neuromuscular disorders, or severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbations. - **Prophylactic Support**: Applied following major surgical procedures or during profound hemodynamic instability to minimize systemic oxygen consumption.

Understanding Ventilator Modes

Ventilator modes dictate how the machine interacts with the patient's native respiratory drive. Selecting a mode depends on the patient's strength, level of consciousness, and recovery trajectory. - **Controlled Mandatory Ventilation (CMV)**: The machine delivers breaths at a preset rate and volume regardless of patient effort. Used primarily in heavily sedated or paralyzed patients. - **Synchronized Intermittent Mandatory Ventilation (SIMV)**: Delivers preset mandatory breaths synchronized with the patient's inspiratory efforts, while allowing spontaneous breaths in between. - **Pressure Support Ventilation (PSV)**: Completely patient-triggered; the ventilator provides a set amount of positive pressure during inspiration to assist spontaneous breaths.

Core Ventilator Parameters and Settings

Operating a mechanical ventilator requires precise prescription and continuous monitoring of baseline settings. **Tidal volume (TV)** represents the volume of gas delivered with each breath, typically calculated at 6 to 8 milliliters per kilogram of ideal body weight to prevent volutrauma. **Respiratory rate (RR)** dictates the number of breaths delivered per minute. **Fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2)** is adjusted between 0.21 and 1.0 to maintain safe arterial oxygen saturations while mitigating oxygen toxicity risks. Additionally, **Positive End-Expiratory Pressure (PEEP)** applies constant pressure at the end of expiration to keep alveoli open, preventing alveolar collapse and improving functional residual capacity. **Peak inspiratory pressure (PIP)** measures the highest pressure reached during inspiration, serving as a vital indicator of airway resistance and lung compliance changes.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Caring for a mechanically ventilated patient extends beyond watching console monitors. The nurse performs systematic physical assessments comparing machine readings with clinical findings. Chest symmetry, bilateral breath sounds, and endotracheal tube depth markings must be verified frequently. In resource-constrained settings where continuous capnography or blood gas machines might experience downtime, clinical vigilance regarding skin color, capillary refill, and chest movement is indispensable. Alarm management is another high-stakes nursing duty. Ventilator alarms are safety nets and must never be silenced or ignored. High-pressure alarms typically indicate obstruction, coughing, or decreased compliance, whereas low-pressure alarms point toward leaks, disconnected tubing, or extubation. Sedation scales, such as the Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale, guide titration of medications to ensure the patient remains comfortable and synchronous with the machine.

Airway Security and Tube Maintenance

Maintaining a patent artificial airway is paramount. Endotracheal tubes and tracheostomy tubes bypass natural upper airway filtration, requiring meticulous nursing care to prevent complications. Endotracheal tube security involves changing adhesive tape or commercial holders regularly to prevent skin breakdown while ensuring the tube never dislodges. Suctioning removes pulmonary secretions that can obstruct the airway and harbor pathogens. This procedure is performed using aseptic technique based on clinical need—such as visible secretions, coughing, or sawtooth patterns on the ventilator flow-loop—rather than on a rigid routine. Pre-oxygenation and limiting suction passes to restricted timeframes prevent severe procedure-induced hypoxemia and cardiac arrhythmias.

Complications and Preventive Care Bundles

Mechanical ventilation carries significant iatrogenic risks. Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP) is a prominent nosocomial threat, managed through evidence-based care bundles including regular oral care with chlorhexidine, maintaining head-of-bed elevation between 30 and 45 degrees, and daily sedation vacations to assess readiness for weaning. Barotrauma and volutrauma arise from excessive pressures and volumes, leading to alveolar rupture and conditions like pneumothorax. Oxygen toxicity damages lung parenchyma when high FiO2 levels are sustained unnecessarily. Furthermore, prolonged immobility predisposes patients to critical illness myopathy, pressure injuries, and deep vein thrombosis, requiring coordinated multi-disciplinary preventive interventions.

Weaning Protocols and Extubation

Weaning is the gradual transition from mechanical ventilatory support back to spontaneous breathing. The process begins once the underlying cause of respiratory failure resolves and clinical stability is achieved. Screening involves assessing hemodynamic stability, adequate oxygenation parameters, and spontaneous breathing capability through a Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT). During an SBT, the patient breathes with minimal or no ventilator support while monitored closely for signs of fatigue, tachypnea, tachycardia, or oxygen desaturation. If the trial succeeds, the clinician performs extubation. Post-extubation care requires vigilant monitoring for upper airway edema, stridor, hoarseness, and swallowing dysfunction, ensuring the patient can maintain a patent airway independently before oral intake is resumed.
