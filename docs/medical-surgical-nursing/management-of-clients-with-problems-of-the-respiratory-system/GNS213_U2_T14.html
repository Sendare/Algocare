Tracheostomy

Introduction and Definition of Tracheostomy

A tracheostomy is a surgical opening made into the anterior wall of the trachea, establishing a direct airway below the larynx. When this surgical stoma is maintained by the insertion of a curved hollow tube, it provides an alternative pathway for breathing. Historically performed primarily for upper airway obstruction, contemporary clinical indications now encompass prolonged mechanical ventilation, severe neurological impairment, and comprehensive management of copious lower respiratory secretions. The procedure bypasses the upper respiratory tract completely, avoiding the nasal and oral cavities. In Nigerian tertiary hospitals, this intervention is frequently managed in specialized ear, nose, and throat wards as well as intensive care units, requiring meticulous surgical and nursing coordination.

Indications and Purposes of Tracheostomy

Clinical situations necessitating a tracheostomy generally fall into categories of acute airway obstruction, respiratory failure, or clearance difficulties.

  • Upper airway obstruction: Caused by tumors, severe maxillofacial trauma, laryngeal edema, or foreign bodies.
  • Prolonged mechanical ventilation: Reduces laryngeal damage associated with prolonged endotracheal intubation, typically considered when ventilation exceeds 14 days.
  • Inadequate pulmonary hygiene: Facilitates deep and frequent removal of thick bronchial secretions in patients with depressed cough reflexes or neuromuscular weakness like myasthenia gravis.
  • Comatose states: Secures a stable airway in patients with severe traumatic brain injury or cerebrovascular accidents who lack a protective gag reflex.

Types of Tracheostomy Tubes and Components

Tracheostomy tubes vary in material, design, and presence of an inflatable cuff. Standard modern tubes are manufactured from medical-grade plastics like polyvinyl chloride or silicone, while older reusable models are made of silver.

  • Outer cannula: The main shaft that maintains the patency of the stoma, secured with neck ties.
  • Inner cannula: A removable insert that locks inside the outer cannula, allowing quick cleaning or replacement to prevent mucus plugging.
  • Cuff: An inflatable balloon near the distal end that seals the trachea for mechanical ventilation and prevents aspiration; requires careful pressure monitoring.
  • Fenestration: Small openings on the shaft of some tubes that permit airflow upward through the vocal cords, enabling the patient to speak.

Pre-operative and Intra-operative Nursing Care

Preparation for an elective tracheostomy involves comprehensive patient assessment, psychological support, and obtaining informed consent. Baseline respiratory parameters, arterial blood gases, and coagulation profiles must be reviewed. The nurse ensures the patient is fasted and that emergency airway equipment, including manual resuscitator bags and suction apparatus, is immediately available at the bedside. Intra-operatively, the nurse assists the surgical team by maintaining a sterile field, monitoring vital signs, and ensuring adequate lighting. Immediate post-insertion, the nurse must verify tube placement by auscultating bilateral breath sounds and observing chest symmetry while awaiting formal radiological confirmation.

Immediate Post-operative Assessment and Management

The initial hours following tracheostomy creation carry the highest risk of acute complications such as accidental decannulation, hemorrhage, and subcutaneous emphysema. Vital signs, oxygen saturation, and respiratory effort are monitored continuously. The nurse inspects the stoma site frequently for excessive bleeding or swelling. The cuff pressure should be monitored and maintained between 20 to 25 mmHg to prevent tracheal wall ischemia while ensuring an adequate seal. Humidified oxygen must be delivered directly to the tracheostomy to prevent mucosal drying, as the normal upper airway filtration and warming mechanisms are bypassed.

Routine Tracheostomy Care and Suctioning Technique

Daily maintenance preserves skin integrity and keeps the airway clear of secretions. Suctioning is performed on an as-needed basis rather than on a rigid schedule, triggered by audible secretions, coughing, or decreased oxygen saturation.

  • Aseptic technique: Wear sterile gloves and use sterile suction catheters to prevent lower respiratory tract infections.
  • Hyperoxygenation: Administer 100% oxygen briefly before and after suctioning to prevent hypoxia.
  • Catheter insertion: Advance the catheter gently without suction applied until resistance is met, then withdraw approximately 1 centimeter.
  • Intermittent suction: Apply suction only during withdrawal while rotating the catheter gently for no longer than 10 to 15 seconds.
  • Stoma cleaning: Clean around the incision with normal saline, pat dry, and replace soiled neck ties while a second person holds the tube securely.

Complications of Tracheostomy and Emergency Interventions

Complications range from immediate procedural mishaps to delayed structural changes. Early hazards include hemorrhage, pneumothorax, subcutaneous emphysema, and accidental tube displacement. Late complications involve tracheal stenosis, infection, and tracheo-esophageal fistula formation.

If accidental decannulation occurs within the first 72 hours, it represents a surgical emergency because the tract is not mature; the nurse must call for medical assistance immediately while preparing to secure the airway. For a mature tract, the nurse can insert a spare obturator-guided tube into the stoma. Tracheostomy obturators and a spare tube of the same and smaller size must remain at the bedside at all times.

Weaning, Decannulation, and Patient Education

Weaning from a tracheostomy begins when the primary condition requiring the airway improves and the patient can maintain adequate ventilation and clear secretions independently. The process often involves downsizing the tube, capping or using a speaking valve to test upper airway breathing and swallowing. Once the patient tolerates complete tube occlusion for 24 to 48 hours, decannulation can proceed. The nurse removes the sutures if present, extracts the tube, and applies a sterile occlusive dressing over the stoma. The site typically closes spontaneously within several days. Comprehensive patient and caregiver education covers stoma inspection, suctioning techniques, signs of infection, and emergency responses.
