Tracheostomy

Introduction and Definition of Tracheostomy

A tracheostomy is a surgical procedure that involves creating an opening into the trachea through the neck to establish a secure airway. For nursing students in Nigeria preparing for professional examinations, understanding this procedure is vital as it is frequently encountered in emergency, intensive care, and surgical nursing units. The resulting artificial opening is called a tracheostomy stoma, while the tube inserted to maintain patent airway access is known as a tracheostomy tube. Historically and clinically, tracheostomies can be performed as an emergency bedside procedure or as an elective surgical intervention in an operating theatre. Unlike endotracheal intubation, which is typically a short-term measure managed via the mouth or nose, a tracheostomy is often indicated when prolonged mechanical ventilation is anticipated or when upper airway obstructions prevent normal breathing. The primary goals of establishing a tracheostomy include bypassing upper airway anatomical obstructions, facilitating long-term mechanical ventilation, reducing the work of breathing, and enabling effective clearance of lower respiratory secretions. As nursing professionals, you must be well-acquainted with the anatomy of the trachea, specifically recognizing that the surgical incision is usually made between the second and third tracheal rings. Mastery of this definition forms the foundation for safe clinical practice, effective patient monitoring, and prompt recognition of respiratory emergencies in patients with altered airway needs.

Indications and Purposes of Tracheostomy

Understanding the indications and purposes of a tracheostomy is essential for safe nursing practice and comprehensive patient assessment in Nigerian healthcare settings. Tracheostomies are performed for several critical medical reasons, primarily categorized into upper airway obstruction, need for prolonged mechanical ventilation, and inadequate pulmonary hygiene. When a patient suffers from severe maxillofacial trauma, laryngeal tumors, foreign body obstructions, or severe edema from burns or anaphylaxis, the upper airway becomes impassable, necessitating a surgical bypass via tracheostomy. Furthermore, patients with chronic respiratory failure, neuromuscular diseases such as myasthenia gravis or Guillain-Barré syndrome, and those requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation benefit immensely from a tracheostomy compared to long-term endotracheal intubation. Endotracheal tubes carry a high risk of laryngeal and vocal cord damage if left in place for more than 14 to 21 days. Additionally, a tracheostomy drastically reduces anatomical dead space, decreases airway resistance, and makes it considerably easier for healthcare providers to perform deep tracheal suctioning to clear thick, copious pulmonary secretions. It also allows conscious patients to swallow, speak with the aid of special valves, and experience greater comfort and mobility during rehabilitation compared to being tethered to an oral breathing tube. Nurses must continuously evaluate their patients against these indications to advocate effectively for timely interventions and ensure optimal respiratory outcomes.

Types of Tracheostomy Tubes and Components

Tracheostomy tubes come in various designs, materials, and sizes, each serving specific clinical functions. As a nursing student, you must be familiar with the core components shared by most tracheostomy tubes: the outer cannula, the inner cannula, and the obturator. The outer cannula is the main body that maintains the patency of the stoma and holds the tube in place via flange (neckplate) ties. The inner cannula fits snugly inside the outer cannula and can be removed for cleaning or replacement, preventing mucus accumulation and tube occlusion. Disposable inner cannulas are common in modern practice, though reusable ones require meticulous cleaning protocols. The obturator is a smooth, rounded guide used solely during the insertion of the tracheostomy tube to prevent trauma to the tracheal wall, and it must be removed immediately after insertion to establish airflow. Tracheostomy tubes can be cuffed or uncuffed. Cuffed tubes feature an inflatable balloon connected to a pilot balloon; when inflated, it creates a seal against the tracheal wall, which prevents aspiration and ensures delivery of prescribed tidal volumes during mechanical ventilation. However, continuous cuff inflation can cause pressure necrosis, necessitating regular pressure monitoring. Uncuffed tubes are utilized in pediatric patients or patients who are breathing spontaneously and no longer require mechanical ventilation, allowing them to speak and cough secretions past the vocal cords. Tubes may be made of polyvinyl chloride (PVC), silicone, or metal (such as silver Negus tubes used historically or for long-term laryngectomy). Understanding these components ensures safe handling, effective hygiene maintenance, and prompt troubleshooting during daily nursing care.

Pre-operative and Intra-operative Nursing Care

Preparing a patient for a tracheostomy involves meticulous pre-operative nursing care and vigilant intra-operative and immediate post-operative observation. Pre-operatively, the nurse plays a critical role in psychological preparation by explaining the procedure to the patient and family, especially regarding temporary changes in speech and communication. Consent verification, baseline vital signs, and ensuring that emergency equipment—such as suction machines, bag-valve-mask resuscitators, oxygen sources, and spare tracheostomy tubes of the same and smaller sizes—is at the bedside are mandatory nursing responsibilities. The nurse must also ensure the patient has fasted appropriately if the procedure is elective. Intra-operatively, nurses assist the surgical team by maintaining a sterile environment, monitoring patient vital signs continuously via pulse oximetry and electrocardiography, and observing for signs of complications such as hemorrhage, subcutaneous emphysema, or accidental extubation. Following the creation of the stoma, immediate nursing care focuses on securing the tracheostomy tube properly using twill tapes or Velcro ties, ensuring that only one finger can fit snugly under the ties to prevent accidental dislodgement while avoiding excessive neck constriction. Documentation of the procedure, tube size, type, and the patient's initial respiratory response concludes the peri-operative nursing cycle, laying the groundwork for safe ongoing recovery.

Immediate Post-operative Assessment and Management

Immediate post-operative assessment and management of a tracheostomy patient are high-priority nursing responsibilities aimed at preserving airway patency and preventing life-threatening complications. Upon arrival from the theatre or immediately following bedside insertion, the nurse must assess airway patency, bilateral chest expansion, and breath sounds auscultated across all lung fields. Respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, and heart rate should be monitored continuously. The nurse must check the stoma site frequently for signs of hemorrhage, excessive bleeding, or subcutaneous emphysema—which feels like crepitus or crackling under the skin around the neck and chest wall, indicating air leaking into subcutaneous tissues. Humidification must be initiated immediately because bypassing the upper airway robs inspired air of the natural warming and filtering provided by the nose and mouth; cold, dry air thickens secretions and can cause mucous plugging. The nurse should also regularly inspect the tracheostomy dressing, keeping it clean, dry, and changed per hospital protocol using sterile technique. Ensuring the obturator is taped securely to the head of the bed or kept within immediate reach is another crucial safety measure for rapid re-insertion in case of accidental decannulation. Prompt recognition of any respiratory distress during this vulnerable period allows nurses to intervene swiftly and save lives.

Routine Tracheostomy Care and Suctioning Technique

Routine tracheostomy care and sterile suctioning technique are core competencies every nursing student must master before clinical placement in Nigeria. Routine care involves cleaning the stoma site, changing ties, and maintaining an unobstructed inner cannula. The stoma site should be cleaned at least once per shift or whenever soiled, using sterile normal saline and sterile gauze swabs to remove dried secretions and prevent skin breakdown. Inner cannula care is performed frequently for reusable types by removing the inner tube, soaking and scrubbing it in sterile saline or hydrogen peroxide solution, rinsing thoroughly, and reinserting it promptly to prevent airway obstruction. When performing tracheal suctioning, nurses must adhere strictly to aseptic technique to prevent lower respiratory tract infections. Suctioning should be done on an as-needed basis rather than on a rigid schedule, looking out for clinical indicators such as audible secretions in the tube, coughing, respiratory distress, or a drop in oxygen saturation. The suction pressure must be regulated safely—typically between 80 to 120 mmHg in adults. Pre-oxygenation with 100% oxygen for 30 to 60 seconds is mandatory before suctioning to prevent hypoxia. The catheter should be inserted gently without applying suction until reaching the desired depth, then withdrawn while applying intermittent suction and gentle rotation for no longer than 10 to 15 seconds. Continuous monitoring of the patient's color, heart rate, and oxygen saturation throughout the procedure ensures patient safety and prevents hypoxia-induced arrhythmias.

Complications of Tracheostomy and Emergency Interventions

A tracheostomy, while life-saving, presents several potential complications that require vigilant nursing surveillance and prompt emergency interventions. Early complications include hemorrhage at the stoma site, accidental decannulation, pneumothorax, subcutaneous emphysema, and posterior tracheal wall damage. Late complications include tracheal stenosis, tracheoesophageal fistula, infection, and granulation tissue formation. Among these, accidental decannulation is a critical emergency, particularly in the immediate post-operative period when the stoma tract has not yet matured (usually within the first 72 hours). If a tube accidentally dislodges, the nurse must remain calm and immediately attempt to reinsert a spare tracheostomy tube of the same or smaller size using the obturator. If re-insertion fails, the nurse must maintain airway patency using a bag-valve-mask over the stoma or cover the stoma while assisting ventilation via the mouth and nose, and summon immediate medical assistance. Another severe emergency is severe hemorrhage, which can stem from erosion of the innominate artery (tracheo-innominate artery fistula), presenting as pulsating blood from the stoma; this requires immediate over-inflation of the cuff and digital compression of the artery against the sternum while rushing the patient to the operating theatre. Recognizing these warning signs and drilling emergency responses ensures that student nurses are well-prepared for crisis management in clinical settings.

Weaning, Decannulation, and Patient Education

Weaning, decannulation, and comprehensive patient education mark the final successful stages of tracheostomy management. Weaning involves gradually reducing the patient's dependence on the tracheostomy tube and mechanical ventilation as their underlying respiratory condition improves. This is typically achieved by downsizing the tracheostomy tube, using progressively smaller fenestrated tubes that allow airflow past the vocal cords, or utilizing speaking valves (such as a Passy-Muir valve) to restore normal upper airway breathing and speech. Once the patient can tolerate capping or complete tube occlusion for 24 to 48 hours without respiratory distress, adequate cough strength, and can clear their own secretions, decannulation can proceed. Decannulation is the permanent removal of the tracheostomy tube. Following removal, the nurse covers the stoma with a sterile, occlusive dressing secured tightly to prevent air leaks, instructing the patient to splint the site with their fingers when coughing or speaking. The stoma typically closes spontaneously within several days to weeks through secondary intention. Patient and caregiver education is vital throughout this phase. Nurses must teach patients and family members how to perform daily stoma care, recognize signs of infection, perform suctioning if going home with a temporary tube, and identify when to seek emergency medical help. Empowering patients with this knowledge ensures a safe transition from hospital to home care.
