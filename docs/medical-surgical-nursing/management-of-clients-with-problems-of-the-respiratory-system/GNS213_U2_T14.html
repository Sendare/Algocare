Tracheostomy

Introduction and Definition of Tracheostomy

A tracheostomy is a surgical opening made directly through the anterior neck into the trachea to establish an airway. Nurses often care for patients who require this procedure when upper airway obstructions prevent normal breathing or when long-term mechanical ventilation is necessary. Unlike an endotracheal tube that enters through the mouth or nose, a tracheostomy tube bypasses the upper respiratory tract entirely. This direct route significantly reduces airway resistance and makes breathing easier for compromised patients. Understanding this fundamental anatomy helps nurses distinguish between temporary emergency airways and more permanent surgical openings.

Indications and Purposes of Tracheostomy

Patients require a tracheostomy for several critical medical reasons. Knowing these clinical drivers helps nurses anticipate patient needs and monitor appropriate parameters.

  • Upper airway obstruction: Severe facial trauma, laryngeal tumors, or acute swelling can block the normal upper airway.
  • Prolonged mechanical ventilation: Patients who need a ventilator for weeks often transition to a tracheostomy to reduce vocal cord damage and improve patient comfort.
  • Secretions management: Individuals with weak cough reflexes or neuromuscular diseases benefit from easy access for airway clearance.
  • Respiratory muscle fatigue: Chronic conditions that exhaust breathing muscles make long-term ventilatory support necessary via a secure airway.

Types of Tracheostomy Tubes and Components

Tracheostomy tubes come in various materials, such as plastic, silicone, or metal, and consist of specific parts designed for safety and function.

  • Outer cannula: The main tube that holds the tracheostomy open against the tracheal walls.
  • Inner cannula: A removable inner lining that slides inside the outer cannula; it can be cleaned or replaced to prevent mucus buildup.
  • Obturator: A rounded guide used during tube insertion to prevent scraping the tracheal tissue, removed immediately after placement.
  • Cuff: A tiny balloon near the tip that inflates to seal the airway, preventing aspiration and ensuring proper delivery of mechanical breaths.

Nursing Care and Suctioning Technique

Maintaining a clean tracheostomy site and keeping the airway clear of secretions are core daily responsibilities for the nurse.

Before suctioning, the nurse must hyperoxygenate the patient to prevent low blood oxygen levels. Sterile technique is vital during deep suctioning to avoid introducing bacteria into the lungs. Insert the suction catheter gently without applying suction until reaching the desired depth, then apply intermittent suction while withdrawing and rotating the catheter. Limit each suction pass to ten to fifteen seconds. Clean the stoma site using sterile saline, changing the soiled dressing and ties while ensuring a second person holds the tube securely to prevent accidental dislodgement.

Complications and Emergency Interventions

A tracheostomy presents unique risks that demand immediate nursing recognition and swift intervention. Accidental decannulation is a medical emergency, particularly in the immediate post-operative phase when the stoma tract has not yet formed. The nurse must keep a replacement tube of the same size, a smaller size, and an obturator at the bedside at all times. Bleeding around the stoma, subcutaneous emphysema, and mucus plugging are other dangerous complications. If a mucus plug causes sudden respiratory distress, the nurse should first attempt to clear the inner cannula or suction the airway before taking further emergency action.

Weaning, Decannulation, and Patient Education

As the patient recovers and regains respiratory strength, the healthcare team begins the process of weaning them off the tracheostomy. Weaning often involves downsizing the tube, capping it with a speaking valve, or using fenestrated tubes that allow air to pass upward through the vocal cords.

Once the patient can breathe independently and clear their own secretions effectively, decannulation takes place. The nurse removes the tube and applies a sterile occlusive dressing over the stoma site, which typically heals closed on its own within days. Comprehensive patient and family education is vital throughout this phase, ensuring they understand stoma care, signs of infection, and emergency responses before hospital discharge.
