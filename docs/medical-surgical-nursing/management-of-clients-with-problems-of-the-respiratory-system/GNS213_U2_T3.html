Upper Airway Infections

Anatomy and Physiology of the Upper Respiratory Tract

The upper respiratory tract serves as the primary gateway for air entering the body, responsible for filtering, warming, and humidifying inspired air. Anatomically, it encompasses structures from the nasal cavity down to the vocal cords in the larynx, including the paranasal sinuses, pharynx (nasopharynx, oropharynx, and laryngopharynx), and the tonsils. The lining throughout this region consists of a ciliated pseudostratified columnar epithelium interspersed with mucus-secreting goblet cells. This mucosal blanket traps inhaled particulate matter and pathogens, while coordinated ciliary beating—often referred to as the mucociliary escalator—propels trapped debris toward the pharynx to be swallowed or expectorated. Rich vascular networks within the nasal turbinates rapidly adjust air temperature. When these delicate defense mechanisms are compromised by environmental irritants or microbial invasion, the local tissues mount an inflammatory response, laying the groundwork for clinical infection.

Etiology and Pathophysiology

Upper airway infections are predominantly viral in origin, accounting for the vast majority of common colds, pharyngitis episodes, and acute laryngitis cases. Common viral agents include rhinoviruses, coronaviruses, adenoviruses, influenza viruses, and parainfluenza viruses. Bacterial pathogens, while less common, are responsible for more severe or complicated conditions such as acute bacterial rhinosinusitis, streptococcal pharyngitis (strep throat), and epiglottitis, with Streptococcus pyogenes and Streptococcus pneumoniae being frequent culprits.

Pathophysiologically, infection begins when a pathogen adheres to the respiratory epithelium, penetrating mucosal cells and triggering local immune cascades. Chemical mediators such as bradykinin and histamine are released, causing local vasodilation, plasma extravasation, and glandular hypersecretion. This results in mucosal edema, nasal congestion, and increased mucus production. Local tissue damage impairs ciliary function, predisposing the patient to secondary bacterial colonization if viral clearance is delayed.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

The clinical presentation of upper airway infections varies according to the specific anatomical site involved, but shared systemic and local symptoms frequently overlap. Initial complaints often include scratchy throat, nasal obstruction, rhinorrhea, sneezing, low-grade fever, malaise, and myalgia.

  • Common Cold (Nasopharyngitis): Characterized by nasal discharge, sneezing, and nasal obstruction with minimal systemic symptoms.
  • Pharyngitis: Presents with severe sore throat, painful swallowing (odynophagia), and visible tonsillar exudates or erythema, particularly when streptococcal bacteria are involved.
  • Laryngitis: Marked by hoarseness, voice loss, and a tickling cough resulting from vocal cord inflammation.
  • Epiglottitis: A medical emergency presenting with the classic triad of drooling, dysphagia, and distress, accompanied by stridor.

Thorough clinical assessment requires inspecting the oropharynx, palpating cervical lymph nodes, auscultating breath sounds to ensure the lower airway remains clear, and checking oxygen saturation.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Most upper airway infections are diagnosed clinically based on history and physical examination alone, reserving diagnostic tests for suspected bacterial etiologies or during public health outbreaks.

  • Throat Swab / Rapid Antigen Detection Test (RADT): Utilized to identify group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus in patients presenting with pharyngitis to guide appropriate antibiotic therapy.
  • Throat Culture: Considered the gold standard for confirming streptococcal pharyngitis when rapid tests are negative but clinical suspicion remains high.
  • Nasopharyngeal Swab / PCR: Employed during viral surveillance or for identifying specific pathogens like influenza, SARS-CoV-2, or respiratory syncytial virus.
  • Lateral Neck Radiograph: May reveal a 'thumbprint sign' in suspected cases of epiglottitis, though physical manipulation of the airway should never be delayed for imaging if the patient is unstable.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Management strategies focus heavily on supportive care for viral infections and targeted antimicrobial therapy when bacterial agents are confirmed or strongly suspected. Antibiotics are ineffective against viral pathogens and should be withheld to prevent antimicrobial resistance.

Analgesics and antipyretics such as paracetamol or ibuprofen help manage fever, sore throat, and generalized body aches. Topical decongestants (e.g., xylometazoline) provide rapid nasal clearance but must be limited to three to five days to avoid rhinitis medicamentosa (rebound congestion). Oral antihistamines and saline nasal irrigations assist in clearing secretions and reducing mucosal irritation. When streptococcal pharyngitis is confirmed, a full course of penicillin or amoxicillin is standard practice unless the patient has a documented allergy, in which case macrolides serve as alternatives.

Nursing Care Plan and Airway Management

Nursing care for clients with upper airway infections centers on maintaining a patent airway, promoting comfort, supporting hydration, and monitoring for signs of respiratory deterioration.

  • Airway Clearance: Encourage effective coughing, provide humidified air to loosen secretions, and maintain the patient in a semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's position to maximize chest expansion and reduce upper airway edema.
  • Fluid Balance: Promote adequate oral fluid intake to thin respiratory secretions, unless contraindicated by underlying cardiac or renal conditions.
  • Comfort Measures: Offer warm saline gargles for pharyngeal discomfort, administer prescribed analgesics on schedule, and apply cool compresses to the forehead for fever management.
  • Monitoring: Regularly assess respiratory rate, depth, work of breathing, and oxygen saturation. Maintain emergency equipment, such as suction apparatus and bag-valve-mask devices, readily available at the bedside for high-risk clients.

Potential Complications and Patient Education

While most upper airway infections are self-limiting, untreated or severe infections can lead to local extension and systemic complications. Local complications include acute otitis media, sinusitis, peritonsillar abscess (quinsy), and cervical lymphadenitis. Rare but serious systemic sequelae of untreated streptococcal infections include acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

Comprehensive patient education should emphasize finishing the entire prescribed course of antibiotics even if symptoms improve, practicing rigorous hand hygiene to prevent transmission, and avoiding crowded spaces during peak viral seasons. Nurses must instruct patients on warning signs that necessitate immediate medical re-evaluation, such as severe difficulty breathing, high persistent fever, inability to swallow saliva, or worsening neck swelling.
