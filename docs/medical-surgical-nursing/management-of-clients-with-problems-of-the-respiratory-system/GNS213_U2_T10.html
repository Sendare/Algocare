Clinical Manifestations of Respiratory Disorders

Introduction to Respiratory Symptoms and Pathophysiology

Respiratory disorders disrupt the primary function of the lungs: gas exchange. When oxygen intake fails or carbon dioxide removal slows, the body triggers warning signals. Nurses must connect these clinical manifestations to underlying physiological changes in the airways, alveoli, or pulmonary circulation. Symptoms rarely occur in isolation, making a systematic assessment crucial for effective nursing care.

Assessment of Dyspnea and Breathing Difficulties

Dyspnea, or shortness of breath, is a subjective feeling of difficult or uncomfortable breathing. Patients often describe it as air hunger, tightness in the chest, or suffocation. Nurses evaluate dyspnea by assessing its onset, duration, and triggering factors, such as exertion or lying flat.

  • Orthopnea: Shortness of breath that happens when lying flat, often relieved by sitting up or using extra pillows.
  • Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (PND): Sudden severe shortness of breath that wakes a sleeping patient, usually caused by fluid overload in heart failure.
  • Tachypnea: Rapid, shallow breathing rate exceeding twenty breaths per minute in adults.
  • Bradypnea: Abnormally slow breathing rate falling below twelve breaths per minute.

Evaluation of Cough and Sputum Production

A cough is a protective reflex that clears the airways of irritants, mucus, and foreign bodies. When evaluating a cough, note whether it is dry and hacking or moist and productive.

Sputum characteristics provide vital diagnostic clues:

  • Clear or white: Often seen in viral infections, bronchitis, or early asthma.
  • Yellow or green: Indicates the presence of white blood cells fighting an infection, such as bacterial pneumonia.
  • Rust-colored: Typically associated with pneumococcal pneumonia.
  • Pink and frothy: A classic sign of acute pulmonary edema.

Hemoptysis and Chest Pain

Hemoptysis involves coughing up blood from the respiratory tract. It can range from mild blood-tinged sputum to massive, life-threatening hemorrhage. Nurses must differentiate hemoptysis from bleeding originating in the stomach, known as hematemesis.

Chest pain associated with respiratory conditions usually stems from pleural irritation, as lung tissue itself lacks pain receptors. Pleuritic chest pain is sharp, stabbing, and worsens significantly during deep breathing or coughing.

Cyanosis and Indicators of Impaired Gas Exchange

Cyanosis is a bluish discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes caused by an excess of deoxygenated hemoglobin in the blood. It appears most noticeably in areas with thin skin, such as the lips, nail beds, and earlobes. Nurses recognize that cyanosis is a late and sometimes unreliable sign of hypoxia, especially in patients with severe anemia, because a certain amount of hemoglobin must be present to show the blue color. Pulse oximetry provides a much earlier and more objective measurement of oxygen saturation.

Auscultatory Findings and Abnormal Breath Sounds

Listening to the lungs with a stethoscope reveals normal vesicular breath sounds as well as abnormal adventitious sounds. Identifying these sounds helps pinpoint airway narrowing, fluid accumulation, or inflammation.

  • Crackles (Rales): High-pitched popping sounds heard during inspiration, caused by fluid in the alveoli or the sudden opening of collapsed airways.
  • Wheezes: Continuous musical or whistling sounds produced by air rushing through narrowed airways, common in asthma.
  • Rhonchi: Low-pitched, snoring sounds indicating secretions in large airways.
  • Pleural friction rub: A grating, leather-like sound caused by inflamed pleural surfaces rubbing together.

Systemic and Chronic Manifestations

Long-standing respiratory diseases affect more than just the lungs; they create systemic changes throughout the body. Chronic hypoxemia prompts the bone marrow to produce more red blood cells, leading to secondary polycythemia. Over time, chronic respiratory strain can cause clubbing of the fingers, characterized by a softening of the nail bed and an increased angle of the nail plate. Patients with advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease often experience significant weight loss and muscle wasting due to the high energy cost of breathing and systemic inflammation.

Documentation and Nursing Considerations

Accurate documentation of respiratory findings ensures continuity of care and quick recognition of patient deterioration. Nurses must record the exact character of respirations, sputum appearance, and the effectiveness of interventions. When a patient exhibits sudden severe dyspnea, nurses must prioritize maintaining a patent airway, administering prescribed oxygen, and placing the patient in an upright position to ease breathing before notifying the physician.
