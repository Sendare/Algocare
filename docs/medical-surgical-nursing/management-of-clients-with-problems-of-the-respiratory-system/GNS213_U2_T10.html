Clinical Manifestations of Respiratory Disorders

Introduction to Respiratory Symptoms and Pathophysiology

Respiratory disorders disrupt the primary physiological function of the lungs: gas exchange. When airways, alveoli, or the pulmonary vasculature become obstructed, inflamed, or scarred, clinical manifestations appear as the body attempts to maintain homeostasis. Understanding these signs requires linking the patient's immediate complaint to underlying pathophysiological changes, such as bronchoconstriction, alveolar collapse, or pulmonary congestion.

  • Hypoxia: A deficiency in tissue oxygenation resulting from impaired systemic or local gas exchange.
  • Hypercapnia: An elevated arterial carbon dioxide tension caused by alveolar hypoventilation.
  • V/Q Mismatch: An imbalance between ventilation and perfusion within the pulmonary capillary bed.

In busy Nigerian clinical settings, patients often present late in the disease trajectory due to financial constraints or reliance on initial self-medication. Consequently, respiratory symptoms observed at presentation are frequently advanced, merging acute distress with chronic systemic deterioration.

Assessment of Dyspnea and Breathing Difficulties

Dyspnea, or subjective shortness of breath, is one of the most distressing symptoms a patient can experience. Because perception varies widely, nurses must evaluate both the qualitative description given by the patient and objective physical markers of respiratory distress.

Observe the patient's work of breathing. The recruitment of accessory muscles in the neck and shoulders, nasal flaring, and intercostal retractions indicate significant respiratory effort. Altered respiratory patterns also provide vital diagnostic clues:

  • Tachypnea: A persistent respiratory rate above twenty breaths per minute, often signaling early compensation for metabolic acidosis or hypoxemia.
  • Orthopnea: Difficulty breathing that worsens when lying flat, classically associated with left ventricular failure or severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.
  • Cheyne-Stokes Respiration: Cycles of gradual hyperpnea alternating with periods of apnea, frequently seen in end-stage cardiac or neurological conditions.

Quantify dyspnea using standardized scales such as the modified Borg scale or visual analogue scales to track clinical trajectory over time.

Evaluation of Cough and Sputum Production

Cough is a vital protective reflex designed to clear the tracheobronchial tree of irritants, mucus, and foreign bodies. However, a persistent cough loses its protective utility and becomes a debilitating sign of pathology. When assessing a cough, determine whether it is acute or chronic, dry and hacking, or productive.

Sputum characteristics offer direct insight into the underlying etiology. The volume, consistency, color, and odor must be documented during every shift:

  • Mucoid: Clear, white, or gray sputum typically associated with chronic bronchitis or viral irritation.
  • Purulent: Yellow or green sputum indicating the presence of white blood cells and bacterial infection.
  • Rust-colored: Sputum mixed with blood, classic for pneumococcal pneumonia.
  • Pink and frothy: Sputum tinged with serum, characteristic of acute pulmonary edema.

Always ask patients if coughing is worse at specific times of day, such as early morning in bronchiectasis or nocturnal coughing linked to bronchial asthma.

Hemoptysis and Chest Pain

The appearance of blood in the sputum, known as hemoptysis, always demands immediate investigation. It ranges from mild blood-streaking caused by mucosal irritation to massive hemoptysis that poses an immediate threat to airway patency and life. The clinician must differentiate true hemoptysis—which is bright red, frothy, and alkaline—from hematemesis, which originates in the gastrointestinal tract, appears dark, and is mixed with food particles. Common pulmonary sources include tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, and pulmonary embolism.

Pulmonary parenchyma lacks pain receptors; therefore, chest pain associated with respiratory disorders usually arises from inflammation of the parietal pleura. Pleuritic chest pain is sharp, stabbing, and intensely aggravated by deep inspiration, coughing, or sneezing. Patients naturally splint the affected side by taking shallow breaths, which unfortunately worsens atelectasis and impairs alveolar ventilation.

Cyanosis and Gas Exchange Indicators

Cyanosis is a bluish discoloration of the skin and mucous membranes caused by an excess of deoxygenated hemoglobin in the blood vessels. It is a late and sometimes unreliable indicator of hypoxia. Assessment requires looking at areas with thin skin and rich capillary beds, such as the lips, earlobes, and nail beds.

  • Central cyanosis: Involves the tongue and oral mucosa, indicating systemic arterial desaturation from cardiac shunts or severe pulmonary dysfunction.
  • Peripheral cyanosis: Confined to the extremities, caused by vasoconstriction or sluggish blood flow due to cold exposure or heart failure.

Anemia can mask cyanosis because a patient requires at least 5 g/dL of unoxygenated hemoglobin to exhibit the blue hue, meaning severely anemic patients may suffer profound tissue hypoxia without ever turning blue. Pulse oximetry and arterial blood gas analysis provide precise quantification of oxygenation status that visual inspection alone cannot achieve.

Auscultatory Findings and Abnormal Breath Sounds

Auscultation is an indispensable bedside skill that translates airflow dynamics into audible clues. Normal breath sounds include vesicular sounds heard over peripheral lung fields, bronchovesicular sounds over major bronchi, and bronchial sounds over the trachea.

When pathology alters airway caliber or lung density, adventitious sounds emerge:

  • Crackles (Rales): Discontinuous, popping sounds produced by the sudden opening of collapsed small airways and alveoli, common in pulmonary edema or pneumonia.
  • Wheezes: Continuous, musical, high-pitched whistling sounds created by air rushing through narrowed, bronchoconstricted airways, typical of asthma.
  • Rhonchi: Low-pitched, snoring sounds indicating secretions within large airways that often clear after coughing.
  • Pleural Friction Rub: A grating, creaking sound caused by inflamed pleural surfaces rubbing together during respiration.

Systemic and Chronic Manifestations

Long-standing respiratory diseases extend far beyond the pulmonary system, producing systemic adaptations and complications. Chronic hypoxia stimulates the bone marrow to produce excess red blood cells, resulting in secondary polycythemia to enhance oxygen-carrying capacity, which unfortunately increases blood viscosity.

Structural changes in the digits, known as clubbing, develop over months or years in chronic hypoxemic conditions such as bronchiectasis, lung cancer, or cystic fibrosis. The nail bed softens and the angle of the nail increases past 180 degrees.

Furthermore, chronic pulmonary hypertension forces the right ventricle to pump against persistently high resistance in the pulmonary vasculature. This leads to right ventricular hypertrophy and eventual right-sided heart failure, clinically termed cor pulmonale, characterized by peripheral pitting edema, jugular venous distension, and an enlarged, tender liver.

Documentation and Nursing Considerations

Accurate documentation of respiratory manifestations bridges the gap between assessment and medical intervention. Nurses must record objective data—such as respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, exact characteristics of sputum, and auscultatory findings—using precise medical terminology rather than vague descriptions like 'patient is breathing funny.'

Clinical vigilance is required to detect early signs of respiratory failure, including restlessness, confusion, and lethargy, which indicate cerebral hypoxia and hypercapnia before overt cyanosis appears. In environments with intermittent electricity or unstable oxygen supplies, nurses play a critical role by performing frequent manual assessments, ensuring emergency suction is functional, and positioning patients upright to optimize lung expansion.
