Under-Water Seal Drainage

Introduction and Principles of Under-Water Seal Drainage

An **under-water seal drainage (UWSD)** system is a specialized medical apparatus used to evacuate air, fluid, or blood from the pleural cavity and re-establish normal negative intrapleural pressure. Normally, the pressure within the chest cavity is negative relative to atmospheric pressure, which keeps the lungs expanded against the chest wall. When air or fluid enters the pleural space due to trauma, surgery, or disease, this negative pressure is lost, causing the lung to collapse. The fundamental principle relies on a one-way valve mechanism. The distal end of the chest tube is submerged under approximately 2 centimeters of sterile water in a collection chamber. This water seal allows air and fluid to exit the patient's pleural space during expiration or forced exhalation, but prevents atmospheric air from being sucked back into the chest during inspiration. Gravity drainage also plays a vital role. The drainage system must always remain positioned below the level of the patient's chest to facilitate the downward flow of fluids. If the unit is raised above the patient, gravity will cause the fluid in the tubing and chamber to drain backward into the pleural space, potentially introducing pathogens or reinfecting the chest cavity.

Indications for Chest Tube Insertion

Chest tubes connected to a drainage system are inserted to restore cardiopulmonary stability across various acute medical and surgical conditions. - **Pneumothorax**: Accumulation of air in the pleural space, which can be spontaneous or traumatic, collapsing the lung. - **Hemothorax**: Presence of blood in the pleural cavity, commonly resulting from blunt or penetrating chest trauma, or post-thoracotomy bleeding. - **Pleural Effusion**: Abnormal collection of serous fluid, often secondary to heart failure, pneumonia, or malignancy, requiring drainage to ease breathing. - **Empyema**: Pus within the pleural space, typically arising from a neglected or complicated bacterial pneumonia. - **Post-operative Thoracic Surgery**: Routine placement following procedures like lobectomy or coronary artery bypass grafting to clear residual air and blood.

Types of Chest Drainage Systems

Modern thoracic drainage relies on configurations ranging from traditional glass bottles to integrated disposable plastic units. - **Traditional One-Bottle System**: Combines collection and water seal functions in a single chamber. As fluid drains, the water level rises, increasing resistance to air outflow. - **Two-Bottle System**: Separates the collection chamber from the water seal bottle. This maintains a constant water seal level regardless of how much fluid drains from the patient. - **Three-Bottle System**: Adds a third bottle dedicated to suction control, regulating the amount of negative pressure applied to the system through a water depth column. - **Disposable Three-Chamber Units**: Plastic, all-in-one commercial units that replicate the three-bottle setup, featuring a collection chamber, water seal chamber, and a dry or wet suction control chamber.

Pre-Procedural and Procedural Nursing Responsibilities

Preparing a patient for chest tube insertion requires thorough clinical organization and patient reassurance. Anxiety is high due to anticipated pain and respiratory distress. Verify that informed consent has been obtained by the medical team. Gather necessary equipment, including the chosen drainage system, sterile water to fill chambers, local anesthetic, scalpel, chest tube of appropriate French size, heavy silk sutures, and an occlusive dressing. Position the patient properly based on the insertion site, usually semi-Fowler's or supine with the arm raised overhead to expose the intercostal spaces. During the procedure, provide continuous vital sign monitoring, watch for signs of vasovagal response or hypoxia, and offer emotional reassurance. Immediately after insertion, connect the chest tube to the drainage system, secure all tubing connections with waterproof tape, apply a petroleum-impregnated gauze and sterile dressing over the insertion site, and arrange for an immediate chest X-ray to confirm tube placement.

Ongoing Assessment and Monitoring of the Patient

Continuous nursing surveillance prevents life-threatening complications. Assess respiratory rate, depth, effort, and oxygen saturation regularly. Auscultate lung sounds bilaterally, noting if breath sounds improve or remain diminished. Inspect the chest tube insertion site every shift for subcutaneous emphysema, swelling, redness, or purulent drainage. Palpate around the dressing; a crackling sensation beneath the skin indicates air leaking into the subcutaneous tissue, requiring prompt reporting. Monitor vital signs for sudden tachycardia, tachypnea, or hypotension, which may signal internal hemorrhage or a developing tension pneumothorax.

Management and Maintenance of the Drainage System

Proper maintenance ensures the drainage system functions without interruption. Keep the entire tubing free of kinks, loops, or dependent coils that can trap fluid and obstruct drainage. Never lift the drainage unit above the patient's chest level. Observe the water seal chamber for **tidaling**—the normal rise and fall of the water level with the patient's respiratory cycle (rising during inspiration, falling during expiration). Absence of tidaling may indicate that the lung has fully re-expanded or that the tubing is obstructed. Look for bubbling in the water seal chamber; intermittent bubbling during expiration is normal when air is evacuating, but continuous bubbling indicates an active air leak in the system. Measure and document drainage output at specified intervals, marking the fluid level directly on the container. Never routinely clamp a chest tube, as clamping can rapidly cause a tension pneumothorax by trapping air inside the pleural space.

Troubleshooting and Emergency Interventions

Prompt action during equipment malfunctions prevents clinical deterioration. | Situation | Probable Cause | Immediate Nursing Action | |---|---|---| | Tube disconnects from unit | Break in sterility or traction | Submerge distal end briefly in sterile water to maintain seal; insert sterile connector | | Tube pulls out of chest | Accidental dislodgement | Apply sterile petroleum gauze and pressure dressing taped on three sides; notify doctor | | Continuous bubbling in water seal | Air leak in system or patient | Check all tubing connections; briefly clamp tubing near chest to locate leak source | | Cessation of tidaling | Lung re-expansion or obstruction | Assess patient breath sounds; check for kinks or dependent loops in tubing | If the drainage unit accidentally breaks, quickly submerge the open end of the chest tubing in sterile water to maintain the water seal while setting up a replacement system.

Chest Tube Removal and Post-Removal Care

Chest tubes are removed when the lung has fully re-expanded, air leakage has ceased, and fluid drainage has decreased to minimal amounts (typically under 100 to 200 mL per 24 hours depending on institutional policy). Gather supplies: suture removal kit, sterile dressings, petroleum gauze, adhesive tape, and trash bag. Pre-medicate the patient for pain approximately 30 to 45 minutes before the procedure, as removal can cause transient discomfort. Explain the procedure and instruct the patient on the Valsalva maneuver (taking a deep breath, holding it, and bearing down) during removal, which increases thoracic pressure and prevents air from entering the pleural space as the tube is withdrawn. The clinician cuts the retention sutures, withdraws the tube rapidly at the end of a deep inspiration while the patient bears down, and immediately applies an airtight petroleum gauze dressing secured with heavy tape. Monitor respiratory status, oxygen saturation, and lung sounds frequently post-removal, and obtain a follow-up chest X-ray within a few hours to ensure the lung remains expanded.
