Under-Water Seal Drainage

Principles of Under-Water Seal Drainage

An under-water seal drainage system is used to remove air, blood, or fluid from the pleural space and restore normal negative pressure in the chest. Normally, the pressure inside the chest cavity is negative compared to the outside atmosphere, allowing the lungs to expand easily. When air or fluid enters the pleural space due to trauma, surgery, or disease, this negative pressure is lost, causing the lung to collapse.

The system relies on a one-way valve mechanism, usually created by a column of water in a chamber. This water seal allows air and fluid to escape from the patient's chest during exhalation but prevents outside air or fluid from flowing back in during inhalation. Gravity also helps drain heavier fluids like blood or pus downward into the collection collection chambers below.

Indications for Chest Tube Insertion

Chest tubes are inserted when the normal seal of the pleural cavity is breached, compromising respiratory function. Common reasons include:

  • Pneumothorax: Accumulation of air in the pleural space, causing lung collapse.
  • Hemothorax: Accumulation of blood in the pleural space, often due to trauma or surgery.
  • Pleural Effusion: Build-up of excess serous fluid in the chest cavity, compressing lung tissue.
  • Empyema: Presence of infected pus within the pleural cavity requiring drainage.
  • Chylothorax: Leakage of lymphatic fluid into the pleural space.

Types of Chest Drainage Systems

Modern chest drainage units typically use a three-chamber system design, consolidating traditional separate glass bottles into one plastic unit:

  1. Collection Chamber: Receives and measures fluid and air draining directly from the patient's chest. It has graduated columns to monitor output hourly or daily.
  2. Water Seal Chamber: Contains sterile water that acts as a one-way valve. As air passes through, bubbling may be observed, and the water level fluctuates with the patient's breathing, known as tidaling.
  3. Suction Control Chamber: Regulates the amount of negative suction applied to the system. This can be regulated by sterile water height or a built-in dry suction regulator dial.

Pre-Procedural and Procedural Nursing Responsibilities

Preparing a patient for chest tube insertion requires both physical and emotional support. Nurses must explain the procedure simply, obtain informed consent if policy permits, and gather necessary supplies including the drainage system, sterile water, local anesthetic, suture kit, and dressing materials.

During the insertion, position the patient semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's to expand the lung and allow safe access to the insertion site. Assist the physician by opening sterile packs, monitoring vital signs closely, and offering reassurance. Immediately after insertion, connect the chest tube to the drainage system, secure all tubing connections with tape or cable ties to prevent accidental dislodgement, and initiate prescribed wall suction.

Ongoing Assessment and Monitoring

Regular nursing assessments ensure the chest drainage system functions correctly and the patient recovers safely. Check vital signs, oxygen saturation, and respiratory effort frequently. Assess the insertion site for bleeding, swelling, subcutaneous emphysema (a crackling sensation under the skin), and secure dressings.

Monitor the drainage unit for tidaling—the rise and fall of water in the water seal chamber with breathing. Tidaling confirms that the tube remains patent and connected to the pleural space. Absence of tidaling may indicate that the lung has fully re-expanded or that the tubing is kinked or blocked. Observe drainage color and volume, reporting any sudden surges of bright red blood immediately.

Management and Maintenance of the Drainage System

Proper day-to-day management prevents complications and keeps the drainage system functioning. Always keep the drainage unit below the level of the patient's chest at all times to prevent fluid from flowing backward into the pleural space. Ensure tubing is coiled loosely on the bed or floor; avoid dependent loops that trap fluid and obstruct drainage.

Never clamp a chest tube routinely during patient transport or ambulation unless specifically ordered or checking for an air leak, as clamping can quickly cause a tension pneumothorax. Monitor water levels in the water seal and suction chambers, adding sterile water as needed to maintain prescribed levels.

Troubleshooting and Emergency Interventions

Nurses must act quickly when complications arise with a chest drainage system. Common issues include:

  • Accidental Disconnection: If the tube pulls apart from the unit, submerge the end of the tube briefly in sterile water to maintain a temporary water seal, then insert a new connector.
  • Chest Tube Dislodgement: If the tube pulls completely out of the chest, cover the wound immediately with a sterile petroleum gauze dressing taped on three sides to allow air escape while preventing large air entry.
  • Continuous Bubbling: Vigorous bubbling in the water seal chamber usually indicates an air leak in the system. Check connections from the chest insertion site down to the unit.

Chest Tube Removal and Post-Removal Care

Chest tubes are removed when the lung has fully re-expanded, air leak has ceased (no bubbling), and fluid drainage decreases below acceptable minimal thresholds (typically less than 100-200 mL per 24 hours). The procedure is performed by a physician or advanced practice nurse.

Explain the procedure to the patient and instruct them to take a deep breath, hold it, and bear down (Valsalva maneuver) during removal to prevent air from entering the pleural space. The clinician pulls the tube swiftly, and an airtight sterile occlusive dressing is applied immediately over the site. Monitor vital signs and respiratory status closely post-removal, watching for signs of recurrent pneumothorax such as shortness of breath or diminished breath sounds.
