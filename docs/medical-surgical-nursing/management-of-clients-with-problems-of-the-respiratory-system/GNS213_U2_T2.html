Assessment of Clients with Respiratory Disorders

Introduction to Respiratory Assessment

Respiratory assessment is a foundational clinical skill required by nurses in Nigeria and globally to evaluate the adequacy of a client's oxygenation and ventilation. A systematic approach ensures that acute respiratory distress, chronic conditions like asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and subtle changes in tissue perfusion are quickly identified. The process begins even before touching the patient, utilizing general observation to gauge respiratory effort, skin color, and level of consciousness. As nursing students, understanding the anatomy and physiology of the upper and lower respiratory tracts is crucial for interpreting clinical findings accurately. Nurses must recognize that respiratory health is closely linked with cardiovascular status, acid-base balance, and overall systemic well-being. By mastering systematic respiratory assessment, nurses can prioritize interventions, administer oxygen or prescribed medications safely, and evaluate the effectiveness of nursing care plans. This introduction sets the stage for a comprehensive evaluation encompassing history taking, physical examination techniques, psychosocial impacts, and diagnostic tests.

Health History

Taking a thorough health history is an essential component of respiratory assessment, providing critical context for current symptoms. The nurse must inquire about the chief complaint, such as shortness of breath (dyspnoea), cough, sputum production, chest pain, or wheezing. Details regarding the onset, duration, precipitating factors, and alleviating factors of these symptoms help differentiate between acute infections, cardiac events, and chronic respiratory disorders. A comprehensive smoking history—measured in pack-years—is vital, along with inquiries regarding occupational exposures to dust, chemicals, or smoke from biomass fuels commonly encountered in various Nigerian settings. The nurse should also review the client's past medical history for asthma, allergies, tuberculosis (TB), or frequent respiratory infections, as well as family history of genetic disorders like cystic fibrosis or alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency. Additionally, evaluating medication history, travel history, and immunization status against influenza and pneumococcal pneumonia completes a holistic health profile, guiding targeted nursing interventions.

Physical Assessment

Physical assessment of the respiratory system follows the traditional sequence of inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. During inspection, the nurse observes the rate, rhythm, and depth of respirations, alongside chest symmetry, use of accessory muscles, and nail bed color for cyanosis. Palpation allows the nurse to assess chest expansion, tenderness, and tactile fremitus, which is the vibration felt on the chest wall during speech. Percussion helps determine whether underlying lung tissue is air-filled, fluid-filled, or solid, yielding notes such as resonance, hyperresonance, or dullness. Auscultation, performed using a stethoscope, is critical for listening to breath sounds—such as vesicular, bronchovesicular, and bronchial sounds—as well as adventitious sounds like crackles, wheezes, rhonchi, and pleural friction rubs. Each finding provides specific clues regarding airway patency, lung compliance, and fluid accumulation within the pleural space or alveoli, enabling nurses to identify abnormalities promptly and communicate findings effectively to the healthcare team.

Psychosocial Assessment

Respiratory disorders frequently impose a heavy psychosocial burden on clients and their families, making psychosocial assessment an integral part of holistic care. Conditions like asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and tuberculosis often cause chronic dyspnoea, leading to severe anxiety, panic, and depression. Nurses must evaluate how respiratory symptoms limit the client's ability to perform activities of daily living, maintain employment, or engage in social interactions. Fear of suffocation or sudden exacerbations can cause persistent psychological distress that actually worsens physiological symptoms through hyperventilation and muscle tension. Furthermore, social stigma associated with chronic cough or infectious diseases like tuberculosis can cause isolation and reluctance to seek timely healthcare. Assessing coping mechanisms, family support systems, and mental health status allows the nurse to provide empathetic counseling, encourage participation in pulmonary rehabilitation, and refer clients to psychological support services when necessary.

Diagnostic Procedures

Diagnostic procedures validate physical findings and pinpoint the etiology of respiratory dysfunctions. Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) analysis is a vital test that measures blood pH, partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), and bicarbonate levels to evaluate gas exchange and acid-base balance. Pulse oximetry provides a continuous, non-invasive estimation of arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2). Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs) measure lung volumes, capacities, and flow rates, helping to differentiate between obstructive and restrictive lung diseases. Sputum cultures and Gram stains identify infectious pathogens, which is especially important in diagnosing bacterial pneumonia or drug-resistant tuberculosis. Imaging studies, including chest radiography (X-ray) and computed tomography (CT) scans, visualize structural abnormalities, consolidation, masses, or pleural effusions. Finally, bronchoscopy allows direct visualization of the tracheobronchial tree and retrieval of tissue biopsies or foreign bodies. Nurses play a crucial role in preparing clients, explaining procedures, and monitoring for post-procedural complications.

Documentation and Nursing Implications

Accurate documentation and understanding nursing implications are the final, critical steps in the respiratory assessment process. Nurses must record all subjective data (history, symptoms) and objective data (vital signs, breath sounds, ABG results, SpO2 levels) clearly, concisely, and objectively in the client's medical record using professional terminology. Timely documentation ensures seamless interprofessional communication, allowing physicians and other healthcare team members to track clinical trends and evaluate treatment efficacy. Nursing implications require the nurse to interpret assessment data rapidly and anticipate potential complications, such as acute respiratory failure, hypoxemia, or respiratory acidosis. Based on these findings, the nurse must initiate independent nursing actions—such as positioning the client in high Fowler's, encouraging deep breathing and coughing exercises, administering humidified oxygen as prescribed, and educating the client on inhaler techniques. Continuous reassessment ensures patient safety and promotes optimal recovery outcomes.
