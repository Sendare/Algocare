Assessment of Clients with Respiratory Disorders

Introduction and Health History

Respiratory assessment begins long before touching the patient. Evaluating a client with potential breathing difficulties requires a systematic inquiry into their subjective experiences. Begin by establishing rapport, which is vital when a client is dyspneic and naturally anxious. Gathering a comprehensive health history uncovers clues that physical examination alone might miss. Focus on several key areas: - **Chief Complaint**: The primary reason for seeking care, often shortness of breath (**dyspnea**), cough, or chest pain. - **History of Present Illness**: Onset, duration, severity, and factors that worsen or relieve the symptoms. - **Past Medical History**: Previous respiratory infections, asthma, chronic bronchitis, or occupational exposures to dust and fumes. - **Smoking History**: Pack-years calculation, including active and secondhand exposure. In many Nigerian healthcare settings, indoor air pollution from biomass fuels like firewood used for cooking is a significant chronic exposure worth exploring during the social history.

Physical Examination Techniques: Inspection and Palpation

Physical examination follows the standard clinical sequence of inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation. Inspection starts at the bedside. Observe the client's breathing rate, rhythm, depth, and effort. Note the use of **accessory muscles** like the sternocleidomastoid during inspiration, which signals respiratory distress. Look at skin color for **cyanosis**, particularly around the lips and nail beds, indicating hypoxemia. Palpation allows the examiner to feel for abnormalities of the chest wall. Assess for tenderness, masses, and thoracic expansion symmetry. Place hands on the posterior chest wall and ask the patient to say 'ninety-nine' to evaluate **tactile fremitus**—vibrations produced by vocal cords. Increased fremitus suggests consolidation of lung tissue, as seen in lobar pneumonia, whereas decreased fremitus occurs when air or fluid blocks the vibrations, as in pleural effusion or pneumothorax.

Percussion and Auscultation

Percussion helps determine whether underlying tissues are air-filled, fluid-filled, or solid. Strike the distal interphalangeal joint of the non-coupled middle finger sharply with the tip of the opposite middle finger. - **Resonance**: Low-pitched, hollow sound heard over normal lung tissue. - **Hyperresonance**: Louder, lower-pitched booming sound indicating excess air, found in emphysema or pneumothorax. - **Dullness**: Thud-like sound heard over dense areas such as consolidated pneumonia, tumors, or pleural effusion. Auscultation requires a stethoscope diaphragm placed firmly against the bare skin. Listen systematically from the apices to the bases. Normal breath sounds include vesicular, bronchovesicular, and bronchial sounds. **Adventitious sounds** are abnormal additions that indicate pathology: 1. **Crackles (Rales)**: Discontinuous, popping sounds heard during inspiration, caused by fluid in the alveoli or opening of collapsed airways. 2. **Wheezes**: Continuous, musical, high-pitched whistling sounds heard mainly on expiration, resulting from narrowed airways in asthma or bronchitis. 3. **Stridor**: Harsh, high-pitched crowing sound caused by upper airway obstruction, requiring immediate intervention.

Diagnostic and Laboratory Investigations

Physical findings are confirmed and quantified through diagnostic modalities. **Pulse oximetry** provides a non-invasive estimate of arterial oxygen saturation ($SpO_2$), though readings can be affected by poor peripheral circulation, cold extremities, or dark nail polish. Arterial Blood Gases (**ABGs**) offer precise measurement of oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base balance. Venous blood samples cannot replace ABGs when assessing respiratory failure. Chest radiography remains the cornerstone imaging modality to visualize lung fields, cardiac silhouette, and bony thorax. Sputum microscopy and culture identify specific infectious agents, which guides targeted antimicrobial therapy. Peak flow meters are routinely used by clients with asthma to monitor daily expiratory airflow changes at home.

Psychosocial Impact and Nursing Documentation

Respiratory distress triggers profound fear and panic. Difficulty breathing creates an immediate sense of suffocation, activating the sympathetic nervous system and worsening the physiological crisis. Assess the client's anxiety level, coping mechanisms, and available support systems. Providing a calm environment and clear, concise explanations during acute episodes reduces psychological distress. Accurate documentation forms the legal and clinical backbone of quality nursing care. Record objective assessment data precisely: respiratory rate, depth, exact locations of adventitious sounds, and exact $SpO_2$ values on room air or supplemental oxygen. Document the client's subjective descriptions using their own words when possible. Timely charting ensures safe communication across the multidisciplinary healthcare team.
