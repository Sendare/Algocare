Assessment of Clients with Respiratory Disorders

Introduction to Respiratory Assessment

Assessing the respiratory system requires a systematic approach to evaluate how well a client is exchanging oxygen and carbon dioxide. Nurses begin this process the moment they first see the client, observing breathing patterns, skin color, and overall comfort before even touching them. A complete respiratory evaluation combines a thorough health history with a focused physical exam, giving vital clues about tissue perfusion and airway patency. Recognizing subtle changes in respiratory status early can prevent acute respiratory distress and save lives.

Health History and Symptom Analysis

Taking a detailed health history helps uncover risk factors and patterns related to breathing problems. Nurses ask about the onset, duration, and triggers of symptoms like cough, shortness of breath, and chest pain. Key areas to explore include:

  • Smoking History: Document pack-years, passive exposure, and current use of vaping products.
  • Occupational Exposure: Inhalation of dust, coal, chemicals, or asbestos in workplaces.
  • Family History: Genetic conditions such as cystic fibrosis or asthma.
  • Allergies: Reactions to pollen, dust mites, pet dander, or specific foods.

Physical Examination Techniques

The physical examination follows the classic sequence of inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation, though percussion and auscultation are flipped for the abdomen, the respiratory exam sticks strictly to traditional order.

  1. Inspection: Look for chest symmetry, use of accessory muscles, and nail bed color.
  2. Palpation: Feel for areas of tenderness, masses, and tactile fremitus vibrations.
  3. Percussion: Tap the chest wall to detect underlying fluid or air density.
  4. Auscultation: Listen with a stethoscope for normal breath sounds and abnormal adventitious sounds like crackles or wheezes.

Recognizing Abnormal Breath Sounds

Auscultation reveals much about what is happening inside the lungs. Normal breath sounds include vesicular, bronchovesicular, and bronchial sounds heard over different areas of the chest. Adventitious sounds are abnormal noises superimposed on normal breath sounds.

  • Crackles: Discontinuous, popping sounds often heard during inspiration due to fluid in the alveoli.
  • Wheezes: Continuous, musical high-pitched sounds caused by narrowed airways during expiration or inspiration.
  • Rhonchi: Low-pitched, snoring sounds indicating secretions in large airways.
  • Pleural Friction Rub: Grating, leather-like sound caused by inflamed pleural surfaces rubbing together.

Diagnostic Tests and Procedures

Physical exams provide surface clues, but diagnostic tests confirm exact respiratory diagnoses.

  • Pulse Oximetry: Non-invasive measurement of arterial oxygen saturation using a light sensor on a finger or earlobe.
  • Sputum Culture: Laboratory analysis of coughed-up mucus to identify infectious bacteria.
  • Chest X-Ray: Imaging to visualize lung structures, fluid accumulation, or masses.
  • Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs): Breathing maneuvers that measure lung volumes and airflow rates.

Documentation and Clinical Implications

Accurate documentation ensures continuity of care and provides a legal record of the client's respiratory status. Nurses must record exact findings, including respiratory rate, depth, effort, breath sounds, and oxygen saturation levels. Vague descriptions like 'lungs clear' are insufficient if certain lobes were never auscultated. Recognizing early signs of respiratory failure—such as restlessness, confusion, and tachypnea—allows nurses to intervene promptly before an emergency occurs.
