Obstruction of the Upper Respiratory Airway

Anatomy and Physiology of the Upper Respiratory Tract

The upper respiratory tract consists of the structures located outside the thoracic cavity: the nose, nasopharynx, oropharynx, laryngopharynx, and larynx down to the vocal cords. Air enters through the nasal passages, where it is warmed, filtered, and humidified before passing the pharynx. The epiglottis, a vital leaf-shaped flap of cartilage, acts as a protective valve during swallowing, folding posteriorly to cover the laryngeal inlet and prevent aspiration of food and fluids into the trachea. The narrowest portion of the adult upper airway is the glottic opening, whereas in infants and young children, the cricoid cartilage forms the narrowest region. Understanding these anatomical landmarks is essential when attempting endotracheal intubation or relieving an obstruction, as any structural narrowing or edema in these specific zones can rapidly lead to total airflow cessation.

Pathophysiology and Etiology of Upper Airway Obstruction

Upper airway obstruction occurs when the passage of air from the nose and mouth down to the trachea is mechanically blocked or functionally impaired. The underlying causes span several categories: - **Foreign Body Aspiration**: Accidental inhalation of food or small objects, frequently seen in children or adults with impaired swallowing reflexes. - **Soft Tissue Swelling**: Severe allergic reactions, angioedema, inhalation burns, or local infections such as epiglottitis leading to rapid mucosal edema. - **Anatomical Collapse**: Loss of pharyngeal muscle tone during deep unconsciousness, causing the tongue to fall backward against the posterior pharyngeal wall. - **Structural Masses**: Laryngeal tumors, polyps, or goiters exerting external or internal pressure on the airway. - **Trauma**: Maxillofacial fractures or laryngeal crush injuries causing displaced bone and soft tissue hematomas.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Recognizing the signs of upper airway obstruction requires keen observation, as complete occlusion can cause death within minutes. Partial obstruction typically produces noisy breathing and labored respiratory efforts. Key clinical indicators include: - **Stridor**: A high-pitched, musical, monophonic sound heard best on inspiration, indicating turbulent airflow through a narrowed upper airway. - **Retractions**: Sternal, supraclavicular, and intercostal muscle pulling as the patient attempts to draw air past the blockage. - **Paradoxical Chest Movements**: In severe distress, the chest and abdomen move in opposite directions during respiratory efforts. - **Cyanosis**: A late, ominous sign of hypoxemia turning the skin, nail beds, and lips a bluish tint. - **Voice Changes**: Hoarseness or complete inability to speak (aphonia) if laryngeal structures are involved.

Emergency Management and Immediate Interventions

When an upper airway obstruction is identified, immediate action is required to restore patency. In conscious choking victims with a foreign body, encouraging coughing or performing abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich maneuver) is indicated. For an unconscious patient, the airway must be opened immediately using the head-tilt/chin-lift maneuver, unless cervical spine injury is suspected, in which case the jaw-thrust maneuver is mandatory. Basic airway adjuncts can maintain patency once the mouth is cleared: an oropharyngeal airway is suitable for unconscious patients without a gag reflex, whereas a nasopharyngeal airway can be safely used in semi-conscious patients because it is less likely to provoke vomiting. Suctioning should be readily available to remove blood, mucus, or vomitus obstructing the view and airflow.

Medical, Surgical, and Advanced Airway Management

If basic maneuvers fail to relieve the obstruction, advanced medical and surgical interventions become necessary. Endotracheal intubation performed via direct laryngoscopy or video assistance allows a secure tube to bypass upper airway pathologies. When facial trauma, severe laryngeal edema, or massive tumors prevent oral or nasal intubation, surgical alternatives are required. An emergency cricothyroidotomy involves puncturing or incising the cricothyroid membrane to establish a temporary airway below the obstruction. For long-term upper airway blockades, an elective tracheostomy is performed in a controlled environment. Pharmacological management often accompanies these procedures, including corticosteroids to reduce laryngeal edema, epinephrine for anaphylaxis-induced swelling, and bronchodilators where lower airway spasm coexists.

Nursing Care Plan and Monitoring Priorities

Nursing care for patients with upper airway obstruction focuses on continuous respiratory monitoring, maintaining airway patency, and reducing patient anxiety. Essential nursing interventions include assessing respiratory rate, depth, and pattern every fifteen minutes during acute phases, and continuously monitoring oxygen saturation via pulse oximetry. The nurse must keep suction equipment, bag-valve-mask devices, and emergency intubation trays at the bedside at all times. Positioning the patient in a semi-Fowler's or high-Fowler's position promotes chest expansion and decreases work of breathing, provided the patient is conscious and stable. Documentation of sputum characteristics, effectiveness of cough, and response to therapeutic interventions ensures continuity of safe clinical care.
