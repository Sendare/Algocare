Obstruction of the Upper Respiratory Airway

Anatomy and Physiology of the Upper Respiratory Tract

The upper respiratory tract serves as the primary passageway for air entering and leaving the lungs, while also filtering, warming, and humidifying that air. It consists of the structures located outside the thoracic cavity.

  • Nasal Cavity: Lined with mucus membranes and tiny hairs called cilia, this space traps dust and warms incoming air before it reaches the lungs.
  • Pharynx: A muscular tube divided into the nasopharynx, oropharynx, and laryngopharynx, acting as a shared pathway for both food and air.
  • Larynx: Also known as the voice box, it houses the vocal cords and is protected by the epiglottis, a leaf-shaped flap of cartilage that folds over the trachea during swallowing to prevent aspiration.
  • Trachea: The windpipe that extends downward from the larynx, supported by C-shaped rings of cartilage that keep the airway open.

A clear understanding of this anatomical layout helps nurses quickly pinpoint where an obstruction is located when a patient struggles to breathe.

Definition and Pathophysiology of Upper Airway Obstruction

Upper airway obstruction occurs when any blockage, narrowing, or swelling physically impedes the flow of air through the nose, pharynx, larynx, or upper trachea. Because this tract is narrow by nature, even minor swelling can drastically reduce airflow.

When the airway narrows, the body must exert significantly more negative pressure during inhalation to draw air past the blockage. This increased effort creates turbulence, audible breathing sounds like stridor, and rapid fatigue of the respiratory muscles. As airflow drops, oxygen delivery to the tissues decreases, leading to rapid systemic hypoxia, carbon dioxide retention, and eventually respiratory arrest if the obstruction is not cleared immediately.

Etiology and Common Causes of Airway Obstruction

Upper airway blockages can develop suddenly or progress slowly over time. Nurses must recognize both acute and chronic triggers in clinical practice.

  • Foreign Body Aspiration: Common in young children who swallow small toys or adults who choke on food, such as poorly chewed meat.
  • Soft Tissue Swelling: Severe allergic reactions like anaphylaxis cause rapid laryngeal edema, as do thermal burns from inhaling hot smoke.
  • Infections: Conditions such as epiglottitis or croup cause severe inflammation of the upper airway structures, particularly in pediatric patients.
  • Structural Changes: Tumors, vocal cord paralysis, or the tongue falling backward against the posterior pharyngeal wall in an unconscious patient can completely seal off the airway.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Recognizing the signs of an upper airway obstruction requires quick observation and a calm, systematic bedside assessment. Patients often display classic behavioral and physical indicators of distress.

You will notice stridor, a harsh, high-pitched sound heard best during inhalation. The patient may use accessory muscles in the neck and chest, pulling the skin in around the ribs and sternum with every breath. Cyanosis around the lips and nail beds appears late, indicating dangerous oxygen deprivation. Incomplete blockages cause hoarseness or a muffled voice, while complete obstructions leave the patient unable to speak, cough, or breathe at all, often instinctively clutching at their throat.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Emergency Airway Assessment

When a patient shows signs of airway compromise, clinical assessment takes absolute priority over diagnostic testing. Wasting time sending an unstable patient for a scan can be fatal.

The initial evaluation relies on the look, listen, and feel method: look for chest movement and retractions, listen for breath sounds or air movement at the mouth, and feel for exhaled breath. If the airway is stable enough for diagnostics, a soft tissue neck X-ray may reveal swelling, such as the classic thumb sign in epiglottitis. Arterial blood gases show falling oxygen and rising carbon dioxide levels if the obstruction is severe, though these tests should never delay life-saving interventions.

Emergency Management and Immediate Interventions

An obstructed airway is a true medical emergency requiring immediate action to restore airflow before brain damage occurs from lack of oxygen.

For a conscious adult with a foreign body obstruction, perform abdominal thrusts, known as the Heimlich maneuver, to force residual air from the lungs and push the object out. If the patient becomes unconscious, lower them safely to the floor and begin cardiopulmonary resuscitation, checking the mouth for the foreign object before giving rescue breaths.

In cases of medical swelling, such as anaphylaxis, administer intramuscular epinephrine immediately. Healthcare providers may use basic airway maneuvers like the head-tilt, chin-lift or jaw-thrust to pull the tongue away from the back of the throat, inserting an oral or nasopharyngeal airway if needed.

Medical and Surgical Management

When basic maneuvers fail or when swelling threatens the airway, advanced medical and surgical treatments become necessary.

  • Endotracheal Intubation: A physician or trained nurse practitioner inserts a plastic tube through the mouth or nose into the trachea to secure a stable airway and connect the patient to a ventilator.
  • Cricothyroidotomy: An emergency surgical procedure where an incision is made through the cricothyroid membrane in the neck to bypass a severe upper airway blockage.
  • Tracheostomy: A planned surgical opening made directly into the trachea through the neck for long-term airway management.
  • Pharmacotherapy: Nebulized bronchodilators, corticosteroids, and anti-inflammatory drugs are given to reduce severe swelling caused by infections or allergic reactions.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care after an airway obstruction focuses on maintaining airway clearance, monitoring respiratory status, and educating the patient to prevent future episodes.

Monitor the patient continuously for signs of respiratory fatigue, restlessness, or stridor. Keep suction equipment, bag-valve-mask devices, and emergency airway trays at the bedside. Teach patients at risk for choking to cut their food into small pieces, chew thoroughly, and avoid talking or laughing while eating. For patients with allergies, ensure they carry an auto-injector of epinephrine at all times and know how to use it.
