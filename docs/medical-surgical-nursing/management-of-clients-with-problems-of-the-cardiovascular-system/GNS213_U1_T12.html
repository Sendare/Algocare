Pathophysiology of Cardiovascular Disorders

Hemodynamics and Normal Cardiac Function

The human heart operates as a dual-pump system driven by electrical impulses and pressure gradients. Cardiac output, the total volume of blood pumped per minute, is determined by heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Stroke volume itself depends on three foundational variables: preload, afterload, and myocardial contractility.

  • Preload: The end-diastolic volume that stretches the ventricular myocardium prior to contraction, closely tied to venous return.
  • Afterload: The systemic resistance or pressure the ventricle must overcome to eject blood into the arterial tree.
  • Contractility: The intrinsic mechanical strength of the cardiac muscle fibers, independent of preload or afterload changes.

Atherosclerosis and Coronary Artery Disease Mechanisms

Atherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease of the arterial intima, characterized by the accumulation of lipids, macrophages, and smooth muscle cells. The process begins with endothelial injury caused by factors like hypertension or smoking, leading to increased vascular permeability. Low-density lipoproteins infiltrate the subendothelial space, undergo oxidation, and are engulfed by macrophages, transforming them into lipid-laden foam cells. These collections form fatty streaks, which eventually mature into fibrous plaques with a necrotic lipid core.

When a fibrous plaque ruptures or erodes within a coronary artery, platelets aggregate rapidly over the site. This triggers acute thrombosis, obstructing myocardial blood supply and precipitating ischemia or infarction.

Pathophysiology of Heart Failure

Heart failure occurs when the myocardium can no longer pump blood efficiently enough to meet metabolic tissue demands. To compensate for declining cardiac output, the body activates neurohormonal pathways, notably the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. While these mechanisms initially sustain blood pressure and organ perfusion, chronic activation leads to detrimental ventricular remodeling, myocardial hypertrophy, and progressive cellular apoptosis.

Left-sided heart failure typically results in pulmonary congestion due to elevated backpressures in the left atrium and pulmonary veins. Right-sided heart failure causes systemic venous congestion, manifested clinically by peripheral edema, jugular venous distension, and hepatomegaly.

Mechanisms of Hypertension and Vascular Remodeling

Systemic hypertension is sustained elevation of arterial blood pressure, driven primarily by an increase in total peripheral resistance, cardiac output, or both. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch constantly monitor pressure, but in chronic hypertension, these sensors reset to a higher threshold.

Sustained high pressure forces vascular smooth muscle cells in small arteries and arterioles to undergo hypertrophy and hyperplasia. This structural thickening narrows the lumen, permanently elevating vascular resistance and locking in a self-perpetuating cycle of rising blood pressure.

Cardiac Arrhythmias and Electrical Conduction Defects

Normal cardiac rhythm relies on coordinated electrical impulse generation at the sinoatrial node and orderly propagation through the specialized conduction system. Arrhythmias arise from disturbances in impulse formation, impulse conduction, or a combination of both.

  • Reentry Circuits: An electrical impulse loops continuously around a block of tissue, firing prematurely and rapidly overriding the normal pacemaker.
  • Ectopic Pacemakers: Irritable foci outside the sinoatrial node spontaneously depolarize, generating premature beats.
  • Heart Blocks: Delays or interruptions in conduction through the atrioventricular node or bundle branches, leading to slowed ventricular rates and diminished cardiac output.

Valvular Heart Disease Pathogenesis

Heart valves maintain unidirectional blood flow through the cardiac chambers. Valvular dysfunction generally manifests in two ways: stenosis, a narrowing of the valve opening that impedes forward flow, and regurgitation, an incomplete closure that allows backward leakage.

Stenosis forces chambers to generate significantly higher pressure to push blood through the narrowed orifice, leading to muscular hypertrophy. Regurgitation causes volume overload as blood flows backward, resulting in chamber dilation. Common etiologies include rheumatic fever, which causes post-inflammatory scarring of valve cusps, and myxomatous degeneration, seen frequently in mitral valve prolapse.
