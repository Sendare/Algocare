Pathophysiology of Cardiovascular Disorders

Hemodynamics and Normal Cardiac Function

The human heart operates as a dual-pump system driven by electrical impulses and mechanical contractions. Normal cardiac output relies on four key factors: preload, afterload, myocardial contractility, and heart rate. Preload is the volume of blood stretching the ventricles at the end of diastole, largely determined by venous return. Afterload represents the resistance the ventricle must overcome to eject blood into the systemic circulation. When these variables remain in balance, tissue perfusion is maintained throughout the body.

A common misconception is that contractility and preload are identical; however, preload is about stretch volume, whereas contractility is the intrinsic strength of the muscle fibers squeezing regardless of stretch. Understanding these foundational mechanics allows nurses to anticipate how fluid shifts or blood pressure changes will directly impact cardiac workload.

Atherosclerosis and Coronary Artery Disease Mechanisms

Coronary artery disease begins with endothelial injury inside the arterial wall. When the inner lining of an artery suffers damage from factors like high blood pressure or smoking, low-density lipoproteins seep into the subendothelial space. These trapped lipids undergo oxidation, triggering an inflammatory immune response where macrophages ingest the fat and transform into foam cells.

Over time, these foam cells accumulate to form a fatty streak, which eventually develops into a fibrous plaque. This plaque narrows the lumen of the coronary arteries, restricting blood flow. If a plaque ruptures, platelets quickly aggregate at the site, forming a thrombus that can completely occlude the vessel and lead to myocardial ischemia or infarction.

Pathophysiology of Heart Failure

Heart failure occurs when the myocardium can no longer pump enough blood to meet the metabolic demands of the body. To compensate for declining cardiac output, the body activates neurohormonal systems including the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system. While these compensatory mechanisms maintain blood pressure in the short term, they ultimately worsen the condition over time.

  • Systolic dysfunction: The left ventricle loses its ability to contract effectively, resulting in a reduced ejection fraction.
  • Diastolic dysfunction: The stiffened ventricular wall cannot relax properly during diastole, impairing adequate filling despite a normal ejection fraction.
  • Ventricular remodeling: Chronic hemodynamic overload alters the shape, size, and function of the heart muscle, making it progressively weaker.

Mechanisms of Hypertension and Vascular Remodeling

Chronic high blood pressure is driven by an interplay between increased peripheral vascular resistance and elevated cardiac output. Small arteries and arterioles bear the brunt of this pressure, responding through structural changes known as vascular remodeling. Smooth muscle cells in the vessel walls hypertrophy and the extracellular matrix thickens, permanently narrowing the lumen and sustaining high resistance.

The kidneys play a central role through the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis. When renal perfusion drops, renin is released, converting angiotensinogen into angiotensin I. Angiotensin-converting enzyme then changes it into angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor that also stimulates aldosterone release, causing the kidneys to reabsorb sodium and water and driving blood pressure even higher.

Cardiac Arrhythmias and Electrical Conduction Defects

The normal heart rhythm originates in the sinoatrial node and travels through specialized conduction pathways to coordinate chamber contractions. Arrhythmias arise when there are disturbances in impulse generation, impulse conduction, or both. Common triggers include electrolyte imbalances like low potassium, myocardial ischemia, and structural heart disease.

Re-entry circuits are a frequent cause of fast arrhythmias. This happens when an electrical impulse loops continuously through damaged or scarred myocardial tissue instead of dying out after a single contraction. Whether the rhythm is too fast, too slow, or chaotic, the primary clinical consequence is a drop in cardiac output because the chambers fail to fill and empty in an orderly sequence.

Valvular Heart Disease Pathogenesis

Heart valves ensure unidirectional blood flow through the cardiac chambers. Valvular disorders generally fall into two categories: stenosis, where the valve opening is narrowed and restricts forward flow, and regurgitation, where the valve fails to close completely, allowing blood to leak backward.

Rheumatic fever, a complication of untreated streptococcal infections, historically accounts for much of the chronic stenosis seen in mitral and aortic valves due to immune-mediated scarring. Degenerative calcification is another major pathway, particularly in older adults where calcium deposits build up on aortic valve cusps. Both pathologies force the heart to work harder, eventually leading to chamber hypertrophy and heart failure.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Implications

Pathophysiological breakdowns in the cardiovascular system manifest through recognizable clinical signs. Left-sided heart failure typically causes pulmonary congestion, leading to dyspnea, orthopnea, and crackles in the lung bases. Right-sided failure results in systemic venous congestion, producing peripheral edema, jugular venous distension, and hepatomegaly.

Nurses must monitor these symptoms closely while watching for signs of low perfusion, such as cool skin, altered mental status, and low urine output. Understanding the underlying mechanisms enables nurses to evaluate the effectiveness of diuretics, vasodilators, and other cardiac medications, as well as provide targeted patient education on fluid restriction and daily weight monitoring.
