Inflammatory Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Inflammatory Cardiac Disorders

Inflammatory cardiac disorders refer to conditions where the different layers of the heart muscle and its surrounding tissues become swollen, irritated, and damaged due to an immune response or infection. The heart is structured in three primary layers: the pericardium (the protective sac surrounding the heart), the myocardium (the muscular middle layer responsible for pumping blood), and the endocardium (the inner lining that covers heart chambers and valves). Inflammation can target one specific layer or spread to affect the entire organ, severely compromising its ability to circulate blood efficiently throughout the body. Common triggers include viral infections, bacterial invasions, autoimmune reactions, and exposure to certain toxins or medications. Recognizing these conditions early is vital for Nigerian nurses working in emergency and medical-surgical wards, as untreated cardiac inflammation can rapidly progress to heart failure, lethal arrhythmias, or permanent structural damage.

Pericarditis: Pathophysiology and Clinical Assessment

Pericarditis is the inflammation of the pericardial sac, leading to friction between the visceral and parietal layers as the heart beats. The hallmark clinical manifestation of acute pericarditis is sharp, pleuritic chest pain that typically worsens when taking a deep breath, coughing, or lying flat. A key assessment finding is the pericardial friction rub, a high-pitched, scratchy sound heard best with the diaphragm of the stethoscope at the left lower sternal border while the patient leans forward.

  • Chest Pain: Sharp, stabbing, and positional pain that improves when sitting upright and leaning forward.
  • Pericardial Friction Rub: An audible grating sound produced by inflamed tissue layers rubbing together.
  • Electrocardiogram Changes: Widespread ST-segment elevation across multiple leads without reciprocal depression.

A common clinical trap is mistaking pericardial chest pain for a myocardial infarction. Nurses must carefully note that pericarditis pain changes with body position and respiration, whereas ischemic cardiac pain does not.

Myocarditis: Etiology, Disease Progression, and Diagnosis

Myocarditis involves inflammatory destruction of the heart muscle cells, most frequently triggered by viral infections such as coxsackievirus, adenovirus, or influenza. Once the infectious agent enters the body, it initiates a direct cellular injury followed by an immune-mediated response. This immune attack causes cellular swelling, necrosis, and eventual weakening of the myocardial walls. Patients often present with non-specific flu-like symptoms followed days or weeks later by progressive fatigue, dyspnea on exertion, palpitations, and signs of heart failure. Diagnostic evaluation relies heavily on elevated cardiac biomarkers like troponin, electrocardiogram abnormalities showing non-specific T-wave changes, and echocardiography demonstrating reduced ejection fraction and ventricular dilation. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging serves as a non-invasive gold standard for visualizing localized myocardial inflammation and scarring.

Endocarditis: Risk Factors and Clinical Presentation

Infective endocarditis is a microbial infection of the inner endothelial lining of the heart, most commonly affecting the heart valves. Bacteria or fungi circulating in the bloodstream attach to damaged heart valves or abnormal endocardial surfaces, forming bulky, infected masses of platelets and fibrin known as vegetations. Major risk factors include pre-existing structural heart disease, history of rheumatic fever, prosthetic heart valves, intravenous drug use, and poor dental hygiene. The clinical presentation is notoriously variable, often mimicking chronic systemic illness. Patients frequently present with a persistent fever, new or changing heart murmurs, fatigue, and night sweats. Because these vegetations can break loose, nurses must remain vigilant for embolic phenomena, which manifest as petechiae, splinter hemorrhages under the fingernails, painful nodes on fingers (Osler's nodes), or painless lesions on palms (Janeway lesions).

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological management of cardiac inflammation targets the underlying etiology and aims to reduce immune-mediated tissue damage. For acute pericarditis, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen or aspirin serve as first-line therapy, often combined with colchicine to prevent disease recurrence. Corticosteroids are generally reserved for cases refractory to standard therapy or those caused by specific autoimmune connective tissue disorders. In infective endocarditis, prolonged courses of targeted intravenous antibiotics are mandatory, frequently lasting four to six weeks based on blood culture sensitivities. Patients with severe heart failure secondary to myocarditis require standard heart failure guidelines, including ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, and loop diuretics. When pharmacological therapies fail to stabilize the patient, surgical intervention such as valve replacement or surgical debridement of infected tissue becomes necessary.

Nursing Care Plans and Patient Monitoring

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with acute inflammatory cardiac disorders focuses on reducing myocardial workload and monitoring for hemodynamic instability. Vital signs must be recorded frequently, paying close attention to temperature trends indicating ongoing infection and respiratory changes signaling fluid overload. Strict fluid balance monitoring and daily weight measurements are essential to catch early signs of congestive heart failure. Nurses must enforce strict bed rest during the acute phase of myocarditis or pericarditis to minimize oxygen demand on the inflamed heart muscle. Pain management requires scheduled administration of prescribed anti-inflammatory agents rather than waiting for pain to become severe, while comfort measures like adjusting bed positions greatly assist rest and recovery.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

Inflammatory cardiac disorders can rapidly progress to life-threatening emergencies if left unmanaged. A primary emergency in acute pericarditis is cardiac tamponade, a condition where fluid accumulates rapidly within the pericardial sac, compressing the heart chambers and preventing adequate ventricular filling. Nurses must watch for Beck's triad: hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distension, alongside pulsus paradoxus (an abnormal drop in systolic blood pressure during inspiration). Another severe complication is sudden heart failure or lethal arrhythmias stemming from myocardial scarring. Nurses must be prepared to assist with emergency pericardiocentesis—the insertion of a needle into the pericardial space to drain excess fluid and relieve pressure on the heart.

Patient Education and Rehabilitation in the Nigerian Context

Rehabilitation following an inflammatory cardiac disorder requires structured patient education tailored to local realities in Nigeria. Patients must understand the critical importance of completing full courses of prescribed antibiotics or anti-inflammatory drugs, even after physical symptoms improve. Premature discontinuation often leads to dangerous relapses, particularly with rheumatic fever and infective endocarditis. Education should also emphasize the value of good oral hygiene and regular dental check-ups, as oral bacteria frequently seed the bloodstream and infect heart valves. In many Nigerian communities, limited access to specialist care makes recognizing early warning signs—such as returning fever, swelling in the lower limbs, or unusual shortness of breath—vital for prompt hospital presentation before emergency complications develop.
