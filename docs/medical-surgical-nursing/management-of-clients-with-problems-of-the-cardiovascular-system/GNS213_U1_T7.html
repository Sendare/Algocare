Inflammatory Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Inflammatory Cardiac Disorders

Inflammatory cardiac disorders represent a group of conditions where the heart muscle, its inner lining, or the outer protective sac becomes inflamed, usually due to infections or immune responses. These conditions affect different layers of the heart structure: the pericardium, the myocardium, and the endocardium. Nurses in Nigerian clinical settings frequently encounter these disorders secondary to untreated infectious diseases like rheumatic fever, tuberculosis, or viral pathogens. Recognizing early signs of inflammation prevents permanent structural damage, heart failure, and life-threatening arrhythmias.

Pericarditis: Pathophysiology and Clinical Assessment

  • Definition: Inflammation of the pericardial sac, leading to friction between the visceral and parietal layers.
  • Hallmark Sign: Sharp, pleuritic chest pain that worsens when lying flat and eases when leaning forward.
  • Auscultation Finding: Pericardial friction rub, heard best at the left lower sternal border with the diaphragm of the stethoscope.
  • Etiology: Often viral in origin, but tuberculosis remains a major differential diagnosis in high-burden regions like Nigeria.

Myocarditis: Disease Progression and Diagnostic Evaluation

Myocarditis involves an inflammatory process within the myocardium, leading to myocyte necrosis and eventual contractile dysfunction. Viral infections are the primary triggers globally, though bacterial and parasitic agents are also documented. Patients often present with non-specific fatigue, dyspnoea, and palpitations that mimic simple viral fatigue. Diagnostic evaluation relies heavily on serial electrocardiograms showing non-specific ST-T wave changes, elevated cardiac biomarkers like troponin, and echocardiography demonstrating reduced ejection fraction. Endomyocardial biopsy remains the gold standard for definitive diagnosis, though it is reserved for severe or refractory cases.

Infective Endocarditis: Risk Factors and Clinical Presentation

Infective endocarditis is an infection of the inner endocardial surface of the heart, most commonly affecting the heart valves. Pathogens—typically bacteria like *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Streptococcus viridans*—colonize damaged heart valves, forming bulky, infected masses called vegetations.

Key risk factors include a history of rheumatic heart disease, congenital heart defects, prosthetic valves, and intravenous drug use. Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological management focuses on eradicating causative pathogens, reducing inflammation, and supporting cardiac output. For infective endocarditis, patients require prolonged courses of intravenous antimicrobial therapy lasting up to six weeks. Pericarditis is managed using non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs combined with colchicine to prevent recurrence. Corticosteroids are reserved for specific autoimmune etiologies or cases refractory to standard anti-inflammatory therapy. Nurses play a vital role in monitoring the administration timing of intravenous antibiotics and observing clients for signs of drug toxicity or superinfections.

Nursing Care Plans and Patient Monitoring

Patients with acute cardiac inflammation require strict rest to minimize myocardial oxygen demand. The nurse must monitor vital signs frequently, paying close attention to temperature spikes, blood pressure trends, and respiratory effort. Intake and output charts are essential to track fluid balance, as heart failure is a common complication. Pain assessment using a standardized pain scale helps evaluate the effectiveness of anti-inflammatory interventions. Furthermore, the nurse must ensure that any prescribed activity restriction is maintained without inducing frustration or anxiety in the patient.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

Inflammatory cardiac disorders can rapidly deteriorate into life-threatening emergencies. Pericardial effusion can progress to cardiac tamponade, where fluid accumulation restricts ventricular filling and causes a sharp drop in blood pressure. Myocarditis can trigger severe heart failure and fatal arrhythmias. Nurses must remain vigilant for signs of cardiac tamponade, known as Beck's triad: hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distension. Emergency pericardiocentesis is required immediately if tamponade develops to relieve pressure on the heart chambers.

Patient Education and Prevention Strategies

Educating patients and their families forms a cornerstone of long-term recovery and prevention. Patients recovering from infective endocarditis must understand the absolute necessity of antibiotic prophylaxis before invasive dental or surgical procedures. Nurses should emphasize compliance with prescribed medication regimens, particularly completing full courses of antibiotics even after symptoms subside. In the Nigerian context, health education must also stress prompt treatment of sore throats caused by streptococcal infections to prevent rheumatic fever and subsequent chronic heart valve damage.
