Inflammatory Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Inflammatory Cardiac Disorders

Inflammatory cardiac disorders represent a group of pathological conditions that affect the three distinct layers of the heart: the pericardium, the myocardium, and the endocardium. Understanding these disorders is crucial for nursing students preparing for clinical practice in Nigeria, as cardiovascular diseases are increasingly prevalent. Inflammation of these cardiac layers can arise from infectious agents, autoimmune reactions, hypersensitivity states, or physical injury. When the pericardium is involved, it results in pericarditis, characterized by sharp chest pain and friction rubs. Myocarditis involves inflammation of the muscular heart wall, which can lead to ventricular dysfunction, heart failure, and arrhythmias. Endocarditis primarily targets the inner endocardial lining and heart valves, predisposing patients to vegetation formation and systemic embolization. Nurses must recognize that these conditions can occur independently or as part of a systemic disease process, such as rheumatic fever or lupus. A comprehensive baseline assessment includes evaluating vital signs, cardiac rhythm, heart sounds, and peripheral perfusion. Early identification of inflammatory markers and prompt medical intervention are vital to prevent irreversible structural damage and improve patient outcomes across diverse healthcare settings in Nigeria.

Pericarditis: Pathophysiology, Clinical Manifestations, and Nursing Assessment

Pericarditis is an inflammation of the pericardium, the protective fibrous sac enclosing the heart. The pathophysiology typically involves viral infections, post-myocardial infarction syndrome (Dressler's), or idiopathic causes, leading to capillary permeability, leukocyte infiltration, and fibrin deposition. Clinically, patients present with classic symptoms including sudden, pleuritic chest pain that is sharp and worse with deep inspiration or lying flat, and is often relieved by sitting up and leaning forward. Another hallmark physical assessment finding is a pericardial friction rub, heard best at the left sternal border with the diaphragm of the stethoscope during expiration. Nursing assessment requires careful auscultation for this high-pitched, scratchy sound, alongside monitoring for signs of cardiac tamponade, such as muffled heart sounds, hypotension, and jugular venous distension (Beck's triad). Nurses should also assess for pulsus paradoxus, an abnormal drop in systolic blood pressure during inspiration. Pain assessment using a validated scale helps evaluate the effectiveness of anti-inflammatory therapies. Recognizing these manifestations promptly allows nurses to differentiate pericardial pain from ischemic cardiac pain, ensuring targeted and safe nursing interventions.

Myocarditis: Etiology, Disease Progression, and Diagnostic Evaluation

Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease of the myocardium caused primarily by viral infections (such as Coxsackievirus, adenovirus, and increasingly COVID-19), though bacterial, fungal agents, and hypersensitivity reactions to medications also contribute. The disease progression starts with an infectious phase causing direct cellular injury, followed by an immune-mediated phase where cellular and humoral immune responses cause further damage, myocyte necrosis, and interstitial edema. As myocardial tissue is destroyed, ventricular contractility decreases, leading to systolic and diastolic dysfunction, chamber dilation, and a high risk for heart failure. Diagnostic evaluation includes an electrocardiogram (ECG), which may show non-specific ST-T wave changes or conduction blocks. Laboratory investigations typically reveal elevated cardiac biomarkers such as troponin and creatine kinase-MB due to ongoing myocyte injury, alongside elevated inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Echocardiography is vital to assess ventricular function, wall motion abnormalities, and ejection fraction. Definitive diagnosis may involve an endomyocardial biopsy, the gold standard, though non-invasive cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging is frequently utilized to visualize myocardial edema, hyperemia, and late gadolinium enhancement.

Endocarditis: Risk Factors, Valvular Involvement, and Clinical Presentation

Infective endocarditis (IE) is a microbial infection of the endothelial surface of the heart, most commonly involving the heart valves. Major risk factors include a history of congenital heart disease, prosthetic heart valves, previous endocarditis, intravenous drug use, and degenerative valve disease. Dental procedures and poor oral hygiene also play significant roles in transient bacteremia that seeds abnormal endocardial surfaces. The pathological hallmark of endocarditis is the formation of vegetations—masses of platelets, fibrin, micro-organisms, and inflammatory cells—on the valve leaflets. These vegetations can disrupt normal valve closure, resulting in new or changing heart murmurs, acute heart failure, and valvular regurgitation. Clinically, patients present with prolonged fever, chills, fatigue, and weight loss. Nurses must be vigilant for classic peripheral manifestations of septic embolization and immune complex deposition, including petechiae, splinter hemorrhages under the fingernails, Janeway lesions (painless erythematous lesions on palms and soles), Osler nodes (painful tender nodes on finger pads), and Roth spots (retinal hemorrhages). Prompt recognition of these signs is critical for early blood cultures and antibiotic initiation.

Medical and Pharmacological Management of Cardiac Inflammation

Medical and pharmacological management of cardiac inflammation focuses on eradicating causative infectious agents, reducing inflammation, managing heart failure symptoms, and preventing complications. For infective endocarditis, prolonged, high-dose intravenous antibiotic therapy tailored to blood culture and sensitivity results is the cornerstone of treatment, often lasting four to six weeks. In contrast, viral myocarditis and acute pericarditis are managed primarily with supportive care and anti-inflammatory medications. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen or indomethacin, combined with colchicine, are first-line therapies for acute pericarditis to relieve pain and prevent recurrence. Corticosteroids may be reserved for cases refractory to NSAIDs, autoimmune etiologies, or specific inflammatory conditions. If myocardial dysfunction and heart failure develop, standard guideline-directed medical therapy is implemented, including ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs), beta-blockers, and loop diuretics to reduce workload and manage fluid overload. Nurses play a critical role in administering these complex medication regimens, monitoring for adverse drug reactions, assessing renal function, and evaluating therapeutic efficacy through serial laboratory and diagnostic testing.

Nursing Care Plans and Patient Monitoring in Acute Inflammatory States

Comprehensive nursing care plans for patients with acute inflammatory cardiac disorders require continuous patient monitoring, symptom management, and prevention of secondary complications. Nurses must prioritize frequent vital sign assessments, closely monitoring for tachycardia, hypotension, tachypnea, and fever which indicate ongoing inflammation or clinical deterioration. Strict intake and output (I&O) recording, alongside daily weight measurements, are essential to detect early fluid retention and assess the effectiveness of diuretic therapy. Cardiac rhythm monitoring is crucial, especially in myocarditis, to detect potentially fatal arrhythmias resulting from myocardial irritability and conduction system involvement. Nursing interventions also include providing adequate rest periods to minimize myocardial oxygen demand and promote healing. Pain management strategies, such as positioning for pericarditis or administering prescribed analgesics and anti-inflammatories, must be evaluated regularly. Furthermore, nurses should monitor laboratory parameters, including complete blood counts, inflammatory markers, and renal function panels. Educating the patient and family on activity restrictions and reporting warning signs is also an integral component of the acute phase nursing care plan.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

Inflammatory cardiac disorders can rapidly progress to life-threatening emergencies requiring immediate nursing and medical intervention. One of the most critical complications of pericarditis is cardiac tamponade, caused by rapid fluid accumulation in the pericardial sac that compresses the heart and impairs ventricular filling. Nurses must recognize the classic signs of cardiac tamponade: Beck's triad (hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distension), alongside pulsus paradoxus and severe respiratory distress. Emergency intervention for tamponade requires prompt pericardiocentesis or surgical pericardial window creation to drain the fluid and restore cardiac output. In myocarditis and endocarditis, severe complications include cardiogenic shock, life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias, heart block, and systemic embolization (such as stroke or pulmonary embolism from dislodged valve vegetations). Nurses must maintain emergency equipment at the bedside, including suction, oxygen, and a defibrillator, while being prepared to initiate cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and administer emergency antiarrhythmic or inotropic medications. Rapid communication with the interprofessional healthcare team is essential to stabilize the patient and arrange for surgical valve replacement if structural damage leads to irreversible hemodynamic collapse.

Patient Education, Rehabilitation, and Prevention Strategies in the Nigerian Context

Patient education, rehabilitation, and prevention strategies are vital components of comprehensive care, particularly within the unique socioeconomic and healthcare context of Nigeria. Nurses must provide culturally sensitive education regarding the importance of completing full antibiotic courses for streptococcal pharyngitis to prevent rheumatic fever, a major precursor to valvular heart disease in resource-limited settings. Patients recovering from infective endocarditis or myocarditis need clear instructions on recognizing signs of recurrence, such as persistent low-grade fever, fatigue, and new or worsening shortness of breath. Prophylactic antibiotic education is essential for high-risk dental and surgical procedures to prevent secondary endocarditis. Cardiac rehabilitation programs, adapted to local resources, should emphasize gradual resumption of physical activity, nutritional counseling focusing on heart-healthy diets low in sodium, and smoking cessation. Furthermore, nurses should empower patients and their families to maintain regular follow-up appointments, adhere to complex medication schedules, and understand how to access emergency care when warning signs develop. Incorporating community health awareness campaigns strengthens primary prevention and reduces the long-term burden of cardiac inflammation in Nigeria.
