Inflammatory Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Inflammatory Cardiac Disorders

Inflammatory cardiac disorders represent a spectrum of pathological conditions affecting the three distinct layers of the heart wall: the pericardium, the myocardium, and the endocardium. These conditions—pericarditis, myocarditis, and endocarditis—frequently share overlapping infectious or immune-mediated etiologies, yet they present with markedly different clinical trajectories and hemodynamic consequences. Understanding the anatomical layer involved is critical because it dictates the primary pathological process, ranging from friction and fluid accumulation in the pericardial sac to electrical instability in the myocardial muscle and structural valvular destruction in the endothelial lining.

Viral infections remain the leading cause of cardiac inflammation globally, particularly enteroviruses and coxsackieviruses. However, bacterial agents, fungal pathogens, autoimmune phenomena, and hypersensitivity reactions to medications also trigger these inflammatory cascades. In many low- and middle-income regions, including Nigeria, rheumatic fever following untreated streptococcal throat infections remains a major precursor to chronic inflammatory valvular disease. Regardless of the specific trigger, the host immune response often perpetuates myocardial and vascular injury well after the initial pathogen is cleared, transforming an acute infection into a chronic debilitating condition if left unrecognized.

Pericarditis: Pathophysiology, Clinical Manifestations, and Nursing Assessment

Pericarditis involves inflammation of the serous and fibrous layers of the pericardium. As inflamed pericardial surfaces rub together during cardiac contraction, they generate a hallmark physical sign: the pericardial friction rub, best auscultated at the left lower sternal border with the patient leaning forward and holding their breath at end-expiration.

  • Chest Pain: Classically sharp, pleuritic, and retrosternal, radiating to the neck, shoulder, or trapezius ridge.
  • Positional Relief: Pain typically worsens when lying supine and improves dramatically upon sitting up and leaning forward.
  • ECG Changes: Widespread, diffuse ST-segment elevation (concave upward) and PR-segment depression across multiple leads, reflecting generalized epicardial irritation.

During clinical assessment, the nurse must remain vigilant for signs of cardiac tamponade, a life-threatening complication marked by Beck's triad: hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distension. Pulsus paradoxus—an excessive drop in systolic blood pressure of greater than 10 mmHg during inspiration—serves as an early indicator of impaired ventricular filling due to fluid accumulation in the rigid pericardial space.

Myocarditis: Etiology, Disease Progression, and Diagnostic Evaluation

Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease of the heart muscle diagnosed by established histological, immunological, and clinical criteria. The disease progression typically follows three phases: initial direct viral injury, a secondary immune-mediated phase where cellular and humoral immunity damage the myocytes, and a final phase of tissue remodeling that can result in dilated cardiomyopathy.

Because clinical presentations vary widely from asymptomatic mild fatigue to fulminant heart failure and sudden cardiac death, diagnostic evaluation requires a multi-pronged approach:

  1. Endomyocardial Biopsy: The definitive gold standard for confirming inflammation and cellular necrosis, though its use is selective due to procedural risks.
  2. Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (CMR): Non-invasive imaging demonstrating characteristic late gadolinium enhancement, indicating myocardial edema, hyperemia, and necrosis.
  3. Cardiac Biomarkers: Elevated serum troponins reflecting active myocyte injury, alongside raised inflammatory markers such as Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP).
  4. Electrocardiography: Demonstrating non-specific ST-T wave abnormalities or lethal ventricular arrhythmias arising from irritable foci in the inflamed myocardium.

Endocarditis: Risk Factors, Valvular Involvement, and Clinical Presentation

Infective endocarditis is a microbial infection of the endothelial surface of the heart, most commonly involving the heart valves. Turbulence within the bloodstream—created by congenital defects, rheumatic heart disease, prosthetic valves, or intravenous drug use—damages the delicate endothelium. This damage prompts the deposition of fibrin and platelets, forming sterile vegetations. Transient bacteremia then seeds these sterile platelet-fibrin clots, establishing active colonies of microorganisms.

The mitral and aortic valves are most frequently targeted in left-sided endocarditis, whereas tricuspid valve involvement is strongly associated with intravenous drug abuse due to direct peripheral injection of pathogens. Clinical manifestations stem from local valve destruction, systemic embolization of vegetative fragments, and circulating immune complexes.

Classic peripheral signs of infective endocarditis, though less commonly seen in modern practice, include Janeway lesions (non-tender hemorrhagic macules on palms and soles), Osler nodes (painful subcutaneous nodules on pulp of digits), Roth spots (retinal hemorrhages with pale centers), and splinter hemorrhages beneath the nail beds. Persistent, unexplained fever combined with a new or changing cardiac murmur remains the cardinal clinical presentation.

Medical and Pharmacological Management of Cardiac Inflammation

Therapeutic strategies for cardiac inflammation focus on eliminating the underlying causative agent, suppressing destructive immune responses, and managing downstream heart failure. For infective endocarditis, prolonged, high-dose intravenous antibiotic therapy—often lasting 4 to 6 weeks—is mandatory to penetrate dense valvular vegetations and eradicate entrenched pathogens. Blood cultures must be drawn prior to initiating antimicrobial therapy to guide targeted treatment adjustments.

In contrast, viral or immune-mediated pericarditis is managed primarily with anti-inflammatory agents. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), such as ibuprofen or aspirin, form the first-line treatment backbone, frequently paired with colchicine to dramatically reduce recurrence rates and speed up symptom resolution. Corticosteroids are generally reserved for refractory cases or specific autoimmune etiologies because they can increase viral replication and prolong viral clearance in myocarditis.

When myocarditis leads to acute left ventricular dysfunction, standard heart failure pharmacology—including ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, and loop diuretics—is instituted cautiously. The clinician must balance the need for afterload reduction with the risk of precipitating hypotension in a compromised, inflamed myocardium.

Nursing Care Plans and Patient Monitoring in Acute Inflammatory States

Managing a patient with acute cardiac inflammation requires rigorous, continuous monitoring to detect subtle hemodynamic deterioration before it escalates into a crisis. The nurse plays a pivotal role in enforcing strict physical rest during the acute phase of myocarditis or pericarditis, as physical exertion increases myocardial oxygen demand and can worsen inflammation or trigger lethal arrhythmias.

Key nursing interventions and monitoring priorities include:

  • Hemodynamic Surveillance: Tracking continuous telemetry for rhythm changes, monitoring blood pressure, and assessing peripheral perfusion.
  • Fluid Balance Management: Accurately measuring strict intake and output, daily weights, and monitoring for dependent edema or pulmonary crackles indicating fluid overload.
  • Pain Management: Evaluating chest pain characteristics, administering prescribed anti-inflammatories or analgesics on schedule, and ensuring non-pharmacological comfort measures like proper positioning.
  • Infection Control: Maintaining strict aseptic technique with central lines and surgical wounds, particularly in patients receiving prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy for endocarditis.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

Inflammatory cardiac disorders can precipitate rapid clinical emergencies that demand immediate, coordinated nursing and medical intervention. Cardiac tamponade is a surgical emergency wherein rapid fluid accumulation in the pericardial space compresses the heart chambers, severely restricting diastolic filling and causing a precipitous drop in cardiac output. The definitive emergency intervention is pericardiocentesis, where a needle or catheter is inserted into the pericardial space to aspirate fluid, instantly restoring hemodynamic stability.

Another critical complication is heart failure and pulmonary edema, driven by acute myocardial dysfunction or severe acute valvular regurgitation caused by leaflet perforation in endocarditis. Emergency care involves administering high-flow oxygen, IV loop diuretics, and vasodilators. Furthermore, patients with myocarditis or severe endocarditis are at high risk for systemic thromboembolism—when valvular vegetations break loose and occlude cerebral, renal, or mesenteric vessels—requiring rapid neurological and vascular assessment.

Patient Education, Rehabilitation, and Prevention Strategies in the Nigerian Context

Long-term recovery and secondary prevention of cardiac inflammation require tailored patient education that addresses local epidemiological realities. In Nigeria and similar resource-constrained settings, rheumatoid fever secondary to untreated or poorly treated streptococcal pharyngitis remains a leading cause of chronic valvular heart disease. Consequently, community and clinical education must emphasize the importance of completing full courses of oral penicillin or receiving benzathine penicillin G prophylaxis for streptococcal throat infections.

Rehabilitation plans must be individualized, helping patients gradually transition from strict bed rest back to normal daily activities without overwhelming the healing myocardium. Nurses must educate patients and their families on:

  • Recognizing early warning signs of recurrence, such as unexplained fever, returning chest pain, or progressive shortness of breath.
  • Adhering strictly to prescribed medication regimens, especially completing multi-week antibiotic courses for endocarditis.
  • Maintaining meticulous dental hygiene and reporting the need for antibiotic prophylaxis prior to invasive dental or surgical procedures for individuals with pre-existing valvular abnormalities.
