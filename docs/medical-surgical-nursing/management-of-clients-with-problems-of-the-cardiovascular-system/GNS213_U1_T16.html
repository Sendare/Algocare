Electrocardiogram (ECG)

Introduction to Electrocardiography

Electrocardiography (ECG) is a fundamental diagnostic tool used in clinical nursing practice to record the electrical activity of the heart over a period of time. Developed as a non-invasive procedure, an ECG graph displays voltage changes produced by the heart muscle during each cardiac cycle. For nursing students in Nigeria and globally, mastering ECG interpretation is vital for assessing cardiovascular health, identifying acute life-threatening emergencies, and monitoring patients with known heart diseases or those undergoing critical care. The ECG machine detects tiny electrical impulses generated by the depolarization and repolarization of cardiac muscle fibers through electrodes placed on the patient's skin and amplifies these signals onto specialized grid paper or a digital monitor. Understanding how this electrical activity translates into visual waveforms allows nurses to detect abnormalities early, intervene promptly, and collaborate effectively with the multidisciplinary healthcare team to improve patient outcomes.

Electrophysiological Principles of the Heart

To understand ECG waveforms, nursing students must grasp the electrophysiological principles governing cardiac function. The heart possesses automaticity, excitability, conductivity, and contractility. Resting cardiac cells maintain a polarized state, meaning the inside of the cell is negatively charged relative to the outside, primarily due to the distribution of potassium, sodium, and calcium ions. When a cardiac cell is stimulated, depolarization occurs, characterized by a rapid influx of sodium ions into the cell, making the interior positive. This electrical wave sweeps across the myocardium, triggering mechanical contraction. Following depolarization, the cell undergoes repolarization, returning to its resting negative state via the movement of ions across the cell membrane. The sinoatrial (SA) node acts as the heart's natural pacemaker, initiating these electrical impulses that travel sequentially through the atria, atrioventricular (AV) node, bundle of His, bundle branches, and Purkinje fibers. Any disruption in this specialized conduction pathway can result in abnormal electrical patterns visible on the ECG.

Standard 12-Lead ECG Placement and Lead Configuration

The standard 12-lead ECG provides a comprehensive view of the heart's electrical activity from twelve different angles or perspectives using ten physical electrodes. These leads are divided into two categories: limb leads and precordial (chest) leads. The six limb leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, and aVF) evaluate electrical activity in the vertical frontal plane. Leads I, II, and III are bipolar leads, while aVR, aVL, and aVF are augmented unipolar leads. The six chest leads (V1 through V6) are unipolar leads that evaluate the horizontal plane, providing crucial views of the anterior, lateral, and septal walls of the left ventricle. Precise anatomical placement of chest electrodes is critical: V1 is placed in the fourth intercostal space at the right sternal border, V2 in the fourth intercostal space at the left sternal border, V3 midway between V2 and V4, V4 in the fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line, V5 in the fifth intercostal space at the anterior axillary line, and V6 in the fifth intercostal space at the midaxillary line. Incorrect electrode placement can lead to misdiagnosis, making anatomical landmarks essential for accurate nursing documentation and patient assessment.

Anatomy of a Normal ECG Waveform

A normal ECG waveform consists of specific waves, intervals, and segments that represent the sequential depolarization and repolarization of the heart. The P wave is the first deflection, representing atrial depolarization. Following the P wave is the PR interval, measured from the beginning of the P wave to the beginning of the QRS complex, indicating the time taken for the impulse to travel through the atria and AV node. The QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization, where the ventricles contract to pump blood; it typically lasts between 0.06 and 0.10 seconds. The ST segment reflects the early phase of ventricular repolarization and should ideally be flat, aligning with the baseline (isoelectric line). Elevation or depression of the ST segment is a major clinical indicator of myocardial ischemia or infarction. The T wave follows, representing ventricular repolarization. Finally, the QT interval represents the total time required for ventricular depolarization and repolarization. Understanding the normal duration and morphology of these components is vital for nurses to quickly spot deviations.

Systematic Approach to ECG Interpretation

Interpreting an ECG strip requires a systematic, step-by-step approach to ensure no critical detail is missed. Nurses should follow a structured sequence: First, determine the heart rate by counting the number of R waves on a 6-second strip and multiplying by 10, or by using the large box method. Second, evaluate the heart rhythm to check if it is regular or irregular by measuring the R-R intervals. Third, inspect for the presence of upright and uniform P waves preceding every QRS complex. Fourth, measure the PR interval to ensure it falls within the normal range of 0.12 to 0.20 seconds (three to five small boxes). Fifth, measure the QRS complex duration to verify it is narrow and within 0.06 to 0.10 seconds. Sixth, examine the ST segment and T wave for any abnormalities, such as ST elevation, ST depression, or T wave inversion. Adopting this methodical workflow helps nursing students and professionals maintain consistency and accuracy during emergency cardiac assessments and routine monitoring.

Common Cardiac Arrhythmias and Dysrhythmias

Cardiac arrhythmias and dysrhythmias represent disruptions in the normal generation or conduction of electrical impulses in the heart. Nurses must be able to recognize common rhythms such as sinus tachycardia, sinus bradycardia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), ventricular tachycardia (VT), and ventricular fibrillation (VF). Sinus tachycardia presents with a normal waveform pattern but a heart rate exceeding 100 beats per minute, often caused by fever, anxiety, or pain. Conversely, sinus bradycardia features a heart rate below 60 beats per minute. Atrial fibrillation is characterized by irregularly irregular rhythm with absent P waves and a chaotic baseline, putting patients at risk for thromboembolism and stroke. Ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation are medical emergencies; VT shows wide QRS complexes in a rapid sequence, while VF exhibits a chaotic, wavy baseline with no recognizable waves or cardiac output. Recognizing these patterns empowers nurses to initiate immediate resuscitation protocols and emergency interventions.

ECG Changes in Myocardial Infarction and Ischemia

Electrocardiographic changes are critical in diagnosing and localizing myocardial infarction (MI) and myocardial ischemia. When coronary blood flow is reduced, the affected heart muscle becomes ischemic, leading to ECG alterations that typically progress through three stages: ischemia, injury, and infarction. Myocardial ischemia is classically represented by T wave inversion or tall, peaked T waves. As the ischemia progresses to acute myocardial injury, the ST segment becomes elevated above the isoelectric line (STEMI). Pathological Q waves indicate established myocardial necrosis or infarction; these waves appear wide (greater than 0.04 seconds) and deep (greater than one-third the height of the QRS complex). Furthermore, specific leads can localize the infarct: leads II, III, and aVF indicate an inferior wall infarction; V1 through V4 point to an anterior wall infarction; and leads I, aVL, V5, and V6 suggest a lateral wall infarction. Identifying these regional changes allows nurses to communicate precise findings to physicians and expedite life-saving reperfusion therapies.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Proper patient preparation and diligent nursing responsibilities ensure the accuracy and reliability of an ECG recording. Before starting the procedure, the nurse should explain the non-invasive nature of the test to alleviate patient anxiety and obtain informed cooperation. Skin preparation is crucial: the nurse must ensure the skin is clean, dry, and free of oils or excessive chest hair (which may require clipping) to ensure optimal electrode contact and minimize artifact interference. The patient should be positioned comfortably in a supine position, relaxed, and instructed to remain still and breathe normally without talking during the brief recording. Nurses must also ensure proper machine calibration, correct lead wire attachment, and accurate documentation of patient details, date, and time on the ECG tracing. Following the procedure, the nurse plays a vital role in analyzing the strip, reporting any critical abnormalities to the medical team immediately, and providing ongoing cardiovascular monitoring.
