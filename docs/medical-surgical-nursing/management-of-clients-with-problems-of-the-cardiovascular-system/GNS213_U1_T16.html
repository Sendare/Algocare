Electrocardiogram (ECG)

Introduction to Electrocardiography

An electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) is a non-invasive diagnostic test that records the electrical activity of the heart over time. By placing electrodes on the skin, nurses and technicians can capture electrical signals moving through heart muscle cells during each cardiac cycle. This test provides a graphical representation of cardiac function, making it an essential tool in clinical practice for detecting rhythm disturbances, structural heart disease, and acute myocardial injury.

Electrophysiological Principles

The heart beats through a continuous cycle of electrical charges moving across cell membranes. At rest, cardiac cells maintain a polarized state with a negative internal charge. When an electrical impulse arrives, positively charged ions rush into the cell, creating depolarization, which triggers muscle contraction. Afterward, the cells return to their resting state through repolarization. This orderly flow of ions moves through the heart's specialized conduction system, starting at the sinoatrial node and spreading to the ventricles.

Standard 12-Lead ECG Placement and Lead Configuration

A standard 12-lead ECG uses 10 physical electrodes to produce 12 different viewing angles of the heart's electrical activity.

  • Limb Leads: Four electrodes placed on the arms and legs create six leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, and aVF) that view the heart in a vertical plane.
  • Chest (Precordial) Leads: Six electrodes placed across the anterior chest wall (V1 through V6) view the heart in a horizontal plane, focusing heavily on the left ventricle.

Anatomy of a Normal ECG Waveform

A single normal heartbeat on an ECG tracing consists of specific waves, intervals, and segments that represent the sequence of electrical activation and recovery.

  1. P wave: Represents atrial depolarization, showing the spread of the impulse through the atria.
  2. PR interval: Measures the time it takes for the electrical impulse to travel from the atria through the AV node to the ventricles.
  3. QRS complex: Represents ventricular depolarization, reflecting the powerful electrical activation of the main pumping chambers.
  4. ST segment: Represents the period when the ventricles are completely depolarized, sitting flat before repolarization begins.
  5. T wave: Represents ventricular repolarization, showing the recovery phase where ventricles prepare for the next beat.

Systematic Approach to ECG Interpretation

Interpreting an ECG requires a consistent, step-by-step method to avoid missing critical findings.

  • Heart Rate: Determine if the rate is normal, too slow, or too fast.
  • Rhythm: Check if the R-R intervals are regular and steady.
  • P Waves: Confirm that every P wave is followed by a QRS complex and that they look upright and uniform.
  • Intervals: Measure the PR interval, QRS duration, and QT interval to ensure they fall within normal limits.
  • ST Segment and T Wave: Look for any abnormal elevation, depression, or inversion.

Common Cardiac Arrhythmias and Dysrhythmias

Arrhythmias occur when the heart beats too fast, too slow, or with an irregular pattern due to disruptions in electrical generation or conduction.

  • Sinus Tachycardia: A regular rhythm with a heart rate exceeding 100 beats per minute, often caused by fever, pain, or anxiety.
  • Sinus Bradycardia: A regular rhythm with a rate below 60 beats per minute, which may be normal in conditioned athletes but concerning in symptomatic patients.
  • Atrial Fibrillation: A chaotic, irregular atrial rhythm with no discernible P waves, putting patients at risk for blood clots and stroke.
  • Ventricular Fibrillation: A fatal, quivering rhythm with no effective cardiac output, requiring immediate CPR and defibrillation.

ECG Changes in Myocardial Infarction and Ischemia

When coronary blood flow is blocked, heart muscle cells become oxygen-deprived, leading to characteristic ECG changes that evolve over time.

  • Ischemia: Appears as T wave inversion or ST segment depression, indicating lack of oxygen.
  • Injury: Appears as acute ST segment elevation, signaling active myocardial damage.
  • Infarction: Appears as significant pathological Q waves, representing dead or necrotic heart tissue.

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Accurate ECG recording depends heavily on proper patient preparation and technique. The nurse must explain the procedure to reduce patient anxiety, as stress can alter heart rates. The patient's chest should be clean, dry, and free of oils or excessive chest hair to ensure good electrode contact. Skin preparation may include light abrasion or clipping hair if necessary. During the recording, the patient must lie still and breathe normally to prevent artifact interference on the printed strip.
