Electrocardiogram (ECG)

Introduction to Electrocardiography

An **electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG)** is a graphical recording of the electrical activity generated by the heart muscle during each cardiac cycle. Placed on the skin, electrodes detect minuscule electrical potentials produced by depolarisation and repolarisation of myocardial fibres. Willem Einthoven developed the practical clinical string galvanometer in the early 20th century, laying the foundation for modern cardiac monitoring. In contemporary Nigerian clinical settings, from tertiary teaching hospitals in Lagos to primary health centres in rural districts, the 12-lead ECG remains an indispensable, non-invasive diagnostic tool. It aids in rapidly identifying life-threatening arrhythmias, conduction blocks, electrolyte imbalances, and acute coronary syndromes without causing patient discomfort.

Electrophysiological Principles of the Heart

Cardiac muscle tissue possesses unique electrophysiological properties that allow coordinated contraction. **Automaticity** enables specialized pacemaker cells, primarily in the sinoatrial (SA) node, to spontaneously generate electrical impulses. These impulses then travel via **conductivity** through specialized pathways across the atria and ventricles. At the cellular level, an action potential relies on shifts of ions across the myocardial cell membrane: - **Resting Membrane Potential**: The inside of the cell is negatively charged relative to the outside, maintained by the sodium-potassium pump. - **Depolarisation**: Rapid influx of sodium ($Na^+$) ions into the cell, creating a positive internal charge that triggers muscular contraction. - **Repolarisation**: Efflux of potassium ($K^+$) ions alongside calcium channel activity, restoring the cell to its resting negative state.

Standard 12-Lead ECG Placement and Lead Configuration

A standard ECG utilizes 10 physical electrodes to produce 12 unique electrical views of the heart. These views are divided into two distinct groups: limb leads and chest (precordial) leads. - **Limb Leads (I, II, III, aVR, aVL, aVF)**: Placed on the four limbs (left arm, right arm, left leg, right leg as a ground), these leads view the heart in the vertical (frontal) plane. - **Precordial Leads (V1 to V6)**: Placed across specific anatomical landmarks on the anterior chest wall, viewing the heart in the horizontal plane. Precise anatomical placement is critical. Placing chest leads even one intercostal space too high or low can distort waveforms and mimic pathology like myocardial infarction.

Anatomy of a Normal ECG Waveform

A single normal cardiac cycle on an ECG consists of distinct waves, intervals, and segments that reflect the sequential electrical activation of the heart chambers. | Wave / Interval | Electrophysiological Meaning | Normal Duration / Features | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **P wave** | Atrial depolarisation | Less than 0.12 seconds; upright in lead II | | **PR interval** | Atrioventricular conduction time | 0.12 to 0.20 seconds (3 to 5 small boxes) | | **QRS complex** | Ventricular depolarisation | Less than 0.12 seconds (narrow) | | **ST segment** | Early ventricular repolarisation | Isoelectric line (flat, baseline level) | | **T wave** | Ventricular repolarisation | Smooth, rounded, concordant with QRS | Recognizing these baseline parameters allows clinicians to immediately spot deviations in timing or morphology.

Systematic Approach to ECG Interpretation

Interpreting an ECG strip requires a disciplined, step-by-step routine to avoid missing subtle abnormalities. Skipping steps often leads to misdiagnosis. 1. **Heart Rate**: Calculate rate by counting R waves on a 10-second strip and multiplying by 6, or by dividing 1,500 by the number of small boxes between consecutive R waves. 2. **Rhythm**: Assess whether R-R intervals are regular or irregular, checking for consistent spacing. 3. **P Waves**: Confirm every P wave is present, upright, and followed consistently by a QRS complex. 4. **Intervals**: Measure the PR interval, QRS duration, and QT interval to verify they fall within normal physiological limits. 5. **Axis and Morphology**: Inspect ST segments, T wave polarity, and Q waves for signs of ischemia, hypertrophy, or infarction.

Common Cardiac Arrhythmias and Dysrhythmias

Arrhythmias represent disturbances in rate, regularity, or the site of impulse origin. Recognizing rhythm disorders is an essential competency for clinical nurses. - **Sinus Tachycardia**: Heart rate exceeds 100 beats per minute with normal P-QRS-T morphology, often driven by fever, pain, anxiety, or hypovolemia. - **Sinus Bradycardia**: Heart rate drops below 60 beats per minute, commonly seen in well-conditioned athletes or resulting from beta-blocker toxicity and increased vagal tone. - **Atrial Fibrillation**: Chaotic, rapid atrial activity resulting in an irregularly irregular ventricular response without discernible P waves, significantly increasing stroke risk due to mural thrombus formation. - **Ventricular Fibrillation**: A fatal, quivering rhythm with no effective cardiac output, requiring immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation and defibrillation.

ECG Changes in Myocardial Infarction and Ischemia

When coronary blood flow is compromised, myocardial cells undergo progressive injury that leaves distinct electrical signatures on an ECG. Identifying these shifts enables rapid reperfusion therapy. - **Ischemia**: Lack of oxygen leads to delayed repolarisation, classically manifested as **T wave inversion** or tall, peaked hyperacute T waves. - **Injury**: Prolonged ischemia causes cellular membrane damage, resulting in **ST-segment elevation** above the baseline (STEMI). - **Infarction**: Cell death produces permanent electrical dead zones, manifesting as significant, deep **pathological Q waves** (width greater than 0.04 seconds or depth exceeding one-third of the total QRS height).

Nursing Responsibilities and Patient Preparation

Obtaining a diagnostic-quality ECG requires careful patient preparation and technical skill. The nurse must explain the procedure clearly to alleviate patient anxiety, reassuring them that electrodes only record electrical signals and deliver no electrical shock. Skin preparation is vital: excessive chest hair may require clipping to ensure proper electrode adhesion, and oily skin should be cleansed with alcohol wipes. Ensure the patient lies completely still and refrains from talking during the short recording to eliminate artifact interference. Finally, check that calibration markers and patient details are accurately recorded on the trace before handing the report to the attending physician.
