Infectious Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Infectious Cardiac Disorders

Infectious cardiac disorders represent a critical group of pathologies affecting the heart, posing significant morbidity and mortality challenges globally and within the Nigerian healthcare landscape. These conditions primarily involve inflammation and microbial invasion of the three distinct layers of the heart: the inner endocardium, the muscular myocardium, and the outer pericardium. Understanding these disorders requires a solid foundation in cardiovascular anatomy, physiology, and microbiology, as pathogens ranging from bacteria and viruses to fungi can incite devastating tissue damage. For nursing students preparing for professional examinations, recognizing that these diseases can manifest acutely or insidiously is paramount. Cardiac infections often mimic other cardiopulmonary illnesses, making meticulous assessment vital for early identification and intervention. In Nigeria, infectious cardiac disorders are frequently secondary to untreated rheumatic fever, advanced systemic infections, or localized dental and skin sepsis. Nurses serve as the frontline defenders in acute care and community settings, responsible for identifying early warning signs, monitoring hemodynamic stability, and facilitating prompt diagnostic workups. A comprehensive grasp of the underlying mechanisms equips nursing professionals to deliver evidence-based care, minimize long-term cardiac remodeling, and prevent catastrophic complications such as heart failure, systemic embolization, or cardiac tamponade.

Infective Endocarditis: Etiology, Pathophysiology, and Clinical Manifestations

Infective endocarditis (IE) is a microbial infection of the endothelial surface of the heart, most commonly involving the heart valves. The primary etiologic agents are bacteria, notably Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus viridans, and enterococci, though fungi can also be responsible. The pathophysiology begins with endothelial damage, which exposes subendothelial collagen and tissue factor, leading to the deposition of platelets and fibrin to form non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE). Transient bacteremia allows circulating microorganisms to adhere to these sterile platelet-fibrin vegetations, proliferating and inducing further fibrin and platelet deposition to form mature infected vegetations. Clinically, patients present with a constellation of signs and symptoms that can affect multiple organ systems. Hallmark manifestations include a new or changing cardiac murmur, persistent fever, chills, fatigue, and night sweats. Because vegetations can fragment and travel through the systemic circulation, peripheral manifestations are common. These include splinter hemorrhages under the fingernails, petechiae on the conjunctiva or oral mucosa, painful subcutaneous nodules called Osler nodes, non-tender macules on the palms and soles known as Janeway lesions, and retinal hemorrhages referred to as Roth spots. Nursing students must recognize that embolic events can cause severe complications such as strokes, renal infarction, or splenic abscesses, necessitating rigorous neurological and abdominal assessments.

Myocarditis: Causes, Pathogenesis, and Clinical Presentation

Myocarditis is an inflammatory disease of the heart muscle (myocardium) diagnosed by established histologic, immunologic, and clinical criteria. While viral infections—particularly Coxsackievirus B, adenovirus, parvovirus B19, and influenza—are the predominant causes in developed and developing nations alike, bacterial, fungal, and parasitic agents (such as Trypanosoma cruzi causing Chagas disease) can also precipitate the condition. Non-infectious triggers include autoimmune diseases, hypersensitivity reactions to certain medications, and toxin exposures. The pathogenesis unfolds in distinct phases: an initial direct viral injury to myocytes, followed by an autoimmune-mediated cellular immune response where T-lymphocytes and macrophages infiltrate the myocardial tissue. This immune onslaught causes myocyte necrosis, edema, and interstitial fibrosis, impairing both systolic and diastolic contractility. Consequently, patients present with a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations ranging from completely asymptomatic states to fulminant heart failure, cardiogenic shock, and sudden cardiac death. Common presenting complaints include fatigue, dyspnea on exertion, palpitations, chest pain that may mimic myocardial infarction, and arrhythmias. In advanced stages, signs of left and right ventricular failure emerge, including peripheral edema, jugular venous distension, and pulmonary crackles. Nurses caring for these patients must vigilantly monitor telemetry for lethal dysrhythmias and assess for early signs of decreasing cardiac output.

Pericarditis: Types, Underlying Causes, and Diagnostic Features

Pericarditis involves the inflammation of the pericardium, the fibroserous sac surrounding the heart. It is broadly categorized into acute, subacute, chronic, and constrictive forms. Acute pericarditis can be infectious—most frequently viral (e.g., enteroviruses) or bacterial (historically associated with tuberculosis, which remains highly prevalent in Nigeria)—or non-infectious, such as following a myocardial infarction (Dressler's syndrome), uremia, trauma, or autoimmune disorders. The underlying pathophysiological process involves hyperemia, capillary leakage, and an exudative response of inflammatory cells into the pericardial space. Clinically, the hallmark feature of acute pericarditis is sharp, pleuritic chest pain that is characteristically retrosternal or left precordial, often radiating to the trapezius ridge. This pain typically worsens with inspiration, coughing, and lying flat, and is uniquely relieved by sitting up and leaning forward. Another critical diagnostic feature is the pericardial friction rub, a scratchy, high-pitched sound heard best at the left lower sternal border with the diaphragm of the stethoscope during end-expiration. Electrocardiogram (ECG) changes are also prominent, usually demonstrating widespread, concave-upward ST-segment elevation and PR-segment depression. Nurses must monitor for pericardial effusion, which can progress rapidly to cardiac tamponade—a life-threatening condition characterized by Beck's triad: hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and jugular venous distension.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation for Cardiac Infections

A thorough nursing assessment and systematic diagnostic evaluation are critical for patients suspected of having an infectious cardiac disorder. The nursing assessment begins with a comprehensive health history, probing for recent dental procedures, skin infections, intravenous drug use, or a history of rheumatic fever. Nurses must evaluate vital signs meticulously, watching for persistent low-grade or high fevers, tachycardia out of proportion to fever, and changes in blood pressure that might indicate hemodynamic instability. Physical examination requires auscultation for new murmurs (suggestive of endocarditis), a pericardial friction rub (suggestive of pericarditis), or extra heart sounds like an S3 gallop (indicating myocardial dysfunction). Diagnostic evaluation relies heavily on laboratory and imaging modalities. Blood cultures (typically drawn in sets from different sites before initiating antibiotic therapy) are the cornerstone for identifying causative organisms in infective endocarditis. Complete blood counts often reveal leukocytosis, and inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) are characteristically elevated across all three disorders. Echocardiography—both transthoracic (TTE) and transesophageal (TEE)—is the gold-standard imaging tool used to visualize valvular vegetations, myocardial wall motion abnormalities, and pericardial effusions. Furthermore, 12-lead electrocardiograms (ECGs) assist in identifying conduction abnormalities, arrhythmias, and diffuse ST changes typical of pericarditis or myocarditis.

Medical and Pharmacological Management of Cardiac Infections

Medical and pharmacological management of infectious cardiac disorders centers on eradicating the offending pathogen, controlling inflammation, and supporting failing hemodynamics. For infective endocarditis, prolonged (often 4 to 6 weeks) course of high-dose intravenous (IV) antimicrobial therapy is mandatory, guided strictly by sensitivity results from blood cultures. Nurses play a crucial role in managing central venous access devices, administering IV antibiotics precisely on schedule, and monitoring for adverse drug reactions or signs of superinfection. In myocarditis, treatment is primarily supportive, focusing on managing heart failure and arrhythmias with standard guideline-directed medical therapy, including ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, and diuretics. Immunosuppressive therapy is generally reserved for specific non-viral or autoimmune etiologies. For pericarditis, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen or aspirin form the therapeutic bedrock for pain and inflammation control, frequently combined with colchicine to prevent recurrences. Corticosteroids are used selectively if symptoms persist despite NSAID therapy or if an autoimmune etiology is confirmed. Throughout medical management, nurses must continuously monitor hemodynamic parameters, intake and output, and daily weights to evaluate the effectiveness of fluid management and pharmacological interventions.

Surgical Interventions and Nursing Care

While medical management is the cornerstone of therapy, surgical intervention becomes necessary when complications of cardiac infections threaten life or organ function. In infective endocarditis, surgery is indicated in the presence of severe heart valve dysfunction leading to intractable heart failure, recurrent systemic embolization despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, annular or myocardial abscess formation, or fungal infections that are notoriously refractory to medical cure alone. Surgical options include valve repair or debridement, and complete valve replacement using mechanical or bioprosthetic valves. For pericardial disorders, particularly cardiac tamponade or chronic constrictive pericarditis, surgical decompression via pericardiocentesis (needle aspiration of fluid) or an open surgical pericardiotomy (pericardial window) is performed to relieve intra-pericardial pressure and restore ventricular filling. Postoperative nursing care for patients undergoing cardiac surgery for infections requires intensive care monitoring. Nurses must closely monitor arterial lines, central venous pressure, urine output, and chest tube drainage for hemorrhage or re-accumulation of fluid. Pain management, early mobilization, meticulous surgical site wound care to prevent hospital-acquired infections, and respiratory hygiene (incentive spirometry and coughing exercises) are vital nursing responsibilities to promote recovery and prevent postoperative pulmonary complications.

Complications and Patient Education for Prevention in Nigerian Clinical Contexts

Managing infectious cardiac disorders extends far beyond acute hospital care, requiring an understanding of potential complications and rigorous patient education tailored to the Nigerian clinical context. Common long-term complications include chronic heart failure, recurrent infections, permanent valvular damage, and thromboembolic events such as stroke or pulmonary embolism. In Nigeria, where environmental factors, limited healthcare access, and the high burden of rheumatic fever complicate recovery, patient education must be culturally sensitive, clear, and actionable. Nurses must educate patients and their families on the absolute importance of completing full courses of antibiotic therapy for streptococcal pharyngitis and other bacterial infections to prevent rheumatic fever and subsequent endocarditis. For patients with structural heart disease, prosthetic valves, or a history of endocarditis, nurses must emphasize the necessity of antibiotic prophylaxis before undergoing invasive dental procedures or surgeries. Education should also focus on recognizing early warning signs of recurrence—such as persistent unexplained fever, fatigue, night sweats, or new dyspnea—and seeking immediate medical attention. Empowering patients with lifestyle modification strategies, proper nutrition, and compliance counseling fosters long-term cardiovascular health and significantly reduces preventable morbidity and mortality.
