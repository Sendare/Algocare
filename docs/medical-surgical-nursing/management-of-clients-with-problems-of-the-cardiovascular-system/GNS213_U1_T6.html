Infectious Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Infectious Cardiac Disorders

Infectious cardiac disorders refer to inflammatory and infectious processes affecting the three structural layers of the heart: the inner lining (endocarditis), the middle muscular wall (myocarditis), and the outer protective sac (pericarditis). These conditions can develop rapidly or progress slowly over months, triggered by bacterial, viral, fungal, or parasitic agents. In many tropical settings, pathogens such as *Streptococcus*, *Staphylococcus*, and enteroviruses are commonly implicated, alongside rheumatic heart disease predisposing individuals to endocardial damage. Understanding these disorders requires recognizing that each layer of the heart reacts differently to infection, leading to unique clinical presentations ranging from subtle fatigue to life-threatening heart failure or shock.

Infective Endocarditis: Causes and Pathophysiology

Infective endocarditis is a microbial infection of the endocardium, most commonly affecting the heart valves. The disease process typically begins when turbulent blood flow damages the endothelial lining, creating a sterile platelet-fibrin deposit called non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis. Transient bacteremia—often introduced via dental procedures, intravenous drug use, or skin sores—allows circulating microorganisms to adhere to these damaged areas. Once attached, the bacteria multiply and trigger local inflammation, forming large, fragile clusters known as vegetations. These vegetations interfere with normal valve closure, resulting in new or worsening heart murmurs, and pieces can break off to travel as septic emboli to the brain, lungs, or kidneys.

Myocarditis and Pericarditis: Clinical Features

Myocarditis involves inflammation of the heart muscle, often triggered by viral infections such as Coxsackievirus or adenovirus, though bacterial and parasitic causes also occur. The invading pathogen and subsequent immune response cause cellular damage and necrosis of myocardial fibers, weakening the heart's pumping ability and potentially leading to cardiomyopathy or arrhythmias.

Pericarditis, by contrast, affects the fluid-filled sac surrounding the heart. It is frequently caused by viral infections or post-myocardial infarction syndrome. Patients with pericarditis typically present with sharp, pleuritic chest pain that worsens when lying flat and improves when sitting forward. A hallmark physical sign of pericarditis is a pericardial friction rub heard best at the left sternal border during auscultation.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Nursing Assessment

Diagnosing cardiac infections requires a systematic combination of patient history, physical examination, laboratory testing, and imaging studies. Nurses play a crucial role in gathering baseline data and recognizing subtle clinical changes.

  • Blood Cultures: Essential for identifying the specific causative organism; two or more sets should be drawn from different venipuncture sites before starting antibiotics.
  • Echocardiography: Transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiograms help visualize valve structures, abscesses, and oscillating vegetations.
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG): Frequently reveals conduction abnormalities in myocarditis or diffuse ST-segment elevations in pericarditis.
  • Inflammatory Markers: Elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) indicate active systemic inflammation.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical treatment of cardiac infections focuses on eradicating the infectious agent, reducing inflammation, and managing hemodynamic instability. For bacterial endocarditis, patients require prolonged courses of intravenous (IV) antimicrobial therapy, often lasting four to six weeks. Nurses must monitor IV access sites closely and watch for adverse drug reactions. In cases of acute pericarditis, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen or aspirin are prescribed to relieve pain and reduce inflammation, sometimes combined with colchicine to prevent recurrence. Corticosteroids may be reserved for cases that do not respond to standard therapy or have specific autoimmune origins. Close monitoring of fluid balance and vital signs remains essential to catch early signs of declining cardiac output.

Surgical Interventions and Nursing Care

When medical management fails to control infection, or when structural damage threatens patient survival, surgical intervention becomes necessary. Surgery is frequently indicated in infective endocarditis if the patient develops heart failure due to severe valve regurgitation, persistent bacteremia despite appropriate antibiotics, or recurrent septic embolization. Procedures typically involve valvular repair or surgical valve replacement using mechanical or bioprosthetic valves, alongside the debridement of infected perivalvular tissue.

Postoperative nursing care requires meticulous monitoring of hemodynamics, strict infection control practices during line care, regular neurological checks for embolic events, and early mobilization to prevent deep vein thrombosis.

Complications, Prevention, and Patient Education

Infectious cardiac disorders can lead to severe long-term complications including chronic heart failure, recurrent arrhythmias, stroke, and myocardial abscess formation. In clinical practice across Nigeria, where rheumatic heart disease and delayed presentation of infections remain significant challenges, patient education is vital for secondary prevention. Nurses must educate patients with pre-existing valvular abnormalities about the importance of prophylactic antibiotics before certain invasive dental procedures. Furthermore, individuals recovering from cardiac infections need clear instructions on completing their full course of medication, recognizing early signs of infection recurrence such as unexplained fever or malaise, and maintaining good personal hygiene to minimize future bacterial exposure.
