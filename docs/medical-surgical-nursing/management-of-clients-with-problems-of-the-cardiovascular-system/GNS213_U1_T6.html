Infectious Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Infectious Cardiac Disorders

Infectious cardiac disorders refer to inflammatory processes affecting the three structural layers of the heart: the endocarditis, myocardium, and pericardium. These conditions typically arise when bloodborne pathogens—most commonly bacteria, viruses, or fungi—invade the endocardial surface, heart muscle, or the surrounding fibroserous sac. While healthy cardiac tissue possesses natural resistance against microbial colonization, preexisting structural abnormalities, immunosuppression, or severe systemic infections create vulnerable niches for pathogens to establish colonies. In Nigerian clinical practice, rheumatic heart disease remains a significant predisposing factor for endocardial infections, whereas viral etiologies frequently drive myocardial inflammation. Understanding these conditions requires mapping each disorder to its specific anatomical layer, as the clinical trajectories, diagnostic markers, and treatment protocols differ markedly between an infection inside the valve chambers versus an inflammation of the heart muscle or its outer lining.

Infective Endocarditis: Etiology, Pathophysiology, and Clinical Manifestations

Infective endocarditis is a microbial infection of the endothelial surface of the heart, predominantly involving the heart valves. - **Etiology**: Gram-positive cocci such as *Staphylococcus aureus* and *Streptococcus viridans* are the leading causative agents. Enterococci and fungi also contribute, particularly in healthcare-associated cases. - **Pathophysiology**: Endothelial damage exposes basement membrane collagen and tissue factor, triggering platelet and fibrin deposition to form sterile thrombotic lesions (non-bacterial thrombotic endocarditis). Transient bacteremia—from dental procedures, intravenous drug use, or skin sepsis—seeds these sterile clots, leading to microbial proliferation protected by a fibrin mesh. - **Clinical Manifestations**: Persistent fever is the hallmark symptom. Physical examination often reveals a new or changing cardiac murmur, petechiae, splinter hemorrhages under the fingernails, painful subcutaneous nodes on fingertips (Osler nodes), and non-tender macules on palms and soles (Janeway lesions). In resource-constrained settings, delayed presentation often leads to advanced valve destruction before medical evaluation occurs.

Myocarditis: Causes, Pathogenesis, and Clinical Presentation

Myocarditis involves inflammation of the heart muscle, leading to myocyte necrosis and functional impairment. Viral infections, particularly Coxsackievirus B, enteroviruses, and adenoviruses, are the most frequent triggers globally. In sub-Saharan Africa, endemic viral strains and parasitic infections like *Trypanosoma cruzi* or nutritional deficiencies can also precipitate myocardial injury. The pathogenesis unfolds in distinct phases: initial viral penetration directly injures myocytes, followed by an immune-mediated phase where cellular and humoral responses attack the heart tissue long after the virus has cleared. Patients present with a spectrum of symptoms ranging from asymptomatic ECG changes to fulminant heart failure, cardiogenic shock, and sudden cardiac death. Common complaints include fatigue, dyspnea on exertion, palpitations, and chest pain that mimics acute coronary syndrome. Physical assessment may reveal tachycardia out of proportion to fever, gallop rhythms (S3 or S4), and signs of systemic congestion.

Pericarditis: Types, Underlying Causes, and Diagnostic Features

Pericarditis is an inflammation of the pericardium, the two-layered sac surrounding the heart. The characteristic ECG finding in acute pericarditis is widespread, concave ST-segment elevation coupled with PR-segment depression.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation for Cardiac Infections

Comprehensive nursing assessment forms the backbone of early identification and stabilization in cardiac infections. Nurses must conduct meticulous health histories focusing on recent dental work, skin infections, intravenous drug use, or a history of rheumatic fever. Vital sign monitoring must be rigorous, tracking patterns of relapsing or persistent fevers, unexplained tachypnea, and blood pressure trends. Diagnostic confirmation relies on several key modalities: 1. **Blood Cultures**: Serial sets drawn from separate venipuncture sites to isolate circulating pathogens in endocarditis. 2. **Echocardiography**: Transthoracic (TTE) or transesophageal (TEE) ultrasound to visualize valvular vegetations, abscesses, or pericardial effusion. 3. **Electrocardiography (ECG)**: Identifies conduction abnormalities in myocarditis or diffuse ST changes in pericarditis. 4. **Laboratory Markers**: Elevated white blood cell counts, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and cardiac biomarkers like troponin indicating myocardial injury. Careful observation for subtle signs of systemic embolization—such as sudden neurological deficits or flank pain—is essential during routine nursing shifts.

Medical and Pharmacological Management of Cardiac Infections

Medical management centers on targeted antimicrobial therapy and hemodynamic stabilization. For infective endocarditis, high-dose intravenous bactericidal antibiotics must be administered for an extended duration—often 4 to 6 weeks—to eradicate bacteria nestled deep within avascular valvular vegetations. Therapy is initiated empirically based on likely pathogens but is subsequently adjusted according to blood culture and sensitivity reports. For acute pericarditis, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen or aspirin, combined with colchicine, serve as first-line therapy to control inflammation and prevent recurrences. Corticosteroids are reserved for refractory cases or specific autoimmune and connective tissue etiologies. Myocarditis management focuses on supportive care for heart failure, including ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, and diuretics, while avoiding intense physical exertion that increases myocardial oxygen demand.

Surgical Interventions and Nursing Care

Surgical intervention becomes necessary when medical therapy fails to control a cardiac infection, or when structural damage threatens patient survival. Indications for urgent or emergent cardiac surgery in infective endocarditis include severe acute heart failure from valvular regurgitation, persistent bacteremia despite appropriate antibiotic therapy, fungal endocarditis, and large mobile vegetations carrying a high embolic risk. Procedures involve valve repair or replacement with mechanical or bioprosthetic valves, alongside debridement of infected perivalvular abscesses. For severe constrictive pericarditis, a pericardiectomy is performed to excise the rigid sac and restore ventricular filling. Postoperative nursing care requires intensive monitoring of hemodynamic parameters, strict fluid balance management, assessment for bleeding complications due to anticoagulation, and vigilant monitoring for signs of recurring infection or conduction disturbances requiring pacemaker placement.

Complications and Patient Education for Prevention in Nigerian Clinical Contexts

Infectious cardiac disorders carry severe, life-threatening complications if unrecognized or inadequately managed. These include irreversible heart failure, cardiogenic shock, systemic and cerebral septic embolization, myocardial abscesses, and permanent conduction system blocks. In Nigeria and similar low- and middle-income regions, delayed healthcare seeking and economic barriers often worsen these outcomes. Patient education is the cornerstone of secondary prevention. Individuals with structural heart disease, prosthetic valves, or a history of endocarditis must receive clear instructions regarding antibiotic prophylaxis before invasive dental or surgical procedures. Nurses must educate patients and their families on recognizing early warning signs—such as recurrent fevers, unexplained fatigue, night sweats, or worsening shortness of breath—and emphasize the critical importance of completing full courses of prescribed antibiotics without premature discontinuation.
