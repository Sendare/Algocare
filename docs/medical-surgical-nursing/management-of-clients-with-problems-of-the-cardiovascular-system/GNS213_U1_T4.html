Coronary Artery Disease

Introduction and Definition of Coronary Artery Disease

Coronary artery disease, commonly referred to as CAD, is a condition where the major blood vessels supplying the heart muscle become damaged or diseased. The primary culprit is the buildup of plaque containing cholesterol inside these coronary arteries, which leads to narrowing and reduced blood flow. When the heart muscle fails to receive an adequate supply of oxygen-rich blood, patients experience ischemia. Understanding this foundational concept is critical for nurses, as CAD remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, serving as the underlying trigger for conditions like angina and myocardial infarction.

Pathophysiology and Atherosclerosis Development

The development of CAD is a slow, progressive process called atherosclerosis. It begins with minor injury to the innermost layer of the artery, the endothelium. Once damaged, low-density lipoprotein particles seep into the arterial wall and undergo oxidation. This triggers an inflammatory response where white blood cells rush to the site and engulf the cholesterol, transforming into foam cells. Over time, these foam cells accumulate to form a fatty streak. Eventually, a fibrous cap forms over the fatty core, creating a mature plaque. If this plaque ruptures, platelets stick to the site and form a clot that can completely block the vessel.

Risk Factors and Etiology

Certain factors increase a person's chance of developing CAD. Some of these are non-modifiable, meaning they cannot be changed, while others are modifiable through lifestyle changes and medical treatment.

  • Non-modifiable risk factors: Advanced age, male gender, family history of premature heart disease, and genetics.
  • Modifiable risk factors: Cigarette smoking, high blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, high blood cholesterol, physical inactivity, obesity, and chronic stress.

Clinical Manifestations: Angina and Acute Coronary Syndromes

The clinical presentation of CAD ranges from stable chest discomfort to sudden cardiac events.

  • Stable Angina: Predictable chest pain triggered by physical exertion or emotional stress, lasting a few minutes and relieved by rest or sublingual nitroglycerin.
  • Unstable Angina: Chest pain that occurs unpredictably at rest or worsens in frequency and severity, signaling an unstable plaque.
  • Myocardial Infarction: Complete blockage of a coronary artery leading to necrosis of the heart muscle tissue. Patients often present with crushing chest pressure radiating to the jaw or left arm, accompanied by diaphoresis, nausea, and shortness of breath.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Investigations

Diagnosing CAD involves a combination of patient history, laboratory tests, and specialized imaging procedures.

  • Electrocardiogram: Detects abnormal electrical activity, ST-segment changes, or signs of past and current injury.
  • Cardiac Biomarkers: Blood tests like troponin rise when heart muscle cells are damaged or dying.
  • Stress Testing: Evaluates how the heart handles physical stress induced by exercise or medications.
  • Coronary Angiography: The gold standard diagnostic test where a catheter is threaded into the coronary arteries and contrast dye is injected to visualize blockages using X-rays.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management aims to relieve symptoms, slow disease progression, and prevent future cardiac events. Commonly prescribed medications include antiplatelet agents like aspirin to prevent clots, statins to lower cholesterol levels, and beta-blockers to reduce the heart's workload by lowering heart rate and blood pressure. Vasodilators such as nitroglycerin are used to open up coronary vessels and relieve acute chest discomfort.

Surgical Interventions: PCI and CABG

When medications are not enough to control blockages, surgical and invasive procedures become necessary.

  • Percutaneous Coronary Intervention: A non-surgical procedure where a balloon catheter is threaded to the blockage site, inflated to flatten the plaque, and usually followed by the placement of a wire mesh stent to keep the vessel open.
  • Coronary Artery Bypass Graft: An open-heart surgical procedure where a healthy blood vessel harvested from another part of the body, such as the saphenous vein or internal mammary artery, is used to bypass the blocked coronary artery, restoring blood flow to the heart muscle.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Care Planning

Nurses play a vital role in monitoring patients with CAD and preventing complications. Assessment focuses heavily on monitoring vital signs, evaluating chest pain characteristics using the old-fashioned PQRST mnemonic, and observing for signs of heart failure such as breathlessness or ankle swelling. Care planning involves educating patients on lifestyle modifications, managing prescribed drug schedules, and promoting safe physical activity levels.

Complications and Cardiac Rehabilitation

Living with CAD exposes patients to serious long-term complications including heart failure, dangerous arrhythmias, and recurrent heart attacks. Cardiac rehabilitation programs are essential to help patients recover safely and improve their overall quality of life. These structured programs combine supervised physical exercise, nutritional counseling, smoking cessation support, and psychological guidance to help patients adopt heart-healthy habits for the long term.
