Coronary Artery Disease

Introduction and Definition of Coronary Artery Disease

Coronary Artery Disease, commonly abbreviated as CAD, represents a pathological condition where the major blood vessels supplying the heart muscle become damaged or diseased. At its core, the disorder is driven by the narrowing or obstruction of the coronary arteries, which are responsible for delivering oxygen-rich blood, glucose, and vital nutrients directly to the myocardium. When these arteries are compromised, the heart muscle cannot meet its metabolic demands, particularly during periods of increased physical exertion or emotional stress.

Pathologically, CAD is the leading manifestation of cardiovascular disease globally and remains a primary contributor to morbidity and mortality within adult populations in Nigeria and worldwide. Understanding this condition requires recognizing that it is rarely an acute event; rather, it represents the chronic, cumulative result of long-term vascular injury. Over time, structural changes within the arterial walls alter blood flow dynamics, setting the stage for ischemia, cardiac dysfunction, and potentially life-threatening acute coronary events.

Pathophysiology and Atherosclerosis Development

The development of Coronary Artery Disease is fundamentally rooted in atherosclerosis, a slow, progressive inflammatory disease of the arterial wall. The pathological sequence unfolds through several distinct stages:

  1. Endothelial Injury: The delicate inner lining of the coronary artery, the endothelium, suffers damage from factors like hypertension, smoking, or elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol.
  2. Lipid Accumulation: Circulating low-density lipoproteins penetrate the damaged endothelium and become trapped in the intima, where they undergo oxidation.
  3. Foam Cell Formation: Macrophages migrate into the arterial wall, engulf the oxidized lipids, and transform into lipid-laden cells known as foam cells, creating early fatty streaks.
  4. Plaque Maturation: Smooth muscle cells migrate from the media to the intima, proliferating and forming a fibrous cap over the lipid core, producing a mature atherosclerotic plaque.
  5. Rupture and Thrombosis: If the fibrous cap is thin or unstable, it may rupture, exposing thrombogenic material to the bloodstream and triggering acute clot formation.

Risk Factors and Etiology

Aetiological factors for Coronary Artery Disease are divided into non-modifiable and modifiable categories. Recognizing these variables guides both primary prevention and clinical risk stratification.

  • Non-Modifiable Risk Factors: Advanced age, male biological sex, a family history of premature cardiovascular disease, and genetic predisposition.
  • Modifiable Risk Factors: Cigarette smoking, systemic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia (high LDL and low HDL cholesterol), obesity, physical inactivity, and chronic psychological stress.

In contemporary Nigerian clinical practice, the epidemiological transition has driven a sharp rise in urban hypertension and type 2 diabetes, directly increasing the local burden of premature CAD. Addressing modifiable lifestyle behaviours remains the cornerstone of reducing long-term cardiovascular events.

Clinical Manifestations: Angina Pectoris and Acute Coronary Syndromes

Clinical presentations of CAD vary along a spectrum from stable chronic conditions to life-threatening emergencies. Angina pectoris presents as transient chest discomfort caused by myocardial ischemia that does not result in cellular death. Stable angina is predictable, triggered by exertion, and relieved by rest or sublingual nitroglycerin.

When plaque erosion or rupture causes abrupt, severe vessel occlusion, the patient experiences an Acute Coronary Syndrome. ACS encompasses unstable angina, non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, and ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Classic symptoms include retrosternal chest pressure radiating to the left arm, neck, or jaw, accompanied by diaphoresis, nausea, and shortness of breath. Nigerian nurses must remain vigilant, as atypical presentations—such as epigastric discomfort or breathlessness alone—frequently occur in elderly patients and those with diabetes.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Investigations

Confirming a diagnosis of CAD and assessing myocardial damage requires a structured diagnostic workup. The standard investigations include:

  • 12-Lead Electrocardiogram: Evaluates electrical activity, identifying ST-segment changes, T-wave inversions, or pathological Q-waves indicative of ischemia or infarction.
  • Cardiac Biomarkers: Blood tests measuring troponin T or I and creatine kinase-MB. Elevated troponins indicate myocardial necrosis and confirm acute myocardial infarction.
  • Stress Testing: Evaluates heart function during physical exertion or pharmacologically induced stress using a treadmill or agents like dobutamine.
  • Coronary Angiography: The gold standard diagnostic procedure involving catheter insertion and radiopaque contrast injection to directly visualize anatomical stenoses in the coronary tree.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacotherapy for CAD aims to reduce myocardial oxygen demand, increase oxygen supply, prevent thrombus formation, and modify underlying risk factors. The foundational drug classes include antiplatelet agents such as aspirin and clopidogrel to prevent platelet aggregation, statins to lower low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and stabilize plaques, beta-blockers to reduce heart rate and myocardial contractility, and nitrates for immediate vasodilation and angina relief. ACE inhibitors are also utilized to manage blood pressure and prevent ventricular remodeling after ischemic insults. Clinical nurses play a pivotal role in monitoring patients for adverse drug reactions, such as bleeding risks with antiplatelet therapy or symptomatic hypotension with nitrates.

Surgical Interventions: PCI and CABG

When medical management fails to control ischemic symptoms or when anatomical lesions are severe, revascularization procedures become necessary. Percutaneous Coronary Intervention is a minimally invasive catheter-based procedure where a balloon-tipped catheter is advanced to the site of the stenosis, inflated to flatten the plaque, and typically secured with a stent to maintain vessel patency.

Alternatively, Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting is an open-heart surgical procedure. It involves harvesting healthy blood vessels—such as the great saphenous vein from the leg or the internal mammary artery from the chest wall—and anastomosing them around the blocked coronary segments to restore robust blood flow to the distal myocardium. Nurses managing post-operative CABG patients must closely monitor haemodynamic stability, chest tube drainage, surgical site integrity, and neurological status.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Care Planning

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with CAD involves rigorous continuous monitoring and individualized care planning. Initial assessment focuses on evaluating chest pain characteristics using standard pain descriptors, checking vital signs for signs of hemodynamic compromise, and assessing peripheral perfusion.

Care planning prioritizes pain management, optimizing cardiac output, reducing anxiety, and delivering targeted health education. Education must emphasize lifestyle modification, including adopting heart-healthy diets low in saturated fats and sodium, smoking cessation counseling, and encouraging safe, graded physical activity. Because cultural dietary patterns in Nigeria often feature high salt and palm oil intake, dietary counseling must be practical, respectful of local food choices, and tailored to the patient's socioeconomic reality.

Complications and Cardiac Rehabilitation

Patients with Coronary Artery Disease remain vulnerable to serious complications, including heart failure, cardiogenic shock, life-threatening arrhythmias such as ventricular fibrillation, and mechanical defects like papillary muscle rupture.

To mitigate these risks and optimize recovery, cardiac rehabilitation is initiated. This structured, multidisciplinary program includes supervised physical exercise, nutritional counseling, risk factor modification, and psychosocial support. Cardiac rehabilitation is divided into phases starting inpatient and progressing to outpatient community maintenance. Participation significantly reduces mortality, decreases hospital readmission rates, and improves the overall quality of life for survivors of cardiovascular events.
