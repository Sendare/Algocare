Assessment of Clients with Cardiovascular Disorders

Introduction to Cardiovascular Assessment

Cardiovascular assessment is a foundational skill for nursing students and professional nurses practicing in Nigeria and globally. It involves a systematic approach to evaluating the heart and peripheral vascular system to detect actual or potential health problems. The process integrates anatomical knowledge with clinical reasoning, enabling nurses to identify hemodynamic instability, heart failure, arrhythmias, and ischemic conditions early. Because cardiovascular diseases are increasingly prevalent in the Nigerian population, mastering this assessment process is vital for prompt intervention and improved patient outcomes. A thorough cardiovascular evaluation includes gathering a comprehensive health history, performing a focused physical examination utilizing inspection, palpation, auscultation, and percussion, and interpreting relevant diagnostic investigations. Furthermore, nurses must understand how cultural and environmental factors within the Nigerian healthcare context can influence a patient's presentation and adherence to treatment regimens. Effective assessment relies on systematic execution, ensuring that subtle changes in a client's condition do not go unnoticed, thereby bridging the gap between clinical theory and practical bedside nursing.

Health History

The health history is a critical component of cardiovascular assessment, providing subjective data about the client's current status and past cardiac health. When interviewing a client in a Nigerian clinical setting, the nurse must establish a trusting therapeutic relationship and use culturally sensitive communication. Key areas to explore include chief complaints such as chest pain, dyspnoea, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, palpitations, fatigue, and peripheral edema. The nurse should utilize the PQRST mnemonic (Provocation, Quality, Region/Radiation, Severity, Time) to thoroughly evaluate pain or discomfort. Additionally, the history must cover past medical history, including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, rheumatic fever, and previous cardiac events. Family history is equally important, as genetic predispositions to cardiovascular diseases are significant risk factors. Social history should evaluate lifestyle habits such as smoking, alcohol consumption, dietary patterns rich in sodium or saturated fats, and physical activity levels. In Nigeria, assessing socioeconomic stressors and traditional medicine use is also crucial, as these factors heavily influence medication adherence and disease progression.

Physical Assessment

Physical assessment of the cardiovascular system follows a structured sequence: inspection, palpation, and auscultation, while percussion is rarely used today for heart size estimation. The nurse begins by inspecting the general appearance, skin color, and mucous membranes for signs of cyanosis or pallor, and examines the neck veins for jugular venous distension (JVD), which indicates elevated right atrial pressure. Palpation focuses on feeling peripheral pulses for rate, rhythm, and amplitude, checking capillary refill time, and locating the point of maximal impulse (PMI) on the chest wall. Auscultation requires a quiet environment and a stethoscope with both a diaphragm and bell. The nurse listens systematically across the traditional auscultatory areas: aortic, pulmonic, erb's point, tricuspid, and mitral areas, identifying normal S1 and S2 heart sounds, and listening for abnormal sounds such as murmurs, gallops (S3, S4), and friction rubs. Peripheral vascular assessment includes checking for dependent edema in the lower extremities, assessing skin temperature, and evaluating peripheral pulses in the arms and legs. These objective findings provide immediate clues about cardiac output, valve function, and tissue perfusion.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnostic evaluations provide objective data to confirm or rule out cardiovascular disorders suspected during history taking and physical examination. As a nursing student in Nigeria, understanding these tests is essential for patient preparation, monitoring, and post-procedure care. The 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is a primary diagnostic tool used to assess the electrical activity of the heart, identifying arrhythmias, myocardial ischemia, and infarction. Laboratory tests are equally critical; cardiac biomarkers such as Troponin I and T, Creatine Kinase-MB (CK-MB), and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) help diagnose acute coronary syndromes and heart failure. Routine blood work, including lipid profiles, electrolytes, and full blood count, evaluates risk factors and systemic health. Imaging studies such as chest radiography evaluate heart size and pulmonary congestion, while echocardiography utilizes ultrasound to visualize cardiac structures, valve motion, and ejection fraction. More advanced procedures like cardiac catheterization and coronary angiography may be available in specialized Nigerian tertiary centers to visualize coronary anatomy directly. Nurses must educate clients, manage anxiety, monitor vital signs, and ensure safe pre- and post-test care for all diagnostic procedures.

Documentation and Reporting in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Accurate documentation and effective reporting are professional legal and ethical duties for nurses practicing in clinical environments across Nigeria. Documentation of cardiovascular assessments must be objective, concise, timely, and legible, utilizing either paper-based nursing kardex systems or electronic health records where available. Nurses must record vital sign trends, specific characteristics of heart sounds, descriptions of edema, pulse qualities, and patient responses to interventions. In Nigerian tertiary or general hospitals, shift reports must follow a standardized format such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to ensure clear communication among the multidisciplinary healthcare team, including physicians, pharmacists, and fellow nurses. Critical findings—such as sudden chest pain, severe dyspnoea, significant blood pressure drops, or lethal arrhythmias—must be reported immediately to the attending physician or medical officer on duty. Proper documentation provides a legal record of care delivered, ensures continuity of care across shifts, and protects both the patient and the nursing professional. Failure to document or report critical cardiovascular changes promptly can lead to delayed medical intervention and compromised patient safety.
