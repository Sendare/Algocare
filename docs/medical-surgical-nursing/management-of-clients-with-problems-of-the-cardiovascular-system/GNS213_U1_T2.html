Assessment of Clients with Cardiovascular Disorders

Introduction to Cardiovascular Assessment

Assessing the cardiovascular system requires a systematic approach to uncover hidden heart problems before they cause severe damage. Nurses combine subjective data from patient interviews with objective findings from physical exams and diagnostic tests. Recognizing normal heart sounds and pulse patterns forms the baseline for spotting dangerous irregularities during daily ward rounds.

Taking a Focused Health History

A thorough cardiac history begins by listening closely to the patient's chief complaints. Nurses must ask targeted questions about specific symptoms that point directly to heart distress.

  • Chest Pain: Ask about the exact location, radiation to the jaw or left arm, and what makes the pain better or worse.
  • Dyspnea: Inquire about shortness of breath during daily activities or when lying flat at night.
  • Palpitations: Check if the client feels a racing, pounding, or irregular heartbeat.
  • Edema: Look for swelling in the feet, ankles, or lower legs, which often signals fluid overload.
  • Past Medical History: Note any personal or family history of hypertension, diabetes, rheumatic fever, or heart disease.

Physical Examination Techniques

The physical examination follows the classic clinical sequence: inspection, palpation, and auscultation. Percussion is rarely used today for routine heart checks because imaging tools are far more accurate.

Vital Signs and Peripheral Vascular Assessment

Blood pressure and pulse readings tell a vital story about the heart's workload. Nurses must check pulses in both upper and lower extremities, comparing them side by side for strength and symmetry. Weak or absent pedal pulses often point to peripheral artery disease or poor cardiac output. Capillary refill time on the fingers or toes offers a quick look at peripheral tissue perfusion.

Diagnostic Tests and Investigations

Physical exams alone cannot confirm every heart condition, so nurses prepare clients for various diagnostic tests. An electrocardiogram (ECG) records the electrical activity of the heart to catch arrhythmias and signs of heart muscle damage. Blood tests like cardiac enzymes show if heart muscle cells are dying. Chest X-rays reveal the overall size of the heart and check for fluid buildup in the lungs.

Documentation and Reporting in Nigerian Clinical Practice

Accurate documentation is a legal and professional duty for Nigerian nurses. Every cardiovascular assessment must be recorded clearly in the patient's bedside chart using approved hospital formats like SOAP or narrative notes. Critical findings, such as a sudden drop in blood pressure or severe chest pain, must be reported immediately to the attending physician or medical officer on duty, with the exact time of the report noted in the records.
