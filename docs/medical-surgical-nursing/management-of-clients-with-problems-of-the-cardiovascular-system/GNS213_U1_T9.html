Cardiac Trauma

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Cardiac Trauma

Cardiac trauma involves physical damage to the heart muscle, valves, or coronary vessels, carrying a notoriously high mortality rate before the patient even reaches a healthcare facility. When severe force impacts the chest, the heart can suffer direct contusion, laceration, or rupture. The physiological consequence depends heavily on whether the injury damages the muscular wall or the pericardial sac surrounding the heart.

If blood leaks rapidly into the rigid pericardial space, it causes cardiac tamponade. This restricts the heart's ability to expand and fill with blood during diastole, causing a drastic drop in cardiac output and systemic blood pressure. Alternatively, massive hemorrhage into the chest cavity from a free wall rupture leads to rapid exsanguination and electromechanical dissociation, commonly presenting as pulseless electrical activity (PEA).

Classification: Penetrating vs. Blunt Cardiac Injuries

Cardiac injuries are broadly divided into two categories based on the nature of the physical force: penetrating and blunt.

  • Penetrating Cardiac Injury: Caused by sharp objects like knives, spikes, or high-velocity projectiles like bullets. These injuries often create clear entry wounds and carry a high risk of severe hemorrhage or cardiac tamponade. The right ventricle is the most commonly injured chamber simply due to its anterior anatomical position in the chest.
  • Blunt Cardiac Injury (BCI): Caused by deceleration forces, direct blows to the sternum, or blast injuries. Unlike penetrating trauma, the skin and chest wall may remain intact, making BCI notoriously difficult to diagnose. It encompasses a spectrum from mild myocardial contusion with localized bruising to complete chamber rupture.

Etiology and Common Mechanisms of Injury

Understanding how an injury happened helps nurses anticipate specific types of internal damage.

  • Motor Vehicle Collisions: Rapid deceleration throws the driver against the steering wheel, causing severe blunt force trauma to the sternum and underlying heart.
  • Stab and Gunshot Wounds: Interpersonal violence or accidents involving sharp instruments directly pierce the pericardium and heart muscle.
  • Crush Injuries: Heavy machinery or structural collapses squeeze the chest, drastically raising intrathoracic pressure.
  • Iatrogenic Causes: Accidental perforation of the heart or great vessels during medical procedures such as central venous catheter insertion, pacemaker placement, or cardiac catheterization.

Clinical Manifestations and Recognition

Recognizing cardiac trauma requires a keen eye for subtle signs of cardiovascular collapse. Patients may present with classic indicators or vague complaints that mask life-internal bleeding.

Beck's triad is the classic clinical hallmark of cardiac tamponade, consisting of muffled heart sounds, distended neck veins, and profound hypotension. However, nurses should remember that this triad is absent in many patients.

Other clinical signs include unexplained shock, chest pain mimicking a myocardial infarction, dyspnea, irregular heart rhythms, and external signs of chest trauma like sternal bruises or seatbelt marks. If a patient with chest trauma has weak, thready pulses and fails to respond to fluid resuscitation, internal cardiac compromise must be suspected immediately.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Emergency Assessment

Rapid diagnosis is vital when managing unstable cardiac trauma patients. Time-consuming tests are avoided in emergency situations where immediate surgical intervention is required.

  1. Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST Exam): Performed right at the bedside to quickly detect fluid in the pericardial sac or abdominal cavity.
  2. Electrocardiogram (ECG): Identifies arrhythmias, ST-segment changes mimicking a heart attack, or conduction blocks resulting from myocardial contusion.
  3. Chest Radiography: May show a widened mediastinum, rib fractures, or an enlarged, water-bottle shaped heart silhouette indicating pericardial effusion.
  4. Echocardiography: Provides a detailed look at heart valve function, wall motion abnormalities, and active bleeding inside the pericardium.

Immediate Nursing Interventions and Resuscitation

When a patient presents with suspected cardiac trauma, nursing actions focus on stabilizing vital signs and preparing for emergency procedures. High-flow oxygen is administered immediately to maximize tissue oxygenation. Large-bore intravenous lines are secured to infuse fluids or blood products, though fluid administration must be carefully monitored to avoid worsening bleeding before surgical repair.

Nurses must continuously monitor cardiac rhythms because life-threatening arrhythmias like ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia can occur suddenly. Emergency equipment, including a defibrillator, suction apparatus, and intubation tray, must be kept ready at the bedside. If the patient goes into traumatic cardiac arrest, rapid preparation for emergency department thoracotomy may be necessary to save their life.

Medical and Surgical Management

Definitive care for severe cardiac trauma almost always requires surgical intervention. Patients with penetrating injuries or cardiac rupture need emergency surgery, usually via sternotomy or thoracotomy, to directly suture lacerations and relieve cardiac tamponade through a pericardiotomy.

For stable patients with blunt cardiac injury and myocardial contusion, management is largely medical. This involves admission to a monitored step-down or intensive care unit for continuous ECG monitoring, pain control, and supportive care for arrhythmias or heart failure. In cases of acute pericardial tamponade where surgery is delayed, an emergency pericardiocentesis—inserting a needle into the pericardial sac to draw out blood—may be performed as a temporary lifesaving measure.

Potential Complications and Post-Operative Care

Post-operative nursing care for cardiac trauma survivors requires vigilance for delayed complications. Heart failure can develop if the heart muscle was severely damaged or if a heart valve was ruptured during the impact. Persistent or new-onset arrhythmias require careful antiarrhythmic therapy and continuous heart rhythm monitoring.

Infections, including surgical site infections, mediastinitis, or pneumonia, are significant risks following chest surgery and open trauma. Nurses must monitor surgical incisions, track white blood cell counts, and encourage incentive spirometry to prevent pulmonary complications. Long-term follow-up is necessary to assess functional recovery and psychological well-being, as survivors frequently experience anxiety or post-traumatic stress disorder.
