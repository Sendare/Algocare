Cardiac Trauma

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Cardiac Trauma

Cardiac trauma encompasses physical injuries to the heart muscle, valves, coronary arteries, or pericardium, representing one of the most critical surgical emergencies in emergency nursing and trauma care. The pathophysiology of cardiac trauma generally splits into blunt and penetrating mechanisms, each leading to distinct disruptions in cardiac output and systemic perfusion. When the heart sustains injury, primary concerns include acute hemorrhage, cardiac tamponade, structural disruption such as valvular incompetence or septal rupture, and lethal dysrhythmias including ventricular fibrillation or pulseless electrical activity (PEA). In blunt cardiac trauma, sudden deceleration or direct compressive forces can bruise the myocardium (cardiac contusion), compromise chamber integrity, or trigger fatal electrical instability without necessarily breaking the chest wall. Conversely, penetrating trauma involves sharp or high-velocity objects directly breaching the pericardial and myocardial layers, resulting in rapid exsanguination or sealing of the pericardial sac that triggers obstructive shock via cardiac tamponade. Understanding these foundational pathophysiological mechanisms is crucial for nursing students in Nigeria, as rapid recognition dictates whether a patient survives the golden hour of trauma resuscitation. Nurses must recognize that even minor-appearing chest trauma can precipitate severe hemodynamic collapse due to the heart's high metabolic demand and limited physiological reserve.

Classification: Penetrating vs. Blunt Cardiac Injuries

Cardiac injuries are broadly classified into two main categories: penetrating cardiac injuries and blunt cardiac injuries. Penetrating cardiac injuries (PCIs) are caused by stab wounds, gunshot wounds, or other foreign objects that directly puncture the chest wall, pericardium, and heart chambers. These injuries frequently present with massive hemothorax or rapidly developing cardiac tamponade due to blood accumulating within the rigid pericardial sac, which restricts ventricular filling. The right ventricle is the most commonly injured chamber in penetrating trauma because of its anterior anatomical position just beneath the sternum. On the other hand, blunt cardiac injuries (BCIs) result from high-energy transfer forces, most commonly motor vehicle collisions involving steering wheel impact, falls from a height, or crush injuries. BCI encompasses a spectrum of pathology ranging from minor sub-epicardial petechiae and myocardial contension to severe chamber rupture, coronary artery dissection, and acute disruption of the chordae tendineae or heart valves. Unlike penetrating injuries where external wounds guide immediate suspicion, blunt cardiac injuries can be notoriously insidious, masking internal cardiac damage behind normal-looking skin and intact ribs. For nursing students, distinguishing between these classifications helps anticipate the specific diagnostic and surgical interventions required, as penetrating injuries often demand immediate surgical exploration, whereas blunt injuries frequently rely on rigorous cardiac monitoring, echocardiography, and supportive hemodynamic management.

Etiology and Common Mechanisms of Injury

The etiology and mechanisms of cardiac trauma vary across different clinical settings, heavily influenced by regional trauma demographics such as road traffic crashes, industrial accidents, and interpersonal violence. Blunt cardiac injuries are most frequently seen in high-speed vehicular accidents where the driver's chest strikes the steering wheel, or pedestrians are struck by cars. Sudden deceleration creates a hydraulic effect, propelling the heart against the sternum, while blast injuries from explosions can also cause severe internal cardiac contusions or rupture. Conversely, penetrating mechanisms involve structural breaches from knives, machetes, arrows, and firearms. Gunshot wounds introduce high-energy cavitation effects that destroy surrounding myocardial tissue in addition to the direct bullet track, whereas stab wounds often cause cleaner lacerations that may temporarily seal via clot formation, delaying the onset of lethal symptoms. Iatrogenic mechanisms represent another important category of etiology that nurses must recognize, including accidental cardiac perforation during central venous catheter insertion, transvenous pacemaker placement, cardiac catheterization, or cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) involving aggressive chest compressions in frail patients. Understanding these etiologies assists emergency and critical care nurses in rapidly correlating the mechanism of injury with the potential internal cardiac pathology during the primary survey.

Clinical Manifestations and Recognition

Recognizing the clinical manifestations of cardiac trauma requires a sharp clinical eye, as signs and symptoms can range from subtle chest discomfort to overt, catastrophic cardiovascular collapse. The hallmark clinical sign of cardiac tamponade is Beck's triad: hypotension, muffled heart sounds, and distended neck veins. However, nurses must remember that Beck's triad is only present in a minority of patients and hypovolemia can mask jugular venous distention. Other classic signs include pulsus paradoxus (an exaggerated decrease in systolic blood pressure during inspiration), signs of obstructive or hemorrhagic shock like cool, clammy skin, tachycardia, tachypnea, and altered mental status. Patients may also complain of sharp chest pain radiating to the back or neck, dyspnea, and restlessness. In cases of blunt myocardial contusion, the presentation can mimic an acute myocardial infarction, featuring chest pain refractory to nitrates, new ST-segment or T-wave abnormalities on an ECG, and elevated cardiac biomarkers such as troponin. Furthermore, traumatic disruption of heart valves or the interventricular septum manifests as sudden, harsh murmurs accompanied by acute heart failure and pulmonary edema. Nurses caring for trauma patients in Nigerian emergency departments must continuously assess vital signs, monitor heart rhythms, and perform serial physical examinations because clinical deterioration can occur within minutes if cardiac tamponade or internal rupture is missed.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Emergency Assessment

Diagnostic evaluation of a patient with suspected cardiac trauma must happen simultaneously with emergency resuscitation, adhering to Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) protocols. The primary diagnostic tool utilized directly at the bedside in the trauma bay is the Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) scan, specifically the pericardial view. A FAST scan can rapidly detect pericardial fluid or blood, confirming cardiac tamponade within seconds without moving an unstable patient. A standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) is essential to identify arrhythmias, conduction delays, or ischemic changes suggestive of myocardial contusion or coronary artery injury. Chest radiography (CXR) may reveal a widened mediastinum, abnormal cardiac silhouette (the classic 'water-bottle' heart in tamponade), rib fractures, or hemothorax, though a normal chest X-ray does not rule out penetrating cardiac injury. For patients who are hemodynamically stable enough to be transported, more advanced diagnostic tools such as Computed Tomography (CT) angiography or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provide definitive visualization of great vessel injury, septal defects, and valvular disruption. Laboratory studies, including complete blood counts, arterial blood gases, type and cross-match for massive transfusion protocols, and serial cardiac biomarkers (troponin I or T), guide ongoing resuscitation. Nursing students must understand that diagnostic delays can be fatal; therefore, the nurse's role includes preparing equipment for immediate bedside diagnostics and monitoring patient stability during every phase of evaluation.

Immediate Nursing Interventions and Resuscitation

Immediate nursing interventions and resuscitation for cardiac trauma focus on restoring effective circulation, maintaining oxygenation, and preparing for urgent surgical intervention. The nurse must immediately secure large-bore peripheral intravenous lines (16-gauge or larger) or assist with central venous access to facilitate aggressive fluid resuscitation and administration of blood products using rapid infusers. Oxygen therapy should be initiated, and endotracheal intubation may be required; however, nurses must exercise extreme caution during positive pressure ventilation in patients with suspected cardiac tamponade or tension pneumothorax, as positive pressure can drastically drop preload and precipitate cardiac arrest. Continuous ECG monitoring, pulse oximetry, and invasive arterial line blood pressure monitoring are vital for real-time hemodynamic tracking. If the patient presents in extremis or suffers traumatic cardiac arrest, emergency department thoracotomy (EDT) may be performed, and the nurse must rapidly assemble thoracotomy trays, internal defibrillator paddles, and massive transfusion supplies. Pericardiocentesis—needle aspiration of the pericardial sac—may serve as a temporary bridge to surgery when cardiac tamponade causes profound shock and immediate surgical opening is unavailable, though it carries risks of coronary artery laceration or myocardial puncture. Throughout this high-stress resuscitation, the nurse maintains clear communication with the trauma team, documents interventions meticulously, and advocates for the patient's urgent transition to the operating theatre.

Medical and Surgical Management

Definitive management of cardiac trauma requires prompt surgical intervention in an operating theatre, where surgeons can directly visualize and repair structural injuries. For patients undergoing emergency department thoracotomy or formal operative exploration, median sternotomy or left anterolateral thoracotomy is performed to gain access to the pericardium and heart. Once the pericardium is opened to relieve tamponade, active bleeding sites are controlled using temporary measures such as digital pressure, Foley catheter balloon tamponade (carefully inflating a catheter bulb inside a stab wound), or non-crushing vascular clamps, followed by definitive suture repair using pledgeted polypropylene sutures to prevent tearing of fragile myocardial tissue. If coronary arteries are lacerated, bypass grafting or primary repair may be necessary depending on the vessel involved. Valvular or septal disruptions identified intraoperatively or post-stabilization are repaired or replaced using cardiopulmonary bypass. Medical management runs parallel to surgical correction, involving inotropic support (such as dobutamine or epinephrine) to optimize myocardial contractility, vasopressors for vascular tone if needed, antiarrhythmic medications for lethal rhythms, and correction of coagulopathies through the administration of fresh frozen plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitate. For nursing students, understanding these surgical principles highlights the critical intersection between intraoperative care, intensive care unit (ICU) recovery, and interprofessional teamwork.

Potential Complications and Post-Operative Care

Post-operative care and the management of potential complications following cardiac trauma require vigilant, round-the-clock nursing surveillance in a cardiothoracic intensive care unit (ICU). Common post-operative complications include low cardiac output syndrome, persistent or recurrent cardiac tamponade, cardiac arrhythmias (such as atrial fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia, or heart blocks), myocardial infarction, thromboembolic events, and surgical site or mediastinal infections. The nurse's responsibilities include continuous hemodynamic monitoring via arterial and pulmonary artery catheters, strict fluid balance management, monitoring chest tube output for hemorrhage or air leaks (reporting sudden surges in blood loss immediately), and administering prescribed inotropes, analgesics, and broad-spectrum prophylactic antibiotics. Nurses must also monitor for signs of systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and acute kidney injury secondary to periods of profound shock during resuscitation. As the patient stabilizes, nursing care shifts toward early extubation, progressive mobilization, pain control, nutritional support, and psychological rehabilitation to address post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), which is highly prevalent among trauma survivors. Providing holistic, empathetic care ensures that Nigerian nursing students graduate ready to support both the physical and emotional recovery of cardiac trauma survivors.
