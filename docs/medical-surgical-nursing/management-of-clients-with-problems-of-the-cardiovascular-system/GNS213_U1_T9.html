Cardiac Trauma

Pathophysiology and Classification of Cardiac Trauma

Cardiac trauma involves structural damage to the heart muscle, valves, coronary vessels, or pericardium. It is broadly divided into two categories: penetrating cardiac trauma, where an external object breaches the pericardial sac and heart wall, and blunt cardiac trauma, which results from rapid deceleration, compression, or blast forces without an open chest wound. Penetrating injuries typically cause rapid exsanguination or cardiac tamponade, while blunt injuries can produce myocardial contusion, chamber rupture, or acute valvular disruption. In Nigerian emergency departments, penetrating wounds from stab or gunshot incidents frequently present alongside blunt trauma from road traffic collisions involving commercial motorcycles or vehicles.

Etiology and Mechanism of Injury

Understanding how the injury occurred guides the initial nursing assessment and anticipation of complications.

  • Stab Wounds: Usually caused by knives, scissors, or local implements; often create a tamponade effect if the pericardial wound seals quickly.
  • Gunshot Wounds: High-energy missile injuries that cause extensive tissue destruction, massive hemorrhage, and multi-chamber damage.
  • Motor Vehicle Accidents: Rapid deceleration throws the sternum against the steering wheel or dashboard, compressing the heart between the sternum and vertebral column.
  • Blast Injuries: Pressure waves from explosions cause direct contusion and microvascular disruption within the myocardium.

Clinical Manifestations and Beck's Triad

Recognizing the signs of acute cardiac compromise is critical for survival. Beck's triad is the classic clinical presentation of cardiac tamponade: muffled heart sounds, distended neck veins, and systemic hypotension. However, this triad does not appear in every patient. Patients may also present with paradoxical pulse, severe dyspnea, agitation, cyanosis, and cool, clammy skin. When a patient with a chest injury develops rapid hemodynamic instability accompanied by elevated central venous pressure, nurses must immediately suspect acute pericardial filling restriction or major internal hemorrhage.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Emergency Assessment

Rapid diagnostic workup is essential when cardiac injury is suspected. The Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) exam is the bedside gold standard in emergency settings to detect pericardial fluid or hemothorax. A 12-lead electrocardiogram helps identify ischemic changes, arrhythmias, or myocardial contusion patterns. Chest radiography may show an widened mediastinum or enlarged cardiac silhouette, though it can appear normal in acute tamponade. Laboratory studies include serial cardiac biomarkers and arterial blood gases to monitor tissue perfusion and oxygenation status.

Immediate Nursing Interventions and Resuscitation

When managing cardiac trauma, the nursing team acts swiftly to secure the airway, optimize breathing, and restore circulation. Large-bore peripheral intravenous lines are established immediately for rapid fluid and blood product administration. However, aggressive fluid loading must be carefully balanced to avoid worsening bleeding before surgical control is achieved. Nurses prepare for emergency procedures such as pericardiocentesis or needle decompression. Continuous electrocardiographic monitoring, pulse oximetry, and frequent vital sign checks are mandatory to detect sudden deterioration.

Surgical Management and Post-Operative Care

Definitive management often requires an emergency department thoracotomy or urgent transfer to the operating theater for sternotomy. Surgeons relieve cardiac tamponade, repair myocardial lacerations, suture coronary vessels if feasible, and evacuate hemothorax. Post-operative care centers on hemodynamic stabilization in the intensive care unit. Nurses monitor chest tube output closely, noting any sudden surges in bright red blood that could indicate ongoing hemorrhage. Pain management, mechanical ventilator weaning, and infection surveillance are central to recovery.
