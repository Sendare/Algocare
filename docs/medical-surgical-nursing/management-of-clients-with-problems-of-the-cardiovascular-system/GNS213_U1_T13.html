Nursing Management of Cardiovascular Disorders

Comprehensive Cardiovascular Assessment and Monitoring

Evaluating the cardiovascular system requires a systematic approach combining subjective history with objective physical examination. The nurse must systematically assess vital signs, noting any discrepancies in blood pressure between arms or orthostatic changes. Inspection and palpation of the precordium help identify abnormal pulsations or a displaced point of maximal impulse. Auscultation demands a quiet environment to detect normal heart sounds as well as pathological murmurs, gallops, or friction rubs using both the bell and diaphragm of the stethoscope. Fluid balance monitoring serves as a cornerstone of cardiac nursing care, particularly in heart failure management. Daily weight tracking is the most sensitive indicator of acute fluid retention, far preceding pitting edema or pulmonary crackles. Accurate intake and output measurement must be maintained, alongside vigilant observation of peripheral perfusion, capillary refill time, and jugular venous distension. Continuous electrocardiographic monitoring allows early detection of lethal arrhythmias, requiring the nurse to maintain proper lead placement and alarm parameters tailored to the individual clinical profile.

Diagnostic Tests and the Nurse's Role

Diagnostic evaluations confirm underlying pathology and guide therapeutic decisions in cardiac care. The nurse acts as coordinator, educator, and safety monitor throughout these procedures. - **Electrocardiogram (ECG)**: A non-invasive tracing of cardiac electrical activity; the nurse ensures proper skin preparation and electrode placement to minimize artifact. - **Echocardiogram**: Uses ultrasound waves to visualize heart structures and ejection fraction; requires patient positioning instructions and reassurance during the test. - **Cardiac Catheterization**: An invasive fluoroscopic procedure; pre-procedure nursing duties include assessing for shellfish or iodine allergies and checking baseline coagulation profiles, while post-procedure care focuses on monitoring the access site for hematoma and checking distal pulses. - **Cardiac Biomarkers**: Blood assays such as troponin and CK-MB; the nurse ensures timely collection to track myocardial injury patterns.

Pharmacological Management in Cardiac Disorders

Managing cardiovascular disease relies heavily on complex medication regimens designed to reduce myocardial oxygen demand, control blood pressure, manage rhythm disturbances, and prevent fluid overload. | Drug Class | Primary Mechanism | Key Nursing Consideration | | --- | --- | --- | | **Beta-Blockers** | Decrease heart rate and myocardial contractility | Monitor for bradycardia and bronchospasm | | **ACE Inhibitors** | Prevent vasoconstriction and aldosterone secretion | Monitor for persistent dry cough and hyperkalemia | | **Diuretics** | Promote renal sodium and water excretion | Monitor serum electrolytes and daily weight | | **Anticoagulants** | Prevent new clot formation or growth | Monitor bleeding times and watch for petechiae | Patient adherence remains a major hurdle in chronic cardiac pharmacology. Nurses must educate individuals on the dangers of abrupt withdrawal, particularly with beta-blockers, which can trigger severe rebound tachycardia or hypertensive crisis. Emphasizing routine blood work for drug level or electrolyte monitoring safeguards against toxicity.

Nursing Interventions for Acute Cardiac Conditions

Acute cardiac events, such as acute coronary syndrome and decompensated heart failure, demand rapid, decisive nursing action to preserve myocardial tissue and stabilize hemodynamics. When a patient reports sudden crushing chest pain, immediate priorities include placing the individual at rest, administering supplemental oxygen if saturation is below baseline, and obtaining an urgent 12-lead ECG. Sublingual nitroglycerin is given to dilate coronary vessels, provided the systolic blood pressure remains stable. In emergency scenarios progressing to cardiac arrest or lethal arrhythmias, the nurse must initiate basic and advanced life support protocols seamlessly. Defibrillation for ventricular fibrillation must occur without delay. For patients experiencing acute pulmonary edema, positioning them in an upright posture with legs dangling reduces venous return, instantly alleviating respiratory distress while awaiting diuretic therapy.

Patient Education and Lifestyle Modification

Sustainable management of cardiovascular disorders extends far beyond hospital discharge, relying heavily on targeted patient education. The nurse translates complex medical terminology into actionable lifestyle goals regarding diet, physical activity, and smoking cessation. Dietary counseling centers on sodium restriction to minimize fluid retention, alongside reducing saturated fats and cholesterol to halt atherosclerotic plaque progression. Cultural dietary practices in Nigeria, such as the use of high-sodium local seasonings and processed condiments, require culturally sensitive counseling. Instead of forbidding traditional meals, the nurse collaborates with the patient to substitute sodium-rich flavorings with local herbs, garlic, and citrus. Furthermore, education must cover recognizing early warning signs of worsening heart failure or impending cardiac events, ensuring patients seek prompt medical intervention before crisis strikes.

Cardiac Rehabilitation and Long-Term Care

Cardiac rehabilitation is a medically supervised program designed to optimize physical, psychological, and social functioning after a cardiac event. The nurse plays an integral role within the multidisciplinary team by coordinating phased recovery milestones, from inpatient mobilization to outpatient endurance training. Phase one begins at the bedside, focusing on self-care activities and light range-of-motion exercises to prevent deep vein thrombosis and deconditioning. Phases two and three transition the patient to outpatient structured exercise regimens coupled with continuous telemetry monitoring. Beyond physical conditioning, rehabilitation addresses psychological distress, as depression and anxiety are prevalent following a heart attack. The nurse offers supportive counseling, monitors emotional coping, and connects patients with support networks to ensure long-term secondary prevention success.
