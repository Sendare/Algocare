Defibrillation

Introduction to Defibrillation and Indications

Defibrillation is a therapeutic intervention that delivers a high-energy electrical shock across the myocardium to depolarize a critical mass of heart muscle simultaneously. This action abruptly terminates chaotic, uncoordinated electrical activity, allowing the heart's natural pacemaker—the sinoatrial node—to resume an organized rhythm. It is exclusively indicated for cardiac arrest presentations driven by shockable rhythms. Attempting defibrillation on a non-shockable rhythm like asystole or pulseless electrical activity wastes precious resuscitation time and interrupts effective chest compressions. - **Ventricular Fibrillation (VF)**: The most common rhythm observed in sudden cardiac arrest, characterized by rapid, erratic quivering of the ventricles with zero cardiac output. - **Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (Pulseless VT)**: A rapid, regular or slightly irregular ventricular rhythm that generates no palpable pulse and rapidly deteriorates into ventricular fibrillation if untreated.

Pathophysiology of Shockable Rhythms

Understanding why a heart arrests helps contextualize why a shock works. In ventricular fibrillation, millions of myocardial cells depolarize and repolarize independently in a chaotic fashion. There is no coordinated contraction, no stroke volume, and systemic perfusion drops instantly to zero. The brain and other vital organs are starved of oxygen. Pulseless ventricular tachycardia occurs when electrical impulses originate from an ectopic focus in the ventricles at an extremely high rate. While the rhythm may appear organized on a monitor screen, the ventricles beat so fast they cannot fill adequately with blood. Cardiac output plummets, resulting in a loss of pulse. Both rhythms represent electrical chaos that only a direct, external electrical current can override.

Monophasic vs. Biphasic Defibrillators

Modern resuscitation relies heavily on biphasic waveforms, though older monophasic devices still exist in resource-limited clinical environments across Nigeria. A **monophasic defibrillator** delivers current in a single direction from one paddle to the other. Because of this unidirectional flow, it requires higher energy settings—typically starting at 360 Joules—to successfully terminate ventricular fibrillation. In contrast, a **biphasic defibrillator** sends current in one direction for a specified duration and then reverses it for the remaining milliseconds of the electrical pulse. This bidirectional approach treats the myocardium more effectively at lower energy levels, usually starting between 120 and 200 Joules depending on the manufacturer. Lower energy thresholds significantly reduce the risk of post-shock myocardial dysfunction and skin burns.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Safety Measures

Safety is the absolute priority when delivering thousands of volts of electricity. Before pressing the shock button, the operator must confirm that no personnel are touching the patient, the bed frame, or any connected equipment to prevent accidental electrocution. Conductive interface material—either conductive gel pads or paste—must be applied properly between the skin and the paddles or self-adhesive pads to reduce transthoracic impedance. Air pockets trapped under pads act as insulators and can cause skin burns. Additionally, oxygen delivery tubing should be kept clear of the chest area during the discharge because concentrated oxygen gas combined with an electrical spark presents a severe fire hazard.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Emergency Defibrillation

Executing a defibrillation safely requires clear communication and a structured sequence of actions. When working in a resuscitation team, the lead nurse or code runner assigns explicit verbal roles to maintain safety and efficiency. 1. **Verify Arrest**: Confirm unresponsiveness, absence of breathing, and no palpable carotid pulse. 2. **Resume CPR**: Maintain high-quality chest compressions while the defibrillator is retrieved, powered on, and charged. 3. **Apply Pads**: Place the sternal pad just below the right clavicle and the apical pad at the fifth intercostal space in the mid-axillary line. 4. **Analyze Rhythm**: Stop compressions briefly and let the machine analyze, or confirm VF/pulseless VT visually on the screen. 5. **Charge Device**: Select the appropriate energy dose and press the charge button. 6. **Clear the Patient**: Shout a loud, clear warning ("Clear! I'm clear, you're clear, everybody is clear") and visually scan all team members. 7. **Deliver Shock**: Press the discharge button(s) simultaneously. 8. **Resume CPR Immediately**: Do not check for a pulse right after the shock; resume chest compressions for two minutes.

Post-Defibrillation Care and Continuous Monitoring

Once the shock is delivered and CPR resumes, attention shifts to post-resuscitation care. If ROSC (Return of Spontaneous Circulation) is achieved, the patient will exhibit signs of life, such as purposeful movement, spontaneous breathing, and a palpable pulse. Continuous cardiac, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation monitoring become critical. Healthcare providers must evaluate the 12-lead ECG to identify underlying triggers like acute myocardial infarction that may require urgent intervention. Managing post-cardiac arrest care also involves targeted temperature management, maintaining adequate cerebral perfusion, and correcting electrolyte imbalances.

Complications and Troubleshooting in Defibrillation

Although life-saving, defibrillation carries potential complications and technical hurdles. Skin burns at the electrode site are common if conductive gel is sparse or old. Myocardial stunning can occur, causing temporary reduction in ventricular contractility following high-energy shocks. Troubleshooting equipment failures is an essential nursing competence during emergencies. If the device fails to charge, verify the power source and check battery connections. If the monitor displays a persistent artifact or 'pads off' message, ensure the cable is plugged in firmly and skin contact is secure. Regular equipment checks prevent tragic delays in emergency response units.
