Defibrillation

Introduction to Defibrillation and Indications

Defibrillation is an emergency medical procedure that delivers a therapeutic dose of electrical current to the heart. This electrical shock depolarizes a critical mass of the heart muscle simultaneously, terminating chaotic electrical activity and allowing the heart's natural pacemaker to resume normal, organized rhythm.

The procedure is indicated exclusively for two life-threatening cardiac arrest rhythms:

  • Ventricular Fibrillation (VF): The ventricles quiver uselessly instead of pumping blood.
  • Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (Pulseless VT): The ventricles beat extremely fast but fail to produce an adequate pulse.

A common misconception among beginners is that defibrillation can fix any flatline. It is completely ineffective for Asystole and Pulseless Electrical Activity, because those rhythms lack any electrical chaos to reset; instead, they require high-quality chest compressions and epinephrine.

Pathophysiology of Shockable Rhythms: VF and Pulseless VT

To understand why a shock works, a nurse must look at what happens inside the ventricles during cardiac arrest. In Ventricular Fibrillation, electrical impulses fire rapidly from multiple ectopic foci in the ventricular myocardium. The muscle fibers twitch randomly like a bag of worms, resulting in zero cardiac output.

Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia occurs when a single abnormal focal point in the ventricle takes over at a very high rate, often exceeding 150 beats per minute. While the heart may briefly pump small amounts of blood at the onset, it soon deteriorates into an ineffective quivering state where no palpable pulse can be felt. Both rhythms cause rapid brain death if untreated, making prompt electrical intervention the absolute priority.

Types of Defibrillators: Monophasic vs. Biphasic

Defibrillators deliver energy in different wave patterns. Clinical practice has largely shifted toward biphasic technology due to superior efficacy at lower energy levels.

FeatureMonophasic DefibrillatorBiphasic Defibrillator
Current FlowTravels in one direction onlyTravels in a positive direction, then reverses
Energy RequiredHigher energy (typically 360 Joules)Lower energy (typically 120 to 200 Joules)
Tissue Damage RiskHigher risk of myocardial burnsLower risk of post-shock heart damage

Because biphasic devices pass current through the heart twice in a single shock, they successfully terminate shockable rhythms with less thermal trauma to the chest wall and heart muscle.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Safety Measures

Safety during defibrillation protects both the patient and the resuscitation team. Before pressing the discharge button, the nurse must ensure several environmental and physical checks are complete.

  • Oxygen Hazard: High concentrations of oxygen support combustion. Pull nasal cannulas and oxygen masks at least one meter away from the patient's chest to prevent sparks from causing a fire.
  • Conductive Interface: Apply defibrillator pads or conductive gel firmly to avoid skin burns and ensure optimal current flow. Never use plain ECG paste or ultrasound gel.
  • Implanted Devices: Avoid placing pads directly over pacemakers or implantable cardioverter-defibrillators. Place pads at least one inch away from these hardware sites.
  • Clear the Patient: Shout a loud, clear warning like "I'm clear, you're clear, everybody clear!" and perform a visual sweep to verify nobody is touching the bed, patient, or equipment.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Emergency Defibrillation

When cardiac arrest strikes, swift execution of the defibrillation sequence saves lives.

  1. Verify Arrest: Confirm unresponsiveness, absence of normal breathing, and no palpable carotid pulse.
  2. Resume Compressions: High-quality CPR must continue while the defibrillator is retrieved and powered on.
  3. Apply Pads: Place the sternal-apex pads firmly on the bare chest.
  4. Analyze Rhythm: Stop CPR briefly and command the team to stand clear while the machine analyzes the rhythm.
  5. Charge and Clear: If a shockable rhythm is advised, press the charge button. Shout your clearance warnings and visually check that all team members are away from the bed.
  6. Deliver Shock: Press the discharge button(s) simultaneously.
  7. Resume CPR: Immediately resume chest compressions for two minutes before the next rhythm check.

Post-Defibrillation Care and Continuous Monitoring

Once the shock is delivered and CPR resumes, attention shifts to stabilizing the patient. If the heart regains a perfusing rhythm, the team enters post-resuscitation care.

The nurse continuously monitors vital signs, oxygen saturation, and cardiac rhythm via telemetry to detect early signs of re-arrest or dangerous arrhythmias. Blood pressure support, targeted temperature management, and airway security take priority.

Document the exact time of the arrest, the number of shocks delivered, the energy levels used in Joules, medications administered, and the patient's immediate response. Frequent reassessment ensures that complications are caught early and treated aggressively.

Complications and Troubleshooting in Defibrillation

While life-saving, defibrillation is not without risks. Skin burns at the pad site are common if conductive gel is dry or missing. Myocardial stunning can occur temporarily, reducing the heart's pumping efficiency even after a normal rhythm returns.

Troubleshooting equipment failures is a critical nursing skill during emergencies:

  • "Check Pads" Error: Usually caused by poor skin contact, dried gel, or loose cable connections. Press firmly on the pads or replace them.
  • Failure to Charge: Often stems from a depleted battery. Immediately plug the defibrillator into an AC power outlet.
  • Artifact on Monitor: Excessive patient movement or loose electrode leads can mimic shockable rhythms. Always assess the patient's clinical state before treating what you see on the screen.
