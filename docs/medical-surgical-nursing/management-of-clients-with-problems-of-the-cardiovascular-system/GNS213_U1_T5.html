Structural Cardiac Disorders

Introduction to Structural Cardiac Disorders

Structural cardiac disorders encompass a wide range of congenital and acquired abnormalities affecting the heart's anatomy, including valves, walls, chambers, and great vessels. Unlike primary electrical disturbances such as arrhythmias, structural defects mechanically disrupt normal blood flow, forcing the heart to work harder against abnormal resistance or volume loads. In Nigerian clinical practice, rheumatic heart disease remains a leading acquired structural disorder, often following inadequately treated streptococcal throat infections in younger populations, while congenital anomalies present earlier in pediatric wards. Understanding these anatomical deviations allows nursing professionals to anticipate complications, recognize subtle signs of hemodynamic compromise, and provide targeted supportive care before irreversible ventricular damage occurs.

Pathophysiology and Hemodynamic Alterations

The mechanical failures in structural heart disease translate directly into pressure and volume overloads. When a valve stenoses or a septal wall develops a hole, blood flows abnormally, creating turbulence and changing intracardiac pressures. Over time, the myocardium undergoes remodeling—hypertrophy or dilation—to maintain cardiac output. Eventually, these compensatory mechanisms fail, leading to elevated end-diastolic pressures, pulmonary congestion, and systemic venous backup. Recognizing these fluid dynamics helps nurses link a localized valve defect to generalized signs of fluid overload, such as peripheral edema and dyspnea.

Classification of Valvular Heart Diseases

Valvular heart diseases are broadly divided into two main functional categories: stenosis and regurgitation (insufficiency).

  • Stenosis: Narrowing or stiffening of the valve orifice that impedes forward blood flow, requiring the upstream chamber to generate higher pressure.
  • Regurgitation: Incomplete closure of valve leaflets that allows blood to leak backward, creating a volume overload in the upstream chamber.
  • Mixed Lesions: Conditions where a single valve exhibits both stenosis and regurgitation simultaneously.
  • Prolapse: Displacement of thickened valve leaflets back into the atrium during systole, commonly seen in the mitral valve.

Congenital Structural Defects: ASD, VSD, and Tetralogy of Fallot

Congenital heart defects arise from abnormal embryonic development during the first trimester of pregnancy.

  • Atrial Septal Defect (ASD): An opening in the interatrial septum allowing left-to-right shunting, often well-tolerated until adulthood.
  • Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD): A hole in the interventricular septum, representing the most common congenital heart anomaly, producing a loud harsh murmur and early signs of heart failure if large.
  • Tetralogy of Fallot: A complex cyanotic defect consisting of four distinct anatomical abnormalities: pulmonary stenosis, ventricular septal defect, overriding aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy.

Infants with cyanotic defects require careful monitoring for hypercyanotic spells, commonly managed by placing the child in a knee-chest position to increase systemic vascular resistance.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

Clinical presentation depends heavily on the severity of the structural defect and the rapidity of its onset. Nurses must systematically assess for signs of both left and right-sided failure, including exertional dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, jugular venous distension, hepatomegaly, and peripheral edema. Auscultation remains a fundamental skill; abnormal turbulent blood flow generates distinct murmurs, clicks, or gallop rhythms (S3 or S4). In pediatric assessments, poor feeding, failure to thrive, and diaphoresis during crying or feeding serve as critical red flags for underlying structural anomalies.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging

Accurate diagnosis relies on a combination of non-invasive and invasive investigative tools.

  • Echocardiography: The gold standard imaging modality, utilizing ultrasound waves to visualize valve structure, chamber size, wall motion, and blood flow velocity via Doppler studies.
  • Electrocardiogram (ECG): Identifies chamber enlargement, conduction delays, and arrhythmias secondary to structural strain.
  • Chest Radiography: Evaluates overall cardiac silhouette size and pulmonary vascular markings for congestion or edema.
  • Cardiac Catheterization: An invasive procedure measuring direct intracardiac pressures and visualizing coronary and structural anatomy through radiopaque contrast media.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Pharmacotherapy does not repair structural defects but aims to optimize cardiac output, relieve symptoms, and delay disease progression. Diuretics reduce preload and pulmonary congestion. ACE inhibitors or ARBs decrease afterload, making it easier for the weakened ventricle to eject blood. Beta-blockers slow the heart rate, allowing for longer diastolic filling time and reducing myocardial oxygen demand. In patients with atrial fibrillation secondary to atrial enlargement, anticoagulants are vital to prevent thromboembolic events such as stroke.

Surgical and Interventional Procedures

Definitive management of severe structural heart disorders ultimately requires surgical repair or replacement. Valvuloplasty uses balloon catheters to dilate narrowed valves percutaneously. When valves are severely damaged, surgical replacement is necessary using either mechanical valves (durable, but require lifelong anticoagulation) or bioprosthetic valves (tissue-derived, lower thrombogenic risk, but degrade over time). Congenital defects like ASDs and VSDs are closed via catheter-based occluder devices or open-heart patch grafting. Nurses prepare patients psychologically and physically for these major interventions while managing complex preoperative investigations.

Postoperative Nursing Care and Patient Education

The immediate postoperative phase requires intensive monitoring in a specialized cardiothoracic unit. Nurses must continuously track hemodynamic parameters, monitor chest tube output for hemorrhage, manage pain, and assess for surgical site infections or neurological deficits. Respiratory care, including early mobilization and incentive spirometry, prevents atelectasis. Discharge education focuses on medication adherence, wound care, recognizing signs of infection or heart failure recurrence, and reinforcing the necessity of antibiotic prophylaxis prior to invasive dental procedures to prevent infective endocarditis.
