Structural Cardiac Disorders

Introduction and Classification of Structural Cardiac Disorders

Structural cardiac disorders involve physical defects or abnormalities in the heart's valves, walls, muscle, or major vessels. Unlike electrical issues that cause arrhythmias, structural problems mechanically disrupt normal blood flow, forcing the heart to work harder. These conditions are broadly split into two main groups:

  • Congenital defects: Present at birth due to abnormal fetal development, such as septal holes or complex malformations.
  • Acquired disorders: Develop later in life due to infections, degenerative wear and tear, ischemia, or chronic hemodynamic stress, such as calcific valve disease or hypertensive cardiomyopathy.

Pathophysiology and Hemodynamic Alterations

When structural defects alter the heart's chambers or valves, hemodynamics change dramatically. Obstructions to outflow, such as a narrowed valve, create pressure overload, forcing the myocardium to hypertrophy to maintain cardiac output. Conversely, regurgitation or shunting creates volume overload, stretching the muscle fibers and leading to chamber dilation.

Over time, these compensatory mechanisms fail. The heart muscle tires, compliance drops, and pressures back up into the pulmonary or systemic circulation. This progressive failure is why structural anomalies eventually manifest as clinical congestion and reduced tissue perfusion.

Valvular Heart Diseases: Stenosis and Regurgitation

Valvular disorders affect the heart's one-way doors, disrupting smooth blood flow. The two main dysfunctions are stenosis and regurgitation:

  • Stenosis: The valve leaflets become stiff, thickened, or fused, failing to open fully and restricting forward blood flow.
  • Regurgitation (Insufficiency): The valve fails to close tightly, allowing blood to leak backward into the previous chamber.

Commonly affected valves include the mitral and aortic valves on the left side of the heart, where pressures are highest. A single valve can also suffer from mixed disease, presenting with both stenosis and regurgitation simultaneously.

Congenital Shunts and Malformations

Congenital structural defects often involve abnormal openings between heart chambers or great vessels. An Atrial Septal Defect (ASD) and a Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD) allow oxygenated blood to shunt from the left side of the heart to the right side due to pressure differences, increasing pulmonary blood flow.

Tetralogy of Fallot is a more complex cyanotic condition featuring four distinct anatomical anomalies: a VSD, pulmonary stenosis, right ventricular hypertrophy, and an overriding aorta. This defect restricts blood flow to the lungs and mixes deoxygenated blood into the systemic circulation, causing cyanosis.

Cardiomyopathies and Myocardial Remodeling

Cardiomyopathies are diseases of the heart muscle itself that alter structural geometry and impair pumping ability. There are three primary forms:

  • Dilated cardiomyopathy: The ventricles stretch and thin out, reducing contractile strength.
  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: The heart muscle thickens abnormally, often blocking blood flow out of the left ventricle and reducing chamber filling space.
  • Restrictive cardiomyopathy: The muscle becomes rigid and stiff, preventing proper ventricular filling during diastole despite normal systolic contraction.

Clinical Manifestations and Nursing Assessment

Patients with structural cardiac disorders present with symptoms stemming from poor forward output and backward congestion. Left-sided lesions typically cause respiratory symptoms like dyspnea, orthopnea, and crackles in the lungs. Right-sided problems lead to systemic venous congestion, manifesting as peripheral edema, jugular venous distension, and hepatomegaly.

During assessment, nurses auscultate for characteristic heart murmurs caused by turbulent blood flow across abnormal valves or shunts. Fatigue, exercise intolerance, and dizziness are also common findings due to compromised tissue perfusion.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging

Accurately diagnosing structural cardiac disorders relies heavily on non-invasive and invasive imaging modalities. Echocardiography (transthoracic or transesophageal) is the gold standard, providing real-time visual assessment of valve structure, chamber size, wall motion, and blood flow velocity using Doppler ultrasound.

Electrocardiograms help detect secondary effects like chamber hypertrophy or arrhythmias, while chest X-rays reveal cardiomegaly and pulmonary congestion. Cardiac catheterization provides precise hemodynamic pressure measurements and direct visualization of coronary and structural anatomy.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

While medications do not cure structural defects, they manage symptoms, reduce workload, and slow disease progression. Diuretics relieve fluid overload and pulmonary congestion. ACE inhibitors or ARBs reduce afterload, easing the heart's pumping burden. Beta-blockers slow the heart rate, allowing more time for ventricular filling and reducing myocardial oxygen demand.

Anticoagulants may be prescribed for patients with atrial fibrillation or mechanical heart valves to prevent thromboembolic events.

Surgical and Interventional Procedures

Definitive treatment for severe structural cardiac disorders often requires surgical or catheter-based intervention. Valvular lesions can be treated with valvuloplasty to open narrowed leaflets, or surgical replacement using mechanical or bioprosthetic valves. Congenital shunts like ASDs and VSDs are closed using transcatheter occlusion devices or open-heart patch grafting.

Minimally invasive approaches and robot-assisted surgeries have shortened recovery times, though open-heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass remains necessary for complex cases.

Postoperative Nursing Care and Patient Education

Postoperative care following structural heart surgery focuses on hemodynamic stability, pain management, and monitoring for complications such as bleeding, tamponade, arrhythmias, or infection. Nurses frequently monitor vital signs, chest tube output, and neurological status in an intensive care setting.

Patient education is critical before discharge. Clients with mechanical valves need thorough instruction on lifelong anticoagulation therapy and bleeding precautions. All patients require guidance on activity pacing, recognizing signs of heart failure exacerbation, and maintaining strict endocarditis prophylaxis before dental or invasive procedures.
