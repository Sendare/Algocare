Anatomy and Physiology of the Cardiovascular System

Introduction and General Functions of the Cardiovascular System

The cardiovascular system functions primarily as the body's distribution and transport network. At its center sits the heart, acting as a muscular double pump that drives blood through an extensive circuit of blood vessels. This continuous movement ensures that oxygen absorbed in the lungs and nutrients digested in the gastrointestinal tract reach every active tissue in the body. Beyond simple delivery, the system plays a critical role in homeostasis by removing metabolic waste products like carbon dioxide and urea, transporting them to the kidneys and lungs for excretion. It also carries hormones from endocrine glands to their target organs, distributes immune cells to sites of infection, and participates heavily in thermoregulation by adjusting blood flow to the skin.

Anatomical Structure and Layers of the Heart Wall

The heart wall is composed of three distinct tissue layers, each serving a unique mechanical or protective purpose.

  • Epicardium: The outermost layer, also known as the visceral layer of the serous pericardium, providing a slippery, smooth protective membrane over the heart surface.
  • Myocardium: The middle, thickest muscular layer consisting of cardiac muscle tissue responsible for the powerful pumping action.
  • Endocardium: The innermost endothelial lining that provides a smooth, frictionless surface for blood inside the chambers and covers the heart valves.

Enclosing these three layers is the pericardium, a fibro-serous sac that anchors the heart within the mediastinum and secretes lubricating fluid to reduce friction during contraction.

Heart Chambers, Valves, and Blood Flow Pathway

The heart is divided into four chambers: two superior atria that receive blood and two inferior ventricles that pump blood outward. Deoxygenated blood enters the right atrium via the superior and inferior venae cavae, passes through the tricuspid valve into the right ventricle, and is pumped through the pulmonary semilunar valve into the pulmonary trunk toward the lungs. After oxygenation, blood returns via the pulmonary veins into the left atrium, flows through the bicuspid or mitral valve into the left ventricle, and is finally forced through the aortic semilunar valve into the aorta for systemic circulation.

Valves maintain unidirectional flow by snapping shut to prevent backpressure, driven entirely by pressure changes across the chambers.

Coronary Circulation and Myocardial Blood Supply

Although the heart chambers are constantly filled with blood, the myocardium is far too thick to absorb nutrients via simple diffusion from the inside lumen. It relies entirely on its own dedicated vascular network called the coronary circulation. The right and left coronary arteries branch directly from the base of the ascending aorta just superior to the aortic valve. The left coronary artery typically divides into the anterior interventricular and circumflex branches, supplying the left atrium and most of the left ventricle. The right coronary artery supplies the right atrium and portions of both ventricles. Deoxygenated blood is collected by cardiac veins, which empty into the coronary sinus, returning blood directly into the right atrium.

The Cardiac Conduction System and Electrophysiology

The heart beats rhythmically without neural stimulation because it possesses an intrinsic conduction system made of specialized autorhythmic cardiac cells. The sequence of excitation follows a precise pathway:

  1. Sinoatrial (SA) Node: Located in the right atrial wall, acts as the natural pacemaker setting the baseline heart rate.
  2. Atrioventricular (AV) Node: Delays the impulse briefly to allow complete ventricular filling.
  3. Bundle of His (AV Bundle): Conducts the signal from the atria into the interventricular septum.
  4. Bundle Branches: Right and left branches transmit the signal down toward the apex.
  5. Purkinje Fibers: Distribute the action potential rapidly through the ventricular myocardium, causing coordinated contraction from the apex upward.

The Cardiac Cycle, Systole, Diastole, and Heart Sounds

The cardiac cycle encompasses all mechanical and electrical events occurring during one single heartbeat, divided into systole (contraction) and diastole (relaxation). During atrial systole, the atria contract to top off ventricular volume. Next, ventricular systole begins; rising pressure closes the AV valves, producing the first heart sound (lub, or S1), and opens the semilunar valves to eject blood. Following ejection, ventricular diastole sets in, pressure drops, and semilunar valves snap shut, producing the second heart sound (dub, or S2). Valvular closure sounds are essential clinical markers used during auscultation to assess mechanical integrity.

Hemodynamics: Cardiac Output, Stroke Volume, and Blood Pressure

Cardiac output (CO) measures the total volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per minute, calculated as heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. Stroke volume represents the volume of blood ejected by a ventricle during each contraction. Three primary factors influence stroke volume: preload (the degree of stretch on the heart before contraction), contractility (the force of the myocardial contraction), and afterload (the arterial pressure the heart must overcome to eject blood).

Blood pressure is determined by cardiac output, blood volume, and total peripheral resistance. Baroreceptors in the carotid sinuses and aortic arch constantly monitor arterial pressure, initiating autonomic nervous system adjustments to maintain hemodynamic stability.

Structure and Function of Blood Vessels

Blood vessels form a closed circuit of conduits categorized by their specific structural adaptations and functions.

Vessel TypePrimary StructureMain Function
ArteriesThick muscular and elastic wallsTransport blood away from the heart under high pressure
CapillariesSingle layer of endothelial cellsSite of gas, nutrient, and waste exchange with tissues
VeinsThin walls with internal venous valvesReturn low-pressure blood back toward the heart

Arteries dampen pressure surges through their elasticity, while arterioles regulate systemic blood pressure through vasoconstriction. Veins utilize skeletal muscle contraction and one-way valves to prevent blood pooling against gravity.
