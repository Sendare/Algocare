Anatomy and Physiology of the Cardiovascular System

Introduction to the Cardiovascular System and Its General Functions

The cardiovascular system is the primary transport network of the human body. At its core, it consists of a muscular pump—the heart—and an extensive network of blood vessels that reach almost every living tissue. Its main job is to deliver oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and immune cells to cells while removing waste products like carbon dioxide and metabolic byproducts for elimination. Without this continuous circulation, cells quickly lose function and die.

In nursing practice, understanding this system is essential because nearly every major disease process—from shock and heart failure to hypertension—affects or stems from cardiovascular function. The system also plays a vital role in maintaining body temperature, pH balance, and fluid balance throughout the body.

Anatomical Structure and Layers of the Heart

The heart sits safely inside the mediastinum of the thoracic cavity, tilted slightly to the left. It is enclosed in a tough, double-walled sac called the pericardium, which anchors the heart in place while secreting lubricating fluid to reduce friction as it beats.

The wall of the heart has three distinct layers:

  • Epicardium: The outer protective layer, which is actually the visceral layer of the serous pericardium.
  • Myocardium: The middle, thick muscular layer composed of specialized cardiac muscle tissue responsible for the heart's pumping action.
  • Endocardium: The smooth inner lining of the heart chambers and valves that prevents blood from clotting inappropriately as it flows through.

Heart Chambers, Valves, and Blood Flow Pathway

The heart is divided into four chambers: two upper atria that receive blood and two lower ventricles that pump blood out. A thick wall called the septum separates the left and right sides, preventing oxygen-poor blood from mixing with oxygen-rich blood.

Blood flows through the heart in a strict, one-way pathway guided by four valves:

  1. Tricuspid valve: Guards the opening between the right atrium and right ventricle.
  2. Pulmonary semilunar valve: Controls blood flow from the right ventricle into the pulmonary artery.
  3. Mitral (bicuspid) valve: Guards the opening between the left atrium and left ventricle.
  4. Aortic semilunar valve: Controls blood flow from the left ventricle into the aorta.

Deoxygenated blood enters the right side of the heart via the vena cava and goes to the lungs. Oxygenated blood returns to the left side via the pulmonary veins and is pumped out to the rest of the body through the aorta.

Coronary Circulation and Blood Supply to the Myocardium

Although the heart chambers are constantly filled with blood, the myocardium is too thick for nutrients to simply soak in from the inside. Instead, the heart has its own dedicated blood supply system called coronary circulation.

The left and right coronary arteries branch directly off the base of the aorta just above the aortic valve, supplying oxygen-rich blood to the heart muscle. On the return trip, cardiac veins collect the deoxygenated blood and empty it into the right atrium through a large channel called the coronary sinus. When a coronary artery becomes blocked by a blood clot or plaque, the underlying heart muscle is starved of oxygen, leading to ischemia and eventually myocardial infarction (heart attack).

The Cardiac Conduction System and Electrophysiology

The heart does not need nerve signals from the brain to beat; it generates its own electrical impulses through specialized autorhythmic tissue. This pathway ensures that the atria contract first, followed smoothly by the ventricles.

  • Sinoatrial (SA) node: Known as the natural pacemaker of the heart, located in the right atrium, setting the basic rhythm.
  • Atrioventricular (AV) node: Located at the junction of the atria and ventricles, briefly slowing down the impulse to allow ventricles to fill.
  • Bundle of His (AV bundle): Transmits the signal down into the interventricular septum.
  • Bundle branches: Carry the signal down the left and right sides of the septum.
  • Purkinje fibers: Spread the electrical impulse rapidly through the ventricular walls, triggering a strong, coordinated contraction.

Electrocardiography (ECG) records these electrical events, where the P wave represents atrial depolarization, the QRS complex represents ventricular depolarization, and the T wave represents ventricular repolarization.

The Cardiac Cycle: Systole, Diastole, and Heart Sounds

The cardiac cycle describes all the mechanical and electrical events that occur during one single heartbeat. It is divided into two main phases: systole (contraction and pumping) and diastole (relaxation and filling).

During atrial systole, the atria squeeze remaining blood into the ventricles. Next, ventricular systole begins; as ventricles contract, pressure rises, snapping the AV valves shut (creating the first heart sound, S1 or 'lub'). When ventricular pressure exceeds the pressure in the aorta and pulmonary artery, the semilunar valves open, and blood is ejected. As the ventricles relax during ventricular diastole, blood starts to flow backward, snapping the semilunar valves shut (creating the second heart sound, S2 or 'dub').

Hemodynamics: Cardiac Output, Stroke Volume, and Blood Pressure Regulation

Cardiac output (CO) is the total amount of blood pumped by each ventricle in one minute. It is calculated by multiplying stroke volume (the volume of blood pumped per beat) by heart rate (beats per minute). Normal resting cardiac output is roughly 5 liters per minute, though it can increase significantly during exercise.

Blood pressure is determined by cardiac output, blood volume, and peripheral resistance (the friction blood encounters as it flows through blood vessels). The body regulates blood pressure dynamically using neural controls (like baroreceptors in the carotid arteries) and hormonal systems (such as the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system). When blood pressure drops, these mechanisms trigger vasoconstriction and fluid retention to restore normal perfusion.

Structure and Function of Blood Vessels: Arteries, Veins, and Capillaries

Blood vessels form a closed circuit of tubes that carry blood away from the heart, through the tissues, and back again.

  • Arteries: Carry blood away from the heart under high pressure. They have thick, elastic walls to handle surges of pressure with each heartbeat.
  • Capillaries: Extremely tiny, microscopic vessels with walls only one cell thick. This thin barrier allows oxygen, nutrients, and wastes to diffuse easily between the blood and surrounding tissue cells.
  • Veins: Return blood back to the heart under low pressure. They have thinner walls and feature internal one-way valves that prevent gravity from pulling blood backward, assisted by the squeezing action of surrounding skeletal muscles.
