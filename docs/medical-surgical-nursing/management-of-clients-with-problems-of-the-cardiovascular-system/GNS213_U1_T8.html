Cardiac Tumours

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Cardiac Tumours

Cardiac tumours are exceedingly rare growths occurring within the heart chambers, myocardium, or pericardium. They are broadly categorized into primary tumours, which originate directly from cardiac tissue, and secondary tumours, which spread from malignancies elsewhere in the body. Primary cardiac masses are overwhelmingly non-cancerous, with a vast majority falling into the benign category, whereas secondary metastatic tumours occur much more frequently in clinical practice.

The pathophysiology of these growths relates closely to their anatomical location, size, and mobility rather than strictly their histological grade. Even a benign mass can cause catastrophic hemodynamic collapse if it physically obstructs normal blood flow through the heart valves or impairs myocardial contractility. Furthermore, fragile tumours can shed fragments, resulting in systemic or pulmonary thromboembolism. Understanding these basic mechanical disruptions helps explain why patients often present with sudden cardiovascular compromise.

Classification and Types of Primary Cardiac Tumours

Primary cardiac tumours are subdivided based on their cellular origin and malignant potential. Encountering these in clinical guidelines or surgical wards requires familiarity with the most common histological subtypes:

  • Atrial Myxoma: The single most common primary cardiac tumour in adults, typically arising in the left atrium attached to the fossa ovalis.
  • Papillary Fibroelastoma: A small, benign, frond-like valvular tumour that poses a high risk of embolization.
  • Lipoma: A benign encapsulated tumour composed of mature fat cells found within the myocardium or epicardium.
  • Rhabdomyoma: The most frequent primary cardiac tumour in infants and children, strongly associated with tuberous sclerosis.
  • Angiosarcoma: The most common malignant primary cardiac tumour, aggressively invading the right atrium and pericardium.

Secondary and Metastatic Cardiac Tumours

Secondary cardiac tumours outnumber primary ones by a significant margin. Malignancies reach the heart through four main routes: direct extension from adjacent structures like the lungs or oesophagus, hematogenous spread via the bloodstream, lymphatic dissemination, or direct intracavitary extension through the venae cavae.

Cancers famous for metastasizing to the heart include lung carcinoma, breast cancer, malignant melanoma, and renal cell carcinoma. When malignant cells infiltrate the pericardium, they frequently trigger a malignant pericardial effusion, which can rapidly progress to cardiac tamponade if fluid accumulation goes unchecked. Autopsy studies confirm that metastatic involvement is far more common than primary cardiac cancer diagnoses suggest.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Presentation

The clinical presentation of a cardiac tumour is notoriously multifaceted, often mimicking other cardiovascular conditions such as mitral stenosis, infective endocarditis, or heart failure. Symptoms generally fall into three distinct categories: obstructive, embolic, and constitutional.

Obstructive symptoms occur when a mobile tumour blocks blood flow through heart valves, causing sudden dyspnea, orthopnea, syncope, or pulmonary edema—classically triggered by changes in body position. Embolic events happen when tumour fragments or thrombi break off, traveling to the brain to cause a stroke or lodging in peripheral vessels. Constitutional symptoms like low-grade fever, weight loss, night sweats, and fatigue are mediated by tumour-released cytokines, frequently misleading clinicians into suspecting chronic infection or autoimmune disease.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Confirming a suspected cardiac mass requires specific imaging modalities capable of visualizing moving internal heart structures. Transthoracic echocardiography serves as the initial bedside screening tool, offering rapid, non-invasive views of chamber size and mass location. However, transesophageal echocardiography provides superior, high-resolution visualization, especially for smaller or posterior lesions located in the left atrium.

Advanced imaging is frequently employed for precise surgical planning and tissue characterization. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging delivers exceptional tissue contrast, helping differentiate between a thrombus, a benign myxoma, and a malignant sarcoma. Computed tomography scans are also useful for evaluating coronary anatomy and detecting distant extracardiac metastases if a malignant process is suspected.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment strategies depend entirely on whether the tumour is benign or malignant, its anatomical accessibility, and the presence of metastasis. Surgical resection remains the definitive and curative standard of care for almost all primary benign cardiac tumours. Operating on these lesions requires cardiopulmonary bypass, meticulous technique to prevent intraoperative tumour fragmentation, and sometimes reconstruction of the atrial septum or heart valves.

For malignant primary tumours like angiosarcomas, complete surgical removal is rarely possible, and treatment shifts toward palliative chemotherapy, radiation therapy, or emerging targeted therapies. Secondary metastatic tumours are generally managed systemically by treating the primary cancer source, reserving surgical intervention strictly for palliative relief of severe obstruction or tamponade.

Nursing Care and Post-Operative Management

Post-operative care for patients recovering from cardiac tumour resection mirrors general open-heart surgery protocols while addressing specific risks associated with intracardiac manipulation. Continuous hemodynamic monitoring in the intensive care unit is vital to detect low cardiac output syndrome, arrhythmias, or bleeding complications. Nurses must remain vigilant for neurological deficits, as air emboli or dislodged tumour debris during surgery can cause acute stroke.

Pain management, respiratory hygiene with incentive spirometry, and early ambulation form the cornerstone of recovery ward nursing. In resource-constrained Nigerian clinical settings, ensuring strict aseptic technique during wound care and closely monitoring fluid balance are critical to preventing surgical site infections and post-pericardiotomy syndrome.

Complications and Prognosis

The overall prognosis for cardiac tumours hinges almost entirely on histological classification. Patients with benign primary tumours enjoy an excellent long-term prognosis after successful complete surgical excision, with very low rates of recurrence. Regular post-operative echocardiographic follow-up remains standard practice to confirm no residual mass was left behind.

Conversely, malignant primary and secondary tumours carry a grim prognosis. Malignant sarcomas often present at advanced stages, making complete resection difficult and leading to rapid recurrence and high mortality. Complications such as intractable heart failure, malignant arrhythmias, embolic stroke, and cardiac tamponade heavily influence the shortened survival timeline for metastatic disease.
