Cardiac Tumours

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Cardiac Tumours

Cardiac tumours are growths that develop within the heart muscle, on its inner lining, or inside the pericardial sac. They are extremely rare compared to cancers in other parts of the body. These growths can disrupt the heart's normal functions by physically blocking blood flow, interfering with the heart valves, or damaging the heart muscle itself. Because the heart is a dynamic, constantly moving organ, even a small growth can cause major mechanical problems depending on where it sits.

Pathophysiologically, these tumours alter intracardiac pressures and blood pathways. When a tumour occupies space inside an atrium or ventricle, it reduces the volume of blood the heart chamber can hold and pump out. This leads to backup of blood into the lungs or systemic circulation, mimicking conditions like heart failure. Furthermore, fragments of the tumour can break off into the bloodstream, travelling as emboli to block blood vessels in the brain, kidneys, or limbs.

Classification: Benign vs. Malignant Primary Cardiac Tumours

Primary cardiac tumours originate directly inside the heart tissue. The vast majority of these primary growths are benign, meaning they do not spread to other organs. However, benign tumours can still be life-threatening because of their location.

  • Myxomas: The most common type of primary cardiac tumour in adults, usually found growing in the left atrium attached to the atrial septum.
  • Papillary fibroelastomas: Small, hair-like benign tumours that typically grow on heart valves, carrying a high risk of blood clots.
  • Rhabdomyomas: Benign muscular tumours most frequently seen in infants and children, often associated with tuberous sclerosis.

Primary malignant cardiac tumours are very rare. Sarcomas, such as angiosarcoma, are the most frequent cancerous growths in the heart. These malignant tumours grow aggressively, invade surrounding heart muscle, and frequently spread to the lungs and other distant organs.

Secondary (Metastatic) Cardiac Tumours

Secondary or metastatic cardiac tumours start somewhere else in the body and spread to the heart. These are much more common than primary heart tumours. Cancers from the lungs, breast, skin (melanoma), and blood (lymphoma or leukemia) frequently spread to the heart and pericardium.

Tumours reach the heart through the bloodstream, through the lymphatic system, or by direct extension from nearby chest structures. When cancer cells reach the heart, they often settle in the pericardium, causing fluid to build up around the heart (pericardial effusion). This fluid collection can press on the heart muscle and impair its ability to fill with blood, leading to a dangerous emergency condition known as cardiac tamponade.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Presentation

The symptoms of a cardiac tumour often mimic other common cardiovascular conditions, making diagnosis tricky. Patients may present with signs of heart failure, such as shortness of breath, swelling in the legs, and fatigue, because the mass blocks blood flow.

Another major way cardiac tumours present is through embolisation. If pieces of the tumour or blood clots sitting on the tumour break loose, they travel through the arteries and can cause a stroke or block blood flow to the kidneys, intestines, or legs.

Some patients experience sudden fainting spells (syncope), especially when changing body positions, because a mobile tumour temporarily plugs up a heart valve. Additionally, constitutional symptoms like unexplained fever, weight loss, night sweats, and joint pain can occur as the body reacts to the presence of the tumour.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Diagnosing a cardiac tumour requires specialized imaging because the heart is hidden inside the chest cavity.

Diagnostic ToolClinical Purpose
Echocardiogram (Transthoracic/Transesophageal)First-line imaging to visualize the size, location, and mobility of the tumour inside heart chambers.
Cardiac MRIProvides detailed tissue characterization to help differentiate between benign and malignant growths.
Computed Tomography (CT)Evaluates the extent of chest involvement and detects metastatic spread to the lungs.
Cardiac CatheterizationDefines coronary artery anatomy prior to surgery, though rarely used to biopsy the mass directly due to embolisation risks.

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is especially helpful because placing the ultrasound probe in the esophagus brings it very close to the back of the heart, offering crystal-clear pictures of small valve tumours.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management depends heavily on whether the tumour is benign or malignant, its exact location, and the patient's overall health status.

For benign primary tumours, surgical removal is the definitive treatment. Surgeons use cardiopulmonary bypass to safely stop the heart or work around it while excising the mass and any surrounding tissue margin needed to prevent recurrence. Valve repair or replacement may be necessary if the tumour has heavily damaged the valve structures.

For malignant cardiac tumours, surgery is often combined with chemotherapy and radiation therapy. Because primary malignant sarcomas are aggressive and rarely completely removable, the prognosis is guarded. In cases of metastatic secondary tumours, treatment focuses mainly on palliative care, managing symptoms, and treating the primary cancer source in the body.

Nursing Care and Post-Operative Management

Post-operative nursing care for a patient who has undergone cardiac tumour resection mirrors general open-heart surgery recovery. Nurses must monitor hemodynamic stability closely, watching for signs of low cardiac output, arrhythmias, and bleeding. Continuous cardiac telemetry is essential because surgical manipulation of the heart tissue can trigger dangerous heart rhythms.

Respiratory management is another top priority. Patients require early extubation when stable, aggressive pulmonary hygiene, and encouragement of deep breathing and coughing to prevent atelectasis and pneumonia. Pain control enables the patient to participate in physical therapy and breathing exercises. Nurses also monitor neurological status meticulously to quickly catch any signs of stroke caused by emboli dislodged during the surgical procedure.

Complications and Prognosis

The outlook for patients with cardiac tumours varies drastically based on the pathology of the growth. Patients with benign tumours who undergo successful surgical removal generally have an excellent long-term prognosis and can return to normal activities. However, routine follow-up echocardiograms are necessary because certain benign tumours, like myxomas, can occasionally recur.

Conversely, the prognosis for malignant cardiac tumours remains poor. Even with aggressive surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation, primary sarcomas tend to recur locally and spread widely. Complications during the disease course include severe heart failure, life-threatening arrhythmias, sudden cardiac arrest, and massive systemic embolisation. Supportive and palliative care plays a central role in maintaining quality of life for these individuals.
