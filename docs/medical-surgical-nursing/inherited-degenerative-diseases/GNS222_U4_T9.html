Epilepsy

Introduction and Definition of Epilepsy

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate epileptic seizures and by the neurobiological, cognitive, psychological, and social consequences of this condition. According to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), a diagnosis of epilepsy is generally established when a person experiences at least two unprovoked or reflex seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart. Alternatively, a single unprovoked seizure with a probability of further seizures similar to the general recurrence risk (at least 60% over the next 10 years) after two unprovoked seizures, or the diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome, also fulfills the criteria. It is crucial for nursing students in Nigeria to differentiate between a single isolated seizure caused by an acute trigger, such as hypoglycemia or severe febrile illness, and epilepsy, which involves chronic, recurring seizure activity. Epilepsy affects people of all ages, cutting across socioeconomic boundaries, and presents a significant public health challenge in developing nations due to treatment gaps and sociocultural stigmas. Understanding the fundamental definition helps nurses provide accurate assessments, educate patients and their families, and dispel dangerous community myths surrounding seizure disorders.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Neurobiological Mechanisms

At the core of epilepsy pathophysiology is an imbalance between cerebral excitation and inhibition within the central nervous system. Normally, neurotransmission is tightly regulated, with glutamate serving as the primary excitatory neurotransmitter and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) acting as the main inhibitory neurotransmitter. In epilepsy, hyperexcitability occurs due to various cellular and molecular alterations. These include abnormal ion channel function (channelopathies involving sodium, potassium, or calcium channels), alterations in extracellular potassium concentration, and deficiencies in GABA-mediated inhibition or excessive glutamate receptor activation, particularly N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors. This pathological state can originate from a localized group of neurons known as the epileptic focus or seizure onset zone. During a seizure, a 'paroxysmal depolarizing shift' occurs, causing neurons to fire at a rapid, sustained rate. This hypersynchronization recruits surrounding healthy neurons, causing the electrical storm to spread across one or both cerebral hemispheres. Understanding these neurobiological mechanisms is vital for nursing practice because antiseizure medications are specifically designed to target these ion channels and neurotransmitter pathways to restore neural balance.

Classification of Seizures and Epileptic Syndromes

Seizures are broadly classified based on three key features: where they begin in the brain, level of awareness during the seizure, and other features such as motor manifestations. The two main categories of onset are focal onset and generalized onset, with a third category of unknown onset. Focal seizures originate within networks limited to one cerebral hemisphere and can be further subdivided into focal aware (formerly simple partial) where consciousness remains intact, and focal impaired awareness (formerly complex partial) where consciousness is altered or lost. Generalized seizures originate at some point within and rapidly engage bilaterally distributed cerebral networks, affecting both hemispheres simultaneously. Examples of generalized seizures include tonic-clonic seizures, absence seizures, myoclonic seizures, and atonic seizures. Epileptic syndromes refer to clusters of features incorporating seizure types, EEG findings, age of onset, and genetic or structural etiology. Recognizing these classifications helps nurses document precise clinical descriptions, anticipate specific safety risks, and communicate effectively with the multidisciplinary healthcare team in Nigerian clinical settings.

Etiology and Common Risk Factors

The etiology of epilepsy is diverse and often categorized by the ILAE into six main groups: structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, and unknown. Structural causes include acquired brain injuries such as traumatic brain injuries from road traffic accidents, stroke, brain tumors, and congenital malformations of cortical development. In developing countries like Nigeria, central nervous system infections such as neurocysticercosis, cerebral malaria, meningitis, and viral encephalitis represent major preventable risk factors for acquired epilepsy. Genetic etiologies involve known or presumed genetic mutations affecting ion channels or synaptic receptors, often displaying familial inheritance patterns. Metabolic abnormalities, such as severe uremia, hypocalcemia, or inherited inborn errors of metabolism, can also precipitate seizures. Immune-mediated epilepsies involve autoimmune reactions directed against neuronal antigens. In a significant number of cases, however, the exact etiology remains unknown despite extensive diagnostic workup. Identifying the underlying risk factor is essential for nurses, as it guides secondary prevention strategies, such as promoting prompt treatment of childhood fevers, advocating for helmet use, and ensuring adequate management of infectious diseases.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Clinical manifestations of epilepsy vary widely depending on the type of seizure, the brain region involved, and the patient's age. A generalized tonic-clonic seizure typically begins with a sudden loss of consciousness, followed by a tonic phase characterized by muscle stiffening, a possible epileptic cry, and cyanosis due to respiratory muscle contraction. This is followed by the clonic phase, which involves rhythmic, synchronous jerking of the extremities. Postictally, the patient experiences deep sleep, confusion, headache, and muscle soreness, known as the postictal state. Conversely, absence seizures present as brief, sudden lapses in awareness lasting only a few seconds, often mistaken for daydreaming in school-aged children, accompanied by minor facial movements like eye blinking. Focal seizures may manifest with sensory symptoms (such as numbness or unusual smells, known as an aura), motor symptoms (such as automatisms like lip smacking or purposeless hand movements), or autonomic signs (such as flushing or tachycardia). Nurses must conduct thorough health assessments, noting pre-ictal triggers, exact descriptions of motor and sensory phenomena during the event, duration, and post-ictal recovery signs to assist physicians in accurate diagnosis.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Monitoring

Diagnosing epilepsy requires a comprehensive clinical evaluation that combines a detailed patient and witness history, neurological examination, and specialized diagnostic testing. The cornerstone neurophysiological test is the electroencephalogram (EEG), which records electrical activity of the brain via scalp electrodes. An EEG can identify abnormal epileptiform discharges, such as spike-and-wave patterns, and help classify the seizure type or epilepsy syndrome. However, a routine interictal EEG can be normal in up to 50% of patients with epilepsy, prompting the use of prolonged ambulatory EEG, sleep-deprived EEG, or video-EEG monitoring to capture seizure events. Neuroimaging studies, particularly Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), are vital to identify structural abnormalities such as hippocampal sclerosis, brain tumors, vascular malformations, or developmental dysplasias. Computed Tomography (CT) may be used in emergency settings or resource-limited environments to quickly rule out acute hemorrhage, stroke, or calcified neurocysticercosis lesions. Laboratory investigations, including full blood counts, electrolytes, renal and liver function tests, and toxicology screens, are also performed to rule out metabolic or toxic causes of acute symptomatic seizures.

Pharmacological and Surgical Management

The management of epilepsy primarily involves long-term pharmacological treatment using antiseizure medications (ASMs), formerly known as anticonvulsants. The goal of pharmacotherapy is complete seizure freedom with minimal adverse effects, allowing the patient to lead a normal life. ASMs work by various mechanisms, such as blocking voltage-gated sodium or calcium channels, enhancing GABAergic inhibition, or inhibiting glutamate transmission. Commonly prescribed ASMs include carbamazepine, sodium valproate, levetiracetam, lamotrigine, and phenytoin. Nurses play a critical role in educating patients about the importance of strict medication adherence, as abrupt cessation can trigger status epilepticus. Nurses must monitor for common drug side effects, such as sedation, dizziness, ataxia, gingival hyperplasia (with phenytoin), or teratogenic risks (particularly with valproate in women of childbearing potential). For patients with drug-resistant epilepsy—defined as failure of adequate trials of two tolerated and appropriately chosen ASM schedules—surgical options may be considered. Surgical interventions include resective surgery (removing the epileptic focus), laser interstitial thermal therapy, corpus callosotomy, or neuromodulation techniques such as vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) and responsive neurostimulation (RNS).

Nursing Care Plan and Status Epilepticus Protocols

Nursing care for patients with epilepsy encompasses acute seizure management, continuous monitoring, and emergency intervention protocols. When a patient experiences a generalized tonic-clonic seizure in a healthcare facility, the nurse's immediate priorities are safety, airway maintenance, and preventing injury. The nurse must gently guide the patient to the floor if standing, clear the surrounding area of hard or sharp objects, loosen tight clothing around the neck, and turn the patient onto their side (recovery position) to maintain a patent airway and prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. Nurses must never insert objects or fingers into the patient's mouth during a seizure, as this can cause dental damage or bite injuries. Continuous observation and precise timing of the seizure are critical. Status epilepticus—a medical emergency defined as continuous seizure activity lasting longer than five minutes or recurrent seizures without full recovery of consciousness between them—requires immediate emergency intervention. The nurse must follow strict hospital protocols: ensure a patent airway, administer high-flow oxygen, establish intravenous access, and promptly administer rapid-acting rescue medications such as IV diazepam or lorazepam, followed by longer-acting maintenance ASMs as prescribed by the physician.

Patient Education, Safety, and Psychosocial Support

Patient education and psychosocial support are indispensable components of holistic nursing care for individuals with epilepsy. Nurses must educate patients and their caregivers about lifestyle modifications, including adequate sleep hygiene, stress reduction, avoidance of known triggers such as excessive alcohol or flashing lights, and adherence to prescribed medication schedules. Safety counseling is paramount, including advising patients against swimming alone, operating heavy machinery, or driving until they meet legal and medical seizure-free requirements. In many Nigerian communities, epilepsy is shrouded in cultural myths, stigma, and misconceptions regarding supernatural causation or contagion, which can lead to social isolation, school drop-out, and reliance on unproven traditional remedies. Nurses serve as patient advocates and educators, debunking myths, promoting community awareness, and linking patients to support groups and mental health services. Addressing the psychosocial impact—such as anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem—ensures that patients with epilepsy achieve optimal quality of life and social reintegration.
