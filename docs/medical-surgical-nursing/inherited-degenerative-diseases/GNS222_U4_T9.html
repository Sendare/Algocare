Epilepsy

Introduction and Definition of Epilepsy

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate unprovoked seizures. A single seizure does not mean a person has epilepsy. Clinically, a diagnosis is established when a patient experiences at least two unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart, or one unprovoked seizure with a high probability of further seizures occurring over the next decade. It ranks among the most common brain disorders worldwide, affecting people of all ages, races, and social classes.

Misconceptions often blur the line between a seizure and epilepsy. While every seizure is a symptom of abnormal electrical activity in the brain, not every person who has a seizure has epilepsy; seizures can be provoked by temporary triggers like high fever, low blood sugar, or acute head trauma. Understanding this distinction is vital for accurate diagnosis and preventing unnecessary long-term medication use in patients who experienced only a single, provoked event.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Neurobiological Mechanisms

At the cellular level, a seizure occurs due to an imbalance between neuronal excitation and inhibition in the brain. Normally, a delicate balance exists between excitatory neurotransmitters, primarily glutamate, and inhibitory neurotransmitters, mainly gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). During an epileptic event, a group of neurons fires abnormally, excessively, and synchronously.

This hypersynchronous discharge can remain localized or spread to recruit adjacent and contralateral brain regions. Key mechanisms contributing to this state include:

  • Ion channel dysfunction: Genetic or acquired abnormalities in voltage-gated sodium, potassium, or calcium channels that lower the seizure threshold.
  • Glutamatergic hyperactivity: Excessive accumulation of extracellular glutamate leading to sustained depolarization.
  • GABAergic failure: Reduced inhibitory neurotransmission, allowing runaway electrical signals to propagate unchecked across neural networks.

Classification and Manifestations of Seizures

Seizures are broadly classified based on where they begin in the brain and how they manifest clinically. The International League Against Epilepsy categorizes them into focal, generalized, and unknown onset.

Etiology and Common Risk Factors

Epilepsy can arise from structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, or unknown causes. In many cases, especially among older adults, a clear structural lesion is identified.

Common risk factors and underlying causes include:

  • Traumatic brain injury: Head trauma from accidents or falls can form scar tissue that acts as an epileptic focus.
  • Stroke and cerebrovascular disease: Vascular damage is the leading cause of new-onset epilepsy in older populations.
  • Central nervous system infections: Meningitis and encephalitis can cause permanent inflammation and neuronal damage.
  • Genetic predisposition: Inherited ion channel mutations or familial epilepsy syndromes lower the seizure threshold.
  • Prenatal brain injury: Oxygen deprivation or infections during fetal development.

Identifying the etiology helps guide treatment choices and provides families with realistic expectations regarding long-term prognosis.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Monitoring

Diagnosing epilepsy requires a thorough clinical history, neurological examination, and targeted diagnostic testing. Because patients rarely experience a seizure during a clinic visit, clinicians rely heavily on descriptions from eyewitnesses alongside objective test results.

The cornerstone diagnostic tools include:

  • Electroencephalogram (EEG): Records brain wave patterns; epileptiform discharges like spikes and sharp waves support the diagnosis, though a normal EEG does not completely rule out epilepsy.
  • Neuroimaging: Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for identifying structural abnormalities such as hippocampal sclerosis, tumors, or developmental malformations.
  • Laboratory blood tests: Used to rule out metabolic disturbances, electrolyte imbalances, and acute systemic infections that can provoke seizures.
  • Video-EEG monitoring: Inpatient continuous recording that captures actual clinical events to correlate physical movements with brainwave patterns.

Pharmacological and Surgical Management

The primary goal of epilepsy management is complete seizure freedom with minimal medication side effects. Anti-seizure medications (ASMs) are the first line of treatment. These drugs work by stabilizing voltage-gated ion channels, enhancing GABA activity, or inhibiting glutamate receptors. Common agents include carbamazepine, levetiracetam, valproic acid, and lamotrigine.

When medications fail to control seizures after trying at least two appropriately chosen and tolerated ASMs, the condition is classified as drug-resistant epilepsy. At this stage, alternative therapies are evaluated:

  • Resective surgery: Removal of the specific epileptogenic zone in the brain, most commonly for temporal lobe epilepsy.
  • Neurostimulation: Devices such as vagus nerve stimulators (VNS) or responsive neurostimulation (RNS) systems that deliver electrical impulses to interrupt abnormal brain activity.
  • Ketogenic diet: A high-fat, low-carbohydrate diet that alters brain energy metabolism, often effective in pediatric drug-resistant cases.

Nursing Care Plan and Status Epilepticus Protocols

Caring for a patient experiencing an active seizure requires immediate action focused on safety and airway maintenance. Never insert objects into a seizing patient's mouth, as this risks breaking teeth, biting fingers, or causing airway obstruction. Instead, gently guide the patient to the floor, clear the surrounding area of hard or sharp objects, and turn them onto their side to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus.

Status epilepticus is a medical emergency defined as continuous seizure activity lasting longer than five minutes, or recurrent seizures without full recovery of consciousness in between. Immediate nursing actions during status epilepticus include:

  • Ensuring a patent airway and administering supplemental oxygen.
  • Establishing intravenous access and drawing baseline lab work.
  • Administering fast-acting rescue medications like IV lorazepam or diazepam as ordered.
  • Preparing for second-line long-acting anti-seizure medications such as levetiracetam or phenytoin if the seizure persists.

Patient Education, Safety, and Psychosocial Support

Living with epilepsy extends far beyond taking medication; it requires comprehensive education to help patients maintain independence while managing risks. Nurses play a crucial role in teaching patients about medication adherence. Abruptly stopping anti-seizure drugs can trigger status epilepticus. Patients must understand the importance of taking their doses consistently and reporting side effects rather than stopping treatment independently.

Lifestyle modifications are also essential for safety and seizure control. Common seizure triggers include sleep deprivation, excessive alcohol intake, extreme stress, and flashing lights in photosensitive epilepsy. Driving restrictions are legally mandated in many regions until a patient remains seizure-free for a specified duration, which can significantly impact employment and social independence. Addressing the psychological burden of anxiety and depression is vital to improving overall quality of life.
