Fits

Introduction to Fits and Epilepsy

A **fit**, medically termed a **seizure**, represents a transient occurrence of signs or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. It manifests as sudden, uncontrolled electrical disturbances that disrupt normal brain function, altering motor activity, sensation, behavior, or consciousness. While a single seizure can be provoked by an acute systemic stressor like severe hypoglycemia or high fever, **epilepsy** is diagnosed when an individual experiences two or more unprovoked seizures occurring more than twenty-four hours apart. Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder rather than a single disease entity, affecting individuals across all age demographics in Nigeria and worldwide. Understanding this distinction prevents mislabeling isolated acute symptomatic events as a chronic seizure disorder.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

At the cellular level, seizures emerge from an imbalance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission within the cerebral cortex. Normally, the brain maintains a delicate equilibrium where **glutamate** promotes neuronal excitation and **gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)** provides inhibitory control. During a seizure, this balance fails. Hyperexcitable neurons undergo sustained depolarization, characterized by a massive influx of calcium and sodium ions into the intracellular space and potassium efflux. This triggers repetitive, synchronized action potentials that recruit adjacent healthy neurons through local circuits. This hypersynchronization spreads across the cerebral hemispheres or remains localized, producing the specific clinical manifestations observed at the bedside.

Classification and Types of Seizures

Seizures are broadly classified based on where they originate in the brain and how the body responds. International classification guidelines divide them into focal, generalized, and unknown onset categories. - **Focal Seizures**: Originates within neuronal networks limited to one cerebral hemisphere. May be aware (formerly simple partial) or impaired awareness (formerly complex partial). - **Generalized Seizures**: Engages bilaterally distributed networks from the onset, immediately impairing consciousness. - **Tonic-Clonic Seizures**: A type of generalized seizure featuring muscle stiffening (tonic phase) followed by rhythmic jerking (clonic phase). - **Absence Seizures**: Brief, sudden lapses in consciousness lasting only a few seconds, commonly seen in children with a blank stare.

Etiology and Common Triggers

Identifying the underlying cause of seizures guides long-term management and prevention. While genetic factors play a significant role in idiopathic epilepsy, acquired causes include traumatic brain injury, central nervous system infections such as cerebral malaria or meningitis, perinatal hypoxia, and cerebrovascular accidents. Beyond structural and genetic etiologies, specific environmental and physiological triggers can provoke breakthrough seizures in diagnosed patients. Common triggers include: - Sleep deprivation and severe fatigue - Missed doses of prescribed antiepileptic medications - High fever or systemic infection - Flashing or strobe lights in photosensitive epilepsy - Excessive alcohol intake or sudden withdrawal

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough clinical assessment begins with a detailed eyewitness account of the seizure event, as patients rarely remember the onset. Nurses must gather precise descriptions of prodromal symptoms, motor involvement, eye deviation, and the post-ictal state. Diagnostic investigations help pinpoint the structural and electrical nature of the disorder. The **electroencephalogram (EEG)** records electrical patterns in the brain, identifying epileptiform discharges. Neuroimaging, specifically **Computed Tomography (CT)** or **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)**, is essential to rule out structural anomalies, tumors, or localized stroke scars. Blood panels are equally vital to check electrolyte balances, blood glucose levels, and exclude systemic toxins or infectious etiologies.

Acute Nursing Management During a Seizure

When encountering a patient experiencing an active tonic-clonic seizure, immediate nursing priorities center on patient safety and airway protection. Do not panic, and never attempt to force objects, padded spatulas, or fingers between clenched teeth, as this risks dental trauma, soft tissue laceration, and airway obstruction. Lower the patient gently to the floor if seated or standing, clearing the immediate surroundings of hard or sharp furniture. Turn the patient gently into the **recovery position** (lateral recumbent position) as soon as the clonic phase subsides to facilitate oral drainage and prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. Loosen restrictive clothing around the neck. Observe and time the duration precisely, staying with the patient until full consciousness returns.

Pharmacological Treatment and Antiepileptic Drugs

Long-term control of epilepsy relies on adherence to **antiepileptic drugs (AEDs)** such as carbamazepine, valproic acid, phenytoin, and levetiracetam. These medications work by stabilizing neuronal membranes, suppressing rapid repetitive firing, or enhancing GABAergic inhibition. Patient adherence is paramount; abrupt cessation can trigger severe withdrawal status epilepticus. Nurses must educate patients about monitoring for common adverse effects, including sedation, ataxia, and idiosyncratic skin rashes. Regular therapeutic drug monitoring is required for medications with a narrow safety margin, such as phenytoin, to ensure serum concentrations remain within the therapeutic window while avoiding systemic toxicity.

Complications and Status Epilepticus

**Status epilepticus** is a life-threatening medical emergency defined as continuous seizure activity lasting longer than five minutes, or recurrent seizures without full recovery of consciousness between episodes. Prolonged cerebral hypermetabolism leads to neuronal destruction, systemic acidosis, hypoxia, hyperthermia, and cardiovascular collapse. Emergency management requires rapid establishment of a patent airway, high-flow oxygen administration, and immediate intravenous access. First-line pharmacological treatment typically involves rapid-acting benzodiazepines like **lorazepam** or **diazepam**, followed by longer-acting non-sedating antiepileptic infusion such as phenytoin or levetiracetam if seizures persist.

Patient Education and Lifestyle Modifications

Comprehensive care extends beyond medication management to encompass practical lifestyle guidance. Nurses should counsel patients on identifying and avoiding personal seizure triggers, maintaining consistent sleep hygiene, and eating regular meals to prevent hypoglycemia. Safety precautions are vital for community reintegration. Patients should be advised to shower rather than take tub baths to minimize drowning risks, avoid swimming alone, and refrain from driving until certified seizure-free according to local transport regulations. Encouraging support groups helps mitigate the psychological stigma often associated with epilepsy in various communities.
