Fits

Introduction to Fits and Epilepsy

Fits, clinically referred to as seizures, represent transient occurrences of signs and/or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or synchronous neuronal activity in the brain. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice in Nigeria, it is vital to distinguish between a single isolated seizure and epilepsy. Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate epileptic seizures and by the neurobiological, cognitive, psychological, and social consequences of this condition. A diagnosis of epilepsy typically requires at least two unprovoked seizures occurring more than 24 hours apart, or one unprovoked seizure with a probability of further seizures similar to the general recurrence risk after two unprovoked seizures. Understanding this distinction helps nurses provide accurate documentation, educate patients, and reduce the heavy social stigma often associated with epilepsy in many communities. Seizures can manifest in diverse ways, ranging from dramatic convulsive movements to subtle sensory disturbances or brief lapses in awareness. Because the brain controls all bodily functions, abnormal electrical discharges can disrupt motor control, sensation, behavior, and consciousness. Recognizing these clinical presentations early allows nurses to implement prompt, safe, and effective interventions, ultimately improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

At the microscopic and cellular level, fits arise from a fundamental imbalance between neuronal excitation and inhibition within the central nervous system. Normally, the brain maintains a delicate equilibrium where excitatory neurotransmitters, primarily glutamate, and inhibitory neurotransmitters, primarily gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), regulate electrical signaling. During a seizure, this balance is disrupted, leading to hyperexcitability. This can occur through an excessive accumulation of extracellular potassium, an influx of calcium ions into neurons, or a deficiency in GABA-mediated inhibition. When a critical mass of neurons begins to fire synchronously, this electrical storm can remain localized to a specific region of the brain, resulting in a focal seizure, or it can rapidly propagate across both cerebral hemispheres, resulting in a generalized seizure. Prolonged seizure activity triggers compensatory mechanisms such as cerebral vasodilation and increased blood flow to meet the massive metabolic demand for oxygen and glucose. However, if the seizure persists, these compensatory mechanisms fail, leading to localized tissue hypoxia, cellular energy depletion, acidosis, and eventually neuronal death. Understanding these underlying mechanisms helps nursing students appreciate why prompt termination of prolonged seizure activity is critical for preventing permanent brain injury.

Classification and Types of Seizures

Seizures are broadly classified based on where they originate in the brain and how they manifest clinically, as defined by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE). The two main categories are focal seizures and generalized seizures. Focal seizures (formerly known as partial seizures) originate within networks limited to one cerebral hemisphere. They may be further subdivided into focal aware seizures, where consciousness remains intact, and focal impaired awareness seizures, where consciousness is altered or lost. Patients experiencing focal seizures may exhibit motor symptoms like rhythmic twitching or non-motor symptoms such as sensory distortions or sudden emotional changes. Conversely, generalized seizures originate at some point within and rapidly engage bilaterally distributed cerebral networks. Examples include generalized tonic-clonic seizures, which involve stiffening of the limbs followed by rhythmic jerking and loss of consciousness; absence seizures, characterized by brief, sudden lapses in consciousness often seen in children; and myoclonic seizures, involving brief, shock-like muscle jerks. Accurately classifying the type of seizure is essential for nursing documentation, selecting appropriate diagnostic tests, and administering targeted anti-seizure medications prescribed by the medical team.

Etiology and Common Triggers

The etiology of fits varies widely depending on the patient's age, underlying health status, and geographic location. In clinical practice, seizures are often categorized as provoked (acute symptomatic) or unprovoked. Provoked seizures occur secondary to an acute systemic or neurological insult, such as severe electrolyte imbalances (e.g., hyponatremia, hypocalcemia), hypoglycemia, high fever (febrile seizures in young children), acute traumatic brain injury, central nervous system infections like meningitis or cerebral malaria, and drug toxicity or withdrawal. In contrast, unprovoked seizures have no immediate identifiable acute trigger and are often linked to genetic predispositions, structural brain abnormalities such as tumors or stroke scars, or neurodegenerative conditions. Common triggers that can precipitate a seizure in an individual with pre-existing epilepsy include sleep deprivation, emotional stress, alcohol or substance abuse, flashing or strobe lights (photosensitive epilepsy), and missing scheduled doses of anti-epileptic drugs. For nurses working in Nigerian healthcare settings, identifying infectious etiologies like cerebral malaria or meningitis is a critical priority during initial patient assessment, as these conditions require urgent specific antimicrobial therapy alongside seizure management.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Clinical assessment of a patient presenting with a fit requires a systematic and comprehensive approach. The nursing and medical evaluation begins with a detailed history from the patient and, crucially, any eyewitnesses who observed the event. Nurses should inquire about prodromal symptoms, the exact time of onset, specific motor or sensory manifestations during the seizure, the duration of the episode, and the postictal state (the recovery period characterized by confusion, lethargy, or headache). A thorough physical and neurological examination must be performed to check for focal neurological deficits, signs of trauma, or systemic illness. Diagnostic evaluations typically include an electroencephalogram (EEG) to detect abnormal electrical patterns or epileptiform discharges in the brain. Neuroimaging studies, such as computed tomography (CT) scans or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are vital to identify structural abnormalities like tumors, infarcts, or developmental malformations. Laboratory investigations are equally important to rule out metabolic and systemic causes; these include blood glucose levels, serum electrolytes, full blood count, renal function tests, and toxicology screenings or infection screens such as malaria parasite tests and lumbar punctures when central nervous system infection is suspected.

Acute Nursing Management During a Seizure

Acute nursing management during an active seizure focuses primarily on maintaining patient safety, ensuring a patent airway, and preventing secondary injury. When a nurse encounters a patient experiencing a tonic-clonic seizure, the immediate action is to remain calm, stay with the patient, and call for assistance. The patient should be gently guided to the floor if seated or standing, and cleared away from hard, sharp, or dangerous objects to prevent trauma. The nurse must loosen any tight clothing around the neck and chest to facilitate breathing. Crucially, the patient should be turned gently onto their side (the recovery position) as soon as possible; this prevents aspiration of saliva, vomitus, or blood by allowing fluids to drain from the mouth. Nurses must never attempt to force objects, padded depressors, or fingers between the patient's clenched teeth, as this can cause dental damage, soft tissue lacerations, or airway obstruction. The nurse should note the exact time the seizure started and monitor its duration continuously. Supplemental oxygen should be administered via a nasal cannula or face mask if postictal hypoxia develops. After the seizure ceases, the nurse must allow the patient to rest in a safe, quiet environment while conducting ongoing neurological and vital sign checks during the postictal phase.

Pharmacological Treatment and Antiepileptic Drugs

Pharmacological management forms the cornerstone of long-term epilepsy control, aiming to eliminate seizures or reduce their frequency and severity while minimizing adverse drug effects. Antiepileptic drugs (AEDs), also known as antiseizure medications (ASMs), work through various mechanisms, such as blocking voltage-gated sodium or calcium channels, enhancing GABAergic inhibition, or inhibiting excitatory glutamate receptors. Common first-line AEDs include sodium valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin, and levetiracetam. Choosing a specific medication depends on the accurate classification of the seizure type, patient age, gender, comorbid conditions, and potential drug interactions. Nurses play an indispensable role in patient education regarding medication adherence, emphasizing that AEDs must be taken consistently as prescribed and should never be stopped abruptly, as sudden withdrawal can trigger status epilepticus. Nurses must monitor patients for common side effects such as drowsiness, dizziness, ataxia, cognitive slowing, and gingival hyperplasia (particularly with phenytoin). Regular monitoring of serum drug levels and hematological or hepatic function tests is often required for specific AEDs to ensure therapeutic efficacy and prevent organ toxicity. In female patients of childbearing age, nurses must also provide counseling regarding the teratogenic risks of certain AEDs, such as sodium valproate, and the importance of appropriate contraception and folic acid supplementation.

Complications and Status Epilepticus

Complications of fits range from physical injuries such as lacerations, burns, and fractures to profound neurological emergencies. The most life-threatening neurological complication is status epilepticus, defined as a continuous seizure lasting longer than five minutes, or recurrent seizures without full recovery of consciousness between episodes. Status epilepticus is a medical emergency that requires immediate intervention to prevent permanent brain damage, respiratory failure, and death. Initial acute management involves ensuring airway patency, administering high-flow oxygen, and establishing intravenous access to administer rapid-acting rescue medications, typically intravenous benzodiazepines such as lorazepam or diazepam. If seizures persist despite initial benzodiazepine administration, second-line intravenous anti-epileptic drugs such as phenytoin, levetiracetam, or sodium valproate must be administered promptly. If refractory status epilepticus occurs, the patient may need to be transferred to an intensive care unit (ICU) for endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, and continuous intravenous infusions of anesthetic agents like midazolam or propofol under electroencephalographic monitoring. Nurses must maintain constant vigilance, accurately time seizure episodes, prepare emergency equipment, and collaborate seamlessly with the multidisciplinary healthcare team during these high-acuity events.

Patient Education and Lifestyle Modifications

Patient education and lifestyle modifications are essential components of holistic nursing care for individuals living with epilepsy. Because epilepsy carries significant social stigma and lifestyle restrictions in many Nigerian communities, comprehensive education empowers patients and their families to take an active role in their care. Nurses should educate patients on identifying and avoiding individual seizure triggers, such as sleep deprivation, excessive alcohol consumption, and missing medication doses. Safety precautions are paramount; patients should be advised to avoid swimming alone, operating heavy machinery, or climbing to high places unless their seizures are fully controlled and they have received medical clearance. Driving restrictions are standard legal requirements that must be explained clearly to patients to protect public safety. Furthermore, nurses should provide guidance on managing daily stress, maintaining a balanced diet, and ensuring adequate rest. Counseling should also address psychosocial support, connecting patients and families with support groups to combat stigma, anxiety, and depression. Finally, family members must be thoroughly trained in basic first-aid measures for seizures, including how to protect the patient from injury, position them safely, and recognize when to seek emergency medical help.
