Fits

Understanding Fits and Epilepsy

A fit, medically known as a seizure, is a sudden surge of electrical activity in the brain that causes temporary changes in movement, behavior, feelings, or awareness. While a single fit can happen to anyone due to a high fever, head injury, or low blood sugar, epilepsy is a chronic neurological condition diagnosed when a person has two or more unprovoked seizures spaced at least 24 hours apart.

Students often confuse the two terms, so remember: every person with epilepsy has fits, but not everyone who has a fit has epilepsy. Recognizing this difference helps prevent misdiagnosis and ensures patients receive the correct long-term care plan.

What Happens in the Brain: Pathophysiology

Inside a healthy brain, nerve cells communicate using a careful balance of chemical signals that excite or calm activity. During a seizure, this delicate balance breaks down. A group of neurons fires off electrical impulses all at once, much too fast and much too strongly.

This abnormal wave of electricity spreads across the brain. Depending on where the wave starts and how far it travels, the patient might experience a twitching finger, a blank stare, or violent full-body shaking. Once the extra energy runs out, the brain enters a recovery phase called the postictal state, where the patient feels deeply confused, exhausted, or sleepy while brain cells restore their normal chemical balance.

Types of Seizures

Seizures are broadly split into two main groups based on how much of the brain is involved at the start:

  • Focal seizures: These start in just one specific area of the brain. The patient might remain fully awake and aware, or their awareness might be altered, causing them to stare blankly or perform repetitive movements like lip-smacking.
  • Generalized seizures: These involve electrical waves sweeping across both halves of the brain at the same time. The patient immediately loses consciousness and usually experiences full-body symptoms.

Within generalized seizures, a tonic-clonic seizure (formerly called grand mal) is the most dramatic, featuring sudden muscle stiffening followed by rhythmic jerking.

Triggers and Common Causes

Finding out why a patient has fits helps guide treatment. Causes vary greatly depending on age. In babies and children, high fevers, birth injuries, and genetic conditions are common. In adults and older people, strokes, head injuries, brain tumors, and infections are frequent culprits.

Aside from underlying diseases, certain everyday triggers can provoke a seizure in someone who is prone to them:

  1. Missed medication: Forgetting prescribed anti-seizure pills is the single most common trigger.
  2. Sleep deprivation: Operating on too little rest lowers the brain's seizure threshold.
  3. Stress and illness: High emotional stress or a raging fever puts extra strain on the nervous system.
  4. Flashing lights: For a small percentage of patients, strobe or flashing lights can spark an episode.

Assessing and Diagnosing Fits

When a patient comes to the hospital after a fit, the nurse and doctor must gather a detailed history. Since the patient rarely remembers the event, questioning a witness is vital. Healthcare staff will ask about what happened before, during, and after the episode, noting how long it lasted and what body parts moved.

Diagnostic tests help pinpoint the cause and confirm the diagnosis:

  • Electroencephalogram (EEG): Records the brain's electrical patterns to look for abnormal seizure spikes.
  • CT or MRI scans: Imaging studies that check for structural problems like bleeding, tumors, or scar tissue in the brain.
  • Blood tests: Check for electrolyte imbalances, low blood sugar, or signs of infection.

Acute Nursing Management During a Seizure

Watching a patient have a generalized seizure can be frightening, but the nurse must stay calm and act quickly to keep the patient safe. Your primary duty is to protect them from injury.

  • Gently guide the patient to the floor if they are standing or sitting in a chair.
  • Clear the surrounding area of sharp objects, furniture, or hard items.
  • Turn the patient gently onto their side (the recovery position) to keep the airway clear and let saliva drain out.
  • Loosen any tight clothing around the neck, such as ties or shirt buttons.

Common misconception: Never force anything into the patient's mouth, such as a spoon, padded tongue depressor, or your fingers. Doing so can break their teeth, cut their gums, or cause them to bite you.

Medications and Treatment

For most patients with epilepsy, antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) are the cornerstone of long-term management. These medications do not cure the underlying condition, but they stabilize brain chemistry to prevent future electrical storms.

Commonly used AEDs include medications like phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproic acid, and levetiracetam. Choosing the right drug depends on the type of seizure, the patient's age, and potential side effects.

Patient adherence is vital. Stopping these drugs suddenly can trigger severe, life-threatening seizures. Nurses must educate patients on taking their medication at the exact same time every day and keeping regular lab appointments to check drug levels in the blood.

Complications: Status Epilepticus

While most seizures stop on their own within a minute or two, a dangerous medical emergency called status epilepticus occurs when a seizure lasts longer than five minutes, or when seizures happen back-to-back without the patient regaining consciousness in between.

Status epilepticus is a critical emergency because the brain uses up massive amounts of oxygen and glucose during continuous firing. If the seizure is not stopped quickly, the brain can suffer permanent damage or oxygen deprivation. Emergency treatment requires maintaining a patent airway, giving supplemental oxygen, and administering fast-acting intravenous medications like diazepam or lorazepam to break the seizure cycle.

Patient Education and Lifestyle Changes

Living with fits requires adjustments to everyday routines to ensure safety and maintain quality of life. Nurses play a major role in teaching patients and their families how to navigate these changes.

  • Safety precautions: Patients should avoid swimming alone, working at dangerous heights, or operating heavy machinery until their seizures are fully controlled by medication.
  • Driving restrictions: Most transport laws require individuals to be seizure-free for a specific period (such as six months to a year) before getting a driver's license.
  • Medical identification: Wearing a bracelet or carrying a card that states they have epilepsy ensures prompt, correct treatment if a fit occurs in public.
  • Lifestyle balance: Getting enough sleep, eating regular meals, and finding healthy ways to manage stress all help keep the brain stable.
