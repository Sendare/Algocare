Alzheimer Disease

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's disease is a progressive, irreversible neurodegenerative disorder that slowly destroys memory, thinking skills, and the capacity to carry out simple tasks. It represents the most common cause of dementia among older adults globally. At the microscopic level, the disease is characterized by two major neuropathological hallmarks: - **Beta-amyloid plaques**: Dense deposits of protein fragments that accumulate outside and around neurons, disrupting cellular communication and triggering an immune response that causes inflammation and cell death. - **Neurofibrillary tangles**: Twisted fibers of a protein called **tau** that build up inside nerve cells, causing the transport system of the neuron to collapse and disintegrate. As these abnormal protein structures spread throughout the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, brain tissue shrinks significantly, a process known as cortical atrophy. Neurotransmitter depletion, particularly involving **acetylcholine**, further impairs cognitive function and memory circuits.

Stages and Clinical Manifestations

The clinical course of Alzheimer's disease is generally categorized into three broad phases: early (mild), middle (moderate), and late (severe). While symptom progression varies from person to person, the trajectory follows a predictable decline in cognitive and functional independence. | Stage | Key Clinical Manifestations | Functional Impact | | --- | --- | --- | | **Early / Mild** | Recent memory loss, word-finding difficulty, misplacing items, getting lost in familiar places. | Independent with instrumental activities, but requires mild prompting for complex tasks. | | **Middle / Moderate** | Increased confusion, personality changes, poor judgment, inability to recognize family, repetitive questioning. | Needs assistance with daily care, bathing, and dressing; urinary incontinence may begin. | | **Late / Severe** | Loss of verbal communication, inability to swallow, bed-bound status, primitive reflexes returning. | Completely dependent for all activities of life, requiring total nursing care. | In Nigerian clinical settings, family members often attribute early memory lapses to normal aging, meaning patients frequently present to healthcare facilities only when moderate or severe behavioral symptoms have already emerged.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Tools

Diagnosing Alzheimer's disease is primarily a process of exclusion, as definitive confirmation requires post-mortem brain autopsy. Clinical evaluation combines a detailed medical history, cognitive testing, neurological exams, and laboratory or imaging studies to rule out reversible causes of cognitive decline such as vitamin B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, neurosyphilis, or normal pressure hydrocephalus. Screening tools such as the **Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)** or the **Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)** help quantify cognitive impairment. Neuroimaging studies, including computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), reveal patterns of generalized or temporal lobe atrophy, while excluding tumors or strokes.

Pharmacological Management and Interventions

Medications do not cure Alzheimer's disease or halt its underlying neurodegeneration, but they can temporarily slow the worsening of symptoms and improve quality of life for some patients. 1. **Cholinesterase inhibitors**: Drugs like **donepezil**, rivastigmine, and galantamine prevent the breakdown of acetylcholine in the synaptic cleft, boosting neurotransmission in mild to moderate stages. 2. **NMDA receptor antagonists**: **Memantine** regulates the activity of glutamate, a chemical messenger that, when produced in excessive amounts during Alzheimer's, leads to brain cell death. This medication is typically used for moderate to severe stages. 3. **Symptomatic adjuncts**: Antipsychotics, antidepressants, or mood stabilizers may be prescribed cautiously for severe behavioral disturbances, though they carry significant risks of sedation and extrapyramidal effects in elderly populations.

Non-Pharmacological Care and Behavioral Management

Behavioral symptoms such as agitation, wandering, aggression, and sundowning (increased confusion and restlessness in the late afternoon or evening) frequently accompany Alzheimer's disease. Non-pharmacological strategies should always serve as the first line of defense before turning to psychotropic medications. Creating a structured, predictable daily routine minimizes cognitive stress. Environmental modifications, such as keeping rooms well-lit to reduce shadows, playing familiar soothing music, and validating the patient's feelings rather than arguing or correcting false beliefs, help diffuse agitation. Distraction and redirection are powerful tools when a patient becomes fixated on an impossible demand or distressed by an imagined scenario.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Safety

Safety is a paramount priority in nursing care for patients with Alzheimer's disease. As cognitive decline impairs hazard recognition, patients face high risks of falls, accidental ingestion of harmful substances, getting lost, and self-neglect. - **Environmental safety**: Install secure door locks, remove tripping hazards, secure cleaning supplies, and maintain adequate lighting. - **Nutrition and hydration**: Monitor food and fluid intake closely, offer finger foods if the patient cannot manage utensils, and watch for signs of aspiration as swallowing difficulties emerge. - **Hygiene support**: Establish step-by-step routines for bathing and dressing, ensuring water temperature is checked beforehand to prevent scald burns.

Support and Education for Family Caregivers

Caring for an individual with Alzheimer's disease places an immense physical, emotional, and financial burden on family members. In many Nigerian households, extended family networks share caregiving duties, but the primary caregiver often suffers from chronic exhaustion, sleep deprivation, and depression, collectively termed **caregiver role strain**. Nurses play a vital role in assessing caregiver well-being during clinical visits. Providing education on disease progression helps families set realistic expectations. Connecting caregivers with local support groups, respite care services, and community resources prevents burnout and reduces the risk of elder neglect or abuse.
