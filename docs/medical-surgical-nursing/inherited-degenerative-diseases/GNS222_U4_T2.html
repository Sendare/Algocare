Alzheimer Disease

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Alzheimer's Disease

Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia, characterized by a progressive and irreversible decline in cognitive function, memory, and behavior. As nursing students in Nigeria, understanding its underlying pathophysiology is vital for holistic patient care. The disease process is primarily marked by two major neuropathological hallmarks in the brain: extracellular beta-amyloid plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau proteins. Beta-amyloid plaques accumulate between neurons, disrupting cell-to-cell communication and triggering an immune response that causes inflammation and cellular damage. Meanwhile, tau proteins, which normally stabilize the internal skeleton of neurons called microtubules, become abnormally altered and clumped together into neurofibrillary tangles. This causes the internal transport system of the neuron to collapse, leading to neuronal death and structural brain atrophy, especially in regions critical for memory such as the hippocampus and the cerebral cortex. Furthermore, there is a significant deficiency in vital neurotransmitters, most notably acetylcholine, which is heavily involved in memory and learning functions. As these structural and chemical changes advance, the brain's ability to process and transmit information severely diminishes, resulting in the classic clinical symptoms observed in affected individuals. Recognizing these microscopic brain changes helps nurses appreciate why patients experience such profound cognitive losses over time.

Stages and Clinical Manifestations

Alzheimer's disease progresses through distinct stages—mild, moderate, and severe—each presenting with unique clinical manifestations that challenge both patients and caregivers. In the early or mild stage, individuals often experience subtle short-term memory loss, misplacing items, difficulty organizing tasks, and getting lost in familiar places. Judgment may become impaired, and subtle personality changes like apathy or mild depression can emerge. As the disease advances to the moderate stage, cognitive decline becomes much more pronounced. Patients struggle with complex activities such as managing finances, cooking, or dressing appropriately for the weather. They may experience confusion about their current location, time, or date, and display behavioral changes including agitation, wandering, suspicion, and hallucinations. In the final, severe stage, individuals lose the ability to respond to their environment, carry on conversations, and eventually control movement. They require full-time assistance with all activities of daily living, including feeding, bathing, and toileting. Physical complications such as dysphagia, rigidity, loss of bowel and bladder control, and vulnerability to aspiration pneumonia are common. For nursing professionals, identifying these stages accurately ensures that appropriate nursing interventions, safety measures, and palliative support are implemented at the right time in the disease trajectory.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Tools

Diagnosing Alzheimer's disease requires a comprehensive clinical evaluation because there is no single definitive test to confirm it while the patient is alive. The diagnostic process begins with a thorough medical history, physical examination, and detailed neurological assessment. Nurses and physicians often interview family members or primary caregivers to obtain an accurate timeline of cognitive and behavioral changes. Standardized screening tools and cognitive tests, such as the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) or the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), are utilized to objectively measure memory, attention, language, and visuospatial skills. Laboratory tests, including complete blood counts, thyroid-stimulating hormone levels, and vitamin B12 levels, are routinely performed to rule out reversible causes of cognitive impairment like infections, metabolic imbalances, or nutritional deficiencies. Furthermore, neuroimaging techniques such as Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) or Computed Tomography (CT) scans help visualize structural brain changes, showing characteristic cortical atrophy and ventricular enlargement while ruling out strokes, tumors, or subdural hematomas. In specialized settings, biomarker testing through cerebrospinal fluid analysis or positron emission tomography (PET) scans can detect abnormal amyloid and tau protein levels. A definitive diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease is ultimately confirmed post-mortem through brain autopsy, but clinical evaluations during life achieve high accuracy in identifying probable cases.

Pharmacological Management and Interventions

Pharmacological management in Alzheimer's disease aims to slow the progression of cognitive symptoms, manage behavioral disturbances, and improve the patient's quality of life, though no cure currently exists. Medications are broadly divided into two main categories: cholinesterase inhibitors and NMDA receptor antagonists. Cholinesterase inhibitors, such as donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine, work by inhibiting the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, thereby increasing the availability of acetylcholine at synaptic clefts. These drugs are typically prescribed for mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease to help maintain thinking, memory, and language skills, and to help with certain behavioral problems. Common side effects that nurses must monitor for include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, bradycardia, and dizziness. For moderate to severe stages, memantine is frequently prescribed. Memantine regulates the activity of glutamate, a major excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain that, when produced in excess during Alzheimer's disease, leads to neuronal overstimulation and cell death. In addition to cognitive enhancers, psychotropic medications such as atypical antipsychotics, antidepressants, or mood stabilizers may be used cautiously to treat severe neuropsychiatric symptoms like persistent hallucinations, delusions, or severe agitation when non-pharmacological methods fail. Nurses play a crucial role in patient education regarding medication adherence, monitoring for adverse drug reactions, and assessing therapeutic efficacy over time.

Non-Pharmacological Care and Behavioral Management

Non-pharmacological care and behavioral management form the cornerstone of daily nursing interventions for patients with Alzheimer's disease, prioritizing dignity, comfort, and safety. Behavioral disturbances such as aggression, wandering, anxiety, and sundowning (increased confusion and agitation in the late afternoon or evening) often stem from unmet needs, pain, fatigue, or environmental overstimulation. Nurses should train caregivers and healthcare staff to identify and address these underlying triggers rather than immediately resorting to chemical restraints. Creating a structured, predictable daily routine helps reduce anxiety and disorientation for the patient. Communication strategies must be adapted: nurses should speak in calm, clear, and simple sentences, maintain eye contact, use non-verbal reassuring gestures, and avoid arguing with a patient who is experiencing delusions or hallucinations. Promoting engagement through meaningful activities—such as listening to familiar music, looking at old photographs, light walking, or simple tactile tasks—enhances self-esteem and decreases agitation. Environmental modifications are equally important; keeping the living space well-lit, uncluttered, and secure helps prevent falls and minimizes confusion. By implementing these compassionate, person-centered strategies, nurses can significantly improve behavioral outcomes and enhance the overall well-being of individuals living with dementia.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Safety

Developing a comprehensive nursing care plan for a patient with Alzheimer's disease requires a continuous focus on patient safety, promotion of functional independence, and prevention of avoidable complications. Because cognitive deficits impair judgment and spatial awareness, environmental safety is a top priority. Nurses must advocate for and implement safety measures such as securing exits to prevent wandering, removing hazardous objects, installing grab bars in bathrooms, and ensuring adequate lighting to prevent falls. Nutrition and hydration status must be closely monitored; as the disease progresses, patients may forget to eat, lose their appetite, or develop dysphagia. Nurses should offer frequent, small, nutrient-dense meals, monitor weight changes, provide finger foods for independent eating, and ensure proper positioning during feeding to prevent aspiration. Maintaining skin integrity is another critical nursing responsibility, especially for patients with immobility or incontinence, requiring regular turning schedules, skin assessments, and prompt hygiene care. Furthermore, nurses should encourage self-care activities within the patient's remaining capabilities to preserve dignity and delay functional decline. Collaborating closely with multidisciplinary healthcare teams—including physiotherapists, dietitians, and social workers—ensures that the complex physical and safety needs of the patient are comprehensively met throughout the disease trajectory.

Support and Education for Family Caregivers

Supporting and educating family caregivers is an indispensable component of comprehensive Alzheimer's disease care, particularly in Nigeria where family-based caregiving is deeply rooted in cultural traditions. Caring for a relative with progressive dementia places immense physical, emotional, and financial strain on family members, often leading to caregiver burnout, depression, and compromised health. Nurses play a pivotal role in assessing caregiver readiness and well-being during clinic visits or home health assessments. Educational interventions must cover disease progression, expected behavioral changes, safe transfer techniques, communication strategies, and stress management skills. Nurses should also connect families with local support groups, community resources, and respite care services to provide temporary relief from the demanding responsibilities of 24-hour care. Encouraging caregivers to maintain their own health, seek emotional support, and share caregiving tasks among extended family members can significantly mitigate the risk of burnout. By empowering families with knowledge, practical skills, and compassionate guidance, nurses help sustain the well-being of both the caregiver and the patient, ensuring a safer and more supportive home environment.
