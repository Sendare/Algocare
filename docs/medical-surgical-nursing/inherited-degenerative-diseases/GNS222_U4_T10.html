Convulsions

Introduction and Definition of Convulsions

A convulsion is a medical emergency characterized by sudden, violent, and involuntary contractions of a group of muscles. Clinically, it presents as uncontrolled shaking and altered motor activity resulting from abnormal electrical discharges within the cerebral cortex. While often used interchangeably with the word seizure, a convulsion specifically denotes the motor manifestations, whereas a seizure is a broader term that can include sensory, autonomic, or psychic disturbances without violent shaking. Understanding this distinction is vital for accurate clinical documentation in Nigerian emergency wards, where rapid identification dictates subsequent resuscitation steps.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Neurological Mechanisms

Normal brain function relies on a delicate balance between neuronal excitation and inhibition. A convulsion erupts when this equilibrium shatters, typically driven by an excess of the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate and a deficiency of the inhibitory neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). Neurons depolarize synchronously, firing in rapid, uncontrolled bursts that sweep across neural networks. This paroxysmal depolarization shift recruits adjacent healthy neurons, amplifying the electrical storm until metabolic exhaustion or inhibitory feedback mechanisms finally terminate the episode.

Classification and Types of Convulsive Seizures

Seizures are broadly categorized based on where they originate in the brain and how the patient presents clinically. - **Generalized onset seizures**: These engage networks bilaterally across both cerebral hemispheres from the very beginning. Patients typically lose consciousness immediately. - **Focal onset seizures**: These originate within neuronal networks limited to one cerebral hemisphere. They may be further subdivided into focal aware (consciousness maintained) or focal impaired awareness (consciousness altered). - **Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic**: A seizure that starts in one localized area of the brain but quickly spreads to involve both hemispheres, culminating in generalized shaking.

Etiology and Common Triggers

Convulsions stem from diverse underlying causes that disrupt cerebral stability. In low- and middle-income settings like Nigeria, infectious diseases such as cerebral malaria, bacterial meningitis, and viral encephalitis are prominent culprits, particularly among pediatric populations. Other major triggers include high fever leading to febrile convulsions, traumatic brain injuries, metabolic derangements like severe hypoglycemia or hypocalcemia, central nervous system tumors, and abrupt withdrawal from alcohol or anticonvulsant medications.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

A classic tonic-clonic convulsion unfolds in distinct phases. During the **tonic phase**, skeletal muscles suddenly stiffen, limbs extend rigidly, and the patient may emit a cry due to forced expiration across closed vocal cords, often turning cyanotic. This transitions into the **clonic phase**, characterized by rhythmic, symmetrical jerking of the extremities. Afterward, the **postictal phase** sets in, featuring deep sleep, confusion, headache, and complete amnesia of the event. When assessing the patient, note the exact start time, body parts involved, progression pattern, and postictal neurological deficits.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Investigations

Diagnosing the root cause of a convulsion requires a methodical clinical workup. The gold standard for investigating brain electrical patterns is the **electroencephalogram (EEG)**, which identifies epileptiform discharges. Neuroimaging, such as computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), helps visualize structural lesions like tumors, infarcts, or abscesses. Laboratory blood tests check for electrolyte imbalances, blood glucose levels, systemic infections, and toxicological screens.

Acute Nursing Management and Emergency Care

When a patient convulses before your eyes, immediate safety is paramount. Do not panic, and never attempt to force objects between clenched teeth, as this risks breaking teeth and causing airway obstruction. Gently roll the patient onto their side into the recovery position to prevent aspiration of saliva or vomitus. Clear the surrounding environment of sharp objects, loosen tight clothing around the neck, and administer supplemental oxygen if cyanosis is present. Always time the duration of the seizure, as prolonged episodes require urgent pharmacological intervention.

Pharmacological Treatment and Patient Education

Long-term control of seizures relies on consistent use of antiseizure medications (ASMs) such as sodium valproate, carbamazepine, or phenytoin. Adherence is vital; sudden cessation can provoke rebound status epilepticus. Patient and family education must emphasize that ASMs manage rather than cure the underlying condition. Counsel patients against driving, operating heavy machinery, or swimming alone until they achieve prolonged seizure freedom. In resource-limited settings, nurses play a key role in addressing cultural stigmas and reinforcing the importance of regular clinic follow-ups.

Complications and Status Epilepticus

The most feared acute complication of a convulsion is **status epilepticus**, defined as a continuous seizure lasting longer than five minutes or recurrent seizures without full recovery of consciousness between episodes. This is a medical emergency because prolonged cerebral hyperexcitability causes systemic exhaustion, hyperthermia, hypoxia, acidosis, and permanent neuronal damage. First-line emergency management requires immediate intravenous access and administration of fast-acting benzodiazepines like diazepam or lorazepam, followed by longer-acting anticonvulsants under intensive monitoring.
