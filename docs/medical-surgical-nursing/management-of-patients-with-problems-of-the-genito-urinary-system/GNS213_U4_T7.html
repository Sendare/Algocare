Chronic Renal Failure

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Chronic Renal Failure

Chronic Renal Failure, also termed chronic kidney disease, represents the progressive, irreversible loss of renal function over months to years. As functioning nephrons are gradually destroyed, the remaining nephrons undergo structural and functional hypertrophy to compensate for the lost filtration capacity. This compensatory hyperfiltration initially maintains the glomerular filtration rate, but over time, the increased workload damages the remaining nephrons, accelerating global glomerular sclerosis and interstitial fibrosis. Eventually, the kidneys can no longer regulate fluid and electrolyte balance, excrete nitrogenous wastes, or produce adequate erythropoietin and active vitamin D. The accumulation of metabolic waste products in the bloodstream leads to uremia, a toxic systemic syndrome affecting virtually every organ system in the body.

Stages and Classification of Chronic Kidney Disease

Chronic kidney disease is classified into five distinct stages based on the estimated glomerular filtration rate, regardless of the presence of kidney damage markers. - **Stage 1**: Kidney damage with normal or high glomerular filtration rate, measuring 90 millilitres per minute or greater. - **Stage 2**: Mild reduction in glomerular filtration rate, ranging between 60 and 89 millilitres per minute with documented kidney damage. - **Stage 3**: Moderate reduction in glomerular filtration rate, spanning 30 to 59 millilitres per minute, often accompanied by early laboratory abnormalities. - **Stage 4**: Severe reduction in glomerular filtration rate, falling between 15 and 29 millilitres per minute, signaling intensive preparation for renal replacement therapy. - **Stage 5**: Kidney failure or end-stage renal disease, with a glomerular filtration rate of less than 15 millilitres per minute or requiring dialysis.

Etiology and Common Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

The driving forces behind chronic renal failure in Nigeria reflect a dual burden of infectious diseases and rising non-communicable disorders. Chronic glomerulonephritis, frequently linked to untreated or recurrent streptococcal and tropical infections in childhood, remains a leading cause of end-stage kidney disease. Concurrently, systemic hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus are surging across urban and rural populations, contributing heavily to adult renal admissions. Other notable local risk factors include the indiscriminate use of over-the-counter herbal concoctions, unregulated non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and heavy metal exposure from contaminated skin-lightening creams. Late presentation to healthcare facilities is a persistent challenge, often occurring only when patients reach advanced uremic stages due to economic constraints and reliance on alternative medicine.

Clinical Manifestations and Systemic Effects

As renal function declines, systemic toxins disrupt multiple organ systems. Fluid retention leads to peripheral edema, pulmonary congestion, and hypertension. Electrolyte imbalances, particularly hyperkalemia, pose life-threatening cardiac arrhythmia risks. Hematologically, diminished erythropoietin synthesis causes normocytic normochromic anemia, presenting with profound fatigue and pallor, while uremic platelet dysfunction increases bleeding tendencies. Neurologically, patients experience uremic encephalopathy, peripheral neuropathy, and asterixis. Integumentary changes include severe pruritus, uremic frost from crystallized urea on the skin, and a yellowish-brown skin pallor. Gastrointestinal symptoms feature anorexia, nausea, vomiting, and a characteristic uremic fetor with a uriniferous breath odor.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Accurate assessment of chronic renal failure relies on a combination of blood tests, urine analysis, and imaging modalities. Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels become markedly elevated as glomerular filtration drops, though serum creatinine alone is an imperfect marker because it varies with muscle mass. The estimated glomerular filtration rate, calculated using formulas incorporating age, sex, and serum creatinine, provides a standardized measure of kidney function. Urinalysis reveals persistent proteinuria, hematuria, and casts. Renal ultrasound typically demonstrates small, shrunken, echogenic kidneys with loss of corticomedullary differentiation, differentiating chronic disease from acute kidney injury where kidneys are usually normal or enlarged.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Treatment

Medical therapy aims to slow disease progression, manage comorbid conditions, and alleviate uremic symptoms. Blood pressure control is paramount, utilizing angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers to reduce intraglomerular pressure and proteinuria. Dietary modifications restrict protein, sodium, potassium, and phosphorus intake to minimize uremic toxin buildup and electrolyte imbalances. Phosphate binders, such as calcium carbonate, are administered with meals to bind dietary phosphorus in the gut. Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents and iron supplements correct anemia, while loop diuretics manage fluid overload. Metabolic acidosis is corrected using oral sodium bicarbonate supplementation.

Renal Replacement Therapy: Dialysis and Transplantation

When conservative medical management fails to sustain life, renal replacement therapy becomes mandatory. Hemodialysis filters blood extracorporeally through a synthetic dialyzer, requiring a reliable vascular access such as an arteriovenous fistula or graft created months in advance. Peritoneal dialysis utilizes the patient's peritoneal membrane as a natural filter, allowing dialysate fluid to be infused and drained through an abdominal catheter. Kidney transplantation offers the best long-term survival and quality of life, involving the surgical placement of a healthy donor kidney from a living or deceased donor. Post-transplantation care requires lifelong immunosuppressive therapy to prevent graft rejection, placing patients at higher risk for opportunistic infections.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for chronic renal failure involves meticulous monitoring of fluid balance, vital signs, and laboratory parameters. Nurses must record daily weights, strict fluid intake and output, and assess for signs of fluid overload or electrolyte crisis. Skin care is crucial to manage pruritus caused by uremia and calcium-phosphate imbalances, using mild cleansers and emollient lotions. Patient education empowers individuals to adhere to complex therapeutic regimens, including fluid restrictions, prescribed low-potassium diets, and medication schedules. Nurses should also teach patients with vascular access how to check for a palpable thrill and audible bruit daily to ensure patency and prevent clotting.
