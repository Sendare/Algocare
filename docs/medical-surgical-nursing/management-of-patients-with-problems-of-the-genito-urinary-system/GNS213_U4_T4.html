Acute Glomerulonephritis

Introduction and Definition of Acute Glomerulonephritis

Acute glomerulonephritis (AGN) is an inflammatory condition affecting the glomeruli, the delicate filtering units of the kidneys. This inflammation leads to a sudden deterioration in renal function, marked by the inability to properly filter blood, regulate electrolyte balance, and excrete waste products. While it can stem from various systemic triggers, post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis remains the classic archetype taught in clinical settings. The glomeruli become hypercellular and edematous, impairing the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and resulting in fluid retention, hypertension, and characteristic urinary changes. Recognizing this condition early prevents permanent renal damage and chronic kidney disease development.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

The pathogenesis of acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis is rooted in an immune-mediated reaction. Following a recent streptococcal infection, circulating antigen-antibody immune complexes form and become trapped within the glomerular basement membrane. Once lodged, these immune complexes activate the complement cascade, triggering a powerful inflammatory response. Neutrophils and mononuclear cells infiltrate the glomeruli, causing proliferation of endothelial and mesangial cells. This cellular crowding narrows the capillary lumen, drastically dropping the glomerular filtration rate. Consequently, salt and water are retained, driving the classic triad of edema, oliguria, and hypertension.

Etiology and Common Bacterial Triggers in Nigeria

While Group A beta-hemolytic Streptococcus is the universally recognized classic trigger, the etiological landscape in Nigeria is heavily shaped by environmental and socioeconomic factors. Streptococcal pharyngitis is a common precursor, but impetigo and infected scabies lesions are equally, if not more, prevalent triggers in tropical settings. Chronic skin infections provide a continuous antigenic stimulus that initiates immune complex deposition. Other infectious agents, including viral pathogens and parasites, can occasionally induce similar glomerular injury, but post-streptococcal sequelae remain the predominant driver among pediatric and young adult admissions in local health facilities.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

  • Periorbital Edema: Puffiness around the eyes, particularly prominent upon waking in the morning due to overnight fluid redistribution.
  • Hematuria: Urine presenting as smoky, cola-colored, or rust-colored, reflecting the passage of red blood cells through the damaged glomerular filter.
  • Oliguria: A notable reduction in daily urine output, signaling compromised renal clearance.
  • Hypertension: Mild to severe elevation in blood pressure resulting from hypervolemia, which can occasionally trigger hypertensive encephalopathy.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Confirming acute glomerulonephritis requires a combination of urinalysis, blood tests, and microbiological confirmation. Urinalysis consistently reveals gross or microscopic hematuria, alongside varying degrees of proteinuria and red blood cell casts. Serum chemistry typically shows elevated urea and creatinine levels, reflecting declining kidney function. To confirm a recent streptococcal infection, clinicians order an anti-streptolysin O (ASO) titer, though throat or skin swabs may also be cultured. Serum complement levels, specifically C3, are frequently transiently depressed due to consumption by the activated immune pathway.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Management

Medical treatment for acute glomerulonephritis is primarily supportive, focusing on managing fluid balance, controlling hypertension, and eradicating any lingering infection. If a streptococcal infection is still suspected or active, a full course of antibiotics, such as penicillin, is administered regardless of culture results. Diuretics, particularly loop diuretics like furosemide, are prescribed to manage fluid overload and reduce blood pressure. Antihypertensive medications may be added if fluid restriction and diuresis fail to normalize blood pressure. Dietary modifications, including sodium and potassium restriction, are instituted to ease the workload on the healing kidneys.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

While many individuals recover completely, severe acute glomerulonephritis can precipitate life-threatening complications. Hypertensive encephalopathy occurs when extreme blood pressure elevation causes cerebral edema, manifesting as severe headache, confusion, vomiting, and seizures. Acute cardiac decompensation and pulmonary edema can also arise from hypervolemia and fluid overload. Furthermore, acute kidney injury may progress rapidly, demanding emergency renal replacement therapy such as peritoneal dialysis or hemodialysis. Nurses must remain vigilant for sudden neurological changes or respiratory distress, acting swiftly to administer emergency antihypertensives or oxygen therapy.

Prognosis, Patient Education, and Home Care

The overall prognosis for pediatric patients with post-streptococcal acute glomerulonephritis is exceptionally favorable, with the vast majority achieving complete recovery. However, convalescence requires careful adherence to post-discharge care guidelines. Families must understand the importance of completing any prescribed antibiotic regimens fully to eradicate the primary infection source. Dietary restrictions on salt may need to continue temporarily until normal renal function and blood pressure are confirmed. Follow-up appointments involving routine urinalysis and blood pressure checks are essential to verify that hematuria and hypertension have completely resolved.
