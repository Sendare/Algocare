Acute Glomerulonephritis

Introduction and Definition of Acute Glomerulonephritis

Acute Glomerulonephritis (AGN) is a sudden inflammation of the glomeruli, the tiny filtering units inside the kidneys. When these structures become inflamed and swollen, they cannot properly filter waste products and extra fluid from the blood. This condition most commonly develops as an immune reaction following an infection elsewhere in the body. While it can affect people of all ages, school-aged children are particularly vulnerable. Understanding how the kidneys suddenly lose their filtering ability helps nurses spot early warning signs before severe kidney damage occurs.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

The disease process usually begins with an antigen-antibody reaction. Immune complexes form in the bloodstream after an infection and become trapped inside the tiny capillaries of the glomeruli. This trapping triggers a strong immune response, releasing inflammatory mediators that damage the delicate filtering membrane. Consequently, the basement membrane becomes more permeable, allowing red blood cells and large protein molecules to leak into the forming urine. As filtration slows down drastically, salt and water are retained in the body, leading directly to fluid overload.

Etiology and Common Causes in the Nigerian Context

In Nigeria and many tropical regions, Post-Streptococcal Glomerulonephritis (APSGN) remains the most frequent form, typically following untreated or poorly treated skin sores, impetigo, or streptococcal throat infections. However, healthcare workers must also watch for secondary causes linked to local infectious burdens.

  • Streptococcal Infections: Group A beta-hemolytic streptococcus skin or throat infections.
  • Malaria: Plasmodium infections can sometimes trigger immune-complex deposition.
  • Chronic Viral Infections: Hepatitis B and C viruses are notable contributors.
  • Skin Sepsis: Boils and infected tropical ulcers common in children.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Patients present with a classic picture that nurses can easily spot during physical assessment. Hematuria is a hallmark sign, giving urine a dark, smoky, or cola-colored appearance due to broken-down blood cells. Periorbital edema, especially noticeable around the eyes upon waking up, develops because of fluid retention. Hypertension occurs because the kidneys fail to excrete sodium and water. Patients often report feeling fatigued, and urine output may drop significantly, a condition known as oliguria.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Confirming the diagnosis requires a combination of blood tests, urine analysis, and sometimes imaging. Urinalysis reveals heavy proteinuria and red blood cells. Blood tests typically show elevated urea and creatinine levels, signaling decreased kidney clearance. To confirm a recent streptococcal link, doctors order an ASO (Anti-Streptolysin O) titer test. Complement levels, specifically C3, often drop sharply during the active phase of the illness because the immune system consumes them.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Medical treatment focuses on supporting kidney function, managing high blood pressure, and clearing any remaining infection. Antibiotics like penicillin are prescribed if a streptococcal infection is still present. Loop diuretics help reduce fluid overload and control edema. Antihypertensive medications manage elevated blood pressure to protect vital organs. Dietary modifications, particularly restricting sodium and potassium, are critical until kidney function fully rebounds.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Management

Nurses play a central role in monitoring the recovery process and preventing complications. Accurate intake and output measurement is non-negotiable; every drop of urine must be recorded. Daily weights provide the most reliable indicator of fluid retention or loss. Nurses must monitor vital signs frequently, watching out for sudden spikes in blood pressure. Providing skin care is also vital because edematous tissue breaks down easily.

Potential Complications and Emergency Interventions

While most patients recover fully, severe cases can lead to life-threatening emergencies. Hypertensive encephalopathy can cause severe headaches, confusion, and seizures due to extremely high blood pressure. Acute kidney injury may progress rapidly, requiring temporary dialysis. Another major risk is circulatory overload leading to acute pulmonary edema, which causes severe respiratory distress. Nurses must recognize these warning signs immediately and alert the medical team.

Prognosis, Patient Education, and Home Care

The overall prognosis for children with Acute Glomerulonephritis is generally excellent, with the vast majority making a complete recovery. Educating families before discharge is crucial to prevent recurrence and catch lingering issues early. Parents should learn how to check for returning swelling and monitor urine color. Nurses must emphasize the importance of completing any prescribed antibiotic courses fully, even after the child starts feeling better. Follow-up appointments for repeat urinalysis ensure the kidneys have healed completely.
