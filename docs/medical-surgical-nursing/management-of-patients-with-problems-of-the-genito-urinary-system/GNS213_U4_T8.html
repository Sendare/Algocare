Nephrotic Conditions of the Kidney

Pathophysiology and Core Manifestations

Nephrotic conditions are fundamentally defined by a massive increase in the permeability of the glomerular capillary wall. Normally, the basement membrane and podocytes restrict large molecules like albumin from entering the urinary space. When these structures are damaged, the glomerular filtration barrier loses its negative charge and structural integrity, leading to heavy proteinuria—specifically exceeding 3.5 grams per 24 hours in adults. This profound urinary loss of protein depletes the intravascular pool, causing severe hypoalbuminemia.

As the oncotic pressure within the capillaries drops, fluid shifts from the intravascular space into the interstitial tissues, triggering generalized edema. The body senses this effective circulating volume depletion as hypovolemia, activating compensatory neurohormonal pathways such as the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and the sympathetic nervous system. Consequently, the kidneys retain sodium and water, worsening the edema. Concurrently, the liver attempts to compensate for low plasma proteins by upregulating lipid synthesis, resulting in marked hyperlipidemia and lipiduria.

Classification and Common Etiologies

Nephrotic syndrome is broadly split into primary causes, which originate directly within the kidney architecture, and secondary causes, where systemic diseases impact renal function.

  • Minimal Change Disease: The most frequent cause in pediatric populations, characterized by normal-appearing glomeruli under light microscopy but effacement of foot processes on electron microscopy.
  • Focal Segmental Glomerulosclerosis: Involves scarring of some, but not all, glomeruli; a leading cause of primary nephrotic syndrome in adults, particularly common in individuals of African descent.
  • Membranous Nephropathy: Caused by immune complex deposition along the glomerular basement membrane, thickening the capillary walls.
  • Systemic Lupus Erythematosus: A major secondary cause where autoimmune complexes trigger lupus nephritis.
  • Diabetes Mellitus: Diabetic nephropathy leads to nodular glomerulosclerosis and chronic filtration barrier damage over years of poorly controlled blood glucose.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Evaluation

The clinical picture of a patient with a nephrotic condition is dominated by fluid overload and metabolic imbalance. The initial and most striking complaint is typically progressive, pitting edema that begins around the eyes periorbitally in the morning and shifts to the dependent lower extremities by evening. Ascites and pleural effusions may develop as fluid retention worsens. Patients often report fatigue, lethargy, and frothy urine caused by heavy proteinuria.

Diagnostic confirmation relies on a combination of blood tests, urine analysis, and sometimes tissue biopsy:

  • Urinalysis: Reveals 3+ to 4+ protein on dipstick, alongside oval fat bodies and fatty casts.
  • Blood Chemistry: Shows marked hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin below 2.5 g/dL), elevated serum cholesterol, and normal or slightly elevated creatinine depending on the chronicity.
  • Renal Biopsy: Performed primarily in adults to pinpoint the exact histological subtype when secondary causes have been ruled out.

Pharmacological Management and Medical Therapy

Treatment strategies aim to halt proteinuria, induce remission, and manage downstream complications like edema and hypertension. Corticosteroids, particularly prednisone, remain the first-line therapy for minimal change disease, though treatment regimens can span several months to prevent relapse. For steroid-resistant cases or membranous nephropathy, cytotoxic agents, calcineurin inhibitors like cyclosporine, or biologic therapies such as rituximab are introduced.

Supportive pharmacological management focuses on protecting the kidneys and reducing fluid overload:

  • ACE Inhibitors or ARBs: These medications reduce intraglomerular pressure and decrease protein leakage across the damaged basement membrane.
  • Loop Diuretics: Furosemide is administered to mobilize excess fluid, frequently combined with albumin infusions in severe hypoalbuminemia to draw fluid back into the vascular space before diuresis.
  • Statins: Prescribed to control hyperlipidemia and lower long-term cardiovascular risks.

Nutritional Support and Fluid Management

Nutritional planning requires a delicate balance. While patients lose large amounts of protein in the urine, aggressive high-protein diets are generally discouraged because they can hyperfilter remaining functional nephrons and accelerate glomerular scarring. Instead, a normal-protein diet providing approximately 0.8 to 1.0 g/kg/day of high biological value protein is recommended.

Sodium restriction is vital to control edema and limit the effectiveness of diuretic therapy. Patients must adhere to a low-sodium diet, typically under 2 grams daily. Fluid restriction may also be instituted if serum sodium levels drop into the hyponatremic range due to excessive free water retention. Energy intake should be adequate to prevent muscle wasting, while saturated fat and cholesterol intake should be minimized to manage concurrent hyperlipidemia.

Complications and Nursing Interventions

The structural and chemical alterations of nephrotic syndrome place patients at high risk for several life-threatening complications. Thromboembolism is a major concern, particularly renal vein thrombosis, because the liver loses important anticoagulant factors like antithrombin III in the urine while overproducing procoagulant factors. Infections, especially peritonitis and cellulitis, occur frequently due to the urinary loss of immunoglobulins and immunosuppressive medication regimens. Acute kidney injury can also develop secondary to severe intravascular volume depletion.

Nursing care centers on rigorous monitoring and preventive actions:

  • Fluid Balance: Track daily weights at the same time on the same scale, measure precise intake and output, and assess abdominal girth or limb circumference.
  • Skin Integrity: Protect edematous skin from breakdown by implementing frequent repositioning and using specialized pressure-relieving mattresses.
  • Vascular Surveillance: Monitor closely for signs of deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolus, such as unilateral calf pain, swelling, or sudden dyspnea.

Patient Education and Home Care Considerations

Long-term management requires extensive education to empower patients and their families to recognize early signs of relapse and manage daily care regimens. Patients must understand how to perform accurate daily weight monitoring at home, noting that a sudden weight gain of more than one to two kilograms over a couple of days usually signals fluid retention rather than true tissue growth.

Education must also cover medication adherence, particularly regarding corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, emphasizing that abrupt cessation can trigger a severe relapse. In resource-limited settings such as parts of Nigeria, reinforcing the importance of accessing primary healthcare centers for routine urine protein testing and blood pressure checks prevents delayed interventions. Patients should be taught to report early signs of infection, such as low-grade fever or sore throat, immediately, given their suppressed immune status.
