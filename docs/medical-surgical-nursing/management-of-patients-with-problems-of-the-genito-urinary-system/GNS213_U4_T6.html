Acute Renal Failure

Introduction and Definition of Acute Renal Failure

Acute renal failure, now widely referred to in clinical practice as acute kidney injury (AKI), is a sudden and rapid decline in kidney function that occurs over a period of hours to days. This abrupt impairment prevents the kidneys from effectively filtering waste products from the blood, maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance, and regulating acid-base homeostasis.

When these vital organs fail acutely, nitrogenous waste products like urea and creatinine rapidly accumulate in the bloodstream, a clinical state known as azotemia. Unlike chronic kidney disease, which develops slowly over months or years and involves permanent scarring, acute renal failure is potentially reversible if the underlying cause is identified and treated promptly. However, untreated or severe episodes can lead to permanent structural damage or death.

Classification of Causes: Prerenal, Intrarenal, and Postrenal

To effectively manage acute renal failure, clinicians categorize the underlying causes into three distinct anatomical and physiological groups:

  • Prerenal causes: Factors that reduce systemic blood flow and decrease perfusion to the kidneys before blood reaches the renal tissue. Common triggers include severe dehydration, hemorrhage, shock, and heart failure.
  • Intrarenal causes: Direct damage to the actual kidney tissue, including the glomeruli, tubules, or interstitium. This is frequently caused by toxic medications, severe infections, or prolonged ischemia leading to acute tubular necrosis (ATN).
  • Postrenal causes: Any mechanical obstruction that blocks the flow of urine out of the urinary tract. Examples include enlarged prostate glands, kidney stones, and bladder tumors that cause urine to back up and damage the nephrons.

Stages and Clinical Manifestations

The clinical course of acute renal failure typically unfolds across four distinct phases:

  1. Onset stage: The initial insult occurs, and kidney injury begins, though outward symptoms may be minimal.
  2. Oliguric stage: Urine output drops significantly, often falling below 400 mL per day. Fluid overload, electrolyte imbalances, and severe azotemia become prominent here.
  3. Diuretic stage: The underlying cause is corrected, and kidney function begins recovering. The kidneys produce a large volume of dilute urine, creating a high risk for dehydration.
  4. Recovery stage: GFR normalizes, and metabolic waste levels return to baseline, though mild tubular weakness may persist for weeks.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnosing acute renal failure requires a careful combination of blood work, urine tests, and imaging studies to determine both the presence of injury and its root cause.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management focuses on correcting the primary trigger, maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance, and preventing further cellular damage. If a prerenal cause is identified, fluid resuscitation using normal saline or blood products is initiated to restore renal perfusion. Conversely, if fluid overload is present, loop diuretics like furosemide may be administered to encourage urine output.

Hyperkalemia is a life-threatening complication of acute renal failure that requires immediate intervention. Medications such as regular insulin combined with glucose, sodium polystyrene sulfonate, or intravenous calcium gluconate are used to shift potassium into cells or remove it from the body, protecting the heart from fatal arrhythmias.

Nursing Care and Fluid Management

Nurses play a critical role in monitoring hemodynamic stability and managing fluid balance in patients with acute renal failure. Accurate measurement of strict fluid intake and output (I&O) is mandatory, tracking every milliliter of fluid entering and leaving the body.

Daily patient weighing provides the most sensitive indicator of fluid retention or loss; an increase of one kilogram generally corresponds to one liter of retained fluid. Nurses must also monitor vital signs closely for signs of hypertension caused by fluid overload or hypotension signaling worsening shock. Dietary restrictions are implemented to limit potassium, sodium, and protein, reducing the metabolic workload on the recovering kidneys.

Complications and Renal Replacement Therapy

When acute renal failure becomes severe or fails to respond to conservative medical management, dangerous systemic complications arise that necessitate renal replacement therapy (RRT). Common indications for dialysis include severe metabolic acidosis, persistent electrolyte imbalances like uncontrolled hyperkalemia, encephalopathy, and profound fluid overload that does not respond to diuretics.

Modalities of RRT include intermittent hemodialysis and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), the latter being preferred for unstable patients in intensive care units because it removes fluids and toxins slowly and gently over 24 hours.
