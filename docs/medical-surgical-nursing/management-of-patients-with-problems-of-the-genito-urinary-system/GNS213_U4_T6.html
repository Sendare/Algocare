Acute Renal Failure

Introduction and Definition of Acute Renal Failure

Acute renal failure, now more commonly termed acute kidney injury (AKI), represents a sudden and rapid decline in renal function occurring over hours to days. This abrupt impairment causes a failure to maintain fluid, electrolyte, and acid-base homeostasis, leading to nitrogenous waste accumulation in the blood. Recognizing this syndrome early is crucial in Nigerian clinical practice, where delayed presentation due to traditional remedies or late referrals can rapidly escalate a reversible insult into permanent organ damage.

Etiological Classification: Prerenal, Intrarenal, and Postrenal

Causes of acute renal failure are systematically categorized into three broad physiological groups:

  • Prerenal causes: Factors reducing systemic blood flow and renal perfusion, such as severe dehydration, hemorrhage, septic shock, or heart failure.
  • Intrarenal causes: Conditions causing direct structural damage to the renal parenchyma, including acute tubular necrosis, glomerulonephritis, and nephrotoxic drugs like certain aminoglycoside antibiotics.
  • Postrenal causes: Mechanical obstructions of the urinary outflow tract, such as bilateral ureteral stones, benign prostatic hyperplasia, or strictures.

Pathophysiological Mechanisms

When renal perfusion plummets or toxins destroy tubular cells, the kidneys undergo complex cellular cascades. In prerenal azotemia, the nephrons attempt to compensate by maximizing sodium and water reabsorption, producing concentrated, scant urine with a very low sodium content. If ischemia persists, cellular hypoxia leads to acute tubular necrosis (ATN), where epithelial cells slough off and plug the tubular lumen, raising intratubular pressure and halting glomerular filtration entirely. Understanding this progression helps explain why prolonged hypotension spells disaster for the renal tubules.

Clinical Manifestations and Stages

The clinical presentation evolves through distinct phases, beginning with the onset phase, followed by the oliguria phase (urine output less than 400 mL per day), the diuresis phase where nephrons start recovering but cannot yet concentrate urine effectively, and finally the recovery phase.

As waste products accumulate, patients exhibit signs of uremia, including generalized edema, hypertension, nausea, lethargy, and encephalopathy. Electrolyte disturbances, particularly hyperkalemia, pose an immediate threat to cardiac conduction.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnosing acute renal failure relies heavily on monitoring biochemical markers and imaging studies. Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels rise sharply as filtration fails. The BUN-to-creatinine ratio helps differentiate prerenal azotemia (where the ratio often exceeds 20:1 due to disproportionate urea reabsorption) from intrarenal damage. Urinalysis reveals casts, protein, or blood cells, while renal ultrasonography is indispensable for ruling out postrenal obstructive causes.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management focuses on correcting the underlying cause, managing fluid balance, and treating life-threatening complications. Loop diuretics may be administered to convert oliguric renal failure to non-oliguric failure, easing fluid management. Hyperkalemia requires aggressive treatment using intravenous calcium gluconate to protect the myocardium, alongside insulin and glucose infusions to drive potassium back into intracellular spaces. If conservative therapies fail to control uremia or acid-base imbalances, emergency dialysis becomes necessary.

Nursing Care and Fluid Management

Accurate calculation of fluid balance is the cornerstone of nursing care in acute renal failure. Nurses must meticulously record daily weights, intravenous infusions, and precise hourly urine output. Fluid intake is typically restricted to match the measured urine output from the previous twenty-four hours plus an estimated allowance for insensible losses. Additionally, nurses enforce dietary restrictions to limit potassium, sodium, and protein intake, minimizing the metabolic workload on the recovering kidneys.
