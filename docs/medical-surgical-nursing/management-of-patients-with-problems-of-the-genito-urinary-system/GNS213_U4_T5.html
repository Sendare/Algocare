Chronic Glomerulonephritis

Introduction and Definition of Chronic Glomerulonephritis

Chronic glomerulonephritis is a slowly progressive, destructive renal disorder characterized by inflammation of the glomerular capillaries, eventually leading to glomerular scarring, nephron destruction, and permanent loss of kidney function. Unlike acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis which typically presents with a sudden onset and high recovery rate, the chronic variant develops insidiously over many years. Patients often remain completely asymptomatic during the early stages, allowing the underlying renal damage to progress silently until a significant portion of functional nephrons are already destroyed. As the disease advances, the kidneys symmetrically shrink in size, developing a granular surface and a thickened capsule. The end-stage structural remodeling transforms functional renal parenchyma into fibrous scar tissue, ultimately resulting in chronic kidney disease and the necessity for renal replacement therapy such as dialysis or kidney transplantation.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

The underlying mechanism of chronic glomerulonephritis centers on immune-mediated injury. Circulating antigen-antibody complexes become trapped within the glomerular basement membrane, or antibodies form directly against local glomerular antigens. This deposition triggers the activation of the complement cascade, local recruitment of inflammatory cells like neutrophils and macrophages, and the release of destructive lysosomal enzymes and reactive oxygen species. Mesangial cells proliferate excessively, and extracellular matrix proteins accumulate abnormally, narrowing the capillary lumen and reducing the glomerular filtration rate. Over time, sustained intraglomerular hypertension forces hyperfiltration in the remaining viable nephrons, accelerating their eventual destruction through sclerosis.

Etiology and Risk Factors

While chronic glomerulonephritis can evolve from an unresolving case of acute glomerulonephritis, many cases arise without any documented history of an acute episode. Systemic autoimmune disorders frequently precipitate this condition, most notably systemic lupus erythematosus and Goodpasture syndrome. Chronic infections, primary vasculitides, and prolonged exposure to certain nephrotoxic agents also contribute significantly to the disease burden.

  • Systemic Lupus Erythematosus: Causes lupus nephritis through widespread immune complex deposition.
  • Primary Hypertension: Accelerates vascular damage and compounds glomerular injury.
  • Diabetic Nephropathy: Often coexists and exacerbates microvascular deterioration.
  • Genetic Predisposition: Family history can influence susceptibility to immune-mediated renal injury.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Patients with chronic glomerulonephritis present with a constellation of findings reflecting failing renal filtration and fluid overload. The physical assessment often reveals peripheral edema, particularly in the periorbital region and lower extremities, driven by sodium retention and hypoalbuminemia. Hypertension is nearly universal and frequently difficult to control. As uremic toxins accumulate in the bloodstream, systemic symptoms emerge, including generalized fatigue, pruritus, nausea, and anorexia.

  • Urinary Changes: Persistent proteinuria, hematuria, and oliguria in advanced stages.
  • Cardiovascular: Elevated blood pressure, peripheral edema, and signs of circulatory overload.
  • Neurological: Mild cognitive impairment, confusion, or asterixis in severe uremia.
  • Dermatological: Pallor from anemia and dry skin with scratch marks from uremic pruritus.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Diagnosing chronic glomerulonephritis requires a combination of laboratory blood tests, urinalysis, imaging, and definitive histological confirmation. Urinalysis typically demonstrates persistent protein, red blood cells, and cellular casts. Serum laboratory panels reveal elevated blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine, alongside a progressively declining glomerular filtration rate. Anemia is frequently noted due to diminished erythropoietin production by the failing kidneys. Renal ultrasound generally shows small, symmetrical kidneys with increased echogenicity, indicating chronic fibrotic change. When the diagnosis remains uncertain and the kidneys are not excessively shrunken, a renal biopsy is performed to visualize the extent of glomerular scarring and guide therapeutic direction.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Therapeutic management focuses on slowing disease progression, controlling blood pressure, minimizing proteinuria, and managing electrolyte imbalances. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers are frontline pharmacologic agents because they reduce intraglomerular pressure and decrease protein leakage across the basement membrane. Diuretics are prescribed to manage fluid retention and control hypertension. Dietary modifications are equally critical, involving restricted sodium, potassium, and phosphorus intake, alongside controlled protein consumption to reduce the metabolic workload on remaining nephrons. When end-stage renal disease is reached, preparation for renal replacement therapy becomes the primary medical objective.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nursing care centers on continuous hemodynamic monitoring, strict fluid balance tracking, and empowering the patient through targeted education. Nurses must record daily weights, measure precise intake and output, and monitor for signs of worsening heart failure or electrolyte disturbances like hyperkalemia. Education must cover complex dietary restrictions, medication adherence, and the importance of monitoring blood pressure at home. Because chronic illness profoundly impacts lifestyle and emotional well-being, nurses also assess coping mechanisms and connect clients with psychosocial support systems.

Complications and Long-Term Prognosis

The long-term prognosis for chronic glomerulonephritis is guarded, as the disease path invariably leads to permanent renal failure. Major complications include severe hypertension, electrolyte imbalances such as hyperkalemia and metabolic acidosis, mineral and bone disorder, and uremic encephalopathy. Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality in these patients, exacerbated by chronic fluid overload, hypertension, and accelerated atherosclerosis. Ultimately, when conservative medical management can no longer sustain life, the patient transitions to long-term dialysis or is evaluated for kidney transplantation to restore homeostatic balance.
