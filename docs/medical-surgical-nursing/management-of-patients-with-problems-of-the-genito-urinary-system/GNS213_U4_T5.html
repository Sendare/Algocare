Chronic Glomerulonephritis

Introduction and Definition of Chronic Glomerulonephritis

Chronic glomerulonephritis is a progressive, irreversible disease characterized by inflammation of the glomeruli, leading to glomerular scarring (glomerulosclerosis) and eventual destruction of renal nephrons. Unlike acute glomerulonephritis, which may present suddenly and often resolve with prompt treatment, the chronic form develops insidiously over many years. As functioning nephrons are progressively destroyed, the remaining nephrons undergo hypertrophy and hyperfiltration to compensate for the lost renal mass. Over time, this chronic compensatory overload damages the remaining nephrons as well, creating a vicious cycle of further loss. Eventually, this progressive decline in the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) culminates in end-stage renal disease (ESRD), requiring renal replacement therapies such as dialysis or kidney transplantation. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding this condition is crucial as the prevalence of chronic kidney disease and its precursors, such as untreated hypertension and chronic infections, poses a significant public health challenge. Nurses must recognize that patients with chronic glomerulonephritis often remain asymptomatic for long periods, leading to delayed presentation until advanced kidney damage has already occurred. Early identification of risk factors and routine screening of vulnerable populations are vital components of modern nursing care aimed at slowing disease progression and improving patient outcomes in resource-limited healthcare settings across West Africa.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

The pathophysiology of chronic glomerulonephritis revolves around immune-mediated mechanisms and non-immune hemodynamic alterations. Often initiated by an unresolved immune response, such as persistent immune complex deposition or cellular-mediated injury within the glomerular capillary walls, the process triggers a chronic inflammatory cascade. Inflammatory cells, including macrophages and neutrophils, release cytokines, chemokines, and reactive oxygen species that damage the glomerular basement membrane and mesangial cells. As the glomerular structure is chronically injured, mesangial matrix expands, and fibroblasts proliferate, culminating in irreversible interstitial fibrosis and glomerulosclerosis. Hemodynamically, the loss of functional nephrons forces the remaining healthy nephrons to increase their single-nephron GFR through intraglomerular hypertension and hyperfiltration. While this compensatory mechanism temporarily maintains overall renal function, the sustained high capillary pressure eventually damages the delicate glomerular architecture, leading to further albuminuria, protein reabsorption toxicity, tubular atrophy, and progressive nephron loss. Understanding these overlapping immunologic and hemodynamic pathways helps nursing students appreciate why blood pressure control and reduction of proteinuria are vital therapeutic targets in halting the relentless progression of chronic kidney disease.

Causes and Risk Factors

Chronic glomerulonephritis can arise from a primary renal disorder or develop secondary to systemic diseases. Primary causes include chronic IgA nephropathy, membranous nephropathy, and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, where the pathological process primarily targets the kidney. Secondary causes are frequently linked to systemic autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), vasculitis, diabetes mellitus, and chronic hypertension. Infections, including chronic hepatitis B and C or persistent streptococcal infections, also serve as significant risk factors by triggering chronic immune complex deposition within the glomeruli. In many settings across Nigeria and sub-Saharan Africa, delayed treatment of acute post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis and poorly controlled hypertension are major contributors to the development of chronic glomerular disease. Genetic predispositions, family history of kidney disease, advanced age, and exposure to certain nephrotoxic substances or heavy metals further elevate an individual's risk. Identifying these diverse causes and risk factors during patient assessment enables nurses to advocate for early screening, lifestyle modifications, and aggressive management of underlying systemic conditions like hypertension and diabetes.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

The clinical manifestations of chronic glomerulonephritis reflect the progressive loss of renal function and the systemic consequences of uremia, fluid overload, and electrolyte imbalances. In the early to moderate stages, patients may experience persistent hypertension, mild peripheral edema (especially periorbital swelling), and mild fatigue. As the disease advances, symptoms become more pronounced and multisystemic. Patients often report generalized fatigue, weakness, and anorexia secondary to anemia and the accumulation of uremic toxins. Fluid retention leads to worsening peripheral edema, ascites, and potentially pulmonary edema, causing dyspnea. Hypertension frequently worsens due to sodium and water retention and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, increasing the risk of hypertensive emergencies and cardiovascular complications. Urinalysis findings typically reveal persistent proteinuria, microscopic or macroscopic hematuria, and cellular casts. In late stages, uremic syndrome manifests with nausea, vomiting, pruritus, confusion, and peripheral neuropathy. Nurses must conduct comprehensive physical assessments, closely monitoring vital signs—especially blood pressure—assessing fluid balance through strict intake and output measurements, and daily weight tracking to detect early signs of fluid overload or worsening renal failure.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Diagnostic evaluation of chronic glomerulonephritis relies on a combination of laboratory blood tests, urinalysis, imaging studies, and renal biopsy. Routine blood tests typically show an elevated serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), reflecting a decreased glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Serum electrolyte panels may reveal hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia (due to impaired vitamin D activation), and metabolic acidosis. Complete blood count (CBC) often demonstrates normocytic, normochromic anemia caused by reduced erythropoietin synthesis by the failing kidneys. Urinalysis reveals persistent proteinuria, hematuria, and red blood cell casts, which point toward glomerular inflammation and damage. Radiological imaging, such as renal ultrasound, usually shows symmetrically small, shrunken kidneys with increased echogenicity, distinguishing chronic kidney disease from acute kidney injury where kidneys are typically normal or enlarged. When feasible and safe, a renal biopsy may be performed to confirm the diagnosis, determine the exact histological subtype, and assess the degree of irreversible fibrosis, although in end-stage disease with severely shrunken kidneys, a biopsy is often contraindicated due to high bleeding risks and lack of modifiable tissue changes.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical and pharmacological management of chronic glomerulonephritis focuses on slowing disease progression, managing complications, and treating underlying causes. Because hypertension accelerates renal destruction, aggressive blood pressure control is paramount; Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or Angiotensin II Receptor Blockers (ARBs) are the preferred first-line agents because they reduce intraglomerular pressure and decrease proteinuria. Diuretics, such as loop diuretics (e.g., furosemide), are prescribed to manage fluid overload, peripheral edema, and hypertension. Dietary protein restriction may be recommended to reduce the workload on remaining nephrons and minimize uremic waste accumulation, though nutritional status must be carefully monitored to prevent malnutrition. Management of mineral and bone disorders involves phosphate binders (to control serum phosphate levels) and active vitamin D analogs. For patients with autoimmune etiologies, immunosuppressive therapy, such as corticosteroids and cytotoxic agents, may be utilized early in the disease course to suppress ongoing glomerular inflammation. As the disease inevitably progresses toward end-stage renal disease, preparations for renal replacement therapies—such as hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or kidney transplantation evaluation—become the core focus of the medical plan.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nursing care for patients with chronic glomerulonephritis requires a holistic, patient-centered approach encompassing clinical monitoring, symptom management, and extensive patient education. Nurses must maintain rigorous monitoring of vital signs, especially blood pressure trends, and perform daily weight checks and accurate intake and output recordings to evaluate fluid status and diuretic efficacy. Assessment for signs of hyperkalemia, fluid overload, and uremia is essential. Patient education plays a pivotal role in empowering individuals to manage their chronic condition effectively. Nurses must educate patients on strict adherence to prescribed medication regimens, emphasizing that antihypertensive and diuretic drugs must be taken consistently even when the patient feels well. Dietary counseling is equally critical; patients must understand the importance of sodium restriction to control blood pressure and edema, potassium restriction to prevent life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias, and appropriate protein moderation. Additionally, nurses should educate patients on recognizing early warning signs of complications, such as sudden weight gain, severe headache, oliguria, or shortness of breath, and encourage regular follow-up visits to monitor kidney function parameters like serum creatinine and GFR.

Complications and Long-Term Prognosis

The long-term prognosis for patients with chronic glomerulonephritis is guarded, as the disease is typically progressive and culminates in end-stage renal disease (ESRD) over a variable period ranging from years to decades. Frequent complications include severe hypertension, electrolyte imbalances (particularly hyperkalemia and hyperphosphatemia), metabolic acidosis, mineral and bone disorder, and anemia-induced cardiovascular strain. Chronic fluid overload and uremia can lead to congestive heart failure, uremic pericarditis, and encephalopathy. The ultimate prognosis depends heavily on early diagnosis, aggressive control of blood pressure, minimization of proteinuria, and patient compliance with dietary and pharmacological regimens. When patients reach ESRD, renal replacement therapy becomes mandatory for survival. Kidney transplantation offers the best long-term survival and quality of life compared to chronic dialysis, provided donor compatibility and post-transplant care are optimized. For nursing students, recognizing the trajectory of these complications underscores the importance of preventive nephrology, early intervention, and compassionate palliative support when curative treatment options are exhausted.
