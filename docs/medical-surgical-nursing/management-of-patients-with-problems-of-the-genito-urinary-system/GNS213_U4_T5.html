Chronic Glomerulonephritis

Introduction and Definition of Chronic Glomerulonephritis

Chronic glomerulonephritis is a slowly progressive, destructive disease of the glomeruli—the tiny filtering units inside the kidneys. Unlike acute forms that appear suddenly and might resolve, this condition develops silently over many years. As the glomeruli are repeatedly damaged, they undergo scarring, a process known as glomerulosclerosis. This gradual destruction leads to a permanent loss of kidney function, eventually reducing the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) to dangerously low levels. Many patients do not realize anything is wrong until significant kidney damage has already occurred, which is why nurses must understand how subtly this disease progresses.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

The underlying cause of most chronic glomerular damage involves an immune-mediated response. Immune complexes form and become trapped within the glomerular capillary membrane, or antibodies attack the basement membrane directly. This triggers a destructive inflammatory cascade involving white blood cells, complement activation, and the release of inflammatory chemicals. Over time, this chronic inflammation causes cell death, thickening of the capillary walls, and replacement of functional kidney tissue with fibrous scar tissue. As nephrons are destroyed, the remaining healthy nephrons work harder to filter the blood, leading to hyperfiltration, high pressure inside the glomeruli, and further long-term damage.

Causes and Risk Factors

Chronic glomerulonephritis can stem from several primary kidney diseases or develop secondary to systemic illnesses. Understanding these roots helps nurses identify vulnerable patients during admission assessments.

  • Unresolved acute glomerulonephritis: A previous acute infection or immune reaction that never fully healed.
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus: An autoimmune condition where the body attacks its own tissues, including the kidneys.
  • Diabetes mellitus: High blood sugar that damages small blood vessels throughout the body, including kidney filters.
  • Chronic hypertension: Long-standing high blood pressure that strains and hardens the delicate glomerular blood vessels.
  • Inherited disorders: Genetic conditions like Alport syndrome that affect the structural proteins of the kidney.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

As kidney function deteriorates, the clinical picture changes from subtle findings to classic signs of fluid overload and toxin buildup. Nurses must perform thorough physical assessments to catch these shifts early.

  • Edema: Swelling, particularly around the eyes in the morning and in the feet and ankles by evening, caused by fluid retention and low blood protein.
  • Hypertension: High blood pressure resulting from fluid overload and increased renin production by struggling kidneys.
  • Fatigue and weakness: General tiredness caused by anemia, which happens because damaged kidneys stop producing enough erythropoietin.
  • Urinary changes: Foamy urine due to protein loss (proteinuria), along with blood in the urine (hematuria).
  • Uremic symptoms: Nausea, itching, and confusion in advanced stages as waste products build up in the bloodstream.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Confirming chronic glomerulonephritis requires a combination of blood tests, urine analysis, and imaging studies. Urinalysis typically reveals persistent proteinuria and microscopic hematuria. Blood tests show elevated serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) levels, indicating that waste products are no longer being cleared efficiently from the blood. The glomerular filtration rate (GFR) will be markedly reduced. Ultrasound imaging often shows symmetrical, small, shrunken kidneys, which is a hallmark sign of long-term, irreversible damage. In some cases, a kidney biopsy is performed to identify the exact cellular pattern of damage, though it may be avoided if the kidneys are already too small and scarred.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical treatment focuses on slowing the progression of the disease, controlling symptoms, and managing complications. Because high blood pressure accelerates kidney destruction, aggressive antihypertensive therapy is the cornerstone of management. Medications like ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) are frequently prescribed because they lower pressure inside the glomeruli and reduce protein leakage. Diuretics are used to control fluid overload and reduce peripheral edema. If an autoimmune process is actively driving the inflammation, immunosuppressive drugs or corticosteroids may be ordered. When kidneys fail completely, renal replacement therapies such as dialysis or kidney transplantation become necessary.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nurses play a vital role in monitoring stability, preventing further injury, and guiding lifestyle changes. Daily weight tracking is essential because a sudden gain often signals dangerous fluid retention before edema is visibly obvious. Nurses must also monitor strict intake and output records and assess blood pressure regularly. Dietary modifications require careful teaching; patients often need to restrict sodium to manage blood pressure and fluid balance, limit potassium to prevent dangerous heart rhythms, and moderate protein intake to reduce the workload on damaged kidneys. Education should also emphasize avoiding nephrotoxic substances like nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).

Complications and Long-Term Prognosis

As chronic glomerulonephritis advances into end-stage kidney disease, systemic complications multiply. Electrolyte imbalances—especially high potassium levels—can trigger fatal cardiac dysrhythmias. Metabolic acidosis develops because the kidneys can no longer excrete hydrogen ions. Bone and mineral disorders occur due to altered calcium and phosphorus metabolism, leading to weak, brittle bones. Ultimately, the prognosis for untreated chronic glomerulonephritis is end-stage renal disease, requiring lifelong dialysis or a kidney transplant for survival. Early intervention and rigorous blood pressure control remain the best strategies to delay this outcome.
