Intravenous Pyelogram

Introduction and Definition of Intravenous Pyelogram

An intravenous pyelogram, commonly known as an IVP, is a specialized diagnostic imaging test that uses x-rays and a special contrast dye to examine the kidneys, ureters, and urinary bladder. When the radiopaque contrast medium is injected into a peripheral vein, it travels through the bloodstream, filters through the glomeruli, and highlights the entire urinary tract as it is excreted. This process allows healthcare providers to visualize the drainage of urine and detect structural or functional abnormalities that routine abdominal x-rays might miss. Although newer modalities like CT urography have become popular, the IVP remains a reliable method for assessing renal architecture.

Indications and Clinical Purposes

Clinicians order an IVP when they suspect pathology within the renal system that requires visual confirmation of urine flow and anatomical structure. Common reasons for performing the test include:

  • Kidney stones: Locating radiopaque calculi in the renal pelvis or ureters.
  • Hematuria: Investigating the underlying source of blood in the urine.
  • Flank pain: Identifying structural obstructions causing severe renal colic.
  • Recurrent urinary tract infections: Checking for congenital anomalies like a duplex collecting system.
  • Trauma: Assessing for renal lacerations or urinary leaks after physical injury.

Contraindications and Risk Assessment

Before scheduling an IVP, nurses and physicians must carefully screen patients to prevent severe adverse reactions. The iodine-based contrast medium poses significant risks for certain populations.

  • Allergy to iodine or shellfish: Increases the risk of an anaphylactic reaction.
  • Renal impairment: Elevated serum creatinine or blood urea nitrogen means the kidneys cannot clear the dye effectively, potentially causing contrast-induced nephropathy.
  • Pregnancy: Ionizing radiation and contrast agents can harm the developing fetus.
  • Metformin use: Must be temporarily withheld to prevent lactic acidosis if renal function declines after contrast administration.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Responsibilities

Thorough preparation ensures high-quality imaging and patient safety during an IVP. The nurse plays a pivotal role in getting the patient ready physically and emotionally.

  1. Obtaining informed consent: Ensure the patient understands the procedure, risks, and benefits.
  2. Allergy screening: Specifically check for past reactions to iodine, shellfish, or adhesive tape.
  3. Bowel preparation: Administer prescribed laxatives or enemas the evening before to clear gas and stool that could obscure the kidneys on x-ray.
  4. NPO status: Keep the patient fasting for several hours before the exam to reduce the risk of aspiration if nausea occurs from the dye.
  5. Voiding before the test: Ask the patient to empty their bladder immediately before the procedure starts so baseline residual urine does not interfere.

The IVP Procedure Step-by-Step

The IVP procedure follows a strict chronological sequence to capture the contrast medium as it moves through the renal system. First, a preliminary plain abdominal x-ray is taken to check patient positioning and bowel preparation. Next, the radiologist or nurse inserts an intravenous line and injects the contrast medium. Serial x-ray films are then captured at timed intervals—typically at one minute, five minutes, fifteen minutes, and thirty minutes—to track the filling and emptying of the kidneys and ureters. Compression bands may be placed across the lower abdomen to slow the flow of urine into the bladder, keeping the upper renal collecting system distended with dye for better visualization. Finally, a post-micturition film is taken after the patient voids to evaluate bladder emptying.

Intra-Procedure and Post-Procedure Nursing Care

During the IVP, the nurse must monitor the patient closely for immediate reactions to the contrast media. Patients frequently report a transient warm flushing sensation, a salty taste in the mouth, or mild nausea right after the injection; these are normal side effects and usually pass quickly. Post-procedure care focuses on promoting the rapid elimination of the contrast dye from the body. Encouraging high fluid intake helps flush the kidneys and prevents nephrotoxicity. The nurse should also monitor urine output and check the injection site for signs of hematoma or infiltration.

Potential Complications and Emergency Management

Although IVPs are generally safe, adverse events can occur unexpectedly. Minor reactions include hives, itching, and mild urticaria, which often respond well to antihistamines. Severe anaphylactoid reactions involve bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, hypotension, and cardiovascular collapse, requiring immediate emergency intervention with epinephrine, oxygen, and intravenous fluids. Contrast-induced nephropathy is another serious risk, characterized by a delayed rise in serum creatinine within forty-eight hours post-procedure.

Interpretation of IVP Results

Reading an IVP film requires an understanding of normal urinary tract anatomy compared to pathological findings. A normal study shows smooth, symmetrical kidney outlines, prompt excretion of dye, and unobstructed ureters emptying into a smooth bladder. Abnormal results can reveal hydronephrosis, which is the swelling of the renal pelvis and calyces due to a blockage downstream. Other findings include filling defects caused by tumors or stones, structural displacement from renal cysts, or delayed excretion indicating impaired kidney function.
