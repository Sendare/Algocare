Needs of the Elderly

Introduction to Gerontology and Aging

Gerontology is the scientific study of the aging process, encompassing physical, mental, and social changes, while geriatrics focuses specifically on the medical care and treatment of older adults. Aging is not merely a disease state, but a progressive, natural physiological decline. In the Nigerian context, chronological age is often secondary to societal roles, where an older person is traditionally revered as an elder, custodian of wisdom, and pillar of the extended family structure. Understanding these foundational concepts helps nurses differentiate normal age-related decline from pathological conditions like dementia.

Physiological and Biological Needs

As the human body ages, multi-system functional reserves diminish. The cardiovascular system experiences arterial stiffening and decreased cardiac output, while the respiratory system loses lung elasticity and ciliary clearance, increasing vulnerability to infections like pneumonia. Integumentary changes include thinning of the epidermis and reduced subcutaneous fat, predisposing patients to pressure ulcers. Sensory organs undergo gradual deterioration, manifesting as presbyopia and presbycusis. Nursing interventions must proactively account for these altered baselines to prevent secondary complications.

Psychological and Emotional Needs

Mental well-being in later life is frequently tested by cumulative life transitions, including retirement, bereavement, and changing family dynamics. Chronic loneliness can trigger or exacerbate depression, which is often masked by somatic complaints rather than overt sadness. Cognitive changes may feature slowed processing speeds and minor memory lapses, though severe deficits indicate underlying neurocognitive disorders like Alzheimer's disease. Establishing a therapeutic, respectful nurse-patient relationship fosters dignity and emotional stability.

Socio-Economic and Cultural Needs

Older adults occupy a unique socio-economic space, particularly in developing settings where formal social security structures are weak or absent. Retirement often leads to a drastic reduction in personal income, shifting financial dependence onto adult children or extended family networks. Culturally, respect for elders is deeply entrenched in Nigerian society, yet modernization and migration are straining these traditional support systems. Nurses must assess the financial and social backing available to patients to ensure realistic discharge planning.

Nutritional and Dietary Management

  • Metabolic Shifts: Basal metabolic rate slows, requiring fewer total calories, but nutrient density must remain high.
  • Dental Status: Missing teeth, ill-fitting dentures, or periodontal disease restrict food choices to soft or mashed textures.
  • Gastrointestinal Changes: Reduced peristalsis and diminished thirst reflex heighten the risk of chronic constipation and dehydration.
  • Cultural Preferences: Incorporating locally available staples like yam, plantain, and bean pottage ensures compliance and cultural acceptability.

Safety, Mobility, and Fall Prevention

Falls are a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among older adults, often precipitating permanent loss of independence. Contributing factors include gait imbalances, visual impairments, orthostatic hypotension, and environmental hazards like loose rugs or poor lighting. Certain medications, particularly sedatives and antihypertensives, further elevate fall risk. Nursing assessments must incorporate validated screening tools, regular medication reviews, and environmental modifications to create a safe living space.

Pharmacological Considerations and Medication Management

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics alter dramatically with age. Reduced hepatic blood flow and decreased glomerular filtration rate prolong drug half-lives, increasing the risk of toxicity. Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—heightens the incidence of adverse drug reactions and dangerous drug interactions. Nurses play a vital safety role by performing medication reconciliations, educating patients on correct dosing schedules, and simplifying regimens whenever possible.

Holistic Nursing Care and Support Systems in Nigeria

Providing holistic care to older adults in Nigeria requires bridging modern clinical nursing with strong community and family structures. Nurses act as advocates, educators, and care coordinators, linking hospitalized patients with primary health centers and family caregivers. Home-visiting programs, community outreach, and caregiver education bridge the gap where institutional geriatric facilities are sparse. Respecting cultural values while promoting evidence-based interventions ensures comprehensive, compassionate health outcomes.
