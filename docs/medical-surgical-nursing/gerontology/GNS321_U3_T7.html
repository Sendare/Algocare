Roles of Various Groups in Elderly Care

Introduction to Gerontological Care Teams

Care for older adults rarely happens in isolation. Because aging often brings a complex web of chronic conditions, functional declines, and psychosocial challenges, a single practitioner cannot adequately address every need. The gerontological care team brings together diverse health professionals, informal caregivers, and community resources to provide comprehensive, person-centered support. This collaborative model shifts the medical focus away from merely treating isolated diseases toward preserving functional independence and enhancing overall quality of life. Within Nigerian healthcare settings, where multi-generational households traditionally shoulder much of the care burden, formalizing this multidisciplinary approach bridges the gap between institutional medicine and home-based realities. Every member of the team contributes specialized expertise, ensuring that physical, emotional, and social vulnerabilities are identified early and managed effectively.

Nursing Roles: Registered Nurses and Nursing Assistants

Nurses form the consistent frontline of elderly care across hospitals, long-term facilities, and home visits.

  • Registered Nurses (RNs): Conduct comprehensive health assessments, coordinate care plans, monitor for subtle shifts in condition like acute delirium, and manage complex medication regimens. They serve as the primary link between the older adult, the family, and the broader medical team.
  • Nursing Assistants / Healthcare Aides: Provide vital assistance with Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) such as bathing, feeding, dressing, and mobility. Because they spend the most direct contact hours with patients, they are often the first to notice skin breakdown, subtle behavioral changes, or declining appetite.

In resource-constrained environments, effective delegation and clear communication between RNs and support staff are vital to prevent missed care and protect vulnerable patients from preventable complications like pressure ulcers and falls.

Medical Leadership: Physicians and Geriatricians

Physicians diagnose pathologies, prescribe medical therapies, and oversee the overarching treatment trajectory. A geriatrician is a medical doctor with specialized fellowship training in the unique physiological, pharmacological, and psychological aspects of aging. Unlike general practitioners who may focus on single organ systems, geriatricians embrace multimorbidity—the presence of multiple chronic conditions simultaneously. They excel at managing polypharmacy, carefully evaluating whether a drug prescribed for one ailment is actively harming another organ system. In settings where geriatricians are scarce, general medical officers and family physicians take on this responsibility, relying heavily on nursing staff to provide accurate, longitudinal observations that guide safe medical adjustments.

Allied Health: Physiotherapy, Occupational Therapy, and Pharmacy

Rehabilitative and therapeutic specialists restore function and prevent catastrophic complications such as falls.

  • Physiotherapists: Focus on gross motor skills, balance, strength training, and safe ambulation. They design regimens to keep mobile seniors out of wheelchairs.
  • Occupational Therapists: Evaluate fine motor control and environmental safety. They recommend adaptive devices—like specialized cutlery, grab bars, or commode chairs—to help patients maintain independence in their homes.
  • Pharmacists: Review medication profiles to catch dangerous drug-drug interactions, improper dosing adjustments for age-related renal decline, and compliance barriers.

Collaboration among these specialists ensures that a patient discharged from a hospital ward can safely navigate their home environment without suffering a debilitating readmission.

Social Workers and Case Managers

Medical stability means little if an older adult returns to an abusive environment, lacks food, or cannot afford prescribed medications. Social workers and case managers navigate the complex socioeconomic landscape of aging. They assess family dynamics, screen for elder abuse or neglect, and connect households with financial assistance programs, community feeding schemes, and long-term placement options. Case managers act as the central hub, synchronizing appointments across different specialties and ensuring that discharge plans are realistic. In Nigeria, where formal social security structures for the elderly remain limited, social workers often mobilize extended family networks, community leaders, and faith-based organizations to construct a viable safety net for destitute older citizens.

Family Caregivers and Informal Support Systems

Informal caregivers—spouses, adult children, and extended relatives—form the bedrock of long-term care across the globe, bearing the heaviest share of day-to-day responsibilities. They provide emotional comfort, manage household tasks, administer medications, and coordinate medical appointments, often at great personal, financial, and emotional expense. While this communal approach reflects strong cultural values, it frequently leads to caregiver burnout, characterized by chronic fatigue, anxiety, depression, and resentment. Nurses must actively assess the well-being of family caregivers, offering education on safe lifting techniques, respite care options, and emotional validation to sustain this vital human resource.

Community Organizations and Interprofessional Communication

Community networks, religious institutions, non-governmental organizations, and senior day centers offer crucial outlets that combat isolation and provide localized support. These groups organize recreational activities, nutritional programs, and health screening camps that keep older adults engaged with society. However, the success of this entire multi-layered team depends on interprofessional communication. When doctors, nurses, therapists, social workers, and family members fail to share vital updates, critical information falls through the cracks, leading to medication errors, redundant testing, and conflicting advice. Structured communication tools—such as standardized handoff reports and shared electronic health records—ensure that every stakeholder speaks with one coordinated voice for the patient's benefit.
