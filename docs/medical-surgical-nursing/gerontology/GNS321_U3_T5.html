Nursing Care of the Elderly

Introduction to Gerontological Nursing and Aging

Gerontological nursing focuses on the care of older adults, promoting health, preventing illness, and managing chronic conditions unique to later life. Aging is a natural, multifaceted process influenced by genetics, lifestyle, and environmental factors. Unlike geriatrics, which centers primarily on the medical diagnosis and treatment of diseases in older people, gerontological nursing takes a holistic view, emphasizing quality of life, functional independence, and emotional well-being.

In Nigerian healthcare settings, caring for older adults often involves navigating extended family structures and cultural perceptions of aging. Many elders live within community settings surrounded by relatives, making family involvement central to care planning. Understanding these socio-cultural dynamics helps nursing professionals deliver culturally competent care that respects generational authority while addressing complex physical needs.

Physiological Changes in the Elderly

Aging brings progressive, predictable alterations across all organ systems. Recognizing these physiological shifts prevents clinicians from mistaking normal age-related decline for acute pathology.

  • Cardiovascular: Arterial walls thicken and stiffen, leading to reduced cardiac output and increased systolic blood pressure.
  • Respiratory: Chest wall compliance decreases, respiratory muscle strength diminishes, and vital capacity declines.
  • Integumentary: The epidermis thins, elasticity drops due to collagen loss, and sweat gland function decreases, raising vulnerability to skin tears and pressure injuries.
  • Renal: Nephron units decline, reducing the glomerular filtration rate and impairing the body's ability to clear drugs and balance electrolytes.
  • Nervous: Neuronal loss occurs alongside slowed nerve conduction velocity, affecting reaction time and deep tendon reflexes.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment

A comprehensive geriatric assessment is a multidimensional, interdisciplinary diagnostic process designed to evaluate an older adult's functional ability, physical health, cognitive and psychological status, and socio-environmental circumstances. Unlike standard medical evaluations, this approach looks beyond the primary disease to uncover hidden impairments that threaten independence.

  1. Functional Status: Assesses performance in Activities of Daily Living such as bathing, dressing, and feeding, alongside Instrumental Activities of Daily Living like managing finances and medication.
  2. Cognitive Status: Screens for memory deficits, disorientation, and executive dysfunction using validated tools like the Mini-Mental State Examination.
  3. Psychological Evaluation: Identifies depression, anxiety, and emotional distress, which frequently mimic or worsen physical complaints.
  4. Environmental Review: Inspects living conditions for safety hazards, lighting quality, and social support availability.

Common Health Problems and Geriatric Syndromes

Geriatric syndromes refer to multifactorial clinical conditions in older persons that do not fit into discrete disease categories. These syndromes—such as falls, delirium, urinary incontinence, frailty, and pressure injuries—arise when accumulated impairments across multiple organ systems push a vulnerable individual past their tipping point.

Delirium is a sudden, fluctuating disturbance in attention and cognition that often signals an underlying acute medical issue like a urinary tract infection or metabolic imbalance. Urinary incontinence, though common, is never a normal part of aging and frequently stems from reversible causes like atrophic vaginitis, urinary tract infections, or medication side effects. Recognizing these interconnected syndromes requires vigilance, as a single presenting complaint like fatigue or confusion may mask a cascading systemic failure.

Pharmacotherapy and Medication Safety

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics alter significantly with age. Decreased hepatic blood flow, reduced enzyme activity, and lowered renal clearance mean drugs remain active in the body longer, increasing toxicity risks. Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—exponentially raises the likelihood of adverse drug reactions, drug-drug interactions, and cognitive blunting.

Nurses must routinely perform medication reconciliations, comparing current regimens against prescribed orders to eliminate redundant or dangerous drugs. Screening tools like the Beers Criteria help identify medications that carry high risk in older populations due to anticholinergic properties, sedation, or orthostatic hypotension. Patient and family education must be clear, using large-print schedules and simplified dosing times to ensure compliance.

Nutritional Care and Hydration Management

Nutritional vulnerability increases in later life due to altered taste perception, poor dentition, social isolation, and chronic illness. Malnutrition impairs wound healing, muscle strength, and immune function, worsening frailty.

Dehydration is equally prevalent and dangerous. Age-related decreases in total body water content, coupled with a blunted thirst sensation and fear of incontinence, make older adults prone to fluid volume deficit. Clinical signs of dehydration—such as dry mucous membranes and poor skin turgor—can be unreliable in elders, making daily weight monitoring and intake-output tracking essential nursing interventions. Meals should be nutrient-dense, visually appealing, and served in settings that encourage socialization and adequate time for eating.

Mobility, Fall Prevention, and Safety

Falls are a leading cause of morbidity, loss of independence, and mortality among older adults. They rarely happen by chance; rather, they result from interacting intrinsic and extrinsic risk factors.

  • Intrinsic Factors: Muscle weakness, impaired balance, visual disturbances, orthostatic hypotension, and neuropathies.
  • Extrinsic Factors: Poor lighting, loose scatter rugs, cluttered walkways, improper footwear, and absence of grab bars in bathrooms.

Nursing care involves rigorous fall-risk screening upon admission and regular reassessment. Interventions must address modifiable risks: encouraging safe footwear, clearing room clutter, ensuring adequate night lighting, and teaching patients to rise slowly from sitting positions to prevent dizziness.

Psychosocial and Mental Health Care

Mental health in later life is frequently overshadowed by physical ailments, yet depression, anxiety, and loneliness are widespread. Retirement, bereavement, loss of physical independence, and changing social roles can trigger profound grief and depressive states. Unlike younger populations, older adults may present with somatic complaints—such as vague aches, fatigue, or gastrointestinal upset—rather than overt expressions of sadness.

Nurses must build trusting therapeutic relationships, actively listening to patient narratives and validating their life experiences. Promoting social engagement through community groups, family visits, and meaningful daily activities helps combat isolation and preserves cognitive vitality.

End-of-Life Care and Palliative Support

Palliative and end-of-life care for older adults focuses on comfort, dignity, and symptom management when curative treatments are no longer effective or desired. Chronic, progressive illnesses such as advanced heart failure, dementia, and terminal malignancies require careful coordination among healthcare providers, patients, and families.

Pain management is a cornerstone of end-of-life nursing. Opioids and adjuvant medications should be titrated effectively without fear of addiction, prioritizing comfort above all else. Nurses also support families by facilitating open communication about advance directives, managing spiritual concerns, and providing bereavement care. Ensuring a peaceful, dignified death honors the older adult's life journey and eases the transition for surviving loved ones.
