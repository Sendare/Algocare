Physiological Changes in the Elderly

Introduction to Gerontology and Aging

Gerontology is the scientific study of aging, spanning physical, mental, and social changes. Geriatrics, by contrast, focuses specifically on the medical care and treatment of older adults. As populations live longer, nurses must understand that aging is a normal physiological process, not a disease. Normal aging brings gradual, progressive functional decline across all body systems, which differs from pathological conditions like Alzheimer's disease or diabetes. A major clinical challenge is distinguishing between normal age-related changes and acute illness. For instance, confusion in an older adult is often falsely dismissed as 'just old age' when it may actually signal a urinary tract infection or hypoxia. Comprehensive nursing care requires establishing a reliable baseline for each elderly patient to catch subtle shifts early.

Cardiovascular and Respiratory System Alterations

The cardiovascular system undergoes distinct structural shifts with age. Blood vessels lose elasticity and become stiffer, forcing the heart to work harder to pump blood through the body, which frequently leads to systolic hypertension. Cardiac muscle cells also experience a slight decrease in maximum pumping capacity during high physical demand.

In the respiratory system, chest wall compliance decreases because the ribs and cartilage become rigid. Respiratory muscles weaken, and lung tissue loses some of its elastic recoil. This reduces overall vital capacity and diminishes the effectiveness of cough reflexes, putting older adults at a higher risk for trapped secretions and lower respiratory tract infections.

Gastrointestinal and Renal Function Declines

Gastrointestinal changes in older adults are characterized by slower motility and reduced digestive secretions. Peristalsis in the esophagus and colon slows down significantly, making constipation a very common complaint. Liver mass and blood flow also decrease, which directly affects how the body metabolizes various drugs.

Renal function experiences a steady decline starting around middle age. The number of active nephrons drops, glomerular filtration rate decreases, and the kidneys become less efficient at concentrating urine and clearing waste products. This reduced clearance rate means medications remain active in the bloodstream much longer, greatly increasing the risk of drug toxicity if dosages are not adjusted.

Musculoskeletal and Integumentary Changes

  • Bone Density: Bone resorption outpaces bone formation, leading to osteopenia and osteoporosis, which increases fracture risks.
  • Joint Cartilage: Articular cartilage wears down over time, causing joint stiffness, pain, and degenerative changes associated with osteoarthritis.
  • Muscle Mass: Sarcopenia results in a gradual loss of muscle fibers, reducing overall strength and physical endurance.
  • Skin Thinning: The epidermis thins, and the dermal layer loses collagen and elastin, making the skin fragile and prone to tearing.
  • Subcutaneous Fat: Loss of the fat layer reduces natural cushioning and impairs temperature regulation, leaving older adults vulnerable to pressure injuries and hypothermia.

Neurological and Sensory System Modifications

Brain weight and volume decrease slightly with age due to a gradual loss of neurons, although the brain compensates remarkably well through neuroplasticity. Reaction time slows down because nerve conduction velocity decreases along neural pathways.

Sensory organs undergo predictable declines as well:

  • Vision: Pupils become smaller and react slower to light changes. The lens yellows and loses flexibility, resulting in presbyopia and increased sensitivity to glare.
  • Hearing: Presbycusis leads to a gradual loss of high-frequency sounds, making speech discrimination difficult in noisy environments.
  • Taste and Smell: Taste buds decrease in number, and olfactory nerve endings degenerate, often leading to a reduced appetite and poor nutritional intake.

Endocrine and Immune System Shifts

Endocrine function shifts in older adults can mimic disease symptoms if nurses are not careful. The pancreas may show reduced sensitivity to insulin, predisposing older individuals to type 2 diabetes even with normal lifestyle habits. Thyroid gland activity often decreases slightly, slowing basal metabolic rate and causing cold intolerance.

Immunosenescence refers to the gradual deterioration of the immune system with age. Both T-lymphocyte function and antibody responses decline. Consequently, older adults face a higher risk of infections, autoimmune disorders, and malignancies. A critical clinical takeaway is that elderly patients often do not mount a high fever or show typical white blood cell counts during severe infections, masking the true severity of their illness.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Implications

Providing effective nursing care for older adults requires adapting standard assessment techniques to account for physiological changes. When taking a health history, nurses should allow extra time for patients to process questions and respond, minimizing environmental distractions like loud hallway noises. Physical assessments must prioritize functional ability—evaluating how well a patient manages activities of daily living (ADLs) rather than just focusing on disease diagnosis.

Medication safety is paramount. Because of altered pharmacokinetics and polypharmacy, nurses must perform regular medication reconciliations, always keeping the rule 'start low and go slow' in mind when new drugs are prescribed.
