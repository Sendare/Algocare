Physiological Changes in the Elderly

Foundations of Aging: Gerontology and Normal Physiology

Aging is a universal, progressive physiological process distinct from pathology, though chronic illness becomes more prevalent later in life. Gerontology examines the biological, psychological, and social facets of human aging. At the cellular level, primary senescence involves telomere shortening, cumulative oxidative damage from free radicals, and a gradual decline in cellular replication capacity. These microscopic alterations culminate in reduced functional reserve across all major organ systems. When a healthy older adult faces acute stressors like dehydration or infection, their organ systems take significantly longer to return to baseline compared to younger adults. This diminished physiological buffer is the core concept underlying geriatric vulnerability.

Cardiovascular and Respiratory Alterations

The cardiovascular system undergoes structural stiffening: myocardial walls thicken, particularly the left ventricle, and arterial compliance decreases due to collagen cross-linking and elastin fragmentation. This results in higher systolic blood pressure and increased afterload. Maximum heart rate drops, and the heart relies more heavily on atrial kick to fill the ventricles during diastole. In the respiratory system, chest wall compliance diminishes because costal cartilages calcify, while respiratory muscle strength wanes. Alveolar walls lose surface area and capillary networks thin out, leading to an age-related rise in residual volume and a drop in vital capacity. Gas exchange efficiency drops, predisposing the older person to rapid hypoxia during mild respiratory infections.

Gastrointestinal and Renal Declines

Gastrointestinal motility slows down markedly due to decreased myenteric plexus neuron density and altered smooth muscle responsiveness, predisposing individuals to chronic constipation. Hepatic blood flow and microsomal enzyme activity decrease, which alters the first-pass metabolism of many medications. In the renal system, nephron units undergo progressive sclerosis. Glomerular filtration rate decreases steadily after middle age, even in the absence of renal disease. Because muscle mass naturally diminishes with age, serum creatinine levels can remain within normal laboratory limits despite a severely compromised glomerular filtration rate, a common clinical pitfall when calculating safe drug dosages.

Musculoskeletal and Integumentary Shifts

- **Bone Density**: Osteoclast activity outpaces osteoblast function, resulting in demineralization and osteoporosis. - **Sarcopenia**: Muscle mass and contractile strength steadily diminish, shifting body composition toward higher relative fat storage. - **Joint Cartilage**: Articular cartilage thins and loses its smooth lubricating properties, frequently manifesting as osteoarthritis. - **Skin Atrophy**: Epidermal cell turnover slows, the dermis thins, and vascular supply drops. - **Glandular Output**: Sweat and sebaceous glands decrease secretions, leading to chronic dryness and impaired thermoregulation.

Neurological and Sensory Modifications

Brain mass decreases gradually, accompanied by ventricular enlargement and loss of cortical neurons, though cognitive reserve and crystallized intelligence often remain stable. Conduction velocity along peripheral nerves slows, increasing reaction times. Sensory modalities experience notable degradation. Presbyopia develops as the lens loses elasticity, while presbycusis involves sensorineural hearing loss, particularly for high-frequency sounds. Taste bud counts drop and olfactory nerve endings atrophy, which blunts flavor perception and can negatively impact nutritional intake. Proprioception and tactile sensitivity also decline, increasing the general risk of accidental falls.

Endocrine and Immune System Adaptations

Endocrine regulation shifts in several subtle ways. Basal metabolic rate drops due to declining thyroid hormone activation and reduced lean muscle mass. While insulin secretion by pancreatic beta cells may remain adequate, peripheral tissue sensitivity to insulin often declines, raising the threshold for glucose tolerance. In the immune system, immunosenescence alters both innate and adaptive responses. Thymic involution leads to a sharp drop in naive T-cell production, while B-cell function becomes less robust. Consequently, older adults show reduced antibody responses to new vaccinations and exhibit muted clinical signs of infection, such as an absent or low-grade fever during severe sepsis.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Implications

Managing the health of older adults requires synthesizing how multiple age-related changes interact. Pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics are altered; lipophilic drugs have a larger volume of distribution due to increased body fat, while hydrophilic drugs concentrate in smaller body fluid volumes. Polypharmacy increases adverse drug reactions. Assessment strategies must account for atypical disease presentations—such as confusion signaling a urinary tract infection instead of localized dysuria. Nursing interventions should prioritize environmental safety to prevent falls, nutritional support tailored to diminished appetite, and careful monitoring of hydration status given the age-related decline in the thirst mechanism.
