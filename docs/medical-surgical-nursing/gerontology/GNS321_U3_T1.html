Introduction to Gerontology

Defining Gerontology and Geriatric Nursing

Gerontology is the scientific study of the aging process from all angles, including physical, mental, and social changes. It looks at how humans age over time and how society deals with an older population. Geriatric nursing, on the other hand, is the hands-on clinical care of older adults. While a gerontologist might study the economic impact of retirement, a geriatric nurse focuses on managing chronic illnesses, preventing falls, and supporting daily living activities for an older patient. Both fields overlap greatly, but gerontology is the broad study while geriatric nursing is the specific clinical practice.

Demographic Trends of Aging

Globally, the population is aging rapidly due to lower birth rates and longer life expectancies. In Nigeria and across sub-Saharan Africa, the absolute number of older adults is growing, even though the continent remains relatively youthful compared to Europe or North America. This demographic shift brings new public health challenges. More citizens are living into their seventies and eighties, which means healthcare systems must prepare to handle chronic conditions like hypertension, arthritis, and diabetes on a much larger scale.

Theories of Aging

Scientists use several theories to explain why and how we age.

  • Biological theories: Focus on cellular damage, genetic programming, and wear-and-tear over time. Examples include the free radical theory and cellular senescence.
  • Psychological theories: Look at how people adapt to aging mentally, dealing with life transitions, ego integrity, and cognitive shifts.
  • Sociological theories: Examine how older adults fit into society. Disactivity theory suggests people naturally withdraw from social roles, while activity theory argues that staying busy and engaged leads to better life satisfaction in old age.

Normal Physiological Changes Across Body Systems

Aging brings gradual, expected changes to every organ system. These changes are normal and distinct from disease, though they lower the body's reserve capacity.

  • Cardiovascular: Blood vessels stiffen, and maximum heart rate decreases during exertion.
  • Respiratory: Chest wall compliance drops, and respiratory muscle strength declines slightly.
  • Integumentary: Skin loses elasticity, thins, and sweat production decreases, raising vulnerability to injury.
  • Nervous: Brain mass shrinks slightly, and reaction time slows down.
  • Musculoskeletal: Bone density decreases and muscle mass reduces, known as sarcopenia.

Psychosocial Aspects and Mental Health

Mental well-being in older age depends heavily on how individuals navigate life changes. Retirement, loss of spouse or friends, and changing family roles can trigger loneliness and depression. A common clinical mistake is assuming depression is a normal part of aging; it is not. Nurses must differentiate between normal grief, dementia, and clinical depression. Preserving cognitive function and social connection helps older adults maintain a strong sense of purpose and dignity.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Care

Caring for older adults often involves navigating complex ethical and legal dilemmas. Issues surrounding informed consent, autonomy, and end-of-life decisions require great sensitivity. Healthcare providers must respect an older adult's right to make their own choices, even when family members disagree, provided the patient has mental capacity. Nurses also play a vital role in protecting vulnerable seniors from abuse, neglect, or financial exploitation by family members or caregivers.
