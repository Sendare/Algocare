Introduction to Gerontology

Defining Gerontology and Geriatric Nursing

Gerontology is the multidisciplinary scientific study of aging, encompassing physical, mental, and social changes in older adults as they progress through the later stages of life. It draws from biology, psychology, sociology, and public health to understand the overall aging experience. In contrast, geriatric nursing focuses specifically on the hands-on clinical care, health promotion, disease prevention, and rehabilitation of older individuals. While a gerontologist might research societal demographic shifts or cellular senescence, a geriatric nurse assesses pressure ulcer risks, manages polypharmacy, and tailors daily bedside care plans for an eighty-year-old patient on a medical-surgical ward. Recognizing this distinction helps healthcare providers appreciate both population-level trends and individual clinical interventions.

Demographic Shifts in Nigeria and Worldwide

Global populations are aging rapidly due to declining fertility rates and rising life expectancies driven by public health advancements. Although developed nations historically held the highest proportions of older citizens, developing regions now experience the fastest absolute growth in aging populations. In Nigeria, the absolute number of older adults is increasing steadily, creating unique socio-economic and healthcare challenges. Traditional extended family structures that historically provided robust care for elders are shifting due to urbanization and economic pressures. Consequently, Nigerian health systems must adapt by training nurses equipped to handle chronic non-communicable diseases and establishing community-based care models tailored to an expanding elderly demographic.

Theories of Aging

Theories of aging explain why and how the human body changes over time. They are broadly categorized into biological, psychological, and sociological frameworks:

  • Biological theories: Examine cellular and molecular deterioration, including the wear-and-tear theory, free radical damage, and programmed genetic clocks.
  • Psychological theories: Focus on behavioral and cognitive adjustments, such as Erikson's developmental stage of ego integrity versus despair.
  • Sociological theories: Address how older adults adapt to changing societal roles through frameworks like disengagement theory and activity theory.

Normal Physiological Changes Across Body Systems

Aging involves progressive, non-pathological physiological decline across every organ system. Nurses must distinguish these normal age-related variations from active disease processes:

Body SystemNormal Age-Related Change
CardiovascularDecreased maximum heart rate, stiffening of arterial walls
RespiratoryReduced elastic recoil, decreased vital capacity
RenalDecreased glomerular filtration rate, reduced nephron count
IntegumentaryThinning epidermis, loss of subcutaneous fat, decreased elasticity
NervousSlower nerve conduction velocity, mild age-related memory slowing

Psychosocial Aspects and Mental Health

Maintaining mental well-being in later life depends on successfully navigating major life transitions, including retirement, bereavement, and shifting family dynamics. While cognitive processing speed may slow slightly, profound memory loss, confusion, or personality changes are never normal parts of aging. Nurses frequently screen for common geriatric mental health concerns such as depression, dementia, and delirium. Depression is often misdiagnosed as normal sadness or masked by physical complaints. Dementia involves a gradual, irreversible cognitive decline, whereas delirium is an acute, fluctuating medical emergency triggered by underlying physiological stressors like infections or dehydration.

Ethical and Legal Considerations in Care

Providing care for older adults requires balancing patient autonomy with beneficence and safety. Nurses must respect the right of competent older adults to make independent healthcare decisions, even when those choices conflict with medical advice. Concepts such as informed consent, advance directives, and power of attorney protect patient preferences regarding end-of-life interventions. Additionally, nurses must remain vigilant in detecting and reporting elder abuse, which can manifest as physical, financial, emotional, or neglectful maltreatment within domestic or institutional settings. Advocacy for the vulnerable older adult remains a foundational pillar of professional nursing practice.
