Preventing Infection in the Hospital

Introduction to Healthcare-Associated Infections

Healthcare-associated infections, traditionally called nosocomial infections, are infections patients pick up while receiving medical care in a hospital or clinic. A patient enters the facility seeking treatment for one problem, only to develop a brand new microbial illness at least 48 hours after admission. These infections place a heavy burden on the healthcare system, lengthening hospital stays, increasing medical costs, and sometimes causing permanent disability or death. Common sites include the urinary tract, surgical wounds, the lungs, and the bloodstream. Vulnerable patients, such as the elderly, newborns, and anyone with a suppressed immune system, face the highest risk. Understanding how these infections originate is the first step every nurse must take to protect the people entrusted to their care.

The Chain of Infection

To stop an infection from spreading, you must understand the chain of infection. This chain has six distinct links:

  1. Infectious Agent: The pathogen, such as a bacterium, virus, fungus, or parasite.
  2. Reservoir: Where the germ lives and grows, including human bodies, water, food, or contaminated surfaces.
  3. Portal of Exit: How the germ leaves the reservoir, such as through respiratory droplets, blood, or open sores.
  4. Mode of Transmission: How the germ travels from one place to another, whether through direct touch, the air, or shared objects.
  5. Portal of Entry: How the germ gets into a new host, like broken skin, the mouth, or the nose.
  6. Susceptible Host: A person whose body cannot fight off the invading germ.

Breaking even one link in this chain stops the infection in its tracks.

Standard Precautions

Standard precautions form the foundational level of infection control used for every single patient, regardless of their diagnosis or suspected infection status. These practices assume that every patient's blood, body fluids, secretions, and excretions can carry transmissible infectious agents. Core elements include consistent hand hygiene, wearing personal protective equipment when exposure is anticipated, maintaining safe injection practices, and ensuring proper environmental cleaning and instrument sterilization. Nurses apply standard precautions during every patient interaction, whether starting an IV line, emptying a urinary catheter bag, or assisting with personal hygiene.

Transmission-Based Precautions

When standard precautions are not enough, healthcare teams add transmission-based precautions based on how a specific pathogen spreads. There are three categories:

  • Contact Precautions: Used for infections spread by direct or indirect touch, such as C. diff or MRSA. Nurses wear gowns and gloves upon entering the room.
  • Droplet Precautions: Used for large respiratory droplets traveling short distances, like influenza or pertussis. Nurses wear a surgical mask when working close to the patient.
  • Airborne Precautions: Used for tiny droplet nuclei that float in the air over long distances, such as tuberculosis or measles. Patients require negative-pressure rooms, and nurses must wear fitted N95 respirators.

Proper Hand Hygiene Techniques

Hand hygiene is the single most effective way to prevent healthcare-associated infections. Nurses must clean their hands frequently using either an alcohol-based hand rub or soap and water. Alcohol-based hand rubs are preferred for routine care because they kill most germs quickly and are gentler on the skin, provided hands are not visibly soiled. When hands are visibly dirty, contaminated with blood or body fluids, or after caring for patients with spore-forming bacteria like Clostridioides difficile, washing with soap and water for at least 20 seconds is mandatory. Alcohol does not reliably kill bacterial spores.

Use of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical barrier between a healthcare worker and infectious materials. Correct usage involves knowing not only what to put on, but the precise order of putting it on and taking it off to avoid contaminating your skin or scrubs.

  • Donning (Putting on): Put on the gown first, then the mask or respirator, followed by eye protection, and gloves last so they cover the gown cuffs.
  • Doffing (Taking off): Remove gloves first because they are the most contaminated, followed by eye protection, the gown, and the mask last. Wash your hands immediately after removal.

Aseptic Technique and Catheter Care

Aseptic technique involves practices designed to keep patients free from pathogenic microorganisms during invasive procedures. Operating rooms, labor and delivery suites, and bedside procedures like inserting a urinary catheter require strict sterile technique. Catheter-associated urinary tract infections are among the most common hospital-acquired infections. Nurses prevent these infections by inserting catheters only when strictly necessary, using strict sterile technique during placement, maintaining a closed drainage system, keeping the collection bag below the level of the bladder, and performing daily perineal hygiene.

Environmental Sanitation and Waste Management

A clean hospital environment is vital for stopping the spread of germs. Pathogens can survive on bed rails, bedside tables, and medical equipment for days or even weeks. Environmental services teams clean room surfaces regularly using hospital-approved disinfectants, paying special attention to high-touch areas like call buttons and door handles. In addition, proper waste management keeps staff and patients safe from injury and contamination. General trash goes into standard bins, infectious biological waste goes into red biohazard bags, and sharp objects like needles must be dropped immediately into rigid, puncture-proof sharps containers without recapping.

The Role of the Nurse in Surveillance

Nurses serve as the front line of defense in infection surveillance and reporting. Because they spend the most time at the bedside, nurses are often the first to notice subtle signs of an emerging infection, such as an unexplained fever, worsening wound redness, cloudy urine, or a change in respiratory status. Promptly reporting these observations allows the healthcare team to collect diagnostic cultures, start appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and implement infection control measures before an outbreak occurs. Diligent documentation and open communication with infection control specialists safeguard both individual patients and the entire hospital community.
