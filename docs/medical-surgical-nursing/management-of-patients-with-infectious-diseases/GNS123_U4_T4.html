Preventing Infection in the Hospital

Introduction and the Chain of Infection

Nosocomial infections, officially termed **healthcare-associated infections (HAIs)**, develop in patients while receiving care in a hospital or other health facility. These infections manifest at least 48 hours after hospital admission or up to 30 days post-discharge. Preventing them requires understanding how pathogens propagate within clinical spaces. The **chain of infection** comprises six distinct links that must remain unbroken for transmission to occur: 1. **Infectious agent**: Pathogens such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites. 2. **Reservoir**: The natural habitat where the pathogen grows and multiplies, including human patients, staff, environmental surfaces, or stagnant water. 3. **Portal of exit**: The route through which the pathogen leaves the reservoir, such as respiratory droplets, blood, or wound drainage. 4. **Mode of transmission**: How the pathogen travels from the reservoir to a susceptible host, occurring via direct contact, indirect contact through contaminated equipment, droplets, or airborne particles. 5. **Portal of entry**: The path the pathogen uses to enter the new host, including broken skin, mucous membranes, or urinary tracts. 6. **Susceptible host**: An individual whose vulnerability is heightened by age, underlying illness, invasive devices, or immunosuppression. Interrupting this cycle at any single link halts the spread of disease.

Standard and Transmission-Based Precautions

Infection control protocols rely on a two-tiered system. **Standard precautions** serve as the foundational level of infection control, applied universally to all patients regardless of their diagnosis or presumed infection status. These measures assume that every body fluid, non-intact skin, and mucous membrane may harbor transmissible infectious agents. Core practices include consistent hand hygiene, appropriate personal protective equipment use, safe injection practices, respiratory hygiene, and meticulous environmental cleaning. When standard precautions alone are insufficient to interrupt pathogen transmission, **transmission-based precautions** are added. They target specific routes of spread and are tailored to patients known or suspected to be infected with epidemiological pathogens: - **Contact precautions**: Implemented for organisms spread by direct or indirect touch, requiring gloves and gowns upon entering the room. - **Droplet precautions**: Used for pathogens transmitted via large respiratory particles traveling short distances, necessitating surgical masks. - **Airborne precautions**: Deployed for tiny droplet nuclei suspended in the air, requiring negative pressure isolation rooms and specialized particulate respirators like N95 masks.

Hand Hygiene Protocols

Hand hygiene remains the single most effective intervention for preventing the transmission of healthcare-associated pathogens. Microorganisms on hospital ward surfaces transfer easily to patient skin and medical apparatuses. Clinicians must perform hand hygiene using either an alcohol-based hand rub or soap and water, depending on the clinical context. Alcohol-based hand rubs are preferred for routine clinical care because they destroy most transient flora rapidly, require less time, and cause less skin irritation. However, when hands are visibly soiled, contaminated with proteinaceous material, or after caring for patients with spore-forming pathogens like *Clostridioides difficile*, washing with soap and running water is mandatory because alcohol fails to kill bacterial spores effectively. Compliance with the **World Health Organization's Five Moments for Hand Hygiene** dictates exact timing: 1. Before touching a patient. 2. Before clean or aseptic procedures. 3. After body fluid exposure risk. 4. After touching a patient. 5. After touching patient surroundings.

Personal Protective Equipment (PPE) Selection

Personal protective equipment acts as a physical barrier between the healthcare worker and potential infectious agents. Correct selection depends on the anticipated mode of transmission during a clinical task. Putting on PPE—termed **donning**—follows a specific sequence to ensure maximum protection: gown first, followed by a mask or respirator, protective eyewear or face shield, and gloves last. Gloves must be pulled over the cuffs of the gown to cover exposed skin fully. Removing PPE—known as **doffing**—carries a high risk of self-contamination and must be performed in an intentional sequence: gloves first, since they are the most contaminated, followed by eyewear or face shield, gown, and finally the mask or respirator. Hand hygiene must be performed immediately after removing any component of PPE.

Aseptic Technique and Invasive Devices

Invasive devices such as peripheral and central vascular catheters, urinary catheters, and endotracheal tubes bypass normal anatomical barriers, creating direct pathways for pathogens into sterile body compartments. **Aseptic technique** encompasses practices designed to maintain objects and areas free from microorganisms, minimizing infection risks during procedures. Surgical asepsis, or sterile technique, demands complete elimination of all microbes from an area or object. When inserting an indwelling urinary catheter, maintaining this technique prevents catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs). Core measures include: - Performing thorough perineal hygiene before insertion. - Utilizing sterile drapes and a sterile insertion kit. - Securing the catheter to prevent urethral traction. - Maintaining a closed gravity drainage system below the level of the bladder. - Removing the device as soon as it is clinically unnecessary.

Environmental Sanitation and Waste Management

Hospital environments harbor resilient pathogens capable of surviving on dry surfaces for days or months. Routine cleaning and disinfection of bedrails, overbed tables, call buttons, and bathroom fixtures disrupt this reservoir. Environmental services rely on hospital-grade disinfectants, paying close attention to high-touch surfaces. Safe **waste management** is equally critical to prevent community spread and occupational injuries. Healthcare waste is categorized to ensure proper disposal: - **General waste**: Non-hazardous domestic refuse, such as paper packaging and food wrappers, disposed of in black bins. - **Infectious waste**: Items contaminated with blood or body fluids, disposed of in leak-proof yellow bags with biohazard symbols. - **Sharps waste**: Needles, scalpel blades, and glass ampoules, deposited immediately into puncture-resistant safety boxes without recapping.

Surveillance and Reporting Responsibilities

Nurses occupy a frontline position in infection control through ongoing clinical surveillance. Detecting subtle shifts in patient temperature, wound appearance, respiratory status, or laboratory parameters allows early identification of emerging infections. Prompt recognition prevents localized issues from escalating into ward-wide outbreaks. When a nosocomial infection is identified, immediate reporting to the hospital's infection control team or committee is necessary. Accurate documentation in the patient record, tracking antibiotic usage, and adhering to institutional outbreak protocols safeguard the wider patient population. Education provided by the nurse to patients and visitors regarding respiratory hygiene and handwashing reinforces these defensive measures.
