COVID-19

Introduction and Pathophysiology of COVID-19

COVID-19 is an infectious disease caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus, which primarily targets the respiratory system. The virus spreads mainly through respiratory droplets when an infected person breathes, talks, coughs, or sneezes. Once inside the human body, the spike protein on the surface of SARS-CoV-2 binds specifically to the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor, which is abundantly found on the cells lining the human respiratory tract.

After binding, the virus enters the host cell and hijacks the cellular machinery to replicate itself. This process damages lung tissue and triggers a powerful local immune response. In some patients, the immune system overreacts, leading to a massive release of inflammatory chemicals known as a cytokine storm. This severe inflammation causes widespread damage to blood vessels, reduces the body's ability to exchange oxygen, and can quickly lead to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and multi-organ failure.

Modes of Transmission and Infection Control Precautions

SARS-CoV-2 is highly contagious and spreads primarily from person to person through close contact. The main route of transmission involves infectious respiratory droplets expelled during breathing, talking, coughing, or sneezing. These droplets can land in the mouths or noses of people nearby or be inhaled directly into the lungs. Airborne transmission can also occur in enclosed, poorly ventilated indoor spaces where tiny infectious particles linger in the air for longer periods. Less commonly, transmission happens when a person touches a contaminated surface and then touches their own eyes, nose, or mouth.

To protect healthcare workers and prevent nosocomial spread, strict infection control measures are essential:

  • Standard Precautions: Consistent hand hygiene, proper waste disposal, and routine environmental cleaning for all patient care.
  • Contact and Droplet Precautions: Wearing gloves, gowns, and surgical masks when entering the room of a suspected or confirmed patient.
  • Airborne Precautions: Utilizing fit-tested N95 or higher respirators, eye protection, and negative pressure isolation rooms (AIIRs) during aerosol-generating procedures like intubation or suctioning.

Clinical Manifestations and Severity Classification

The clinical presentation of COVID-19 varies widely, ranging from completely asymptomatic infection to critical illness and death. Common symptoms include fever, dry cough, fatigue, shortness of breath, muscle aches, sore throat, and the sudden loss of taste or smell.

Healthcare providers classify the severity of the disease to guide treatment decisions:

  • Mild Illness: Patients have various signs and symptoms without shortness of breath or abnormal chest imaging.
  • Moderate Illness: Evidence of lower respiratory disease during clinical assessment or imaging, with an oxygen saturation of 94% or higher on room air.
  • Severe Illness: Oxygen saturation drops below 94% on room air, respiratory frequency exceeds 30 breaths per minute, or lung infiltrates cover more than half of the lung fields.
  • Critical Illness: Patients experience respiratory failure, septic shock, or multiple organ dysfunction that requires mechanical ventilation and intensive care.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Accurate diagnosis of COVID-19 relies on a combination of viral testing, imaging, and routine blood work. The Reverse Transcription-Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-PCR) test remains the gold standard for detecting active viral RNA from a nasopharyngeal swab. Rapid antigen tests provide quicker results by detecting specific viral proteins, though they are slightly less sensitive than PCR tests. Chest X-rays and computed tomography (CT) scans often reveal bilateral, peripheral ground-glass opacities in the lungs of affected individuals.

Laboratory blood panels provide crucial clues about the patient's inflammatory and immune response. Common findings include lymphopenia, which is a low white blood cell count involving lymphocytes. Inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), ferritin, and D-dimer are frequently elevated. High levels of D-dimer indicate an increased risk of abnormal blood clotting, which is a common and dangerous complication of the disease.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring of COVID-19 Patients

Continuous nursing assessment is vital for detecting sudden deterioration in hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Nurses must closely monitor vital signs, placing special emphasis on respiratory rate, work of breathing, and continuous pulse oximetry. A dangerous phenomenon known as happy hypoxemia can occur, where patients have dangerously low blood oxygen levels yet display no obvious signs of shortness of breath or distress. Because of this, nurses must never rely solely on patient comfort to gauge oxygenation status.

Regular checks of neurological status, urine output, and skin integrity are equally important. Nurses should monitor trends in laboratory results, watching for rising inflammatory markers or falling lymphocyte counts. Frequent repositioning and encouraging movement help prevent pressure injuries and venous stasis in immobile patients.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological treatment for COVID-19 focuses on antiviral therapy to stop viral replication and anti-inflammatory medications to calm the overactive immune response. Antivirals like ritonavir-boosted nirmatrelvir (Paxlovid) or remdesivir are most effective when given early in the course of the disease to high-risk patients. These drugs help reduce the viral load and shorten recovery times.

For hospitalized patients requiring supplemental oxygen, anti-inflammatory agents are the cornerstone of therapy. Corticosteroids, such as dexamethasone, suppress the damaging inflammatory response and significantly lower mortality rates in severe and critical cases. Immunomodulators like baricitinib or tocilizumab may also be administered to block specific inflammatory pathways. Anticoagulants, such as low-molecular-weight heparin, are routinely prescribed to prevent dangerous blood clots from forming due to immobility and systemic inflammation.

Respiratory Support and Oxygen Therapy

Managing respiratory failure is central to caring for patients with severe COVID-19. Initial oxygen therapy is often delivered through nasal cannulas or simple face masks. If oxygen requirements increase, High-Flow Nasal Cannula (HFNC) therapy provides heated and humidified oxygen at high flow rates, which helps reduce the work of breathing and wash out exhaled carbon dioxide.

When non-invasive methods fail, advanced respiratory support is necessary:

  • Prone Positioning: Placing conscious or intubated patients lying face down on their stomachs to improve lung expansion and oxygen exchange.
  • Non-Invasive Ventilation: Using BiPAP or CPAP machines with tight-fitting masks to push air into the lungs, though monitored closely due to aerosol risks.
  • Mechanical Ventilation: Invasive intubation and mechanical ventilation for patients suffering from profound respiratory failure.
  • Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO): A life support machine that pumps and oxygenates a patient's blood outside the body for the most critical cases.

Potential Complications and Long COVID

While many individuals recover from COVID-19 within a few weeks, others experience severe acute complications or prolonged health issues. Acute complications include acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), secondary bacterial pneumonia, cardiac injuries like myocarditis, acute kidney injury, and thromboembolic events such as pulmonary embolism or stroke.

Many survivors also suffer from Long COVID—a condition characterized by persistent symptoms lasting months after the initial infection. Common long-term complaints include chronic fatigue, shortness of breath, brain fog, joint pain, and sleep disturbances. Nurses play a crucial role in validating these ongoing symptoms, providing rehabilitation support, and connecting recovering patients with multidisciplinary follow-up care teams.

Patient Education and Public Health Prevention

Public health measures and patient education remain the most effective tools for controlling the spread of COVID-19. Nurses educate individuals and communities on the importance of vaccination, which significantly reduces the risk of severe illness, hospitalization, and death. Vaccination stimulates the immune system to produce neutralizing antibodies against the virus without causing active disease.

Additional preventive education includes promoting proper hand hygiene, wearing well-fitting face masks in crowded indoor settings, ensuring good indoor ventilation, and staying home when sick. Nurses also advise high-risk individuals on when to seek prompt medical attention and how to safely isolate at home to protect family members and the wider community from exposure.
