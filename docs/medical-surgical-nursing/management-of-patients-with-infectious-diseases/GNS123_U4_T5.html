Emerging and Re-emerging Infectious Diseases

Introduction to Emerging and Re-emerging Infectious Diseases

Infectious diseases continue to shape public health landscapes across the globe, presenting constant challenges to healthcare systems. Emerging infectious diseases are entirely new infections that have recently appeared within a human population, or established infections that are rapidly spreading into new geographic areas or expanding their host range. Conversely, re-emerging infectious diseases are well-known conditions that previously showed a significant decline in incidence but have now returned as major public health threats. Understanding the distinction between these two categories helps nurses anticipate outbreaks, recognize unusual clinical presentations, and participate effectively in disease containment efforts. While medical science advances daily, microbes constantly mutate, adapt, and exploit vulnerabilities in human environments, travel networks, and ecological systems.

Epidemiological Factors and Drivers of Emergence

Outbreaks do not happen by accident; several distinct environmental, social, and biological pressures drive the emergence and return of infectious pathogens.

  • Human Demographics and Behavior: Rapid urbanization, overcrowding, and changing lifestyle patterns bring large groups of people into close contact, accelerating person-to-person transmission.
  • Environmental Changes: Deforestation, agricultural expansion, and climate shifts push wildlife out of natural habitats, increasing the chances of zoonotic spillover into human populations.
  • Global Travel and Trade: International flights allow a pathogen to travel across continents within hours, turning a localized rural outbreak into a global health emergency.
  • Microbial Adaptation: Pathogens undergo rapid genetic mutations, sometimes developing resistance to antimicrobial drugs or escaping human immune defenses.
  • Breakdown in Public Health Systems: Weakened infrastructure, lack of clean water, and poor vaccination coverage create fertile ground for infectious agents to spread unchecked.

Major Public Health Threats in Nigeria

Nigeria faces a heavy burden of both endemic and epidemic-prone infectious diseases due to its tropical climate, ecological diversity, and population density. Viral hemorrhagic fevers such as Lassa fever, transmitted primarily by multimammate rats, cause recurring seasonal outbreaks in several states. Cholera outbreaks frequently emerge during rainy seasons, fueled by contaminated water sources and inadequate sanitation infrastructure. Mpox (formerly monkeypox) and cerebrospinal meningitis also demand constant vigilance, especially across the meningitis belt during the dry harmattan season. Additionally, vector-borne illnesses like malaria and yellow fever remain persistent threats that strain local primary healthcare facilities and demand robust community surveillance.

Pathophysiology and Modes of Transmission

To stop an infection, nurses must understand how pathogens enter the human body, replicate, and cause tissue damage. Transmission routes vary widely depending on the organism. Airborne transmission occurs when tiny droplet nuclei remain suspended in the air over long distances, as seen with tuberculosis. Droplet transmission involves larger respiratory secretions expelled during coughing or sneezing, which typically travel short distances before landing on mucous membranes. Vector-borne transmission relies on living intermediaries like mosquitoes or ticks to inject pathogens directly into the bloodstream. Once inside a host, infectious agents attach to specific cell receptors, multiply, trigger intense local or systemic inflammatory responses, and ultimately lead to cellular destruction and organ dysfunction.

Clinical Manifestations and Patient Assessment

Clinical presentations of emerging infections often mimic common tropical illnesses initially, making early detection a true diagnostic puzzle. A thorough patient assessment must capture a detailed travel history, occupational exposures, and contact with sick individuals or animals. Common systemic signs include high-grade fever, severe fatigue, generalized body aches, and chills. Depending on the specific pathogen, nurses should watch for organ-specific red flags such as petechial rashes in viral hemorrhagic fevers, neck stiffness in meningitis, or acute watery diarrhea leading to rapid dehydration in cholera. Identifying these clinical patterns allows nurses to isolate suspected cases immediately before laboratory results are even finalized.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Laboratory testing provides the definitive confirmation needed to manage outbreaks and guide treatment protocols. Common diagnostic tools include polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays to detect viral or bacterial genetic material, and serological tests to identify specific antibodies or antigens in the blood. In addition to pathogen-specific tests, routine baseline investigations like complete blood counts, electrolyte panels, and liver or kidney function tests reveal the physiological toll the infection is taking on the body. Nurses play a critical role in collecting specimens safely, labeling them accurately, and transporting them under strict biosafety conditions to prevent accidental exposure or sample contamination.

Nursing Management and Infection Prevention and Control

Infection prevention and control (IPC) forms the bedrock of safe hospital operations during an infectious disease outbreak. Nurses must rigorously apply standard precautions for all patients, alongside transmission-based precautions—contact, droplet, or airborne—tailored to the specific pathogen. Proper hand hygiene before and after patient contact remains the single most effective intervention to break the chain of transmission. Correct use of personal protective equipment (PPE), including donning and doffing sequences without self-contamination, protects healthcare workers on the frontline. Furthermore, managing environmental hygiene, ensuring safe medical waste disposal, and establishing isolation wards are vital duties led by nursing personnel.

Pharmacological Treatment and Supportive Care

Management of emerging infections often relies heavily on meticulous supportive care because specific antiviral or antimicrobial drugs may not exist for newly discovered pathogens. When available, targeted medications like antivirals (e.g., ribavirin for Lassa fever) or specific antibiotics must be administered promptly according to strict clinical guidelines. Supportive nursing care focuses on stabilizing vital signs, managing high fevers with antipyretics, and maintaining fluid and electrolyte balance through oral rehydration or intravenous therapy. Nurses must monitor for signs of clinical deterioration, such as decreasing urine output, altered mental status, or dropping blood pressure, which may indicate the onset of septic shock or multi-organ failure.

Public Health Surveillance, Policy, and Global Health Security

No single hospital can fight an outbreak in isolation; effective disease containment requires coordinated public health surveillance and policy enforcement at local, national, and global levels. Surveillance systems rely on frontline healthcare workers reporting unusual disease clusters, unexplained deaths, or suspected outbreak cases immediately to public health authorities. Agencies like the Nigeria Centre for Disease Control (NCDC) and the World Health Organization (WHO) use this data to track trends, deploy rapid response teams, and issue travel or health advisories. Nurses serve as the eyes and ears of public health systems, as their prompt notification of unusual clinical cases often triggers the very first alert that prevents a widespread epidemic.
