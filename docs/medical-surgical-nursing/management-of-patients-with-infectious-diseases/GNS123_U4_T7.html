Review of the Nursing Process

Introduction to the Nursing Process

The **nursing process** serves as the foundational framework for clinical practice, guiding the delivery of individualized, patient-centered care. Far from being a rigid bureaucratic checklist, it represents a dynamic, systematic problem-solving approach. Clinical environments in Nigerian healthcare facilities demand this structured adaptability, where resources may be scarce and rapid prioritization saves lives. - **Assessment**: Systematic collection of baseline health data. - **Diagnosis**: Clinical judgment regarding human responses to health conditions. - **Planning**: Setting measurable goals and selecting interventions. - **Implementation**: Executing the devised care plan. - **Evaluation**: Appraising patient response and modifying strategies. This cyclical methodology ensures continuity of care, prevents omissions, and fosters critical thinking among practitioners. Each phase builds upon the previous one, creating a continuous loop of clinical refinement.

Assessment: Data Collection and Validation

Assessment is the deliberate and systematic collection of data to determine a patient's current and past health status, functional status, and coping patterns. Data is broadly categorized into two distinct types: - **Subjective data**: Information gathered from patient statements, feelings, and perceptions, such as nausea or pain intensity. - **Objective data**: Measurable and observable findings detected through physical examination, diagnostic tests, and vital sign measurement, such as a temperature of 38.5°C or a blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg. Sources of data are similarly divided. **Primary sources** involve direct information from the patient, whereas **secondary sources** include family members, previous medical records, and other healthcare professionals. In busy clinical settings, validating data—double-checking abnormal findings or conflicting statements—prevents clinical errors before formulating a nursing diagnosis.

Nursing Diagnosis: Formulating Clinical Judgments

A nursing diagnosis is a clinical judgment concerning a human response to health conditions or life processes, or a vulnerability to that response, by an individual, family, or community. It provides the basis for the selection of nursing interventions to achieve outcomes for which the nurse is accountable. It is vital to distinguish between a **medical diagnosis** and a **nursing diagnosis**. A medical diagnosis identifies a disease process (e.g., *Plasmodium falciparum malaria*), remaining constant while the disease persists. Conversely, a nursing diagnosis focuses on the patient's response to that disease (e.g., *Hyperthermia* or *Acute Pain*), which changes as the patient's condition improves or deteriorates. Standardized formats, such as the **PES structure**, guide diagnosis formulation: 1. **P (Problem)**: The NANDA-I diagnostic label. 2. **E (Etiology)**: The related factors or probable cause introduced by 'related to'. 3. **S (Signs and symptoms)**: Defining characteristics or objective/subjective data introduced by 'as evidenced by'.

Planning: Setting Priorities and Expected Outcomes

During the planning phase, the nurse collaborates with the patient to establish priorities and design a customized care strategy. **Priority setting** often utilizes frameworks like Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs, placing life-threatening physiological concerns—such as maintaining a patent airway or addressing severe hemorrhage—at the very top, ahead of psychosocial needs. Following prioritization, the nurse writes **expected outcomes** (or goals). Effective outcomes must follow the **SMART** mnemonic to ensure they are clinically useful: - **Specific**: Clear and unambiguous description of what the patient will achieve. - **Measurable**: Observable indicators such as vital signs, intake and output, or pain scales. - **Attainable**: Realistic considering the patient's current health status and prognosis. - **Relevant**: Closely aligned with resolving the diagnostic label. - **Time-bound**: Featuring a clear deadline for achievement (e.g., 'within 24 hours').

Implementation: Executing Nursing Interventions

Implementation involves putting the established care plan into action. Interventions can be categorized into three distinct types based on the level of autonomy required: | Type of Intervention | Description | Example | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Independent** | Activities nurses are licensed to initiate based on their own knowledge and skills. | Repositioning a bedridden patient every two hours to prevent pressure ulcers. | | **Dependent** | Actions requiring a physician's or advanced practitioner's direct order. | Administering prescribed intravenous antibiotics or analgesics. | | **Interdependent** | Collaborative actions performed jointly with other members of the healthcare team. | Working with a physiotherapist to ambulate a post-operative orthopedic patient. | Before performing any intervention, the nurse must reassess the patient to ensure the action remains appropriate. Clinical judgment dictates adapting techniques to fit the specific resources available in the clinical setting while maintaining safety standards.

Evaluation: Assessing Patient Outcomes and Revising the Plan

Evaluation is the final, ongoing phase of the nursing process, though it occurs concurrently with other steps. During evaluation, the nurse compares the patient's actual behavioral or physiological responses against the expected outcomes established during the planning phase. Based on this comparison, three potential conclusions emerge: 1. **The goal was met**: The patient achieved the expected outcome, and the care plan for that specific diagnosis can be resolved and discontinued. 2. **The goal was partially met**: Progress was made, but the target has not yet been fully reached. The interventions may need continuation or minor adjustment. 3. **The goal was not met**: The patient's condition is unchanged or deteriorated. The nurse must reassess the patient, review the data, and revise the diagnosis, goals, or interventions. Evaluation prevents stagnation in patient care, ensuring that ineffective strategies are quickly identified and replaced.

Critical Thinking and Clinical Reasoning in the Nursing Process

Critical thinking in nursing is an intentional, disciplined, active process of examining underlying assumptions, evaluating evidence, and forming reasoned clinical judgments. It is the cognitive engine that drives every phase of the nursing process. Without critical thinking, the nursing process degenerates into a mindless, rote routine. Clinical reasoning applies critical thinking to specific patient situations. It involves recognizing patterns, weighing competing priorities, anticipating potential complications before they occur, and reflecting on past clinical encounters to improve future practice. Developing these cognitive skills allows nurses to manage complex, unstable patients safely, even in environments with high patient-to-nurse ratios.

Documentation and Communication in Medical-Surgical Nursing

Documentation is the legal and clinical record of all nursing care delivered, serving as proof that the nursing process was properly executed. A well-known legal maxim in nursing states: 'If it was not charted, it was not done.' Records must be accurate, concise, factual, and timely, avoiding vague terms like 'patient had a good day.' Effective interprofessional communication relies heavily on structured tools such as **SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation)**. SBAR provides a standardized format for handovers and emergency phone calls to physicians, ensuring critical information is transmitted rapidly and without ambiguity. Clear documentation and communication safeguard patient safety and maintain professional accountability across shifts.
