Review of the Nursing Process

Introduction and Overview of the Nursing Process

The nursing process is a systematic, rational method used by nurses to plan and provide individualized patient care. It forms the foundation of professional nursing practice across all clinical settings. Far from being a rigid checklist, it is a dynamic, continuous cycle that adapts as the patient's health status changes.

  • Assessment: Systematic collection of health data.
  • Diagnosis: Clinical judgment about health problems.
  • Planning: Setting measurable goals and selecting interventions.
  • Implementation: Carrying out the planned nursing actions.
  • Evaluation: Assessing whether goals were met and revising the plan.

Using this framework helps nurses avoid guesswork and ensures that care is organized, safe, and focused on achieving the best possible patient outcomes.

Assessment: Data Collection and Validation

Assessment is the first step of the nursing process, involving the deliberate and systematic collection of data to determine a patient's current health status. Data is broadly categorized into two types: subjective data, which is what the patient says (such as feeling nauseous or having a sharp pain), and objective data, which is what the nurse observes, measures, or tests (such as a blood pressure reading of 140/90 mmHg or a surgical incision showing redness).

Sources of data include the patient as the primary source, family members when appropriate, health records, and other members of the healthcare team. Once collected, data must be validated. Validation means double-checking the information to ensure it is accurate and complete, preventing the nurse from acting on incorrect assumptions.

Nursing Diagnosis: Formulating Clinical Judgments

A nursing diagnosis is a clinical judgment concerning a human response to health conditions or life processes, or a vulnerability to that response, by an individual, family, or community. It is distinct from a medical diagnosis. While a medical diagnosis focuses on identifying the disease itself (such as pneumonia or appendicitis), a nursing diagnosis focuses on how the patient and their body are responding to that disease (such as ineffective airway clearance or acute pain).

Using standardized nursing diagnoses helps nurses communicate clearly across teams. A common format for problem-focused nursing diagnoses links the patient problem with its related cause and defining characteristics, often remembered through diagnostic statements that connect the issue to clinical evidence.

Planning: Setting Priorities and Expected Outcomes

During the planning phase, the nurse refers to the nursing diagnoses to establish priorities and design a personalized plan of care. Priority setting involves deciding which patient problems require immediate attention using frameworks like Maslow's hierarchy of needs, placing life-threatening physiological issues like airway obstruction above psychosocial concerns.

Once priorities are set, the nurse writes expected outcomes or goals. Good outcomes must be patient-centered and SMART: Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, and Time-bound. For example, instead of writing "patient will breathe better," a proper outcome states, "patient will maintain oxygen saturation above 92% on room air within 24 hours." These goals guide the selection of nursing interventions.

Implementation: Executing Nursing Interventions

Implementation is the action phase of the nursing process where the nurse puts the personalized care plan into practice. Interventions can be independent (actions nurses are licensed to initiate, such as repositioning a patient or teaching relaxation techniques), dependent (actions requiring a physician's order, such as administering medications or starting intravenous fluids), or collaborative (actions carried out jointly with other health team members like physical therapists or dietitians).

Before performing any intervention, the nurse must reassess the patient to ensure the action remains safe and appropriate. During execution, the nurse involves the patient, monitors for immediate responses, and prepares to manage potential complications.

Evaluation: Assessing Outcomes and Revising the Plan

Evaluation is the final step of the nursing process, though it occurs continuously throughout patient care. In this phase, the nurse compares the patient's actual progress against the expected outcomes established during planning.

  • Goal met: The patient achieved the expected outcome; the care plan for that issue can be discontinued.
  • Goal partially met: Progress was made, but the goal is not fully achieved; the intervention may need more time or adjustment.
  • Goal not met: The patient did not achieve the outcome; the nurse must investigate why, re-examine the assessment data, and revise the nursing diagnosis, goals, or interventions.

Evaluation ensures accountability and proves whether the nursing care provided made a positive difference.

Documentation and Communication

Accurate documentation and effective communication are vital threads connecting every phase of the nursing process. If an intervention or assessment is not documented, legally it is considered not done. Nursing notes must be objective, timely, accurate, and concise, reflecting the patient's responses and the care provided.

Handover communication, such as shift reports, requires structured tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) to ensure critical details are not missed during transitions of care. Clear communication prevents medical errors and keeps the entire healthcare team aligned with the patient's goals.
