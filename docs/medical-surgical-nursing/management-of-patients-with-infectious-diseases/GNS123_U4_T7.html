Review of the Nursing Process

Introduction to the Nursing Process

The nursing process is a foundational, systematic, and dynamic problem-solving framework utilized by professional nurses to deliver individualized, holistic patient care. Rooted in the standards of clinical practice, it ensures that nursing care is organized, purposeful, and patient-centered. The framework consists of five distinct, interrelated phases: Assessment, Nursing Diagnosis, Planning, Implementation, and Evaluation. These phases do not always occur in a strictly linear fashion; rather, they frequently overlap and require continuous re-evaluation as the patient's clinical status changes. For nursing students in Nigeria and across the globe, mastering the nursing process is critical because it forms the core of clinical decision-making, licensing examinations, and safe bedside practice. By applying this systematic approach, nurses minimize errors, promote optimal health outcomes, foster therapeutic nurse-patient relationships, and uphold professional accountability. Furthermore, the nursing process serves as a common language among multidisciplinary healthcare teams, ensuring continuity of care from admission to discharge.

Assessment: Data Collection and Validation

Assessment is the critical first step of the nursing process, involving the systematic collection, organization, validation, and documentation of patient data. Data can be categorized as subjective (what the patient states, such as nausea or pain) or objective (what the nurse observes, measures, or assesses, such as vital signs, lab results, and physical exam findings). Sources of data include the patient (primary source), as well as family members, healthcare records, and other health professionals (secondary sources). Once data is collected, the nurse must validate it to ensure accuracy and completeness by double-checking abnormal findings, comparing cues with normal standards, and clarifying ambiguous statements. Validation prevents clinical errors and ensures that subsequent nursing diagnoses are built on truthful, reliable information. In medical-surgical nursing settings, a comprehensive baseline assessment helps identify acute physiological alterations, chronic conditions, and psychosocial needs, guiding all future phases of patient care management.

Nursing Diagnosis: Formulating Clinical Judgments

A nursing diagnosis is a clinical judgment concerning a human response to health conditions/life processes, or a vulnerability to that response, by an individual, family, or community. Unlike a medical diagnosis, which identifies a disease pathology and remains constant, a nursing diagnosis changes as the patient's response changes. Using standardized taxonomies such as NANDA-International (NANDA-I), nurses formulate diagnostic statements that guide patient-centered interventions. A standard problem-focused nursing diagnosis typically includes three parts, commonly known as the PES format: the Problem (diagnostic label), the Etiology (related factors or probable cause), and the Signs and Symptoms (defining characteristics or clinical evidence). Nurses may also formulate risk diagnoses for patients vulnerable to developing a problem, or health-promotion diagnoses reflecting a motivation to improve well-being. Formulating accurate nursing diagnoses requires strong analytical skills to synthesize assessment data and target the correct underlying issues.

Planning: Setting Priorities and Expected Outcomes

The planning phase involves establishing priorities among the identified nursing diagnoses, setting measurable and patient-centered expected outcomes, and selecting appropriate nursing interventions. Priority setting often utilizes frameworks like Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs or the ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation) to ensure that life-threatening issues are addressed first. Once priorities are established, the nurse collaborates with the patient and multidisciplinary team to write SMART (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound) goals and expected outcomes. These outcomes serve as benchmarks to evaluate the success of nursing care. Following outcome identification, the nurse selects specific, evidence-based nursing interventions designed to mitigate the etiology of the nursing diagnosis and help the patient achieve the desired results. All of these decisions are formally documented in the patient's care plan, providing a clear roadmap for the entire healthcare team.

Implementation: Executing Nursing Interventions

Implementation is the fourth step of the nursing process, where the care plan is put into action. It involves executing the planned nursing interventions, which can be independent (actions nurses are licensed to initiate based on their own clinical judgment, such as positioning or teaching), dependent (actions requiring a physician's or provider's order, such as administering medications or managing intravenous fluids), or collaborative (actions carried out in conjunction with other health team members like physiotherapists or dietitians). During implementation, the nurse continues to assess the patient, monitor for adverse reactions, perform the interventions skillfully, and document all care provided. Clinical judgment is essential during this phase to adapt interventions to the patient's current response, ensuring patient safety and comfort. Effective communication and delegation to other nursing personnel, such as auxiliary nurses or healthcare assistants, are also vital components of executing the implementation phase successfully.

Evaluation: Assessing Patient Outcomes and Revising the Plan

Evaluation is the final, ongoing phase of the nursing process, though it informs a continuous cycle of care. During evaluation, the nurse compares the patient's actual clinical outcomes against the SMART expected outcomes established during the planning phase. The nurse collects data regarding the patient's response to interventions and determines whether the goals were completely met, partially met, or not met. Based on these findings, the nurse judges the effectiveness of the care plan. If goals are met, the plan may be discontinued or updated for new goals. If goals are partially met or unmet, the nurse must analyze why—perhaps the assessment data was incomplete, the diagnosis was inaccurate, the interventions were inappropriate, or the timeframe was unrealistic. The care plan is then revised accordingly, restarting the nursing process cycle to ensure continuous improvement in patient health and safety.

Critical Thinking and Clinical Reasoning in the Nursing Process

Critical thinking and clinical reasoning are the cognitive engines driving the entire nursing process. Critical thinking is a broad, disciplined process of actively conceptualizing, analyzing, and evaluating information gathered from observation, experience, or reflection. Clinical reasoning refers specifically to the mental processes nurses use to think about clinical issues, make inferences, and decide on the best actions for a given patient situation. In medical-surgical nursing, patients can deteriorate rapidly, requiring nurses to move beyond rote memorization to analyze complex clusters of data, recognize subtle trends, anticipate complications, and make rapid, evidence-based decisions. Developing strong critical thinking skills empowers nursing students to question assumptions, prioritize conflicting clinical demands, and individualize care safely and effectively, ultimately protecting patients from harm and improving overall healthcare outcomes.

Documentation and Communication in Medical-Surgical Nursing

Documentation and communication are vital legal, professional, and clinical responsibilities that connect every phase of the nursing process. Accurate, objective, and timely documentation ensures continuity of care, facilitates interprofessional communication, provides a legal record of care delivered, and supports quality improvement audits. In medical-surgical nursing, failure to document assessments, interventions, or patient responses can compromise patient safety and lead to professional liability. Communication must be clear and concise, utilizing structured handoff tools such as SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) during shift changes or when reporting critical changes in patient status to attending physicians. Maintaining patient confidentiality, adhering to legal and ethical standards, and ensuring that records are legible and contemporaneously entered are essential competencies for all nursing students entering clinical practice.
