Body Defence Mechanisms

Introduction to Body Defence Mechanisms

The human body is constantly exposed to harmful microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. To survive in this microbe-rich environment, the body relies on an intricate network of cells, tissues, and organs known as the immune system. Body defence mechanisms are generally divided into two main categories: innate immunity, which is present from birth and provides non-specific protection, and adaptive immunity, which develops over time and targets specific invaders.

Understanding how these defences work helps nurses anticipate how patients will respond to infections, injuries, and medical treatments. When these barriers fail or become compromised, the risk of severe infection increases dramatically.

Innate Immunity: The First Line of Defence

The first line of defence consists of physical and chemical barriers that prevent pathogens from entering the body tissues. These structures act as guards at the entry points.

  • Skin: The outer layer provides a tough, impermeable mechanical barrier, while sweat and sebum create an acidic environment that inhibits bacterial growth.
  • Mucous membranes: These line the respiratory, digestive, and urogenital tracts, trapping foreign particles in sticky mucus.
  • Cilia: Tiny hair-like structures in the respiratory tract sweep trapped dust and germs upward away from the lungs.
  • Chemical barriers: Tears, saliva, and stomach acid contain antimicrobial substances like lysozymes that destroy bacterial cell walls.

Innate Immunity: The Second Line of Defence and Inflammatory Response

When pathogens breach the first line of defence, the body triggers the second line, which includes internal non-specific processes. Phagocytes, such as macrophages and neutrophils, engulf and digest foreign invaders.

Simultaneously, tissue injury prompts the inflammatory response. Damaged cells release chemical alarms like histamine, causing local blood vessels to dilate and become more permeable. This increases blood flow to the area, bringing white blood cells, plasma, and clotting factors. The classic signs of inflammation are redness, heat, swelling, and pain. Fever is another systemic innate response that slows bacterial replication while boosting immune cell activity.

Adaptive Immunity: The Third Line of Defence

If innate defences fail to clear an infection, the adaptive immune system takes over. This system is highly specific and has immunological memory, meaning it remembers past invaders for a faster response in the future. It involves two main arms:

  • Humoral immunity: Driven by B-lymphocytes, which mature into plasma cells that produce specific antibodies to neutralize extracellular pathogens.
  • Cell-mediated immunity: Driven by T-lymphocytes, which directly destroy infected, cancerous, or foreign cells.

Vaccination relies on this adaptive mechanism, exposing the body to a harmless form of an antigen to build memory cells without causing illness.

Role of the Lymphatic System in Immunity

The lymphatic system is a vital component of the immune network, acting as a drainage and filtration system. It consists of lymph fluid, lymphatic vessels, lymph nodes, the spleen, and the thymus gland.

Lymph fluid picks up cellular waste, proteins, and foreign particles from interstitial spaces and carries them through lymph nodes. Lymph nodes serve as surveillance stations packed with lymphocytes and macrophages that filter the fluid and trap pathogens before they enter the general bloodstream. Swollen, tender lymph nodes often indicate that the body is actively fighting off a nearby infection.

Factors Affecting Immune Competence in Patients

Many variables can weaken a patient's immune system, making them vulnerable to opportunistic infections. Nurses must identify these risk factors during assessment.

  • Age: The very young have immature immune systems, while older adults experience immunosenescence, a gradual decline in immune function.
  • Nutrition: Malnutrition, especially protein and calorie deficits, impairs immune cell production.
  • Stress: Chronic psychological or physical stress releases cortisol, which suppresses immune responses.
  • Medications: Immunosuppressants, chemotherapy, and long-term corticosteroids directly blunt immune activity.
  • Chronic illnesses: Conditions like diabetes, HIV/AIDS, and renal failure compromise cellular defenses.

Nursing Assessment of Immunocompromised Patients

Assessing an immunocompromised patient requires vigilance because traditional signs of infection may be muted. For instance, a neutropenic patient might not develop pus or pronounced swelling because they lack enough white blood cells to mount a standard inflammatory reaction.

Nurses should perform thorough head-to-toe assessments, paying close attention to vital signs. Even a mild fever may be the only warning sign of a life-threatening systemic infection and must be reported immediately. Inspect the skin, mucous membranes, intravenous catheter sites, and surgical wounds daily for subtle signs of redness, warmth, or tenderness.

Clinical Management and Infection Control Strategies

Protecting patients with weakened immune defenses is a core nursing responsibility. Standard precautions must be maintained for all patients, but protective isolation may be required for those with extremely low white blood cell counts.

Hand hygiene remains the single most effective measure to prevent the spread of healthcare-associated infections. Nurses must also educate patients and visitors on proper handwashing, avoiding crowds, eating well-cooked foods, and reporting any early symptoms of illness promptly. Prophylactic antimicrobial medications may also be prescribed to ward off opportunistic infections in high-risk individuals.
