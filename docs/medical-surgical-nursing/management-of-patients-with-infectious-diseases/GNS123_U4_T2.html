Body Defence Mechanisms

Introduction to Body Defence Mechanisms

The human body is constantly exposed to pathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. To survive, the body relies on an intricate network of cells, tissues, and organs collectively known as the immune system. Defence mechanisms operate in a tiered, highly coordinated sequence designed to prevent entry, halt local proliferation, and mount a targeted counter-offensive against specific invaders. Understanding these barriers forms the bedrock of clinical nursing, particularly when planning protective isolation or identifying breaks in sterile technique. Without these overlapping layers of protection, even minor abrasions would quickly progress to fatal systemic infections.

Innate Immunity: The First Line of Defence

The first line of defence consists of physical, chemical, and mechanical barriers that prevent pathogens from entering the body tissues. These structures are non-specific and act immediately upon exposure to any foreign agent. - **Intact Skin**: The tough, keratinized outer layer of epidermis creates a dry, impenetrable physical barrier. Sebaceous secretions produce an acidic mantle that inhibits bacterial growth. - **Mucous Membranes**: Respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary tracts secrete mucus that traps foreign particles. Cilia in the respiratory tract sweep trapped debris upward toward the throat for expulsion. - **Chemical Secretions**: Lysozyme found in tears, saliva, and sweat enzymaticly destroys bacterial cell walls. Gastric acid in the stomach creates a hostile, highly acidic environment that destroys swallowed microbes. - **Normal Flora**: Commensal microorganisms colonize mucosal surfaces and the skin, outcompeting pathogenic invaders for nutrients and attachment sites.

Innate Immunity: The Second Line of Defence and Inflammatory Response

When pathogens breach surface barriers, the second line of defence activates internally. This tier relies on cellular components and chemical mediators that respond rapidly in a generalized, non-specific manner. Phagocytes, such as neutrophils and macrophages, engulf and digest foreign particles through phagocytosis. Natural killer cells patrol the body, inducing apoptosis in virus-infected or malignant host cells without prior sensitization. Complement proteins circulate in an inactive state until triggered, forming membrane attack complexes that punch holes in bacterial walls. Meanwhile, tissue injury triggers the inflammatory response, characterized by vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and leukocyte migration. Clinically visible as redness, heat, swelling, and pain, inflammation dilutes toxins, delivers immune cells to the site, and paves the way for tissue repair.

Adaptive Immunity: The Third Line of Defence

Unlike innate immunity, the third line of defence is highly specific and possesses immunologic memory. It engages when innate mechanisms are insufficient, mounting a tailored attack orchestrated by lymphocytes. - **Humoral Immunity**: Mediated by B-lymphocytes that mature in the bone marrow. When activated by specific antigens, B-cells differentiate into plasma cells that secrete circulating antibodies. These proteins tag pathogens for destruction. - **Cell-Mediated Immunity**: Orchestrated by T-lymphocytes that mature in the thymus. Cytotoxic T-cells directly destroy infected or cancerous host cells, while helper T-cells release cytokines to coordinate the entire immune response. - **Immunological Memory**: Following initial exposure, specialized memory cells persist for years or decades, ensuring a rapid, robust antibody surge upon subsequent encounters with the identical antigen.

Role of the Lymphatic System in Immunity

The lymphatic system functions as a drainage network and immune surveillance highway. It comprises lymphatic vessels, lymph fluid, lymph nodes, the spleen, tonsils, and the thymus gland. Interstitial fluid that leaks from blood capillaries is collected by lymphatic capillaries, becoming lymph. This fluid is filtered through numerous lymph nodes distributed strategically along lymphatic pathways. Inside these nodes, resident macrophages and lymphocytes intercept foreign antigens, filtering out debris and initiating adaptive immune responses. The spleen filters blood-borne pathogens and recycles worn-out red blood cells, while mucosal-associated lymphoid tissue guards vulnerable entry portals in the gut and respiratory tracts.

Factors Affecting Immune Competence in Patients

Immune competence fluctuates based on numerous physiological and environmental determinants. Age is a major factor; neonates possess immature immune systems, while older adults experience immunosenescence, marked by declining T-cell function and reduced antibody diversity. Chronic diseases such as diabetes mellitus impair neutrophil function and wound healing. Malnutrition, particularly protein-energy deficit, starves rapidly dividing immune cells of essential building blocks. Medical treatments including chemotherapy, radiation, and long-term corticosteroid therapy intentionally or secondarily suppress immune reactivity. Environmental toxins, chronic psychological stress, and severe sleep deprivation further compromise host resistance.

Nursing Assessment of Immunocompromised Patients

Assessing patients with compromised immune defenses requires heightened vigilance, as classic signs of infection may be muted or absent. A subtle rise in temperature can signal a life-threatening systemic crisis in a neutropenic patient. Nurses must meticulously evaluate integumentary integrity, inspecting surgical incisions, intravenous insertion sites, and pressure areas for minimal erythema, warmth, or exudate. Reviewing laboratory profiles, particularly absolute neutrophil counts and white blood cell differentials, is mandatory. Nurses also assess nutritional status, hydration levels, and respiratory function, remaining alert to subtle behavioral shifts that often serve as the earliest indicators of sepsis.

Clinical Management and Infection Control Strategies

Managing vulnerable patients centers on aggressive infection prevention and early medical intervention. Standard precautions must be maintained with all patients, supplemented by transmission-based precautions when specific pathogens are identified. For profoundly neutropenic patients, protective isolation minimizes exposure to environmental microbes. Nurses enforce strict hand hygiene, ensure environmental cleanliness, and restrict fresh flowers, raw fruits, and unpasteurized dairy products that harbor fungal spores or bacteria. Prophylactic antimicrobial therapy is frequently prescribed. Patient education focuses on meticulous personal hygiene, avoiding crowded environments, and recognizing early warning signs of infection to ensure prompt reporting.
