Body Defence Mechanisms

Introduction to Body Defence Mechanisms

The human body is constantly exposed to a multitude of pathogenic microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, which can cause disease and disruption of normal physiological functions. To survive in this microbe-rich environment, the human body relies on a sophisticated and highly coordinated network known as the immune system, which executes body defence mechanisms. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice and professional examinations in Nigeria, understanding these defence mechanisms is foundational to providing holistic patient care, preventing healthcare-associated infections, and promoting health recovery. Body defence mechanisms are broadly categorized into non-specific (innate) defences and specific (adaptive) defences. Innate immunity is present from birth and acts rapidly as a generalized barrier against any foreign invader, whereas adaptive immunity develops over time through exposure to specific antigens, providing tailored, long-lasting protection with a memory component. These two systems do not work in isolation; rather, they constantly communicate and collaborate to identify, neutralize, and eliminate foreign threats while preserving the host's own healthy tissues. Disruptions or deficiencies in these mechanisms lead to various clinical states, ranging from mild allergies to severe, life-threatening immunodeficiencies or autoimmune disorders. As nurses, observing how a patient's body reacts to pathogens provides vital clues regarding their overall health status. Furthermore, recognizing the limits of these defences informs the rationale behind aseptic techniques, isolation protocols, and the administration of immunizations. Mastery of this topic bridges basic biomedical sciences with clinical nursing interventions, ensuring that future nurses can effectively safeguard vulnerable populations across diverse healthcare settings in Nigeria.

Innate Immunity: The First Line of Defence

The first line of defence in the human body is part of innate immunity and comprises structural, physical, chemical, and biological barriers that prevent pathogens from entering the internal environment. These barriers are non-specific, meaning they act equally against all types of foreign invaders without prior exposure. Anatomically, the skin serves as the largest mechanical barrier; its outer layer of keratinized cells is tightly packed and continuously shed, removing attached microorganisms. Additionally, mucous membranes line the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary tracts, trapping foreign particles in sticky mucus. Chemical barriers complement these physical structures. For example, sweat, sebum, and tears contain acidic compounds, salt, and antimicrobial peptides like lysozyme, which can destroy bacterial cell walls. Gastric acid in the stomach creates an extremely hostile environment that neutralizes many ingested pathogens. Biological barriers include the normal resident microbiota—the non-pathogenic bacteria and fungi that inhabit the skin and mucous membranes. These normal flora protect the host by competing with harmful pathogens for space and essential nutrients, and by secreting bacteriocins that inhibit pathogenic growth. When any of these first-line components are compromised—such as through burns, surgical incisions, or the prolonged use of broad-spectrum antibiotics that destroy normal flora—the risk of infection increases significantly. For student nurses, maintaining the integrity of these barriers and understanding how breaks in the skin or mucous membranes facilitate pathogen entry is critical for planning effective patient hygiene, wound care, and infection prevention strategies in the clinical ward.

Innate Immunity: The Second Line of Defence and Inflammatory Response

When pathogens successfully breach the body's first line of defence, they encounter the second line of defence, which is also a component of innate immunity. This internal tier includes antimicrobial proteins, phagocytic white blood cells (such as neutrophils and macrophages), natural killer (NK) cells, and the inflammatory response. Phagocytosis is a core cellular process where specialized leukocytes engulf, digest, and destroy foreign particles and cellular debris. Complement proteins, circulating in the blood in an inactive state, are rapidly triggered by microbial surfaces to form a membrane attack complex that lyses invading cells and promotes inflammation. Inflammation is a localized, protective tissue response to injury, infection, or irritation, characterized by the classical cardinal signs: redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor), and loss of function (functio laesa). These signs are mediated by chemical mediators such as histamine, prostaglandins, and cytokines released by damaged cells and mast cells. Vasodilation and increased capillary permeability allow plasma, clotting factors, and white blood cells to rush from the blood vessels into the infected tissue spaces, walling off the pathogen and initiating tissue repair. Fever is another systemic component of the second line of defence, triggered by pyrogens that reset the hypothalamic thermostat to elevate body temperature, which inhibits bacterial replication and enhances immune cell activity. For nursing students, recognizing these inflammatory signs helps differentiate normal healing from abnormal pathological processes like spreading cellulitis or wound infection.

Adaptive Immunity: The Third Line of Defence

If innate defences are insufficient to clear an infection, the body activates adaptive immunity, which serves as the third line of defence. Adaptive immunity is antigen-specific, systemic, and possesses immunological memory, allowing the body to mount a faster and much stronger response upon subsequent encounters with the same pathogen. This branch is mediated by lymphocytes originating from lymphoid stem cells in the bone marrow: B-lymphocytes (B cells) and T-lymphocytes (T cells). B cells mature in the bone marrow and are responsible for humoral immunity. When activated by an antigen, B cells differentiate into plasma cells that synthesize and secrete large quantities of specific glycoproteins called antibodies (immunoglobulins). These antibodies circulate in blood and lymph, binding to target antigens to neutralize toxins, agglutinate pathogens, and mark them for destruction by phagocytes. T cells mature in the thymus and drive cell-mediated immunity. Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) directly bind to and destroy infected, cancerous, or foreign host cells by releasing perforins and granzymes. Helper T cells (CD4+) play a master regulatory role by releasing cytokines that stimulate both B cells and cytotoxic T cells, effectively orchestrating the entire adaptive immune response. Memory B and T cells remain dormant long after the infection is cleared, providing long-term immunity that forms the biological basis for successful vaccination programs. Student nurses must grasp these concepts to understand how vaccines protect individuals and communities against vaccine-preventable diseases prevalent in Nigeria.

Role of the Lymphatic System in Immunity

The lymphatic system plays a critical structural and functional role in supporting the body's immune defences. It is a specialized circulatory network consisting of lymphatic vessels, lymph fluid, lymph nodes, the spleen, the thymus, and mucosal-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT). The primary function of this system is to collect excess interstitial fluid from tissue spaces, filter it to remove foreign particles, pathogens, and cellular waste, and return the purified fluid back into the venous bloodstream. As lymph flows through strategically placed bean-shaped lymph nodes, it encounters dense populations of resident B cells, T cells, and macrophages. These immune cells inspect the fluid for antigens, initiate adaptive immune responses when foreign threats are detected, and proliferate rapidly, which often causes the physical enlargement or tenderness of lymph nodes (lymphadenopathy) commonly observed during infections. The spleen, the largest lymphoid organ, filters blood rather than lymph, destroying aged erythrocytes and mounting immune responses against blood-borne pathogens. The thymus is essential for the maturation and differentiation of T-lymphocytes during childhood. Furthermore, lymphatic tissues in the gut, tonsils, and respiratory tracts (MALT) act as front-line sentinel stations guarding entry portals into the body. Understanding the anatomy and physiology of the lymphatic system helps nurses interpret physical assessment findings, such as localized edema and swollen lymph nodes, giving critical insights into infection localization and dissemination pathways in patients.

Factors Affecting Immune Competence in Patients

A patient's immune competence—their ability to mount an effective immune response against pathogens—is influenced by a wide array of intrinsic and extrinsic factors. Age is a major determinant; neonates have immature immune systems with underdeveloped barriers and antibody reserves, while elderly individuals experience immunosenescence, a gradual deterioration of both innate and adaptive immunity characterized by reduced T-cell function and lower antibody affinity. Nutritional status is equally critical; protein-energy malnutrition, vitamin deficiencies (such as vitamins A, C, D, and zinc), and dehydration drastically impair leukocyte function, antibody production, and mucosal integrity. Chronic illnesses such as diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and HIV/AIDS directly suppress immunological pathways, making patients highly susceptible to opportunistic infections. Furthermore, pharmacological agents, including immunosuppressants used in organ transplantation, chemotherapy drugs for malignancies, and the prolonged or indiscriminate use of corticosteroids, significantly depress immune function. Lifestyle factors, such as chronic psychological stress, fatigue, lack of sleep, and substance abuse (alcohol and tobacco), elevate cortisol levels and suppress cellular immunity. Environmental exposures and socioeconomic factors common in resource-limited settings, such as poor sanitation and overcrowding, also increase pathogen load and strain immune capacities. For nurses, evaluating these risk factors during admission assessments allows for the early identification of immunocompromised patients who require targeted preventive care and monitoring.

Nursing Assessment of Immunocompromised Patients

Nursing assessment of the immunocompromised patient requires a meticulous, systematic approach to detect subtle signs of infection, as classic inflammatory symptoms may be muted or absent due to impaired immune responses. For instance, a severely neutropenic patient may not develop localized swelling or purulent exudate, with a low-grade fever serving as the only warning sign of a potentially fatal systemic infection. The nursing assessment begins with a comprehensive health history, reviewing the patient's underlying diagnosis, medication profile (such as chemotherapy or immunosuppressants), nutritional habits, and vaccination history. A thorough physical examination involves checking vital signs, paying special attention to temperature trends (even a slight elevation can indicate sepsis), respiratory rate, blood pressure, and heart rate. Nurses must inspect all potential portals of entry, including skin integrity, intravenous catheter insertion sites, surgical incisions, and mucosal membranes in the oral cavity for signs of candidiasis (thrush) or ulcers. Diagnostic laboratory values must be closely monitored, particularly complete blood counts (CBC) with differential, noting absolute neutrophil counts (ANC), leukocyte counts, and inflammatory markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Any drop in ANC below safe thresholds requires immediate protective measures. By maintaining a high index of suspicion and performing diligent head-to-toe assessments, nurses can detect early clinical deterioration and initiate prompt life-saving interventions.

Clinical Management and Infection Control Strategies

Clinical management and infection control strategies for immunocompromised patients aim to minimize pathogen exposure and support physiological defences. A cornerstone of this management is strict adherence to standard and transmission-based precautions, including meticulous hand hygiene before and after every patient contact, which remains the single most effective method to prevent cross-contamination in healthcare facilities. For patients with profound neutropenia or severe immune deficiencies, protective isolation (reverse isolation) may be instituted, placing the patient in a private room with specialized air filtration where visitors and staff adhere to strict barrier nursing protocols (masks, gowns, and gloves). Nurses must educate patients and their families regarding dietary restrictions, such as avoiding raw fruits, unpasteurized dairy, and undercooked meats that may harbor harmful microorganisms like Listeria or Salmonella. Environmental controls include prohibiting live plants or flowers in the patient's room, as stagnant water and soil host dangerous fungal spores like Aspergillus. Invasive devices, such as peripheral IV lines, central venous catheters, and urinary catheters, should be maintained using strict aseptic technique and removed as soon as they are no longer clinically indicated. Finally, prophylactic antimicrobial medications and vaccinations (non-live vaccines, as live vaccines are contraindicated in immunocompromised hosts) may be administered as prescribed. Through these rigorous infection control practices, nurses protect vulnerable patients from preventable healthcare-associated infections.
