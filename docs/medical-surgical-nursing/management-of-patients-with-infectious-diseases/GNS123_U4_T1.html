Review of Anatomy and Physiology of the Body Defence System

Introduction and Overview of Body Defence

The human body is constantly exposed to harmful microorganisms in the environment, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites. To survive, the body relies on the immune system, a complex network of cells, tissues, and organs that work together to defend against these invaders.

Defense mechanisms are broadly divided into two main categories: innate immunity, which is present from birth and acts rapidly, and adaptive immunity, which develops over time and targets specific pathogens with high precision. These systems do not work in isolation; they communicate constantly through chemical messengers called cytokines to mount an effective response without harming the host's own tissues.

Anatomy of Primary and Secondary Lymphoid Organs

Lymphoid organs are categorized based on their functions in lymphocyte maturation and activation.

  • Primary Lymphoid Organs: The bone marrow and the thymus are where lymphocytes are formed and mature. B-cells mature in the bone marrow, while T-cells migrate to the thymus to complete their maturation.
  • Secondary Lymphoid Organs: The lymph nodes, spleen, and mucosal-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) are where mature lymphocytes encounter foreign antigens and initiate an immune response.

Lymph nodes filter lymph fluid drained from body tissues, trapping pathogens so that resident immune cells can destroy them. The spleen performs a similar filtering role, but it monitors the blood for blood-borne antigens and old red blood cells.

Cells of the Immune System

All blood cells originate from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow. White blood cells, or leukocytes, are the primary cellular agents of defense.

  • Neutrophils: The most abundant white blood cells, acting as rapid-responder phagocytes that engulf and destroy bacteria.
  • Macrophages: Large phagocytic cells that dwell in tissues, consuming debris and presenting antigens to helper T-cells.
  • Lymphocytes: Comprise B-cells, T-cells, and natural killer (NK) cells, driving adaptive immunity and targeted cell destruction.
  • Eosinophils and Basophils: Specialized granulocytes involved in combating parasitic infections and mediating allergic reactions.

Innate Immunity and Barriers

Innate immunity forms the first and second lines of defense. The first line consists of physical and chemical barriers that prevent pathogen entry. The skin provides a tough, dry, keratinized outer layer, while mucous membranes secrete sticky mucus that traps foreign particles in the respiratory and digestive tracts.

Chemical barriers include acidic secretions in the stomach and antimicrobial peptides found in sweat, saliva, and tears, such as lysozyme. If a pathogen breaches these barriers, internal innate defenses take over, utilizing phagocytic cells, antimicrobial proteins, and the inflammatory response to contain the threat without needing prior exposure.

Adaptive Immunity: Humoral and Cell-Mediated Pathways

When innate defenses are insufficient, the adaptive immune system activates. It features specificity and memory, tailoring responses to exact molecular signatures called antigens.

  • Humoral Immunity: Driven by B-lymphocytes, which mature into plasma cells that secrete soluble proteins called antibodies. These antibodies circulate in body fluids and neutralize extracellular pathogens or mark them for destruction.
  • Cell-Mediated Immunity: Governed by T-lymphocytes. Helper T-cells coordinate the immune response by releasing signaling chemicals, while cytotoxic T-cells directly identify and destroy infected, cancerous, or foreign host cells.

The Complement System and Inflammation

The complement system is a group of plasma proteins produced by the liver that circulate in an inactive state. When triggered by infection, these proteins activate in a cascading sequence.

Activation of complement enhances inflammation, attracts phagocytes to the infection site through chemotaxis, and forms a membrane attack complex (MAC) that punches holes in bacterial cell membranes, causing lysis.

Similarly, inflammation is a protective localized response to tissue injury or infection. Characterized by redness, heat, swelling, and pain, inflammation increases local blood flow and vascular permeability, allowing white blood cells to exit the bloodstream and reach the site of injury quickly.

Immune Tolerance and Regulation

While destroying foreign invaders is vital, the immune system must also practice immune tolerance to prevent attacking the body's own healthy tissues. Self-tolerance is achieved during lymphocyte development in the primary organs, where immature cells that react strongly against self-antigens are eliminated through apoptosis.

Regulatory T-cells (T-regs) help suppress excessive immune responses and prevent autoimmune reactions once an infection has been cleared. When regulation fails, the immune system may mistakenly attack self-tissues, leading to autoimmune conditions such as systemic lupus erythematosus or rheumatoid arthritis.

Clinical Relevance for Nursing Practice

Understanding immune anatomy and physiology directly guides safe and effective nursing care in clinical settings across Nigeria. Nurses monitor patients for signs of infection, such as fever, localized warmth, and purulent drainage, which indicate active inflammatory and immune responses.

In routine practice, nurses administer vaccines to stimulate adaptive immunity and promote immunological memory. Furthermore, nurses must recognize how immunosuppressive conditions, malnutrition, or certain medications can compromise a patient's defense mechanisms, increasing vulnerability to opportunistic infections.
