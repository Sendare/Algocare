Review of Anatomy and Physiology of the Body Defence System

Introduction to the Body Defence System

The human body defence system is a complex network of cells, tissues, and organs that work collectively to protect the host against pathogens, including bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, as well as abnormal cells like tumor cells. Understanding this system is crucial for nursing students practicing in Nigeria, as infectious diseases like malaria, tuberculosis, and typhoid place a heavy burden on the healthcare system. The defence system is broadly categorized into two main branches: innate immunity, which provides immediate, non-specific protection from birth, and adaptive immunity, which develops specific, tailored responses over time and establishes immunological memory. Both systems must function in a coordinated, harmonious manner to maintain homeostasis and prevent disease. When the defence system fails or becomes hyperactive, it can lead to immunodeficiency disorders, hypersensitivities, or autoimmune diseases. As nurses, a strong grasp of these foundational concepts allows us to better assess patients, understand disease pathophysiology, and deliver effective patient education regarding vaccination, infection control, and immune health. Furthermore, recognizing how the body reacts to foreign invaders helps nurses interpret diagnostic lab results, such as complete blood counts with differentials, providing holistic and evidence-based care to patients across diverse clinical settings in Nigeria.

Anatomy of Primary and Secondary Lymphoid Organs

The lymphoid organs of the immune system are categorized into primary (central) and secondary (peripheral) organs based on their functions in lymphocyte maturation and activation. Primary lymphoid organs consist of the red bone marrow and the thymus gland. The red bone marrow is the site of hematopoiesis, where all blood cells originate, and it serves as the maturation site for B lymphocytes. The thymus, located in the superior mediastinum, is where T lymphocytes migrate from the bone marrow to mature and undergo rigorous selection processes to ensure they can distinguish self from non-self. Secondary lymphoid organs include the lymph nodes, spleen, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT), and gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) such as Peyer's patches. These structures trap and concentrate foreign pathogens, presenting them to mature lymphocytes to initiate an adaptive immune response. The lymph nodes filter lymph fluid drained from peripheral tissues, while the spleen filters blood-borne antigens and destroys aged erythrocytes. In the context of nursing care in Nigeria, assessing peripheral lymph nodes for enlargement (lymphadenopathy) is a critical physical examination skill that often indicates localized or systemic infection, such as tuberculosis or HIV. Knowing the anatomical layout of these organs enables nurses to understand how pathogens spread through lymphatic and circulatory channels.

Cells of the Immune System and Their Functions

The cells of the immune system originate from hematopoietic stem cells in the red bone marrow and differentiate into myeloid and lymphoid lineages. Myeloid progenitors give rise to innate immune cells such as neutrophils, macrophages, dendritic cells, basophils, eosinophils, and mast cells. Neutrophils are the most abundant white blood cells and act as rapid-response phagocytes during acute bacterial infections. Macrophages are versatile phagocytic cells that engulf pathogens, present antigens, and secrete inflammatory cytokines. Dendritic cells are premier antigen-presenting cells that bridge innate and adaptive immunity by migrating to secondary lymphoid organs to activate naive T cells. Eosinophils and basophils play vital roles in combating parasitic infections and mediating allergic responses, respectively. The lymphoid lineage produces Natural Killer (NK) cells, which belong to the innate system and destroy virus-infected or tumor cells, as well as B and T lymphocytes, which drive the adaptive immune response. B cells differentiate into plasma cells that secrete antibodies, while T cells differentiate into helper T cells that coordinate immune responses and cytotoxic T cells that directly destroy infected host cells. Understanding these cell types and their specific laboratory markers is essential for Nigerian nurses when interpreting complete blood counts to evaluate infection severity.

Innate Immunity: Anatomical Barriers and Non-Specific Responses

Innate immunity represents the body's first line of defense and is operational from birth, reacting rapidly to pathogens without requiring prior exposure. It comprises anatomical, physical, chemical, and biological barriers. Physical barriers include the intact skin, which blocks pathogen entry, and the mucous membranes lining the respiratory, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary tracts, which trap foreign particles with mucus and expel them via cilia or peristalsis. Chemical barriers consist of antimicrobial secretions such as stomach acid, lysozyme in tears and saliva, and sebum on the skin, which create a hostile environment for microorganisms. Normal microbial flora act as a biological barrier by outcompeting pathogenic bacteria for space and nutrients. When these barriers are breached, internal non-specific responses are triggered, involving pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). This activates phagocytosis, antimicrobial peptides, and acute phase proteins. For nurses, assessing the integrity of anatomical barriers—such as inspecting surgical wounds, intravenous cannulation sites, or pressure ulcers—is critical in preventing hospital-acquired infections, particularly in resource-constrained environments where infection control protocols must be rigorously maintained.

Adaptive Immunity: Humoral and Cell-Mediated Pathways

Adaptive immunity provides a delayed, highly specific response tailored to particular pathogens, characterized by immunological memory that ensures a rapid response upon re-exposure. It is divided into two main pathways: humoral immunity and cell-mediated immunity. Humoral immunity is mediated by B lymphocytes and antibodies dissolved in body fluids (blood and lymph). When a naive B cell encounters its specific antigen and receives secondary signals from helper T cells, it proliferates and differentiates into plasma cells that secrete antigen-specific antibodies. These antibodies neutralize toxins, opsonize pathogens for phagocytosis, and activate the complement cascade. Cell-mediated immunity involves T lymphocytes and is directed primarily against intracellular pathogens, such as viruses and certain bacteria, as well as cancer cells. Helper T cells (CD4+) secrete cytokines that stimulate other immune cells, while cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) directly induce apoptosis in infected or malignant host cells. Immunization programs in Nigeria, such as the Expanded Programme on Immunization (EPI), rely entirely on the principles of adaptive immunity to induce memory cells without causing full-blown disease. Nurses play a frontline role in administering these vaccines and educating communities on the importance of complete immunization schedules.

The Complement System and Inflammatory Response

The complement system is a cascade of over 30 plasma proteins produced primarily by the liver that circulate in an inactive state and work synergistically with both innate and adaptive immune responses. Activation of the complement system occurs through three pathways: the classical pathway (triggered by antibody-antigen complexes), the lectin pathway (triggered by carbohydrates on pathogen surfaces), and the alternative pathway (triggered directly by pathogen surfaces). Regardless of the initiation pathway, all three converge to form a membrane attack complex (MAC) that bores holes in bacterial cell walls, causing osmotic lysis. Additionally, complement activation promotes inflammation, opsonizes pathogens to enhance phagocytosis, and attracts immune cells through chemotaxis. The inflammatory response itself is a local protective reaction to tissue injury or infection, characterized by the cardinal signs: redness (rubor), heat (calor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor), and loss of function (functio laesa). Vasodilation and increased vascular permeability allow leukocytes and plasma proteins to exit the bloodstream and enter the affected tissue. For nursing practice, recognizing signs of inflammation helps differentiate normal healing from abnormal responses like wound infection or systemic sepsis, guiding prompt clinical interventions such as wound care and antibiotic administration.

Physiological Regulation and Immune Tolerance

Physiological regulation and immune tolerance are essential mechanisms that prevent the immune system from attacking the body's own healthy tissues and cells. Immune tolerance is established through central tolerance, which occurs during lymphocyte development in the primary lymphoid organs where self-reactive T and B cells are eliminated through apoptosis, and peripheral tolerance, which neutralizes or suppresses self-reactive lymphocytes that escape into the circulation. Regulatory T cells (Tregs) play a vital role in peripheral tolerance by suppressing excessive immune responses and preventing autoimmune reactions. Furthermore, anti-inflammatory cytokines like Interleukin-10 (IL-10) and Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-beta) act as physiological brakes, dampening inflammation once a pathogen has been successfully cleared to prevent collateral tissue damage. When these regulatory mechanisms fail, the immune system mounts destructive responses against self-antigens, leading to autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or rheumatoid arthritis. For nursing students, understanding immune tolerance provides insight into the pathophysiology of autoimmune conditions, the use of immunosuppressive drug therapy, and the management of hypersensitivity reactions, enabling comprehensive patient care and medication monitoring.

Clinical Relevance for Nursing Practice in Nigeria

A comprehensive understanding of the body defence system has direct clinical relevance for nursing practice in Nigeria, influencing infection control, public health initiatives, and patient assessment. Nurses must apply knowledge of anatomical barriers and asepsis during procedures such as wound dressings, urinary catheterization, and intravenous cannulations to prevent hospital-acquired infections, which are a major challenge in under-resourced healthcare settings. In public health nursing, immunization campaigns rely on the principles of adaptive immunity to protect communities against preventable infectious diseases like measles, polio, and tetanus. Nurses are responsible for vaccine cold-chain management, administration, and addressing vaccine hesitancy through health education. Additionally, nurses monitor patients for signs of immune dysfunction, such as chronic fatigue, recurrent infections, or opportunistic illnesses often seen in immunocompromised individuals. Interpreting laboratory parameters—such as elevated white blood cell counts indicating infection or altered CD4 counts in HIV management—allows nurses to evaluate treatment efficacy and collaborate effectively with multidisciplinary healthcare teams to improve patient outcomes across Nigeria.
