Concepts in Medical-Surgical Nursing

Introduction to Medical-Surgical Nursing

Medical-surgical nursing forms the backbone of adult healthcare settings. Practitioners in this specialty care for physiologically unstable patients across a spectrum of acute and chronic conditions. The scope demands a solid grasp of human anatomy, advanced pharmacology, and rapid clinical reasoning. In busy Nigerian teaching hospitals, medical-surgical nurses frequently triage high patient volumes, manage complex comorbidities with limited resources, and coordinate multi-disciplinary care plans under intense pressure.

The Nursing Process in Med-Surg Practice

The nursing process provides a systematic, rational framework for clinical problem-solving in medical-surgical wards.

  1. Assessment: Systematic collection of subjective and objective health data.
  2. Diagnosis: Clinical judgment regarding human responses to actual or potential health problems.
  3. Planning: Setting measurable goals and selecting specific nursing interventions.
  4. Implementation: Executing the planned care safely and effectively.
  5. Evaluation: Determining whether patient outcomes have been met and modifying the plan if necessary.

Skipping steps often leads to missed complications, particularly in rapidly deteriorating ward patients.

Fluid, Electrolyte, and Acid-Base Balance

Cellular survival depends on precise regulation of body fluids and chemical buffers. Imbalances frequently complicate medical-surgical admissions, stemming from conditions like severe diarrhea, burns, or renal failure.

Potassium disturbances demand extreme vigilance because cardiac arrhythmias can develop rapidly. Nurses must monitor intake and output charts diligently, paying close attention to signs of third-spacing and peripheral edema.

Inflammation, Immunity, and Wound Healing

Tissue injury triggers complex inflammatory and immune cascades aimed at neutralizing pathogens and restoring structural integrity. Healing progresses through hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling phases.

  • Primary Intention: Clean surgical incisions with approximated edges heal quickly with minimal scarring.
  • Secondary Intention: Wounds with extensive tissue loss require granulation tissue to fill the gap, taking longer to heal.
  • Tertiary Intention: Delayed primary closure occurs when a contaminated wound is left open temporarily before surgical closure.

Infections significantly delay healing. Nutritional deficiencies, especially inadequate protein and vitamin C intake, further impair collagen synthesis.

Pain Assessment and Management

Pain remains subjective, famously defined as whatever the experiencing person says it is. Comprehensive assessment evaluates location, intensity, quality, and aggravating or relieving factors using validated tools like the Numerical Rating Scale. Multimodal analgesia combines non-opioid medications, adjuvant drugs, and opioids to target different pain pathways while minimizing adverse effects. Nurses play a pivotal role in evaluating treatment efficacy and monitoring for opioid-induced respiratory depression.

Perioperative Nursing Care

Surgical care encompasses the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative phases. Preoperative preparation focuses on informed consent, baseline diagnostics, and anxiety reduction. Intraoperative safety mandates strict aseptic technique, correct surgical site verification, and physiological monitoring. Postoperative nursing centers on preventing complications such as atelectasis, deep vein thrombosis, and surgical site infections through early ambulation, incentive spirometry, and vigilant vital sign tracking.

Management of Shock and Multiorgan Dysfunction

Shock is a life-threatening medical emergency characterized by systemic cellular hypoperfusion and oxygen mismatch. Categorized primarily into hypovolemic, cardiogenic, septic, and anaphylactic types, shock rapidly progresses to multiorgan dysfunction syndrome if left untreated. Initial management prioritizes securing a patent airway, restoring intravascular volume with rapid fluid resuscitation, and administering vasopressors when hypotension persists. Nurses must monitor urine output closely as an early indicator of renal perfusion.

Palliative and End-of-Life Care

Palliative care improves the quality of life for patients and families facing serious, life-limiting illnesses through symptom relief, spiritual support, and multidisciplinary coordination. End-of-life care shifts focus entirely away from curative treatments toward comfort, dignity, and peaceful dying. Nurses manage distressing symptoms like dyspnea, pain, and terminal secretions while providing compassionate emotional support to grieving family members.
