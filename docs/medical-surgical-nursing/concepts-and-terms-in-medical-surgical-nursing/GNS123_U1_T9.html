Illness Behaviour

Introduction and Core Concepts of Illness Behaviour

Illness behaviour refers to the ways in which given individuals respond to bodily symptoms, monitor their internal states, interpret symptoms, take remedial action, and make use of the health care system. While disease is a physical or biological alteration in bodily organs or systems, illness is the subjective, human experience of feeling unwell. A patient with severe hypertension might have no disease symptoms at all, whereas someone with mild indigestion might experience intense distress and seek immediate medical attention.

David Mechanic originally defined this concept, pointing out that two people with the exact same physical pathology can act completely differently. One person continues going to work while feeling feverish, while another immediately stays in bed and calls a doctor. Understanding these differences helps nurses look beyond lab results and appreciate the person behind the diagnosis.

Stages of the Illness Experience

When people fall sick, they generally pass through distinct psychological and behavioural phases. Recognizing these stages helps nurses anticipate patient needs and reactions.

  1. Symptom Experience: The individual notices a physical change, pain, or strange sensation. This is the 'something is wrong' moment.
  2. Assumption of the Sick Role: The person accepts that they are sick and seeks validation from family or peers. Normal social duties begin to be put aside.
  3. Medical Care Contact: The individual decides that self-care is not enough and consults a healthcare professional for diagnosis and treatment.
  4. Dependent-Patient Role: The person undergoes treatment and hands over control of their care to medical staff, relying on nurses and doctors.
  5. Recovery and Rehabilitation: The patient drops the sick role, gives up the dependent status, and returns to normal daily routines and responsibilities.

Determinants of How People React to Illness

Not everyone reacts to pain or discomfort the same way. A complex web of personal, social, and psychological factors shapes why one person shrugs off a symptom while another panics.

  • Perception of Pain: Personal pain tolerance and past experiences strongly influence how threatening a symptom feels.
  • Knowledge and Education: Understanding how the body works helps individuals separate minor ailments from true medical emergencies.
  • Financial Status: The cost of transport, consultation, and drugs dictates whether a person can afford to seek timely care.
  • Stress and Coping: High stress levels can amplify physical symptoms and drain a person's emotional resilience.
  • Past Encounters: A previous traumatic hospital visit can make someone avoid seeking care in the future.

Cultural and Societal Influences

Culture acts as a lens through which people view health and sickness. Different communities hold unique beliefs about what causes disease, how pain should be expressed, and who should treat it.

In some cultures, expressing pain openly is encouraged and expected as a way to gain communal support. In other settings, stoicism is valued, and individuals are praised for bearing pain in silence. Cultural background also dictates whether a person turns first to traditional healers, herbal remedies, prayer houses, or orthodox medical hospitals. Nurses must respect these backgrounds without judgment, understanding that cultural beliefs directly drive patient compliance and health-seeking patterns.

Normal vs. Abnormal Illness Behaviours

Illness behaviour exists on a wide spectrum. Normal illness behaviour matches the severity of the underlying physical problem and allows the person to cooperate with treatment and return to normal life eventually.

Abnormal illness behaviour occurs when a person's response is out of proportion to the actual physical findings. This can manifest in two major ways:

  • Hypochondriasis or Health Anxiety: Persistent, unwarranted fear of having a serious disease despite medical reassurance and negative test results.
  • Denial and Minimization: Refusing to acknowledge obvious, life-threatening symptoms like chest pain or bleeding, leading to dangerous delays in seeking medical help.

Nursing Assessment of Illness Behaviour

Assessing illness behaviour goes beyond taking vital signs and checking temperature charts. Nurses must observe how patients interact with their environment, talk about their pain, and respond to hospital routines.

Assessment AreaWhat the Nurse Looks For
Verbal ExpressionHow the patient describes pain and emotional distress
Coping StyleWhether the patient is cooperative, aggressive, or withdrawn
Support SystemsFamily involvement and visitors present at the bedside
BeliefsWhat the patient thinks caused their current sickness

Asking open-ended questions like 'How has this sickness affected your daily life?' helps uncover hidden fears and expectations that standard medical tests miss.

Clinical Management and Nursing Interventions

Managing challenging illness behaviours requires patience, active listening, and clear communication. When a patient exhibits extreme denial or excessive anxiety, confrontation rarely works.

Instead, nurses should validate the patient's feelings while gently providing objective information. For patients who minimize symptoms, education about warning signs can prevent medical emergencies. For those with high anxiety, reassurance, relaxation techniques, and structured routines help restore a sense of control.

Collaborating with medical social workers, psychologists, and spiritual leaders ensures that the patient receives holistic care that addresses both body and mind.
