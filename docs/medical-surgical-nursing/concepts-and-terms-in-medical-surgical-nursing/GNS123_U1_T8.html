Stress

Introduction to Stress and the General Adaptation Syndrome

Stress is a universal human experience defined as any disturbance in homeostasis that triggers physical, emotional, or behavioral changes. Hans Selye formulated the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) to describe how the body mounts a standardized physiological response to various stressors. This response unfolds across three distinct phases:

  1. Alarm Reaction: The initial shock phase where the body recognizes the stressor and mobilizes defenses via the sympathetic nervous system. Resistance momentarily drops below baseline during the initial shock before rebounding.
  2. Stage of Resistance: The body actively attempts to adapt to the persistent stressor. Cortisol and catecholamines remain elevated, and physiological functions attempt to normalize while maintaining high vigilance.
  3. Stage of Exhaustion: Prolonged exposure depletes adaptive energy. The body can no longer maintain heightened defense, leading to systemic collapse, burnout, or increased vulnerability to disease.

Pathophysiology and the Neuroendocrine Response

When a person encounters a threat, the central nervous system rapidly activates two primary pathways: the sympathomedullary (SAM) pathway and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

The SAM pathway represents the acute fight-or-flight response. The hypothalamus stimulates the sympathetic nervous system, prompting the adrenal medulla to release epinephrine and norepinephrine. This causes immediate hemodynamic changes, including tachycardia, bronchodilation, and vasoconstriction in non-essential organs.

The HPA axis sustains the response over longer periods. The hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), stimulating the anterior pituitary to secrete adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH). ACTH then signals the adrenal cortex to synthesize and release cortisol. Cortisol elevates blood glucose via gluconeogenesis, suppresses non-essential immune functions, and dampens inflammatory pathways.

Classification and Types of Stressors

Stressors are classified based on their origin, duration, and predictability. Recognizing these categories helps nurses understand patient triggers in diverse clinical and community settings.

  • Internal Stressors: Originating within the individual, such as a terminal diagnosis, chronic pain, or unrealistic personal expectations.
  • External Stressors: Environmental or social pressures, including financial hardship, natural disasters, occupational demands, or interpersonal conflict.
  • Acute Stressors: Sudden, short-lived events like an accidental injury or an unexpected code blue.
  • Chronic Stressors: Long-term, relentless conditions such as poverty, long-term caregiving for a relative with dementia, or unresolved marital discord.

Physiological and Psychological Manifestations of Stress

The clinical presentation of stress spans both body and mind. Physiological signs stem from autonomic nervous system overactivity and hormonal surges. Patients commonly exhibit elevated blood pressure, palpitations, shallow tachypnea, tension headaches, gastrointestinal upset (such as nausea or diarrhea), and dry mouth.

Psychological manifestations affect mood, cognition, and behavior. Anxiety, irritability, feelings of helplessness, and depression frequently accompany sustained pressure. Cognitive symptoms include impaired concentration, forgetfulness, and poor decision-making. Behavioral shifts may involve social withdrawal, changes in appetite, insomnia, or increased reliance on maladaptive coping mechanisms like smoking or alcohol consumption.

Impact of Chronic Stress on Body Systems

When the stress response remains chronically activated, systemic wear and tear—known as allostatic load—damages multiple organ systems.

Body SystemChronic Stress Impact
CardiovascularSustained hypertension, endothelial damage, accelerated atherosclerosis, and heightened myocardial infarction risk.
ImmuneProlonged cortisol exposure suppresses leukocyte function, increasing susceptibility to infections and delaying wound healing.
GastrointestinalExacerbation of peptic ulcer disease, irritable bowel syndrome, and alterations in gut microbiome integrity.
NeurologicalProlonged high cortisol levels can cause atrophy of neurons in the hippocampus, impairing memory and emotional regulation.

In Nigerian clinical practice, the somatic toll of chronic economic and social stress often presents as vague physical complaints—such as 'body heat,' generalized aches, or crawling sensations—without organic pathology.

Clinical Assessment and Screening for Stress

Assessing stress requires a holistic nursing approach that goes beyond physical vital signs. Nurses must evaluate both verbal and non-verbal cues. Screening tools such as the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS) or the Depression, Anxiety, and Stress Scale (DASS-21) provide structured ways to quantify a patient's subjective stress level.

Assessment should explore recent life changes, sleep patterns, coping methods, and support systems. A thorough physical assessment must check for stress-related signs such as bruxism, muscle stiffness, and elevated baseline vitals. Establishing a trusting, non-judgmental nurse-patient relationship is essential, as cultural stigma around mental health in many communities may cause patients to mask psychological distress behind physical symptoms.

Nursing Management and Coping Strategies

Nursing management of stress focuses on promoting adaptive coping strategies, reducing environmental triggers, and restoring physiological balance. Coping is generally divided into problem-focused coping (addressing the root cause of stress, such as time management or conflict resolution) and emotion-focused coping (managing the emotional response, such as through mindfulness, deep breathing, or journaling).

Practical interventions include teaching progressive muscle relaxation, encouraging regular physical exercise, promoting healthy sleep hygiene, and connecting patients with community or spiritual support systems. Nurses also act as advocates by helping patients prioritize tasks and set realistic boundaries to prevent burnout and foster long-term resilience.
