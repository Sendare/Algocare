Primary Nursing

Introduction to Primary Nursing

Primary nursing is a total patient care delivery model where one registered nurse assumes 24-hour responsibility for planning, directing, and evaluating the care of a specific group of patients from admission right up to discharge. Developed in the early 1970s, this model shifts the focus away from task-oriented nursing—where different staff members handle scattered chores like medication administration, vital signs, or bed baths—toward a holistic, relationship-based approach. The primary nurse builds a direct therapeutic alliance with the patient and their family, ensuring seamless continuity of care. Even when the primary nurse goes off duty, associate nurses follow the customized care plan crafted by the primary nurse, maintaining consistency across shifts.

Core Principles of Primary Nursing

Four foundational pillars uphold the structure of primary nursing:

  • Decentralized Decision-Making: Authority and accountability for clinical choices rest directly at the bedside with the nurse caring for the patient, bypassing layers of bureaucratic middle management.
  • 24-Hour Accountability: The primary nurse remains responsible for the patient's comprehensive care outcomes around the clock, even when absent, by utilizing clear care plans executed by associate nurses.
  • Direct Communication: Communication bypasses complex chains of command, flowing openly between the primary nurse, patient, family, and multidisciplinary team members.
  • All-RN Staffing: The model works best when care is delivered exclusively by registered nurses who possess the advanced critical thinking required to independently design and manage complex care plans.

Roles and Responsibilities of the Primary Nurse

The primary nurse wears multiple hats to ensure optimal patient recovery. Upon admission, the primary nurse conducts a comprehensive head-to-toe assessment and formulates the individualized nursing care plan. Throughout the hospital stay, this nurse coordinates diagnostic tests, collaborates with doctors and physiotherapists, and directly administers complex treatments. Advocacy is a daily duty, protecting patient rights and ensuring informed consent. Furthermore, discharge planning begins on day one; the primary nurse prepares the patient and family for home care, coordinating follow-up visits and teaching warning signs to watch for after leaving the facility.

Comparing Nursing Care Delivery Models

Healthcare facilities use several models to organize nursing staff. Understanding how primary nursing compares to functional and team nursing highlights its unique strengths and resource demands.

Advantages and Challenges in Patient Outcomes

Primary nursing yields notable benefits for both patients and healthcare providers, but it is not without hurdles. When one nurse takes charge, patients report feeling safer, more valued, and better informed because they deal with a consistent face instead of a revolving door of strangers. Nurses also experience greater job satisfaction, feeling genuine professional pride and autonomy over their clinical practice. However, challenges persist. Burnout can be high due to continuous emotional and mental investment in complex cases. Furthermore, staff shortages quickly strain the system; if there are not enough registered nurses to maintain primary assignments, the unit may be forced to revert temporarily to fragmented task-based care.

Application within the Nigerian Healthcare System

Implementing primary nursing in Nigerian hospitals presents a unique mix of opportunities and systemic roadblocks. Nigeria's healthcare facilities often face severe shortages of registered nurses, high nurse-to-patient ratios, and an over-reliance on task-oriented or team methods due to resource constraints. Many tertiary and private hospitals utilize modified care delivery models where primary nursing is adapted by assigning specific ward sections to individual nurses during a shift, though true 24-hour accountability remains rare. Overcoming these hurdles requires institutional commitment, task-shifting policy reforms, improved nursing remuneration to curb brain drain, and ongoing professional development to empower nurses to practice with full clinical autonomy.
