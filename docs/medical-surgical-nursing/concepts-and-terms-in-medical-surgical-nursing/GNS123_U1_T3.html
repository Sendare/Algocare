Primary Nursing

Introduction and Core Principles of Primary Nursing

Primary nursing is a total patient care delivery model designed to fix the fragmentation often found in shift-by-shift hospital routines. In this system, one registered nurse assumes twenty-four-hour responsibility for planning, directing, and evaluating the care of a specific caseload of patients from admission right through to discharge. Unlike functional nursing where tasks are broken up and handed out to different workers, primary nursing centers on a single point of accountability.

The core philosophy rests on continuity of care, direct accountability, and autonomous decision-making. The primary nurse establishes a therapeutic, trusting relationship with the patient and their family. When that primary nurse is off duty, an associated nurse follows the established care plan to maintain consistency. This model bridges the gap between fragmented ward tasks and holistic nursing practice.

Roles and Responsibilities of the Primary Nurse

Operating as a primary nurse demands a high level of clinical competence and leadership. The responsibilities stretch far beyond basic bedside tasks:

  • Comprehensive Assessment: Performing the initial admission database collection and identifying complex physical, psychological, and social needs.
  • Care Plan Formulation: Developing a personalized nursing care plan that addresses specific patient diagnoses and recovery goals.
  • Direct Care Delivery: Providing hands-on nursing interventions during scheduled shifts, modeling expert clinical judgment for junior colleagues.
  • Collaborative Communication: Acting as the central liaison between the patient, attending physicians, physiotherapists, and family members.
  • Discharge Coordination: Planning for continuity of care post-discharge, including medication education and community follow-up referrals.

Advantages and Challenges in Patient Outcomes

Primary nursing yields notable benefits for both recipients of care and the nursing workforce, though operational hurdles exist.

DimensionAdvantagesChallenges
Patient CareEnhanced trust, fewer communication errors, faster recovery recognitionInconsistent care if associate nurses fail to follow the care plan
Nurse SatisfactionHigher autonomy, professional pride, deep clinical engagementBurnout from high emotional involvement and round-the-clock worry
AdministrationClear accountability, reduced hospital stay duration, better safety auditsRequires an all-registered-nurse staff mix, which increases staffing costs

Relevance and Implementation in the Nigerian Healthcare System

Implementing primary nursing within Nigerian hospitals involves navigating distinct local realities. Many tertiary, secondary, and private health facilities operate under mixed staffing models where functional nursing or team methods predominate due to severe workforce shortages and high patient-to-nurse ratios.

Transitioning toward primary nursing in Nigerian wards requires administrative restructuring and a shift in ward culture. When nurse managers assign specific patient caseloads instead of general chore lists, accountability rises. Patients and relatives frequently express relief when they can identify 'their' specific nurse among the rotating shifts. However, chronic staff shortages, heavy workloads, and a high brain drain of registered nurses abroad can strain the feasibility of maintaining a true 24-hour primary nursing framework.
