Physical Assessment in Metabolic and Endocrine Disorders

Introduction to Endocrine Physical Assessment

The endocrine system regulates vital body functions through hormone secretion. Physical assessment of this system relies heavily on inspection and palpation, as many glands are not easily accessible for direct examination. Nurses must observe subtle external changes because hormonal imbalances develop slowly over time. Clinical findings rarely appear in isolation. A systematic approach ensures that even minor integumentary or metabolic shifts are not missed during a routine client evaluation.

General Appearance and Vital Signs

Client observation begins the moment the nurse enters the room. Note body habitus, posture, and facial expression. Vital signs provide immediate clues regarding metabolic rate:

  • Temperature: Heat intolerance often points to hyperthyroidism, whereas cold intolerance suggests hypothyroidism.
  • Blood Pressure: Widened pulse pressure can accompany hyperthyroidism, while hypertension is frequently linked to Cushing syndrome.
  • Heart Rate: Tachycardia correlates with excess thyroid hormone, and bradycardia often signals a sluggish metabolism.
  • Weight: Unexplained rapid weight loss despite increased appetite strongly suggests diabetes mellitus or hyperthyroidism.

Skin, Hair, and Nail Examination

Integumentary changes reflect systemic hormonal shifts vividly. Inspect skin texture, moisture, and pigmentation.

  • Hypothyroidism: Skin is dry, rough, and cold; hair becomes coarse and brittle.
  • Hyperthyroidism: Skin is warm, smooth, and moist; hair is fine and soft.
  • Cushing Syndrome: Skin appears thin with purple striae and easy bruising due to excess cortisol.
  • Addison Disease: Generalized hyperpigmentation occurs, especially in sun-exposed areas and skin creases, caused by high melanocyte-stimulating hormone.

Palpate skin turgor to assess hydration status, which is frequently altered in uncontrolled diabetes due to osmotic diuresis.

Head, Eyes, and Neck Assessment

Facial features coarsen in growth hormone excesses like acromegaly, marked by prominent jaw and brow lines. Examine the eyes carefully for exophthalmos, a forward protrusion of the eyeballs common in Graves disease.

For the neck examination, position the client sitting upright with the neck slightly flexed to relax muscles. Inspect the anterior lower neck for visible enlargement of the thyroid gland, known as a goiter. Palpate the thyroid using either a posterior or anterior approach. Feel for symmetry, nodularity, and smooth texture as the client swallows a sip of water, which causes the gland to rise.

Extremities, Musculoskeletal, and Neurological Signs

Endocrine imbalances profoundly affect the neuromuscular system. Inspect the hands and feet for disproportionate growth or localized swelling. Peripheral neuropathy in diabetes alters sensation, putting feet at risk for unnoticed trauma. Test deep tendon reflexes, noting that hyporeflexia often accompanies hypothyroidism, whereas hyperreflexia points toward hyperthyroidism.

Assess muscle strength in proximal muscle groups, as steroid excess or thyroid disorders commonly cause distinct myopathies. Check for tremors by asking the client to hold their hands out straight with fingers spread apart, a classic test for hyperthyroidism.

Documentation and Abnormal Findings Synthesis

Accurate documentation requires precise descriptive language rather than vague terms. Record gland size in centimeters, skin texture changes, specific vital sign deviations, and exact reflex grades. Correlate physical findings with laboratory data, such as blood glucose logs or thyroid panel results, to present a complete clinical picture. Identifying clustering symptoms allows the multidisciplinary team to intervene promptly before severe metabolic crises occur.
