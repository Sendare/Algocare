Physical Assessment in Metabolic and Endocrine Disorders

Introduction to Endocrine Physical Assessment

The endocrine system regulates vital body functions through hormone secretion from glands like the thyroid, pituitary, and adrenals. When these glands malfunction, widespread physical changes occur across multiple body systems. Because hormones influence everything from metabolism to fluid balance, physical assessment requires a systematic head-to-toe approach. Nurses must connect subtle exterior signs, such as skin texture or changes in voice, to internal hormonal imbalances. Recognizing these early patterns helps prevent severe complications like myxedema coma or thyroid storm.

Preparation and General Inspection

Before touching the client, general inspection offers immediate clues about metabolic status. Observe the client's overall build, posture, facial expression, and level of consciousness. Notice any obvious weight changes, truncal obesity, or muscle wasting. Room temperature should be comfortable, yet some clients may sweat profusely or shiver violently depending on their metabolic rate. A client who appears sluggish and apathetic may indicate hypothyroidism, whereas restlessness and fine tremors often point toward hyperthyroidism.

Skin, Hair, and Nail Assessment

Hormones exert a profound influence on integumentary structures, making the skin, hair, and nails rich sources of clinical data.

  • Skin Texture and Moisture: Hyperthyroidism causes warm, moist, and smooth skin due to increased blood flow. Conversely, hypothyroidism results in dry, coarse, and cold skin.
  • Pigmentation: Addison's disease causes hyperpigmentation, especially in sun-exposed areas and pressure points, due to excess melanocyte-stimulating hormone.
  • Hair Distribution: Cushing's syndrome can cause hirsutism, which is abnormal facial and body hair growth in women. Hypothyroidism often leads to thinning hair or loss of the outer third of the eyebrows.
  • Nail Changes: Brittle, thin, or slow-growing nails frequently accompany chronic metabolic slowdowns.

Head, Eyes, and Neck Examination

Inspection of the head and neck focuses heavily on the thyroid gland and facial structure.

  • Facial Features: Acromegaly presents with coarse facial features, a prominent jaw, and enlarged tongue due to excess growth hormone in adulthood. Exophthalmos, or bulging eyes, is a classic sign of Graves' disease.
  • Thyroid Palpation: Stand behind the client, gently displace the trachea to one side, and ask the client to swallow while you feel for the thyroid lobes. A normal thyroid is barely palpable; enlargement is termed a goiter.
  • Auscultation: If the thyroid is enlarged, listen with the bell of the stethoscope for a bruit, which indicates turbulent blood flow from hypervascularity.

Cardiovascular and Vital Signs Evaluation

Vital signs reflect how metabolic shifts strain the heart. Hyperthyroidism increases the metabolic demand, leading to tachycardia, a bounding pulse, and widened pulse pressure. Hypothyroidism does the opposite, often presenting with bradycardia, distant heart sounds, and hypertension. Blood pressure changes are also critical in adrenal disorders; Addison's disease causes severe hypotension due to sodium and water loss, whereas Cushing's syndrome causes hypertension due to sodium retention.

Abdomen and Musculoskeletal Assessment

Abdominal and musculoskeletal checks reveal clues about fat distribution, fluid balance, and muscle strength. Inspect the abdomen for purple striae, which indicate cortisol excess in Cushing's syndrome. Palpate for tenderness or organ enlargement, keeping in mind that metabolic disorders can alter gastrointestinal motility. Assess muscle strength in the proximal limbs; endocrine myopathies often cause noticeable weakness when a client attempts to rise from a deep chair or lift their arms overhead.

Neurological and Reflex Examination

Neurological evaluations assess mental status, sensory function, and deep tendon reflexes. Hypothyroidism often slows cognitive processing, memory, and speech. Conversely, hyperthyroidism causes irritability, anxiety, and hyperactive reflexes. Calcium regulation by the parathyroid glands directly impacts nerve excitability. Low calcium levels lead to tetany, which can be tested using Chvostek's sign by tapping the facial nerve, or Trousseau's sign by inflating a blood pressure cuff on the arm.

Documentation and Diagnostic Correlation

Accurate documentation bridges physical findings with laboratory and imaging results. Record objective physical details using precise language rather than vague terms. Correlate your observations with diagnostic data such as TSH, free T4, serum cortisol, and blood glucose levels. Recognizing patterns among physical signs and lab values enables the healthcare team to confirm diagnoses quickly and evaluate how well current hormone replacement or suppression therapy is working.
