Physical Assessment in Metabolic and Endocrine Disorders

Introduction to Endocrine Physical Assessment

The endocrine system regulates homeostasis through hormone secretion, and physical assessment serves as a non-invasive window into these complex regulatory loops. Hormonal imbalances often manifest subtly over months, requiring systematic observation rather than acute detection. When evaluating a client with suspected metabolic dysfunction, the nurse looks for structural and functional changes driven by excess or deficient hormone levels. Unlike systems with isolated localized signs, endocrine pathology typically produces multisystem alterations. Recognizing these patterns early prevents severe complications such as thyrotoxic crisis or myxedema coma. Physical examination relies heavily on inspection, palpation, and targeted questioning to correlate visible stigmata with underlying glandular dysfunction.

Preparation and General Inspection of the Client

Before touching the client, a thorough general survey provides critical clues regarding metabolic status. Observe overall body build, posture, and nutritional state from the moment the client enters the room. Note whether body proportions suggest acromegaly or Cushing syndrome, such as truncal obesity paired with thin extremities. Assess the client's apparent age versus chronological age, as chronic endocrine disease frequently alters physical maturation and vitality. Evaluate affect, level of consciousness, and psychomotor activity. Agitation, tremors, and restlessness point toward hypermetabolic states like hyperthyroidism, whereas lethargy, slowed speech, and apathy strongly suggest hypothyroidism or severe electrolyte disturbances.

Assessment of the Skin, Hair, and Nails

Integumentary changes reflect alterations in tissue perfusion, hydration, and cellular metabolism driven by endocrine control.

  • Skin texture and moisture: Hypothyroidism produces dry, coarse, cold skin due to reduced sweat and sebaceous gland activity. Conversely, hyperthyroidism results in warm, smooth, moist skin driven by increased thermogenesis and vasodilation.
  • Pigmentation: Addison disease causes generalized hyperpigmentation, especially over pressure points and sun-exposed areas, due to high levels of melanocyte-stimulating hormone associated with elevated ACTH.
  • Hair distribution: Hypothyroidism leads to thinning scalp hair and loss of the lateral third of the eyebrows. Hyperandrogenism or Cushing syndrome can cause hirsutism in female clients.
  • Nail changes: Brittle, thin, or ridged nails often accompany chronic nutritional and metabolic disturbances.

Examination of the Head, Facial Features, and Neck

Craniofacial inspection reveals structural shifts caused by growth hormone or thyroid abnormalities. Facies can be pathognomonic: coarse facial features, prominent supraorbital ridges, and a large tongue point directly to acromegaly. Examine the eyes for exophthalmos, lid lag, and stare, which are classic ocular signs of Graves disease.

When examining the neck, inspect the thyroid gland with the client's neck slightly extended and swallowing a sip of water. A normal thyroid may not be visible, but enlargement or nodules require palpation. Stand behind the client, use your fingers to displace the trachea gently, and palpate each lobe as the client swallows. Auscultate the thyroid lobes with the bell of the stethoscope; the presence of a bruit indicates hypervascularity associated with thyrotoxicosis.

Evaluation of the Cardiovascular System and Vital Signs

Endocrine disorders profoundly alter hemodynamic stability. Thyroid hormones directly impact myocardial contractility and peripheral vascular resistance. Consequently, hyperthyroidism frequently presents with tachycardia, widened pulse pressure, and bounding pulses, whereas hypothyroidism manifests as bradycardia, distant heart sounds, and narrow pulse pressure. Blood pressure readings are equally critical; sudden hypertensive crises can signal catecholamine-secreting tumors like pheochromocytoma. Temperature regulation is another vital indicator. Heat intolerance and low-grade fevers accompany hypermetabolic states, whereas hypothermia is a dangerous sign in advanced hypothyroidism or myxedema.

Abdominal and Musculoskeletal Assessment

Abdominal inspection can reveal central adiposity, purple striae from collagen breakdown in Cushing syndrome, or hepatomegaly secondary to metabolic syndrome. In clients with diabetes mellitus, assess for gastrointestinal autonomic neuropathy by inquiring about early satiety, nausea, or erratic glycemic control due to gastroparesis.

Musculoskeletal evaluation focuses on muscle strength, tone, and skeletal integrity. Hyperthyroidism often causes proximal muscle weakness, making it difficult for the client to rise from a squatting position. Conversely, hypocalcemia secondary to hypoparathyroidism can trigger tetany, manifested by carpopedal spasms when testing for Trousseau sign or facial twitching during Chvostek testing.

Neurological and Reflex Examination

Neurological screening evaluates cortical function, cranial nerves, sensory status, and deep tendon reflexes, all of which are sensitive to metabolic shifts. Deep tendon reflexes provide a classic discriminatory tool: hyperactive reflexes characterize hyperthyroidism, while sluggish, delayed relaxation of reflexes (especially the Achilles tendon reflex) is a hallmark of hypothyroidism. Peripheral neuropathy is common in chronic diabetes mellitus, presenting as symmetric distal sensory loss, burning pain, or loss of protective sensation tested with a monofilament. Mental status changes ranging from mild anxiety and emotional lability to profound confusion or coma must be documented immediately.

Documentation and Specialized Diagnostic Correlation

Accurate documentation bridges physical findings with laboratory and imaging results. Record all observations objectively, noting specific measurements like goiter dimensions or waist-to-hip ratios rather than vague descriptions. Correlate physical signs with diagnostic studies such as thyroid-stimulating hormone panels, fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, serum electrolytes, and cortisol assays. Recognizing discrepancies between clinical presentation and lab values ensures safe, comprehensive nursing care. For example, a client with normal lab results but profound physical signs of distress warrants immediate escalation and interprofessional communication.
