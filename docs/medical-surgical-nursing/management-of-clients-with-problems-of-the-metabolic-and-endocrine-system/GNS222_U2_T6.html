Disorders of the Gallbladder

Anatomy, Physiology, and Pathophysiology of the Gallbladder

The gallbladder is a small, pear-shaped organ situated right beneath the liver. Its primary job is to store and concentrate bile, a fluid produced by the liver that helps digest fats. When you eat a meal containing fat, the stomach and small intestine release hormones that signal the gallbladder to contract. This squeezing pushes bile through the cystic duct and into the common bile duct, eventually reaching the duodenum to break down dietary fats.

Most gallbladder disease starts when the normal flow of bile is disrupted. Bile is made of water, cholesterol, bile salts, and bilirubin. If the chemical balance of bile changes, or if the gallbladder does not empty completely, solid particles begin to form. These particles can grow into gallstones, also known as cholelithiasis. Once stones form, they can block the ducts, causing inflammation, pressure buildup, and severe pain.

Types of Gallbladder Disorders and Risk Factors

Gallbladder disorders encompass several conditions ranging from simple stone formation to severe inflammation and infection.

  • Cholelithiasis: The presence of gallstones in the gallbladder, which may remain silent or cause intermittent pain.
  • Cholecystitis: Acute or chronic inflammation of the gallbladder wall, almost always triggered by a gallstone blocking the cystic duct.
  • Choledocholithiasis: A condition where gallstones slip out of the gallbladder and lodge directly inside the common bile duct.
  • Biliary Dyskinesia: A functional disorder where the gallbladder fails to squeeze properly despite the absence of physical stones.

Certain factors increase a person's chances of developing these problems. The classic clinical profile is often remembered by traditional medical mnemonics focusing on middle-aged individuals. Key risk factors include:

  1. Female gender: Estrogen increases cholesterol excretion in bile.
  2. High-fat diet and obesity: Promotes supersaturation of cholesterol.
  3. Rapid weight loss: Causes the liver to release extra cholesterol into bile while the gallbladder empties poorly.
  4. Age: Being over forty years old.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

The classic presentation of a gallbladder attack is biliary colic, a steady, severe ache in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen that often radiates to the right shoulder or back. This pain typically starts suddenly after eating a greasy or fatty meal and lasts for several hours before fading away.

When assessing a patient with suspected cholecystitis, nurses look for specific physical signs:

  • Murphy's sign: The patient stops breathing in halfway when the examiner presses deeply on the right upper quadrant under the liver edge, due to sharp pain as the inflamed gallbladder touches the fingers.
  • Fever and chills: Suggest an active infection or inflammation.
  • Jaundice: A yellowing of the skin and eyes, which occurs if a stone blocks the common bile duct and stops bilirubin from draining into the gut.
  • Nausea and vomiting: Common responses to severe visceral pain and fat maldigestion.

Diagnostic Investigations

Confirming a gallbladder disorder requires a combination of blood work and imaging studies.

Laboratory tests help reveal inflammation and organ strain. White blood cell counts rise during acute infection. Liver function tests, including bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, and aminotransferases, often elevate if stones are obstructing the biliary tree.

Imaging provides direct visualization of the organ:

  • Abdominal Ultrasound: The gold standard test. It is painless, uses no radiation, and easily visualizes gallstones, gallbladder wall thickening, and fluid surrounding the organ.
  • HIDA Scan: A nuclear medicine test where a radioactive tracer is injected to track the production and flow of bile through the biliary system, checking if the gallbladder empties properly.
  • CT Scan: Used mainly to look for complications like abscesses, perforation, or inflammation of surrounding organs like the pancreas.

Medical, Dietary, and Surgical Management

Treatment depends on whether the condition is an acute emergency or a chronic, stable issue. For sudden attacks, patients are often placed on bowel rest with nil per os (NPO) status, given intravenous fluids, and administered pain medications and antibiotics.

Dietary changes play a major role in managing symptoms before definitive treatment. Patients are advised to follow a low-fat diet to minimize the release of cholecystokinin, the hormone that causes the gallbladder to contract and trigger pain. Smaller, frequent meals are better tolerated than large, heavy dishes.

When gallstones cause repeated symptoms, the definitive treatment is surgical removal of the gallbladder, known as a cholecystectomy.

  • Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy: The preferred, minimally invasive approach using several small abdominal incisions. Recovery is fast and postoperative pain is relatively low.
  • Open Cholecystectomy: A traditional approach requiring a larger incision under the right ribs, reserved for complicated cases with severe scarring, anatomical abnormalities, or suspected bile duct injury.

Nursing Care, Complications, and Patient Education

Postoperative nursing care focuses on monitoring recovery and watching for dangerous complications. Nurses must routinely assess vital signs, inspect surgical dressings for bleeding or bile leakage, and manage pain. Encouraging early ambulation and deep breathing exercises prevents pulmonary complications like atelectasis after laparoscopic surgery.

A common, minor postoperative symptom is referred shoulder pain, which happens because residual carbon dioxide gas used during laparoscopy irritates the phrenic nerve. Walking and heating pads help relieve this discomfort.

Serious complications of untreated or severe gallbladder disease include gangrene, perforation, and peritonitis, which occur if the organ wall dies and leaks infected bile into the abdominal cavity. Patients must be taught to report warning signs such as persistent fever, worsening abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, or yellowing skin before discharge.
