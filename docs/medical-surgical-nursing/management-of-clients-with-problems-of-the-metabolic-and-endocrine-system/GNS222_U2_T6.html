Disorders of the Gallbladder

Anatomy and Physiology of the Gallbladder

The gallbladder is a small, pear-shaped muscular sac situated in the fossa on the visceral surface of the right lobe of the liver. It typically stores and concentrates about 30 to 50 milliliters of bile produced by hepatocytes. Bile consists of water, bile salts, cholesterol, bilirubin, and electrolytes, all essential for the emulsification and absorption of dietary fats in the small intestine.

When chyme containing fats and amino acids enters the duodenum from the stomach, the intestinal mucosa releases the hormone cholecystokinin. This hormone stimulates the contraction of the gallbladder's smooth muscle wall and simultaneously relaxes the sphincter of Oddi, allowing concentrated bile to flow through the cystic duct and common bile duct directly into the duodenum. Without a functioning gallbladder, bile drips continuously into the intestinal tract rather than releasing in concentrated spurts during fat digestion.

Pathophysiology and Types of Gallbladder Disorders

Gallbladder disorders encompass several distinct pathological conditions that disrupt normal bile storage and flow. The two most clinically significant categories are cholecystitis (inflammation of the gallbladder wall) and cholelithiasis (presence of gallstones).

  • Calculous Cholecystitis: Acute inflammation triggered by a gallstone obstructing the cystic duct, causing bile stasis, increased intraluminal pressure, ischemia, and secondary bacterial infection.
  • Acalculous Cholecystitis: Acute inflammation occurring without gallstones, typically associated with severe trauma, prolonged fasting, critical illness, or total parenteral nutrition, driven by bile stasis and gallbladder ischemia.
  • Cholelithiasis: Formation of stones within the gallbladder, categorized primarily into cholesterol stones (formed from supersaturated hepatic bile) and pigment stones (composed of calcium bilirubinate).
  • Choledocholithiasis: The presence of migrated gallstones within the common bile duct, which can obstruct the flow of both bile and pancreatic secretions, leading to jaundice or pancreatitis.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Understanding the risk factors for gallstone formation helps clinicians identify vulnerable populations, especially among middle-aged adults. Traditionally remembered through clinical mnemonics like the 'female, forty, fertile, and fat' profile, gallstone pathophysiology involves an imbalance in the chemical composition of bile, leading to precipitation of cholesterol or bilirubin.

  • Demographics and Genetics: Higher prevalence among females due to estrogen enhancing hepatic cholesterol secretion. Native American and Hispanic populations also show high genetic predisposition.
  • Metabolic Factors: Rapid weight loss, strict fasting, and obesity promote biliary stasis and increased cholesterol saturation.
  • Medical Conditions: Cirrhosis, Crohn's disease, and chronic hemolysis elevate the risk of pigment stone formation due to altered bilirubin metabolism.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Many individuals with gallstones remain entirely asymptomatic. However, when a stone intermittently blocks the cystic duct or common bile duct, patients experience acute pain known as biliary colic. This pain typically presents as severe, steady aching in the epigastric region or right upper quadrant, often radiating to the right scapula or shoulder.

Physical assessment relies heavily on eliciting specific pain triggers. Murphy's sign is a classic physical exam finding for acute cholecystitis: the examiner presses fingers gently beneath the right costal margin while the patient takes a deep breath. A positive response occurs when the patient abruptly halts inspiration due to sharp pain as the inflamed gallbladder descends and touches the examiner's fingers. Nausea, vomiting, and a low-grade fever frequently accompany these episodes. If a stone obstructs the common bile duct, outflow of bilirubin is blocked, resulting in jaundice, dark amber urine, and pale, clay-colored stools.

Diagnostic Investigations

Accurate diagnosis of gallbladder disease combines laboratory blood work with non-invasive and invasive imaging modalities.

  • Abdominal Ultrasonography: The gold standard initial imaging tool. It visualizes gallstones as echogenic foci with posterior acoustic shadowing and can detect gallbladder wall thickening and pericholecystic fluid.
  • Laboratory Panels: Complete blood count often reveals leukocytosis in acute cholecystitis. Liver function tests (bilirubin, AST, ALT, alkaline phosphatase, and GGT) help evaluate for biliary tract obstruction.
  • HIDA Scan (Cholescintigraphy): Injects a radioactive tracer to evaluate the patency of the cystic duct and functional ejection fraction of the gallbladder when ultrasound findings are equivocal.
  • ERCP (Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography): Combines endoscopy and fluoroscopy to visualize the biliary tree directly and therapeutically remove stones lodged in the common bile duct.

Medical and Dietary Management

Initial management for acute flare-ups focuses on resting the gastrointestinal tract and providing supportive care. Patients are typically kept nil per os (NPO) during acute pain episodes to minimize gallbladder stimulation, and a nasogastric tube may be inserted if severe nausea and vomiting are present.

Pharmacological management includes intravenous hydration, correction of electrolyte imbalances, administration of antiemetics, and prompt pain control with analgesics such as NSAIDs or opioids. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics are initiated if secondary bacterial infection or systemic inflammation is suspected.

Dietary counseling is vital once acute symptoms resolve. Patients are advised to follow a low-fat diet to prevent the release of cholecystokinin, which stimulates painful gallbladder contractions. Small, frequent meals are better tolerated than large, heavy meals. Oral dissolution therapy using ursodeoxycholic acid is occasionally utilized for small cholesterol stones in patients who are poor surgical candidates, though recurrence rates remain high.

Surgical Interventions and Nursing Care

Definitive treatment for symptomatic gallstone disease is the surgical removal of the gallbladder, known as a cholecystectomy.

  • Laparoscopic Cholecystectomy: The current gold standard approach. Carbon dioxide is used to insufflate the abdomen, and specialized instruments remove the gallbladder through tiny puncture incisions. This technique offers shorter hospital stays, less postoperative pain, and faster recovery.
  • Open Cholecystectomy: Performed via a right subcostal incision when severe inflammation, extensive scarring, or anatomical complications preclude the laparoscopic approach.

Postoperative nursing care involves monitoring vital signs, assessing surgical incision sites for bleeding or infection, managing incisional pain, and encouraging early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary complications. For patients with a T-tube in the common bile duct following open exploration, nurses must monitor and record the color, consistency, and volume of drainage daily.

Complications and Patient Education

Untreated or severe gallbladder disease can lead to life-threatening systemic complications. Empyema occurs when a chronically obstructed, inflamed gallbladder fills with purulent exudate. Gangrene and perforation may ensue if ischemia compromises the integrity of the gallbladder wall, leading to localized peritonitis or generalized sepsis. Furthermore, obstruction of the pancreatic duct by a passing gallstone frequently causes acute biliary pancreatitis.

Comprehensive patient education focuses on recognizing warning signs that necessitate immediate medical evaluation, such as high fever, persistent vomiting, worsening abdominal pain, or the development of jaundice. Nurses should also instruct patients on proper incision care, gradual resumption of dietary fats as tolerated, and the importance of attending follow-up appointments.
