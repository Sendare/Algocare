Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction and Classification of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder marked by high blood glucose levels resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Insulin is a vital hormone produced by the beta cells of the pancreas that allows glucose from the bloodstream to enter body cells for energy. When this process fails, glucose accumulates in the blood, leading to hyperglycemia.

The condition is broadly categorized into distinct types:

  • Type 1 Diabetes: An autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells resulting in absolute insulin deficiency, typically diagnosed in younger individuals.
  • Type 2 Diabetes: Characterized by progressive insulin resistance combined with a relative decrease in insulin secretion, strongly linked to lifestyle and genetics.
  • Gestational Diabetes: Glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy, which usually resolves after delivery but increases future risk for type 2 diabetes.
  • Other Specific Types: Diabetes resulting from genetic defects, medications, or exocrine pancreatic diseases like cystic fibrosis.

Pathophysiology and Metabolic Changes

Without sufficient insulin or effective cellular response, glucose cannot enter peripheral tissues such as skeletal muscle and adipose tissue. Sensing starvation despite high circulating blood sugar, the body initiates alternative metabolic pathways.

Glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis accelerate in the liver, dumping more glucose into an already overloaded bloodstream. Concurrently, the lack of intracellular glucose triggers lipolysis—the breakdown of stored fats into free fatty acids. The liver converts these fatty acids into ketone bodies, which are acidic byproducts that can rapidly lower blood pH if allowed to accumulate unchecked. This cascade explains why disturbances in carbohydrate, protein, and fat metabolism all stem from a single primary defect in insulin availability or action.

Risk Factors and Etiology

The development of diabetes depends heavily on a combination of genetic predisposition and environmental triggers. Type 1 diabetes is strongly influenced by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) genetic markers and environmental factors such as viral infections that may trigger an autoimmune response.

Type 2 diabetes shares a strong genetic link but is heavily accelerated by modifiable lifestyle factors. Key risk factors include:

  • Obesity: Excess visceral fat increases systemic insulin resistance.
  • Physical Inactivity: Lack of exercise reduces cellular glucose uptake.
  • Age: Risk increases steadily after age forty-five.
  • Ethnicity: Higher prevalence occurs in Black, Hispanic, Asian, and Indigenous populations.
  • Family History: Having a first-degree relative with type 2 diabetes significantly raises personal risk.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

The classic clinical presentation of uncontrolled diabetes is defined by three hallmark signs: polyuria (excessive urination), polydipsia (excessive thirst), and polyphagia (excessive hunger).

Polyuria occurs when blood glucose exceeds the renal threshold, pulling water into the urine through osmotic diuresis. Polydipsia is the body's compensatory mechanism to combat the severe fluid loss from polyuria. Polyphagia develops because cells are starving for glucose despite high circulating blood levels, signaling the brain to demand more food. Additional assessment findings often include unexplained weight loss (especially in Type 1), blurred vision from osmotic fluid shifts in the eye lens, fatigue, and slow-healing skin infections.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnosing diabetes relies on standardized blood tests that measure circulating glucose or long-term glycemic control. A fasting plasma glucose (FPG) test of 126 mg/dL or higher on two separate occasions confirms the diagnosis. Alternatively, an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) reading of 200 mg/dL or higher at two hours confirms hyperglycemia.

The glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) test measures average blood glucose over the past two to three months by assessing glucose attached to hemoglobin inside red blood cells. An HbA1c level of 6.5% or higher is diagnostic for diabetes. A random plasma glucose reading of 200 mg/dL or higher accompanied by classic symptoms of hyperglycemia also establishes the diagnosis without needing a fasting test.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Managing diabetes requires a balanced approach combining medical nutrition therapy, regular physical activity, and pharmacological agents tailored to the specific type of diabetes.

For Type 1 diabetes, lifelong exogenous insulin replacement is mandatory. This is typically delivered via multiple daily injections or a continuous subcutaneous insulin pump. For Type 2 diabetes, treatment often begins with lifestyle modifications and the oral medication metformin, which decreases hepatic glucose production and improves insulin sensitivity. Additional oral agents and injectables, such as sulfonylureas, DPP-4 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors, may be added progressively if glycemic targets are not met.

Acute and Chronic Complications

Uncontrolled blood glucose leads to severe acute and chronic complications that affect multiple organ systems.

Acute emergencies include Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA), seen mostly in Type 1 diabetes due to severe insulin lack and ketoacidosis, and Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar State (HHS), seen mostly in Type 2 diabetes with severe dehydration and extreme high blood sugar without significant ketosis. Both are medical emergencies requiring fluid resuscitation and insulin therapy.

Chronic complications develop gradually from vascular damage caused by prolonged hyperglycemia:

  • Microvascular Complications: Retinopathy (eye damage), nephropathy (kidney damage), and neuropathy (nerve damage).
  • Macrovascular Complications: Coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, and peripheral vascular disease leading to poor wound healing and foot ulcers.

Nursing Care Plan and Client Education

Comprehensive nursing care focuses on monitoring glycemic control, preventing complications, and empowering clients through targeted education. Nurses must teach clients proper self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) techniques, insulin administration protocols, and recognition of hypoglycemia symptoms such as shakiness, sweating, and confusion.

Foot care education is another vital nursing priority. Clients must learn to inspect their feet daily for cuts or redness, wear well-fitting shoes, avoid walking barefoot, and keep feet clean and dry. In addition, nurses guide clients on sick-day management: never stopping insulin during illness, checking blood glucose frequently, and maintaining adequate fluid intake.
