Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction and Overview of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic, systemic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. As a major global and national health challenge in Nigeria, it places a heavy burden on healthcare facilities and requires comprehensive nursing care. Insulin, a vital hormone synthesized by the beta cells of the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas, plays a primary role in facilitating the uptake of glucose from the bloodstream into body cells for energy production and storage. When this delicate hormonal balance is disrupted, glucose accumulates in the blood, leading to widespread metabolic dysfunction. Chronic hyperglycemia damages various organ systems over time, particularly the blood vessels and nerves, predisposing patients to severe microvascular and macrovascular complications. As student nurses, understanding the foundational concepts of diabetes is crucial for early identification, effective management, and patient advocacy. Nurses serve on the front lines of healthcare delivery in Nigerian hospitals and community health centres, making it essential to recognize both the acute presentations and long-term consequences of uncontrolled blood glucose levels. Through structured education, diligent monitoring, and holistic support, nurses help bridge the gap between complex medical therapies and everyday client self-management, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and quality of life for individuals living with this lifelong condition.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Metabolic Changes

The pathophysiology of diabetes mellitus revolves around the body's inability to properly utilize glucose due to absolute or relative insulin deficiency. In Type 1 diabetes, an autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells results in little to no endogenous insulin production, forcing the body to rely entirely on exogenous insulin for survival. Conversely, Type 2 diabetes is characterized by insulin resistance—where peripheral target tissues such as skeletal muscle, liver, and adipose tissue fail to respond normally to circulating insulin—coupled with a progressive decline in insulin secretion. Because glucose cannot enter the cells efficiently, the body perceives a state of cellular starvation despite high blood sugar levels. This triggers counter-regulatory hormones like glucagon, cortisol, and epinephrine to stimulate glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver, further worsening the hyperglycemia. Additionally, without adequate intracellular glucose, the body shifts to alternative energy pathways, breaking down stored fats and proteins. The rapid breakdown of fats leads to the production of ketone bodies, which are acidic byproducts that can accumulate in the blood and cause diabetic ketoacidosis, particularly in clients with absolute insulin deficiency. Understanding these intricate metabolic alterations allows nursing students to anticipate clinical signs, interpret laboratory reports accurately, and deliver targeted interventions that protect clients from life-threatening metabolic crises.

Classification and Types of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is clinically classified into four main categories: Type 1 diabetes, Type 2 diabetes, gestational diabetes mellitus, and other specific types due to various causes. Type 1 diabetes typically develops during childhood or adolescence, presenting with a rapid onset of symptoms requiring lifelong insulin replacement therapy. Type 2 diabetes is the most prevalent form globally and locally in Nigeria, accounting for the vast majority of cases, and is strongly linked to lifestyle factors, aging, and genetic predisposition. Gestational diabetes mellitus is glucose intolerance recognized for the first time during pregnancy, posing risks to both the mother and the developing fetus, and necessitating careful dietary modification or pharmacological control. Other specific types include diabetes secondary to pancreatic diseases, endocrinopathies, drug or chemical-induced toxicities, and genetic defects of beta-cell function or insulin action. Each classification carries distinct diagnostic criteria, progression patterns, and therapeutic approaches that nursing professionals must master. Recognizing the specific type of diabetes a client has ensures that nursing interventions, nutritional counselling, and medication administration are tailored to the exact underlying etiology rather than a generalized treatment plan.

Etiology and Risk Factors

The etiology and risk factors for diabetes vary significantly between the different types of the disease. Type 1 diabetes is rooted in a combination of genetic susceptibility and environmental triggers, such as viral infections or toxins, that provoke an autoimmune response against pancreatic beta cells. In contrast, Type 2 diabetes is heavily influenced by modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors, including advanced age, family history of diabetes, ethnicity, obesity, physical inactivity, and poor dietary habits characterized by high refined sugar and fat intake. In the Nigerian context, rapid urbanization, adoption of westernized diets, and sedentary lifestyles have contributed to a rising prevalence of Type 2 diabetes across urban and rural populations. Gestational diabetes shares similar risk factors with Type 2 diabetes, alongside a history of previous macrosomia, advanced maternal age, and specific ethnic backgrounds. Additionally, certain medications such as chronic systemic corticosteroids, thiazide diuretics, and atypical antipsychotics can induce hyperglycemia and act as precipitating factors. Identifying these risk factors during client assessments enables nurses to implement primary prevention strategies, target high-risk groups for community screening, and educate the public on lifestyle modifications that can delay or prevent the onset of Type 2 diabetes.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

The clinical manifestations of diabetes mellitus are classically dominated by the three 'Ps': polyuria, polydipsia, and polyphagia. Polyuria, or excessive urination, occurs as high blood glucose levels exceed the renal threshold, leading to osmotic diuresis where glucose pulls water into the urine. Polydipsia, or excessive thirst, is a direct compensatory mechanism triggered by dehydration resulting from significant fluid loss through polyuria. Polyphagia, or excessive hunger, arises because cells are starved of glucose, prompting the brain to stimulate appetite despite high circulating blood sugar levels. Additional assessment findings commonly include unexplained weight loss (particularly in Type 1 diabetes due to fat and protein breakdown), chronic fatigue, blurred vision caused by osmotic swelling of the eye lens, and frequent infections such as slow-healing skin ulcers or recurrent candidiasis. In Nigerian clinical settings, patients often present late with advanced complications like peripheral neuropathy, characterized by numbness, tingling, or burning sensations in the lower extremities, or diabetic foot ulcers. Thorough nursing assessment involves systematically evaluating the client for these classic symptoms, inspecting the skin and feet, assessing hydration status, and taking a detailed history to gauge the duration and severity of hyperglycemia.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Investigations

Diagnosing diabetes mellitus relies on precise laboratory investigations and standardized blood glucose criteria established by health authorities. Key diagnostic tests include the fasting plasma glucose (FPG) test, the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), random plasma glucose sampling accompanied by classic symptoms of hyperglycemia, and the glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) test. A fasting plasma glucose level of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher, after a minimum fast of eight hours, is diagnostic for diabetes. An HbA1c test result of 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or higher reflects average blood glucose levels over the preceding two to three months and is widely used for diagnosis and long-term monitoring. A random plasma glucose level of 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher in a client exhibiting symptoms like polyuria and polydipsia also confirms the diagnosis. In addition to glucose-specific tests, nurses must monitor routine screening panels such as renal function tests, lipid profiles, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratios, and routine urinalysis to detect early target-organ damage. Understanding these investigations empowers nursing students to interpret diagnostic reports correctly, explain test procedures to anxious clients, and ensure appropriate fasting requirements are met prior to specimen collection.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Therapy

Medical management and pharmacological therapy for diabetes focus on normalizing blood glucose levels, preventing acute complications, and minimizing the progression of chronic vascular damage. The therapeutic regimen combines medical nutrition therapy (MNT), regular physical exercise, and pharmacological agents tailored to the specific type of diabetes. For Type 1 diabetes, exogenous insulin therapy administered via subcutaneous injections or continuous pumps is mandatory for survival, utilizing a combination of basal and prandial regimens. For Type 2 diabetes, management typically begins with lifestyle modifications and oral anti-diabetic medications, such as biguanides (e.g., metformin), which decrease hepatic glucose production and improve insulin sensitivity. Other oral agents include sulfonylureas, DPP-4 inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 receptor agonists, which may be used individually or in combination. If oral medications fail to maintain glycemic control over time, insulin therapy may also be introduced for clients with Type 2 diabetes. Nurses play an active role in administering these medications, teaching clients self-injection techniques, monitoring for adverse effects like hypoglycemia, and educating patients on the importance of adherence to their prescribed therapeutic regimen.

Nursing Care Plan and Client Education

Developing a comprehensive nursing care plan and delivering effective client education are pivotal for successful long-term diabetes management. Key nursing diagnoses for clients with diabetes include risk for unstable blood glucose level, deficient knowledge regarding self-care, risk for peripheral neurovascular dysfunction, and risk for infection. The nursing care plan involves monitoring blood glucose levels via glucometers, assessing dietary intake, tracking physical activity, and inspecting the skin and feet daily for cuts, bruises, or fungal infections. Client education must be individualized, culturally sensitive, and easy to understand, particularly within Nigerian communities where local dietary staples and traditional health beliefs must be respectfully integrated into the care plan. Nurses must teach clients how to perform self-monitoring of blood glucose, recognize the signs and symptoms of hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia, store insulin correctly, and care for their feet properly to prevent diabetic foot ulcers and amputations. Emphasizing regular follow-up appointments and encouraging family support ensures that the client is empowered to take an active role in managing their condition, thereby preventing avoidable hospital admissions.

Acute and Chronic Complications of Diabetes

Diabetes mellitus is associated with severe acute and chronic complications that threaten life and drastically impair functional capacity if left uncontrolled. Acute complications include diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state (HHS), and hypoglycemia. DKA is characterized by severe hyperglycemia, metabolic acidosis, and ketonuria, predominantly seen in Type 1 diabetes, while HHS features extreme hyperglycemia, severe dehydration, and high serum osmolality without significant ketosis, typically seen in Type 2 diabetes. Hypoglycemia, or low blood sugar, is an acute emergency caused by excess insulin, delayed meals, or strenuous exercise, presenting with tremors, sweating, confusion, and potential loss of consciousness. Chronic complications are divided into microvascular and macrovascular disorders resulting from prolonged vessel damage. Microvascular complications include diabetic retinopathy (leading cause of blindness), diabetic nephropathy (leading cause of end-stage renal disease), and diabetic neuropathy (causing nerve damage and loss of protective sensation). Macrovascular complications involve accelerated atherosclerosis, predisposing clients to coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular accidents, and peripheral arterial disease. For nursing students, vigilance in recognizing early signs of these complications is essential for timely emergency intervention and comprehensive client rehabilitation.
