Disorders of the Pancreas

Anatomy and Physiology of the Pancreas

The pancreas is a unique, elongated organ nestled behind the stomach in the upper left abdomen. It serves a dual purpose, functioning as both an exocrine and an endocrine gland.

  • Exocrine Function: Accounts for the vast majority of pancreatic tissue. It produces digestive enzymes like amylase, lipase, and proteolytic enzymes that travel through the pancreatic duct into the small intestine to break down carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.
  • Endocrine Function: Consists of specialized cell clusters called the islets of Langerhans. These cells secrete vital hormones directly into the bloodstream to regulate blood glucose levels, including insulin to lower sugar and glucagon to raise it.

Understanding this dual role is vital for nurses, because disease in the pancreas almost always disrupts either digestion, blood sugar balance, or both.

Acute Pancreatitis: Causes and Pathophysiology

Acute pancreatitis is a sudden inflammation of the organ that can range from a mild, self-limiting discomfort to a life-threatening systemic condition. The two leading triggers worldwide are gallstones, which block the common bile duct, and heavy alcohol abuse.

The underlying mechanism is premature enzyme activation. Normally, digestive enzymes are produced in an inactive form and only become active once they reach the small intestine. In acute pancreatitis, these enzymes activate *inside* the pancreas itself. They begin digesting the organ's own tissues, a process called autodigestion. This triggers intense local inflammation, swelling, bleeding, and sometimes tissue death, known as necrosis.

Clinical Manifestations of Acute Pancreatitis

The hallmark symptom of acute pancreatitis is severe, sudden abdominal pain. Patients typically describe it as a deep, piercing, constant pain located in the mid-epigastric region or left upper quadrant. Crucially, this pain frequently radiates straight through to the back and often worsens after eating or lying flat.

Physical findings reflect systemic distress. Nausea and persistent vomiting are almost universal, though throwing up rarely relieves the abdominal pain. Nurses should watch for low-grade fever, tachycardia, and low blood pressure due to fluid shifting out of the blood vessels. In severe hemorrhagic cases, bruising may appear around the umbilicus, known as Cullen's sign, or along the flanks, known as Grey Turner's sign.

Chronic Pancreatitis and Its Progression

Chronic pancreatitis is a progressive, irreversible destruction of the pancreatic tissue characterized by chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and loss of functional cells. Unlike the acute form, chronic damage leads to permanent structural changes. Long-term alcohol misuse remains the primary cause in adults, while genetic mutations or cystic fibrosis can trigger pediatric cases.

As healthy tissue is gradually replaced by scar tissue, two major problems develop:

  1. Malabsorption: Loss of exocrine cells means digestive enzymes are missing, leading to foul-smelling, fatty stools called steatorrhea and severe weight loss.
  2. Diabetes Mellitus: Destruction of the islets of Langerhans eventually destroys insulin production, resulting in high blood sugar.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosing pancreatic disorders requires a combination of blood tests, imaging studies, and clinical assessment.

  • Laboratory Tests: Serum amylase and lipase levels elevate sharply in acute pancreatitis. Lipase is more specific to the pancreas and stays elevated longer than amylase. Liver enzymes and bilirubin may rise if gallstones caused the block.
  • Imaging Studies: Abdominal ultrasound helps identify gallstones. A computed tomography (CT) scan with contrast is the gold standard for visualizing pancreatic swelling, necrosis, or fluid collections. Magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) provides detailed images of the pancreatic and bile ducts without invasive scopes.

Nursing Assessment and Management of Pancreatitis

Managing acute pancreatitis focuses on resting the pancreas, controlling pain, and maintaining fluid balance.

  1. Resting the Pancreas: Patients are often kept nil per os (NPO) during acute flare-ups to prevent stimulating enzyme release. Nasogastric suction may be used if severe vomiting is present.
  2. Fluid Resuscitation: Massive fluid shifts into the abdominal cavity can cause severe shock. Aggressive intravenous fluid replacement is critical to maintain blood pressure and organ perfusion.
  3. Pain Management: Administering prescribed IV opioids, such as hydromorphone or fentanyl, is essential to keep the patient comfortable.
  4. Nutritional Support: Once pain subsides and bowel sounds return, oral feeding starts slowly with clear liquids, advancing to a low-fat diet. For chronic pancreatitis, nurses must ensure patients take pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) with every meal.

Pancreatic Cancer: Presentation and Management

Pancreatic cancer, most commonly an adenocarcinoma, carries a poor prognosis because it often grows silently before causing noticeable symptoms. When symptoms do appear, they depend heavily on the tumor's location in the organ.

  • Tumors in the head of the pancreas press on the bile duct early, causing painless jaundice (yellowing of the skin and eyes), dark urine, and clay-colored stools.
  • Tumors in the body or tail cause vague upper abdominal pain that radiates to the back, accompanied by profound, unexplained weight loss and new-onset diabetes.

Treatment depends on staging. Surgical options include the Whipple procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy) for resectable tumors, where the head of the pancreas, duodenum, part of the bile duct, and gallbladder are removed. Advanced cases rely on palliative care, chemotherapy, and pain management.

Postoperative Nursing Care and Complications

Postoperative care following pancreatic surgery requires vigilant monitoring due to the complex nature of the procedure and surrounding vital structures. Nurses must watch closely for specific, high-risk complications:

  • Pancreatic Fistula: Leakage of pancreatic fluid from the surgical anastomosis site, which can erode nearby blood vessels and cause severe infection or bleeding.
  • Hemorrhage: Internal bleeding from surgical suture lines, monitored via vital signs, abdominal girth, and drainage output.
  • Hyperglycemia: Resulting from the sudden loss of functional pancreatic tissue, requiring regular blood glucose checks and insulin sliding scales.

Nurses should maintain strict patency of all surgical drains, track output characteristics meticulously, and assist with early mobilization to prevent respiratory complications.
