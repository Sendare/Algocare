Hepatic Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Liver

The liver is the largest solid organ in the human body, snugly located in the right upper quadrant of the abdominal cavity just beneath the diaphragm. It has a remarkable dual blood supply that sets it apart from most other organs. The hepatic artery brings oxygen-rich blood directly from the heart, while the portal vein carries nutrient-rich, deoxygenated blood straight from the digestive tract and spleen. Inside the liver, tiny functional units called lobules process these blood supplies. Hepatocytes, the primary liver cells, perform hundreds of vital chemical tasks every second.

Key functions of the liver include:

  • Metabolism: Processing carbohydrates, fats, and proteins absorbed from the gut, and storing glucose as glycogen.
  • Synthesis: Producing essential blood proteins, most notably albumin and major clotting factors.
  • Detoxification: Breaking down circulating toxins, alcohol, and medications into less harmful substances for excretion.
  • Storage: Holding onto vital fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and minerals like iron.
  • Bile Production: Creating about 500 to 1000 mL of bile daily to help digest fats and absorb fat-soluble nutrients.

Viral Hepatitis: Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations

Viral hepatitis is widespread inflammation of the liver tissue caused by specific hepatotropic viruses. The most common types encountered in clinical practice are Hepatitis A, B, and C. While each virus behaves differently, they all trigger a cell-mediated immune response where the body's own immune system attacks infected hepatocytes, leading to liver cell swelling, necrosis, and temporary disruption of normal liver function.

  • Hepatitis A (HAV): Spreads primarily through the fecal-oral route via contaminated food or water, typically causing an acute, self-limiting infection with complete recovery.
  • Hepatitis B (HBV): Transmitted through infected blood, semen, and other body fluids. It can cause acute illness or quietly transition into a chronic infection.
  • Hepatitis C (HCV): Spread mainly through direct blood-to-blood contact, frequently becoming a silent chronic infection that slowly damages the liver over many years.

Common clinical manifestations during the acute phase include fatigue, nausea, right upper quadrant discomfort, dark urine, pale stools, and jaundice (yellowing of the skin and sclera due to elevated serum bilirubin).

Cirrhosis and Portal Hypertension

Cirrhosis represents the late stage of progressive hepatic fibrosis, characterized by widespread distortion of the normal liver architecture. Healthy liver tissue is gradually replaced by permanent scar tissue and regenerative nodules, cutting off normal blood flow through the organ. Chronic alcohol abuse and long-standing chronic Hepatitis C infection are leading causes of this irreversible scarring.

As scar tissue builds up inside the liver, blood meeting resistance can no longer flow smoothly through the hepatic portal system. This backup of blood creates a dangerous pressure spike known as portal hypertension. To escape this high-pressure zone, venous blood seeks alternative pathways back to the heart through smaller, fragile blood vessels. These swollen collateral vessels commonly form in the lower esophagus and stomach, becoming known as esophageal varices, which carry a severe, life-threatening risk of sudden rupture and massive upper gastrointestinal bleeding.

Complications of Liver Failure: Ascites and Hepatic Encephalopathy

When the liver fails to perform its critical synthetic and filtering duties, systemic complications quickly cascade throughout the body. Ascites is the abnormal accumulation of protein-rich fluid within the peritoneal cavity. It results from a combination of portal hypertension pushing fluid out of vessels, and low serum albumin levels caused by poor liver synthesis, which drops the blood's oncotic pressure and lets fluid leak into the tissues.

Hepatic encephalopathy is a complex neurological syndrome caused by the liver's inability to filter toxins out of the bloodstream. Chief among these toxins is ammonia, a byproduct of protein breakdown in the gut. When high levels of ammonia cross the blood-brain barrier, they disrupt brain function. Nurses must watch closely for early signs like mild confusion, slurred speech, sleep cycle changes, and asterixis (a characteristic flapping tremor of the hands when wrists are extended).

Diagnostic Evaluation of Liver Function

Assessing liver health requires a combination of blood tests, imaging studies, and sometimes direct tissue sampling. Routine blood work often includes liver function tests (LFTs), which measure enzymes released into the bloodstream when hepatocytes are injured. Elevated levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) point toward active liver cell damage.

Additional diagnostic markers include:

  • Serum Bilirubin: Elevated levels indicate impaired processing and excretion of bile pigments, pointing toward hemolysis, hepatitis, or biliary obstruction.
  • Serum Albumin and Prothrombin Time (PT/INR): These assess the synthetic capacity of the liver; low albumin and a prolonged clotting time mean the liver is struggling to make vital proteins and clotting factors.
  • Abdominal Ultrasound or CT Scan: Non-invasive imaging used to visualize liver size, detect fatty infiltration, identify nodules, and evaluate blood flow through the portal system.
  • Liver Biopsy: The definitive gold-standard procedure for grading and staging the exact degree of inflammation and fibrosis.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management of hepatic disorders focuses on halting ongoing liver injury, treating underlying causes, and managing acute complications. For chronic viral hepatitis, direct-acting antiviral medications can clear the virus and prevent progression to cirrhosis. In patients with ascites, dietary sodium restriction and diuretic medications like spironolactone and furosemide are standard therapies to help the body excrete excess fluid.

To manage and prevent hepatic encephalopathy, lactulose is prescribed. Lactulose works by trapping ammonia in the bowel and promoting its rapid excretion through frequent loose stools. Additionally, antibiotic drugs such as rifaximin are often given to reduce the number of ammonia-producing bacteria living in the gut. For patients suffering from severe bleeding esophageal varices, emergency endoscopic procedures like band ligation are performed to physically tie off the bleeding vessels.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education for Hepatic Clients

Comprehensive nursing care for clients with liver disorders requires careful monitoring, symptom management, and structured education. Nurses must perform frequent abdominal girth measurements, track daily weights, and accurately monitor strict intake and output to evaluate fluid shifts in patients with ascites. Neurological checks should be performed routinely to catch early signs of worsening hepatic encephalopathy.

Safety is a major priority for clients with advanced liver disease. Because coagulopathies are common, nurses must teach patients to use soft toothbrushes, avoid razor blades, and report any signs of bruising or dark tarry stools immediately. Clients and families must also be educated to completely avoid alcohol and strictly review all over-the-counter medications and herbal supplements with their healthcare provider to prevent further toxic injury to the liver.
