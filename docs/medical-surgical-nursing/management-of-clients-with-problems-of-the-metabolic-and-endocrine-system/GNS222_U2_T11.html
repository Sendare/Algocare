Parathyroid Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Parathyroid Glands

The parathyroid glands are typically four small, yellowish-brown, ovoid structures embedded on the posterior surface of the thyroid gland. Despite their close anatomical proximity to the thyroid, they have a distinct embryological origin and an entirely separate function. Each gland is roughly the size of a grain of rice, containing chief cells responsible for synthesizing and secreting parathyroid hormone (PTH).

Physiologically, these glands act as the primary thermostat for extracellular calcium concentration. When serum ionized calcium levels drop, chief cells detect the change via calcium-sensing receptors and release preformed PTH into the bloodstream. This hormone targets bone, kidney, and the gastrointestinal tract to restore normocalcemia, operating through a classic negative feedback loop independent of the pituitary-hypothalamic axis.

Pathophysiology and Calcium Homeostasis

Calcium homeostasis relies on the coordinated actions of parathyroid hormone, calcitriol (active vitamin D), and calcitonin. Parathyroid hormone elevates serum calcium through three main mechanisms:

  • Bone resorption: PTH stimulates osteoclast activity, dissolving bone matrix to release calcium and phosphate into the extracellular fluid.
  • Renal tubular reabsorption: PTH increases calcium reabsorption in the distal convoluted tubules while simultaneously promoting the excretion of phosphate.
  • Intestinal absorption: PTH activates renal hydroxylase, converting inactive vitamin D into calcitriol, which subsequently enhances dietary calcium absorption in the gut.

Phosphate metabolism is inversely linked to calcium; therefore, excess PTH typically results in hypercalcemia coupled with hypophosphatemia.

Hyperparathyroidism: Causes and Types

Hyperparathyroidism represents an excess of parathyroid hormone, categorized into primary, secondary, and tertiary forms based on underlying physiological triggers.

  • Primary Hyperparathyroidism: Caused by intrinsic abnormality of the glands, most commonly a benign parathyroid adenoma, leading to autonomous overproduction of PTH and subsequent hypercalcemia.
  • Secondary Hyperparathyroidism: An adaptive response to chronic hypocalcemia, frequently seen in chronic kidney disease due to impaired vitamin D activation and phosphate retention.
  • Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism: Occurs when secondary hyperparathyroidism persists so long that the parathyroid glands become hyperplastic and autonomous, no longer responding to normal calcium feedback loops.

Hypoparathyroidism: Causes and Types

Hypoparathyroidism is characterized by insufficient parathyroid hormone secretion or peripheral resistance to its action, leading to profound hypocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia.

Iatrogenic trauma or inadvertent removal during anterior neck surgeries, such as total thyroidectomy or radical neck dissection, stands as the most frequent clinical cause. Autoimmune destruction can also target the glands, occasionally as part of polyglandular autoimmune syndromes. Pseudohypoparathyroidism, a rare inherited disorder, features adequate or elevated PTH levels, but target organs fail to respond due to receptor signaling defects, rendering biochemical findings identical to true hypoparathyroidism despite normal structural glands.

Clinical Manifestations of Parathyroid Imbalances

Clinical presentations mirror the direction of the calcium imbalance. Hypercalcemia from excess PTH depresses neuromuscular excitability, summarizing the classic mnemonic: stones, bones, abdominal groans, and psychic moans. Patients experience nephrolithiasis, bone pain from increased resorption, constipation, peptic ulcers, and neuropsychiatric changes ranging from lethargy to psychosis.

Conversely, hypocalcemia increases neuromuscular irritability, presenting with paresthesias, muscle cramps, and tetany. Clinicians routinely assess for Chvostek's sign (facial twitching elicited by tapping the facial nerve anterior to the ear) and Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm induced by inflating a sphygmomanometer above systolic pressure for three minutes). Severe hypocalcemia can trigger laryngospasm and cardiac arrhythmias.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Confirming parathyroid pathology requires a systematic laboratory panel correlating serum calcium, ionized calcium, intact parathyroid hormone, phosphorus, and renal function.

Imaging modalities assist in localizing abnormal tissue prior to intervention. Sestamibi scintigraphy combined with neck ultrasonography helps pinpoint adenomas or hyperplastic glands. Bone mineral density scans, such as dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry, quantify skeletal demineralization resulting from chronic excess PTH.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical therapy varies entirely based on whether the patient is experiencing hyperfunction or hypofunction. For hypercalcemia of hyperparathyroidism, aggressive hydration with normal saline promotes renal calcium excretion, while loop diuretics like furosemide prevent fluid overload while enhancing calcium clearance once volume is restored.

Calcimimetics, such as cinacalcet, mimic calcium to increase the sensitivity of the calcium-sensing receptors on the parathyroid glands, suppressing PTH secretion. Bisphosphonates are occasionally utilized to inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. For chronic hypoparathyroidism, management focuses on oral calcium supplementation paired with active vitamin D metabolites like calcitriol to maintain safe serum calcium thresholds.

Surgical Intervention: Parathyroidectomy

Surgical removal of hyperactive parathyroid tissue remains the definitive cure for primary hyperparathyroidism. Minimally invasive radio-guided surgery has largely replaced traditional bilateral neck exploration when preoperative imaging successfully isolates a single adenoma. During the procedure, surgeons verify excision success by monitoring intraoperative PTH levels, expecting a rapid drop of at least fifty percent within ten minutes of gland removal.

Postoperative monitoring centers on airway patency due to the anatomical risk of compressive hematoma or laryngeal edema. Nurses must maintain emergency equipment—including a tracheostomy tray and intravenous calcium gluconate—at the bedside for immediate intervention if acute hypocalcemic tetany or respiratory distress arises.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Nursing care revolves around continuous physiological monitoring, symptom management, and structured discharge planning. Nurses must track intake and output, monitor for signs of neuromuscular instability, and encourage early ambulation to prevent bone demineralization in hypercalcemic clients.

Education for patients managed medically emphasizes adherence to complex medication schedules involving calcium and vitamin D, stressing the dangers of both hypercalcemia and hypocalcemia. For postoperative clients, instruction focuses on incision care, recognizing signs of hypocalcemia like numbness around the mouth, and keeping follow-up appointments to evaluate serum calcium recovery.
