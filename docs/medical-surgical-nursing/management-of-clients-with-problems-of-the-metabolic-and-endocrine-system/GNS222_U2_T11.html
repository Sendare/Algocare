Parathyroid Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Parathyroid Glands

The parathyroid glands are four tiny, pea-sized endocrine glands located on the posterior surface of the thyroid gland in the neck. Despite their small size, they carry out the massive job of regulating calcium levels in the blood and extracellular fluid. They secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH), which acts directly on the bones, kidneys, and intestines to raise serum calcium when it drops too low.

When blood calcium levels fall, the parathyroid glands sense the change and release stored PTH into the bloodstream. In the bones, PTH stimulates osteoclast activity, breaking down bone tissue to release calcium and phosphate into the blood. In the kidneys, the hormone increases calcium reabsorption from the urine while promoting the excretion of phosphate. Additionally, PTH stimulates the kidneys to activate vitamin D, which in turn enhances calcium absorption from food in the intestines. This feedback loop ensures that vital functions like nerve conduction and muscle contraction continue uninterrupted.

Pathophysiology and Calcium Homeostasis

Calcium is not just a building block for bones; it is essential for blood clotting, cardiac function, nerve transmission, and muscle contraction. Because these processes are so critical, the body tightly controls serum calcium within a narrow range, usually 8.5 to 10.5 mg/dL. This delicate balance relies on a three-way partnership between parathyroid hormone, calcitonin (secreted by the thyroid gland), and active vitamin D.

When disruptions occur, the body quickly attempts compensation. For example, if dietary calcium is low, chronic secretion of parathyroid hormone can gradually deplete bone density, leaving bones fragile and prone to fractures. Understanding this balance helps nurses recognize why a disorder in the parathyroid glands quickly triggers widespread symptoms throughout the entire neuromuscular and skeletal systems.

Hyperparathyroidism: Causes and Types

Hyperparathyroidism occurs when the parathyroid glands produce too much parathyroid hormone. It is categorized into primary, secondary, and tertiary forms:

  • Primary Hyperparathyroidism: Caused by a benign tumor (adenoma) or hyperplasia of one or more parathyroid glands, leading to autonomous overproduction of PTH and high blood calcium.
  • Secondary Hyperparathyroidism: A normal compensatory response to chronic hypocalcemia, most commonly seen in chronic kidney disease where the kidneys fail to activate vitamin D or excrete phosphate.
  • Tertiary Hyperparathyroidism: Occurs when parathyroid glands become hyperplastic and autonomous after long-standing secondary hyperparathyroidism, often persisting even after a kidney transplant.

Hypoparathyroidism: Causes and Types

Hypoparathyroidism is a rare condition characterized by abnormally low levels of parathyroid hormone, resulting in hypocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia.

  1. Iatrogenic Hypoparathyroidism: This is the most frequent cause, typically happening accidentally during thyroidectomy, parathyroidectomy, or radical neck dissection when the glands are damaged or removed.
  2. Idiopathic Hypoparathyroidism: A rare autoimmune or genetic condition where the glands fail to develop properly or undergo destruction without an obvious surgical trigger.
  3. Transient Hypoparathyroidism: Temporary suppression of the remaining glands after surgery or inflammation, which often recovers within a few weeks.

Clinical Manifestations of Parathyroid Imbalances

The signs and symptoms of parathyroid disorders depend entirely on whether calcium is too high or too low.

Hypercalcemia (from hyperparathyroidism) slows down the nervous and muscular systems. Patients often complain of fatigue, muscle weakness, constipation, and abdominal pain. A common mnemonic for severe hypercalcemia is "bones, stones, abdominal groans, and psychic moans." Kidney stones frequently form due to excess calcium filtering through the urinary tract.

Conversely, hypocalcemia (from hypoparathyroidism) makes nerve and muscle tissues dangerously excitable. Nurses look closely for Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm induced by inflating a blood pressure cuff) and Chvostek's sign (facial twitching when tapping the facial nerve). Patients may report numbness and tingling around the mouth, muscle cramps, or in severe cases, tetany and seizures.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Diagnosing parathyroid disorders requires a combination of blood tests, urine studies, and imaging scans. A basic metabolic panel reveals serum calcium, phosphorus, and magnesium levels. Intact PTH levels are measured simultaneously with calcium to determine if the glands are responding appropriately. In primary hyperparathyroidism, both calcium and PTH levels are elevated. In hypoparathyroidism, calcium is low while PTH is also low or undetectable.

Imaging studies help locate abnormal glands or kidney stones. Ultrasound of the neck, sestamibi scans, and CT scans can pinpoint a parathyroid adenoma before surgery. 24-hour urine calcium tests are also ordered to evaluate how much calcium the kidneys are excreting and to rule out familial conditions.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical management focuses on correcting calcium imbalances and protecting target organs. For mild hyperparathyroidism without symptoms, providers may choose active surveillance with regular lab checks and hydration. When treatment is needed, medications such as calcimimetics (which trick the parathyroid glands into releasing less PTH) or bisphosphonates (to protect bone density) are prescribed.

For hypoparathyroidism, management requires lifelong oral calcium supplementation combined with active vitamin D metabolites (like calcitriol) to enhance intestinal absorption. Thiazide diuretics are sometimes used in hyperparathyroidism to reduce urinary calcium excretion, but care must be taken to monitor hydration and kidney function.

Surgical Intervention: Parathyroidectomy

Surgical removal of abnormal parathyroid tissue is the definitive cure for primary hyperparathyroidism. During a parathyroidectomy, the surgeon identifies and removes the overactive adenoma or hyperplastic glands. Minimally invasive approaches are common when preoperative imaging successfully locates a single offending adenoma.

A critical postoperative risk is hungry bone syndrome, where bones rapidly absorb calcium and phosphate to rebuild lost density after the sudden drop in PTH, leading to severe hypocalcemia. Nurses must monitor for signs of airway compromise due to neck swelling or laryngeal nerve damage, keeping emergency calcium gluconate and tracheostomy equipment nearby.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with parathyroid disorders centers on monitoring laboratory values, ensuring safety, and providing clear education. For patients at risk for hypocalcemia after neck surgery, the bedside must always be stocked with calcium gluconate ready for emergency administration. Nurses should regularly assess voice quality to detect any injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve.

Patient education is vital for long-term management. Individuals taking oral calcium and vitamin D must understand the importance of adherence and regular blood test follow-ups to prevent hypercalcemia or kidney stones. Encouraging high fluid intake helps prevent renal stone formation in hypercalcemic patients, while teaching safe mobility reduces the risk of pathological fractures.
