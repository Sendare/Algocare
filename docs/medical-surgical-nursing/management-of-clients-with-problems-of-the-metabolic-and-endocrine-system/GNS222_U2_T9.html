Pituitary Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Pituitary Gland

Often called the master gland, the pituitary is a pea-sized, bean-shaped organ nestled in the sella turcica at the base of the brain. It sits right beneath the hypothalamus, which acts as the master control center, sending chemical and nervous signals to regulate the gland. The pituitary is divided into two distinct lobes, each with a very different job.

The anterior pituitary, or adenohypophysis, makes up about 80 percent of the gland and produces its own hormones through a rich network of blood vessels called the hypophysial portal system. Key hormones released here include growth hormone (GH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), prolactin, and the gonadotropins (FSH and LH).

Conversely, the posterior pituitary, or neurohypophysis, does not actually synthesize hormones. Instead, it acts as a storage and release site for two hormones manufactured directly by the hypothalamus: antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which controls water balance in the kidneys, and oxytocin, which stimulates uterine contractions and milk letdown during breastfeeding.

Pathophysiology and Classification of Pituitary Disorders

Pituitary disorders generally arise from either an overproduction or an underproduction of hormones. Most cases stem from benign tumors known as pituitary adenomas. These tumors are further classified as functioning, meaning they actively secrete excess hormones, or non-functioning, meaning they do not produce hormones but cause trouble simply by growing large and crushing surrounding normal tissue.

When a tumor or other lesion expands upward, it frequently presses against the optic chiasm. This mechanical compression produces a classic visual deficit known as bitemporal hemianopsia, where the patient loses the outer half of their field of vision in both eyes.

Another dangerous process is pituitary infarction or hemorrhage, known as a pituitary apoplexy. This is a true medical emergency that causes sudden, severe headache, collapse, and rapid loss of vision due to bleeding inside the confined sella turcica.

Hyperpituitarism: Acromegaly and Cushing's Disease

Hyperpituitarism involves the excess secretion of one or more anterior pituitary hormones, most frequently growth hormone or ACTH.

  • Acromegaly: Caused by excess growth hormone secretion after the epiphyseal plates have closed in adults. Patients slowly develop coarsened facial features, enlarged hands and feet, gaps between the teeth, and generalized enlargement of internal organs like the heart.
  • Cushing's Disease: Caused by an ACTH-secreting pituitary tumor that overstimulates the adrenal cortex to produce too much cortisol. This leads to characteristic physical changes including central obesity, a buffalo hump, purple abdominal striae, and thin, fragile skin.
  • Prolactinomas: The most common functioning pituitary tumor. Excess prolactin causes galactorrhea, amenorrhea in women, and erectile dysfunction or loss of libido in men.

Hypopituitarism and Panhypopituitarism

Hypopituitarism occurs when the pituitary gland fails to produce one or more of its hormones. When all anterior and posterior hormones are deficient, the condition is called panhypopituitarism. The onset is often insidious because the body has reserve capacity until a large portion of the gland is destroyed.

The loss of hormones follows a relatively predictable clinical pattern. Growth hormone and gonadotropins (FSH and LH) are usually lost first, leading to fatigue, sexual dysfunction, and muscle weakness. As the failure progresses, TSH and ACTH deficiencies follow, causing secondary hypothyroidism and secondary adrenal insufficiency, which is life-threatening if untreated.

Common causes include pituitary tumors, surgical removal, radiation therapy, and Sheehan's syndrome, a form of postpartum ischemic necrosis of the pituitary caused by severe obstetric hemorrhage.

Diabetes Insipidus and SIADH

Fluid balance disorders stem from abnormalities in antidiuretic hormone (ADH) regulation at the posterior pituitary.

  • Diabetes Insipidus (DI): Caused by a deficiency of ADH or an inability of the kidneys to respond to it. The hallmark is massive polyuria (up to 20 liters a day) of pale, dilute urine, leading to extreme thirst and hypernatremia if fluid intake cannot keep up.
  • Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH): Caused by excessive, unsuppressed release of ADH. Water is reabsorbed excessively by the kidneys, leading to concentrated urine, fluid retention, weight gain, and severe hyponatremia as sodium is diluted in the bloodstream. Low serum sodium can quickly trigger confusion, seizures, and coma.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluations

Assessing a patient with a suspected pituitary disorder requires a meticulous head-to-toe evaluation focusing on hormonal imbalances, fluid status, and neurological function.

  • Vision Assessment: Routinely check visual acuity and peripheral fields using confrontation tests to catch early compression of the optic chiasm.
  • Fluid Balance: Track strict intake and output, daily weights, and specific gravity of urine, especially after brain surgery.
  • Laboratory Testing: Review serum and urine osmolarity, electrolyte panels (particularly sodium), and baseline hormone levels such as cortisol, TSH, and prolactin.
  • Diagnostic Imaging: MRI of the brain with contrast is the gold standard for visualizing microadenomas or macroadenomas inside the sella turcica.

Medical and Surgical Management of Pituitary Disorders

Treatment goals for pituitary disorders focus on normalizing hormone levels, removing tumor mass when present, and relieving pressure on nearby brain structures.

Medical therapy is often the first line for hormone-secreting tumors. For instance, dopamine agonists like bromocriptine or cabergoline effectively shrink prolactin-secreting tumors. Somatostatin analogs are used to lower growth hormone levels in acromegaly.

When surgery is required, the preferred approach is transsphenoidal hypophysectomy. The surgeon reaches the pituitary gland through the nasal cavity and sphenoid sinus, avoiding an open craniotomy. Post-operative care requires watching for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks, meningitis, diabetes insipidus, and bleeding. Patients who undergo total hypophysectomy will require lifelong hormone replacement therapy including glucocorticoids, thyroid hormones, and sex hormones.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Post-operative nursing care following pituitary surgery centers on preventing complications and ensuring a smooth recovery. Nurses must enforce strict post-operative precautions: instruct the patient not to blow their nose, sneeze forcefully, cough, strain during bowel movements, or use a straw, as these actions increase intracranial pressure and can dislodge the surgical graft or cause a CSF leak.

Monitor nasal drainage closely. A sweet-tasting nasal discharge or a halo sign (a yellowish ring around a blood stain on gauze) strongly suggests a cerebrospinal fluid leak, which must be reported immediately. Check for signs of meningitis such as fever and nuchal rigidity.

Discharge education should cover lifelong medication schedules, the importance of wearing a medical alert bracelet, recognizing symptoms of hormone imbalances, and keeping follow-up appointments for endocrinology and ophthalmology evaluations.
