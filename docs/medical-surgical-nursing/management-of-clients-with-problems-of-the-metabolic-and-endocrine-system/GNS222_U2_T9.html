Pituitary Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Pituitary Gland

Often called the master gland, the pituitary sits snugly within the sella turcica of the sphenoid bone at the base of the brain. It is anatomically and functionally divided into two distinct lobes: the anterior pituitary (adenohypophysis) and the posterior pituitary (neurohypophysis). The anterior pituitary comprises roughly 80% of the gland's weight and synthesizes its own hormones under the regulatory control of hypothalamic releasing and inhibiting factors delivered via the hypophyseal portal system. Key hormones produced here include growth hormone (GH), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), luteinizing hormone (LH), and prolactin (PRL). Conversely, the posterior pituitary does not synthesize hormones. Instead, it acts as a storage and release site for oxytocin and antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which are manufactured directly by the cell bodies in the hypothalamus and travel down axons through the pituitary stalk.

Pathophysiology and Classification of Pituitary Disorders

Disorders of the pituitary gland generally stem from hypersecretion, hyposecretion, or structural mass effects. The vast majority of functional and non-functional pituitary tumors are benign adenomas. When these lesions expand, they place upward pressure on the optic chiasm, classically causing bitemporal hemianopsia—a visual defect where peripheral vision is lost in both eyes. Pathological processes are broadly categorized into: - **Primary disorders**: Originating directly from the pituitary gland itself, such as an adenoma or localized infarction. - **Secondary disorders**: Resulting from abnormalities in the hypothalamus that disrupt releasing hormones. - **Mass effects**: Compression of surrounding neurological structures, cranial nerves, or normal pituitary tissue, leading to panhypopituitarism as healthy cells are destroyed.

Hyperpituitarism: Acromegaly and Cushing's Disease

Hyperpituitarism involves the excessive secretion of one or more anterior pituitary hormones, most frequently driven by a hormone-secreting adenoma. - **Acromegaly**: Caused by overproduction of growth hormone in adulthood after epiphyseal plate closure. Manifestations include coarsening facial features, enlargement of the hands and feet, jaw prognathism, diaphoresis, and organomegaly. Left untreated, it substantially increases cardiovascular morbidity. - **Cushing's Disease**: Caused by an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma, leading to adrenal hyperstimulation and excessive cortisol production. Patients present with truncal obesity, a 'buffalo hump,' moon facies, purple striae, muscle wasting, and secondary hypertension.

Hypopituitarism and Panhypopituitarism

Hypopituitarism occurs when the secretion of one or more anterior pituitary hormones is diminished. When all tropic hormones are deficient, the condition is termed panhypopituitarism. The loss of hormones typically follows a predictable chronological sequence: growth hormone and gonadotropins (LH/FSH) are usually lost first, followed by TSH, and finally ACTH. Causes include pituitary tumors, surgical removal, radiation therapy, and ischemic necrosis. A dramatic example of ischemic necrosis is Sheehan's syndrome, which occurs following severe postpartum hemorrhage and hypovolemic shock. The massively enlarged postpartum pituitary outgrows its blood supply, suffering infarction that leads to acute or gradual anterior pituitary failure.

Diabetes Insipidus and SIADH

Disorders of the posterior pituitary involve antidiuretic hormone abnormalities, leading to profound fluid and electrolyte imbalances. Diabetes Insipidus (DI) results from a deficiency of ADH or an inability of the kidneys to respond to it. Patients excrete massive volumes of dilute urine with a very low specific gravity, leading to severe dehydration, intense thirst (polydipsia), and hypernatremia. Conversely, the Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone (SIADH) is characterized by excessive, unsuppressible release of ADH regardless of serum osmolality. Water is reabsorbed excessively by the renal tubules, resulting in concentrated urine, low urine output, fluid retention, and dilutional hyponatremia, which can trigger cerebral edema and seizures.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluations

Evaluating pituitary function requires combining a detailed clinical history with targeted laboratory and imaging studies. Nurses must monitor fluid balance strictly through daily weights, accurate intake and output records, and urine specific gravity measurements, especially when posterior pituitary disorders are suspected. Diagnostic confirmation relies heavily on hormone assays measured in serum and random or timed urine samples. Dynamic stimulation and suppression tests are frequently utilized to evaluate feedback loops—for instance, administering oral glucose to check if growth hormone suppresses properly in suspected acromegaly. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the sella turcica with contrast remains the gold standard for visualizing microadenomas and macroadenomas.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management strategies vary based on whether a lesion is functioning, its size, and the specific hormonal disturbance present. Pharmacotherapy often serves as a first-line defense or presurgical preparation; dopamine agonists like bromocriptine or cabergoline effectively shrink prolactin-secreting adenomas. When surgical intervention is required, the transsphenoidal approach is preferred. The surgeon accesses the pituitary gland through the nasal cavity and sphenoid sinus, avoiding a craniotomy. Medical hormone replacement (such as levothyroxine, hydrocortisone, and sex steroids) becomes lifelong if normal pituitary function cannot be preserved following surgery or radiation therapy.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Postoperative nursing care following a transsphenoidal hypophysectomy centers on preventing complications and monitoring neurological status. Nurses must regularly check nasal drip pads for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), identified by a yellow halo ring or a positive glucose test. Patients must be explicitly instructed to avoid blowing their nose, coughing vigorously, sneezing, or straining during bowel movements, as these activities increase intracranial pressure and threaten the surgical graft. Endocrine replacement therapy compliance is another critical educational focus. Patients facing lifelong hormone replacement must understand medication schedules, recognize signs of under- or over-replacement, and know when to seek emergency medical care for acute adrenal crisis.
