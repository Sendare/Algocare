Adrenal Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Adrenal Glands

The adrenal glands are small, triangular organs located on top of each kidney. Each gland consists of two distinct parts: the outer cortex and the inner medulla. The adrenal cortex makes up the bulk of the gland and is divided into three zones, each producing specific steroid hormones necessary for life.

  1. Zona Glomerulosa: The outermost layer producing mineralocorticoids, mainly aldosterone. Aldosterone controls sodium and water reabsorption while promoting potassium excretion in the kidneys.
  2. Zona Fasciculata: The middle layer producing glucocorticoids, primarily cortisol. Cortisol helps the body manage stress, raises blood sugar, and dampens inflammation.
  3. Zona Reticularis: The inner layer producing adrenal sex hormones, mainly androgens.

The adrenal medulla acts as part of the sympathetic nervous system. It produces catecholamines, which are epinephrine and norepinephrine, triggering the classic fight-or-flight response during acute stress.

Pathophysiology and Classification of Adrenal Disorders

Adrenal disorders arise when the glands either produce too much hormone, known as hyperfunction, or too little hormone, known as hypofunction. These imbalances can originate directly within the adrenal gland itself, which doctors call a primary disorder. Alternatively, the problem can stem from outside the gland, usually involving the pituitary gland or hypothalamus, which is classified as a secondary disorder.

  • Primary Hypofunction: Destruction or dysfunction of the adrenal tissue itself, leading to a shortage of all adrenal hormones.
  • Secondary Hypofunction: Lack of adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) production from the anterior pituitary, which usually spares aldosterone secretion because that zone is regulated by the renin-angiotensin system.
  • Primary Hyperfunction: Tumors or hyperplasia within the adrenal cortex or medulla causing excess hormone release.
  • Secondary Hyperfunction: Excessive stimulation of the adrenal glands by overactive pituitary control centers.

Addison's Disease: Causes, Clinical Manifestations, and Crisis

Addison's disease is chronic primary adrenal insufficiency caused by the destruction of the adrenal cortex. Autoimmune destruction is the most common cause worldwide, while infections like tuberculosis remain significant contributors in regions with high disease burdens. Because both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids are missing, patients experience profound systemic changes.

Clinical signs develop slowly and include weakness, fatigue, weight loss, and low blood pressure. A hallmark physical finding is hyperpigmentation, seen most clearly on skin creases, pressure points, and gums. This darkening happens because low cortisol triggers high levels of ACTH, which shares a chemical precursor with melanocyte-stimulating hormone.

An Addisonian crisis is a life-threatening medical emergency triggered by sudden physical stress, infection, or abrupt withdrawal of steroid medication. It causes severe hypotension, vascular collapse, vomiting, and high fever requiring immediate intravenous hydrocortisone and fluid resuscitation.

Cushing's Syndrome and Cushing's Disease

Cushing's condition results from chronic exposure to excess glucocorticoids. When the excess cortisol comes from a tumor in the pituitary gland producing too much ACTH, it is specifically called Cushing's disease. When the excess comes from outside sources—such as long-term prescription steroid medications—or from an adrenal tumor, it is called Cushing's syndrome.

Excess cortisol alters fat distribution, protein synthesis, and electrolyte balance. Patients typically develop a round moon face, a fatty pad on the upper back known as a buffalo hump, and central trunk obesity with notably thin arms and legs. Skin becomes fragile, leading to purple stretch marks called striae and easy bruising. Muscle wasting causes weakness, while high cortisol levels often induce secondary diabetes and hypertension.

Pheochromocytoma: Recognition and Acute Presentation

A pheochromocytoma is a rare, usually benign tumor of the adrenal medulla that secretes massive amounts of catecholamines—epinephrine and norepinephrine. This sudden, unregulated flood of hormones places the patient in a state of continuous, severe sympathetic nervous system overactivation.

The classic triad of symptoms includes pounding headaches, profuse diaphoresis (sweating), and severe tachycardia accompanied by dangerously high hypertension. Episodes can be triggered by physical exertion, pressure on the abdomen, certain foods, or medications. Recognizing this disorder quickly is vital because a missed diagnosis and untreated hypertensive crisis can lead to stroke, heart failure, or death.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Diagnosing adrenal disorders requires measuring hormone levels in blood, urine, or saliva to confirm excess or deficiency, followed by imaging studies to locate any structural abnormalities.

  • Cortisol Testing: Measured using a 24-hour urinary free cortisol test, a late-night salivary cortisol test, or an ACTH suppression or stimulation test to see how the adrenal gland responds to chemical triggers.
  • Catecholamine Testing: Assessed by checking fractionated metanephrines in a 24-hour urine collection or blood sample, which are breakdown products of adrenaline.
  • Imaging Studies: Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans of the abdomen and head help visualize tumors in the adrenal glands or the pituitary gland.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment for adrenal disorders depends entirely on whether the gland is overactive or underactive.

For adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease), lifelong hormone replacement is mandatory. Patients take oral hydrocortisone to replace cortisol and fludrocortisone to replace aldosterone. Dosage must be increased during physical stress, illness, or surgery to prevent a crisis.

For hyperfunction disorders like Cushing's syndrome or pheochromocytoma, surgical removal of the tumor via adrenalectomy or transsphenoidal removal of a pituitary tumor is the primary goal. Prior to surgery for a pheochromocytoma, patients must receive alpha-blockers for several weeks to control blood pressure and expand blood volume before any manipulation occurs, preventing dangerous postoperative hypotension.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education in the Nigerian Clinical Context

Nursing care for patients with adrenal disorders requires vigilant monitoring of vital signs, fluid balance, and electrolyte levels. In resource-constrained clinical settings across Nigeria, nurses play a vital role in patient education, ensuring clients understand long-term self-management despite potential economic and supply challenges.

Key nursing responsibilities include:

  • Monitoring Fluid Balance: Tracking daily weights, intake, output, and watching closely for signs of dehydration or fluid overload.
  • Emergency Preparation: Teaching patients with Addison's disease how to carry an emergency injectable hydrocortisone kit and instructing family members on its administration.
  • Dietary Guidance: Advising high-sodium, adequate fluid intake for Addison's disease, while promoting low-sodium, high-potassium, and calorie-controlled diets for Cushing's syndrome.
  • Infection Prevention: Educating clients on strict hygiene, recognizing early signs of infection, and seeking prompt medical review since stress and infection can quickly provoke an adrenal crisis.
