Adrenal Disorders

Anatomy and Physiology of the Adrenal Glands

The adrenal glands are small, pyramid-shaped endocrine organs positioned like caps on the superior pole of each kidney. Anatomically, each gland is divided into two distinct regions: an outer cortex and an inner medulla. Both parts function independently yet coordinate closely to regulate metabolism, fluid balance, and the body's response to physical and psychological stressors. The adrenal cortex accounts for roughly 90% of the gland's total weight and is further subdivided into three functional zones, each producing specific steroid hormones derived from cholesterol: - **Zona glomerulosa**: The outermost zone. It synthesizes and secretes mineralocorticoids, primarily **aldosterone**, which drives sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion in the distal renal tubules. - **Zona fasciculata**: The middle and largest zone. It produces glucocorticoids, chiefly **cortisol**, which influences glucose metabolism, suppresses inflammation, and maintains vascular tone. - **Zona reticularis**: The innermost cortical zone. It secretes weak androgens, such as dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), contributing to secondary sex characteristics. The adrenal medulla, lying centrally, functions as part of the sympathetic nervous system. It is composed of chromaffin cells that synthesize and release catecholamines—primarily **epinephrine** and, to a lesser extent, **norepinephrine**—in response to acute sympathetic stimulation, producing the classic fight-or-flight response.

Pathophysiology and Classification of Adrenal Disorders

Adrenal gland disorders arise from either a hormone excess or a hormone deficit, and they are further classified by where the primary defect originates along the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Primary disorders stem from dysfunction within the adrenal gland itself. For example, destruction of the adrenal cortex leads to primary hypofunction. Secondary disorders originate outside the gland, typically involving the anterior pituitary gland or the hypothalamus, leading to altered trophic hormone signaling such as deficient adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) production. Tertiary disorders involve hypothalamic failure to release corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH). Pathophysiologically, hyperfunction states present as autonomous hormone hypersecretion, uninhibited by normal negative feedback loops. Conversely, hypofunction reflects tissue destruction, enzymatic blocks, or abrupt withdrawal of exogenous glucocorticoid therapy. Recognizing whether a pathology is primary, secondary, or tertiary dictates the diagnostic testing strategy and pharmacological replacement pathway.

Addison's Disease: Causes, Clinical Manifestations, and Crisis

Addison's disease, or primary chronic adrenocortical insufficiency, develops when at least 90% of the adrenal cortex is destroyed. In sub-Saharan Africa, historical and regional infectious etiologies such as **tuberculosis** remain significant contributors alongside autoimmune destruction. Because both glucocorticoids and mineralocorticoids are lost, the resulting metabolic disturbances are profound. Clinical manifestations appear gradually due to the slow destruction of tissue: - **Hyperpigmentation**: A hallmark sign caused by elevated levels of circulating ACTH stimulating melanocytes, especially noticeable over pressure points, palm creases, and mucous membranes. - **Hypotension**: Driven by volume depletion and loss of vascular tone resulting from sodium and water wasting. - **Metabolic shifts**: Persistent hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia. - **Gastrointestinal symptoms**: Anorexia, nausea, weight loss, and generalized salt craving. An **Addisonian crisis** is a life-threatening medical emergency precipitated by acute stressors like infection, trauma, or sudden withdrawal of steroid therapy. It presents with explosive hypotension, vascular collapse, severe abdominal pain, and shock requiring immediate intravenous hydrocortisone and fluid resuscitation.

Cushing's Syndrome and Cushing's Disease

Cushing's syndrome describes the clinical state resulting from chronic, prolonged exposure to excess circulating glucocorticoids. When this hypercortisolism is specifically caused by an **ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma**, the condition is termed Cushing's disease. Other causes include functional adrenal tumors or, most frequently on the global health landscape, **iatrogenic** administration of exogenous corticosteroids for inflammatory or autoimmune disorders. The clinical presentation reflects the widespread metabolic actions of sustained high cortisol levels: - **Fat redistribution**: Accumulation of adipose tissue resulting in truncal obesity, a dorsal cervical fat pad known as a **buffalo hump**, and a rounded **moon face**. - **Skin changes**: Purple striae over the abdomen, thin paper-like skin, easy bruising, and poor wound healing due to protein catabolism. - **Endocrine and metabolic dysfunction**: Secondary diabetes mellitus or insulin resistance, hypertension from fluid retention, and generalized proximal muscle wasting and weakness. - **Psychological alterations**: Emotional lability, depression, and frank psychosis in severe cases.

Pheochromocytoma: Recognition and Acute Presentation

A pheochromocytoma is a rare, typically benign catecholamine-secreting tumor that originates from the chromaffin cells of the adrenal medulla. By autonomously discharging massive bursts of epinephrine and norepinephrine, these tumors provoke a dramatic and dangerous physiological response. The classic clinical triad consists of **paroxysmal headaches**, profuse **diaphoresis**, and **tachycardia** accompanied by severe episodic hypertension. Attacks can be triggered by physical exertion, abdominal pressure, or certain medications. During a hypertensive crisis, blood pressure may skyrocket to dangerous levels, placing the patient at immediate risk for stroke, acute myocardial infarction, or pulmonary edema. Diagnosis relies on biochemical confirmation of elevated urinary or plasma fractionated metanephrines. Preoperative alpha-adrenergic blockade is mandatory before any surgical manipulation or beta-blocker initiation to prevent catastrophic, unmitigated vasoconstriction and rebound hypertensive crises.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Laboratory Findings

Confirming adrenal pathology requires a combination of biochemical assays, provocative stimulation tests, suppression protocols, and advanced neuroimaging. For suspected cortisol abnormalities, the **low-dose dexamethasone suppression test** is standard for Cushing's syndrome, where failure of cortisol levels to suppress indicates autonomous hypersecretion. Conversely, a **cosyntropin (ACTH) stimulation test** evaluates adrenal reserve; a blunted cortisol response confirms primary adrenal insufficiency. Catecholamine excess is screened via 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines and catecholamines. Once biochemical abnormalities are established, radiological localization follows. Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the abdomen and sella turcica helps visualize adrenal masses, pituitary adenomas, or structural atrophy.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management strategies for adrenal disorders depend entirely on whether the underlying pathology involves hormone deficiency or hormone excess. For **hypofunctional states** like Addison's disease, lifelong hormone replacement is mandatory. Patients require daily oral glucocorticoids (such as hydrocortisone or prednisone) and mineralocorticoids (fludrocortisone) to maintain electrolyte and fluid balance. Dosage must be increased dynamically during periods of physical stress, illness, or surgery. For **hyperfunctional states** and hormone-secreting tumors, surgical resection is often the definitive treatment. Transsphenoidal hypophysectomy treats Cushing's disease caused by a pituitary tumor, while unilateral or bilateral adrenalectomy addresses adrenal adenomas or hyperplasia. Preoperative medical stabilization—such as ketoconazole to block cortisol synthesis in Cushing's or alpha blockade for pheochromocytomas—is essential to prevent intraoperative hemodynamic instability.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education in the Nigerian Clinical Context

Managing adrenal disorders within Nigerian healthcare settings requires resource-conscious nursing interventions that bridge institutional care with community realities. Nurses must monitor fluid balance strictly through daily weights, accurate fluid intake and output charts, and serial blood pressure measurements to detect early signs of crisis or fluid overload. Patient education is paramount for lifelong safety: - **Sick-day rules**: Instructing patients on glucocorticoid replacement to never abruptly stop therapy and to double or triple doses during febrile illnesses, bouts of gastroenteritis, or malaria episodes. - **Emergency preparedness**: Teaching family members how to recognize early symptoms of an Addisonian crisis and ensuring the patient carries a medical alert card or bracelet. - **Dietary modifications**: Guiding patients with Cushing's syndrome on low-sodium, high-protein, and calorie-controlled diets, while advising those with Addison's disease to maintain adequate salt intake. - **Infection vigilance**: Emphasizing strict personal hygiene and early reporting of minor infections, given that immunosuppression or baseline insufficiency impairs stress responses.
