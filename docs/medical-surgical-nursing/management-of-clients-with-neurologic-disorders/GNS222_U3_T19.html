Pathophysiology of Neurologic Disorders

Cellular and Molecular Mechanisms of Neuronal Injury

Neurons are metabolically demanding cells with high oxygen and glucose requirements yet negligible energy storage capacity. When metabolic substrates fail or cellular integrity is breached, a destructive molecular cascade follows regardless of the initial insult—whether ischemic, traumatic, or toxic. Understanding these pathways clarifies why secondary brain injury often exceeds the primary trauma. - **Excitotoxicity**: Excessive extracellular **glutamate** overstimulates postsynaptic N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, flooding the cytoplasm with calcium ions. - **Calcium Overload**: Unchecked intracellular calcium activates destructive enzymes including phospholipases, proteases, and endonucleases, which dismantle structural proteins, the cell membrane, and DNA. - **Oxidative Stress**: Mitochondrial dysfunction surges the production of **reactive oxygen species (ROS)**, outstripping endogenous antioxidant defenses and causing lipid peroxidation. - **Apoptosis and Necrosis**: Severe energy depletion forces necrotic cell lysis and surrounding tissue inflammation, while milder injury triggers programmed cell death pathways.

Mechanisms and Consequences of Increased Intracranial Pressure

The cranial vault is a rigid, fixed compartment containing three essential components: brain tissue (about 80%), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF, about 10%), and blood (about 10%). According to the **Monro-Kellie doctrine**, because the skull cannot expand, an increase in volume of any single component must be compensated by a reciprocal decrease in the volume of the others. Initial compensatory mechanisms involve shifting CSF into the spinal subarachnoid space and collapsing cerebral veins. Once these reserves are exhausted, small volume increases trigger exponential spikes in **intracranial pressure (ICP)**. As pressure mounts, cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) decreases, calculated as mean arterial pressure minus intracranial pressure (CPP = MAP - ICP). When CPP drops too low, global brain ischemia ensues. Unchecked ICP gradients can force brain tissue to shift across intracranial compartments, resulting in fatal herniation syndromes such as uncal or tonsillar herniation.

Pathophysiology of Cerebrovascular Disorders

Cerebrovascular disease disrupts blood flow to the brain, manifesting primarily as ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, or transient ischemic attacks. In **ischemic stroke**, a thrombus or embolus occludes a cerebral artery. Deprived of glucose and oxygen, the affected core tissue undergoes rapid cell death within minutes. Surrounding this necrotic core lies the **penumbra**, a zone of marginally perfused, electrically silent tissue that remains viable temporarily. Clinical management focuses heavily on salvaging this penumbra before it progresses to infarction. Conversely, **hemorrhagic stroke** stems from blood vessel rupture, frequently driven by chronic hypertension or ruptured aneurysms. The extravasation of blood creates a localized hematoma that mechanically compresses adjacent brain structures, while the degradation of leaked red blood cells releases free iron and triggers severe localized vasospasm and neuroinflammation.

Mechanisms of Seizures and Epilepsy

Seizures arise from paroxysmal, uncontrolled electrical discharges from a hyperexcitable network of cerebral neurons. At the cellular level, an imbalance between excitatory neurotransmission (primarily mediated by glutamate) and inhibitory neurotransmission (primarily mediated by gamma-aminobutyric acid, or **GABA**) drives this hyper-synchrony. During a seizure, large populations of neurons fire rapidly and synchronously. This metabolic storm increases cerebral oxygen and glucose consumption by up to 250%. Although the brain initially ramps up cerebral blood flow to compensate, prolonged seizure activity (**status epilepticus**) exhausts energy reserves, culminating in cellular hypoxia, lactic acidosis, and permanent neuronal death.

Degenerative Neurological Diseases and Proteinopathies

Neurodegenerative disorders like Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis share a common pathogenic hallmark: the misfolding and aggregation of specific cellular proteins, termed **proteinopathies**. Under normal conditions, chaperone proteins assist in folding newly synthesized proteins or targeting damaged ones for degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system. In degenerative diseases, these quality-control mechanisms fail. Misfolded proteins acquire abnormal beta-sheet configurations, causing them to clump together into neurotoxic oligomers, fibrils, and inclusion bodies. - **Alzheimer's Disease**: Accumulation of extracellular amyloid-beta plaques and intracellular hyperphosphorylated tau neurofibrillary tangles. - **Parkinson's Disease**: Deposition of alpha-synuclein within dopaminergic neurons of the substantia nigra, forming Lewy bodies. - **Prion Diseases**: Conversion of normal cellular prion protein into an infectious, protease-resistant conformer.

Demyelinating and Inflammatory Conditions

Demyelinating disorders are characterized by the selective destruction of the myelin sheath that insulates nerve axons, leaving the underlying axon intact initially. Multiple sclerosis (MS) serves as the primary prototype, driven by an autoimmune response where autoreactive T-lymphocytes cross the blood-brain barrier and mount an inflammatory attack against myelin components. This immune assault triggers localized inflammation, macrophage activation, and the formation of sclerotic plaques or scars in the white matter. The loss of myelin strips axons of their saltatory conduction capacity, causing nerve signal transmission to slow down or block completely. While initial remyelination by oligodendrocyte progenitor cells can occur, repeated inflammatory cycles lead to permanent axonal transection and progressive neurological decline.

Clinical Assessment and Monitoring of Neurological Deterioration

Detecting acute neurological decline requires systematic, serial assessment because subtle clinical shifts often precede catastrophic brain herniation. The **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** remains the foundational tool for evaluating level of consciousness across three distinct behavioral parameters: eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. A downward trend in GCS score warrants immediate escalation. Beyond global consciousness, clinicians assess brainstem function through pupillary light reflexes and oculovestibular testing. As intracranial pressure rises, compression of the oculomotor nerve (CN III) typically manifests as a sluggish, then fixed and dilated pupil on the side of the lesion. Late indicators of dangerous intracranial hypertension are collectively known as **Cushing's triad**: widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations. This triad represents a desperate physiological reflex to maintain cerebral perfusion against failing brainstem centers and demands emergency intervention.
