Pathophysiology of Neurologic Disorders

Introduction to Neurological Pathophysiology

The nervous system relies on a delicate balance of electrical signaling, constant blood supply, and structural integrity. When disease or injury disrupts this balance, neurological pathophysiology explains how and why clinical signs and symptoms appear. Neurons have extremely high metabolic demands. They depend almost entirely on aerobic respiration and require a continuous supply of glucose and oxygen. Unlike many other tissues in the body, mature neurons have a very limited capacity to regenerate if destroyed. Therefore, understanding how neurological insults progress at a cellular level helps nurses anticipate complications long before obvious physical decline sets in.

Cellular and Molecular Mechanisms of Neuronal Injury

Neuronal injury rarely happens in isolation; it usually triggers a destructive cascade at the cellular level.

  • Excitotoxicity: Excessive release of the neurotransmitter glutamate overstimulates postsynaptic receptors, allowing massive amounts of calcium ions to flood into the neuron.
  • Oxidative Stress: Damaged mitochondria produce high levels of reactive oxygen species, destroying cellular membranes and DNA.
  • Apoptosis: Intracellular signaling pathways activate programmed cell death, leading to delayed tissue loss days after the initial insult.

A common misconception is that all neuronal death happens immediately during a stroke or trauma. In reality, the secondary injury cascade unfolds over hours and days, offering a critical window for nursing and medical intervention.

Mechanisms of Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

The cranium is a rigid, fixed box containing three main components: brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). According to the Monro-Kellie doctrine, if the volume of one component increases, the volume of the other components must decrease to maintain normal intracranial pressure. When compensatory mechanisms like the displacement of CSF fail, ICP rises sharply.

As pressure mounts, intracranial compliance is lost. The brain tissue can shift across structures, leading to herniation syndromes. This mechanical displacement compresses vital brainstem centers controlling breathing and heart rate, which is rapidly fatal if uncorrected.

Pathophysiology of Cerebrovascular Disorders

Cerebrovascular disorders disrupt normal blood flow to regions of the brain, leading to ischemia or hemorrhage. Ischemic strokes occur when a thrombus or embolus occludes a cerebral artery, starving downstream tissue of oxygen. Within minutes, cellular energy failure halts the sodium-potassium pump, causing cellular edema. Conversely, hemorrhagic strokes involve vessel rupture, causing blood to leak directly into brain parenchyma or subarachnoid spaces. This extravasated blood exerts mass effect, increases local pressure, and triggers severe vasospasm in surrounding vessels, compounding the ischemic damage.

Pathophysiology of Seizure Disorders and Epilepsy

Seizures arise from sudden, abnormal, and excessive electrical discharges from hypersynchronized populations of cortical neurons. Normally, inhibitory neurotransmitters like GABA balance excitatory signals driven by glutamate. In epilepsy, a localized seizure focus often develops due to structural scar tissue, genetic channelopathies, or trauma. Neurons at this focus exhibit a lower threshold for firing and can recruit surrounding healthy neurons into a synchronized storm of electrical activity. This high metabolic state dramatically increases cerebral blood flow and oxygen demand; if a seizure persists too long, metabolic exhaustion and permanent neuronal death occur.

Degenerative Neurological Diseases and Proteinopathies

Many chronic neurodegenerative disorders share a common pathological root: the abnormal folding and accumulation of specific proteins. These misfolded proteins, or proteinopathies, form toxic aggregates that disrupt cellular transport, impair mitochondria, and eventually kill neurons.

  • Alzheimer's Disease: Accumulation of extracellular amyloid-beta plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tau tangles.
  • Parkinson's Disease: Aggregation of alpha-synuclein into Lewy bodies, specifically damaging dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra.
  • Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis: Protein aggregation leading to the degeneration of both upper and lower motor neurons.

These conditions are progressive and irreversible, slowly robbing individuals of cognitive, motor, or autonomic function depending on the anatomical region affected.

Demyelinating and Inflammatory Conditions

Myelin acts as the insulation around axons, ensuring rapid saltatory conduction of electrical impulses. In demyelinating conditions like multiple sclerosis (MS), the immune system mistakenly attacks central nervous system myelin and oligodendrocytes. This inflammatory assault strips away insulation, slows nerve conduction, and eventually results in axonal transection and permanent neurological deficits. The clinical presentation is notoriously varied and often relapsing-remitting because scar tissue, or sclerosis, forms across different sites in the white matter at different times.

Nursing Implications in Monitoring Neurological Deterioration

Detecting subtle neurological changes early can prevent irreversible brain injury. Nurses use tools like the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) to track changes in level of consciousness, which is often the earliest indicator of rising intracranial pressure. Pupillary responses also offer vital clues; compression of the oculomotor nerve (cranial nerve III) from uncal herniation causes a sluggish or fixed, dilated pupil on the affected side. Regular, systematic neuro checks allow the healthcare team to catch secondary brain injury during the reversible stage.
