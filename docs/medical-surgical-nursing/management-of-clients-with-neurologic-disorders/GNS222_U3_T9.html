Electroencephalography (EEG)

Introduction and Basic Neurophysiology of EEG

Electroencephalography, commonly known as EEG, is a non-invasive diagnostic test that records electrical activity generated by the brain. Small metal discs called electrodes are placed on specific areas of the scalp using a conductive paste or gel. These electrodes pick up tiny electrical signals produced when billions of brain cells, or neurons, communicate with one another through electrical impulses.

The brain's electrical activity forms distinct waveforms categorized by their frequency, measured in Hertz (Hz), and amplitude.

  • Alpha waves: Frequencies between 8 to 13 Hz, seen when a person is resting quietly with their eyes closed.
  • Beta waves: Frequencies above 13 Hz, present during active concentration, thinking, or alert states.
  • Theta waves: Frequencies between 4 to 7 Hz, commonly observed during deep relaxation, light sleep, or in children.
  • Delta waves: Frequencies below 4 Hz, dominant during deep, dreamless sleep in healthy adults.

Indications and Clinical Uses

Healthcare providers order an EEG to help diagnose and evaluate several neurological conditions. The most common indication is the investigation and classification of seizure disorders or epilepsy, where the test can pinpoint abnormal spike-and-wave discharges.

Beyond epilepsy, EEGs are valuable for assessing sleep disorders, evaluating unexplained encephalopathy or confusion, confirming brain death, and monitoring brain function during complex surgical procedures like open-heart surgery. It helps differentiate between true physiological seizures and non-epileptic events of psychological origin.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Preparation

Proper preparation ensures accurate EEG results and minimizes patient anxiety. The nurse must explain the procedure clearly, reassuring the client that electrodes only record electricity and do not deliver electrical shocks into the head.

  • Hair hygiene: Instruct the client to wash their hair thoroughly the night before to remove oils, sprays, or conditioners that interfere with electrode contact.
  • Medication management: Check physician orders regarding whether seizure medications should be withheld, as stopping them abruptly can trigger a seizure.
  • Sleep modification: For sleep-deprived EEGs, instruct the client to sleep only a few hours or stay awake all night prior to the test.
  • Dietary restrictions: Tell the client to avoid caffeine, coffee, tea, and cola drinks for at least 8 to 12 hours before the test because stimulants alter normal brain wave patterns.

Intra-Procedure Care and Monitoring

During the recording, the client lies comfortably on a bed or sits in a reclining chair. The technician measures the scalp and attaches 16 to 25 electrodes using a sticky, conductive paste.

Standard activation procedures are routinely performed during the test to provoke hidden abnormal brain waves:

  1. Hyperventilation: Asking the client to take deep, rapid breaths for about 3 minutes, which lowers carbon dioxide levels and causes blood vessel constriction in the brain.
  2. Photic stimulation: Flashing a bright, strobe-like light at varying frequencies into the client's eyes to check for photosensitive seizure activity.
  3. Sleep recording: Allowing or inducing the client to fall asleep to observe changes in brain wave patterns.

The nurse observes the client closely throughout these activation steps, ensuring safety and watching for any signs of seizure activity.

Post-Procedure Care and Client Safety

Once the EEG recording is finished, the technician removes the electrodes and cleans the sticky paste from the client's scalp and hair. Warm water and a mild shampoo are often required to remove all remnants of the conductive adhesive.

If the client's routine anticonvulsant medications were withheld before the test, the nurse must ensure they are resumed promptly as prescribed. If sleep deprivation was part of the testing protocol, the client may feel drowsy and should be assisted with safe transportation home. Standard seizure precautions should remain in place until the client's baseline neurological status is fully confirmed.

Interpretation and Clinical Limitations

A neurologist or clinical neurophysiologist interprets the recorded tracings by comparing the client's waveforms against normal age-specific standards. Normal findings show consistent background rhythms without abnormal focal slowing or sudden sharp spike discharges. Abnormal findings include focal spikes indicating a localized seizure focus, or generalized paroxysmal discharges pointing to systemic or widespread brain dysfunction.

However, EEGs have limitations. A normal EEG does not completely rule out epilepsy, because abnormal electrical activity may not occur during the brief recording window. Conversely, some healthy individuals display non-specific abnormal variants without ever having a neurological disease.
