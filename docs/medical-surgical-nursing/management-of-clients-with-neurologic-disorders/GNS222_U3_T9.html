Electroencephalography (EEG)

Introduction to Electroencephalography (EEG)

Electroencephalography (EEG) is a non-invasive diagnostic test that measures and records the electrical activity of the brain. By placing multiple small, metallic electrodes onto specific locations on the scalp using an internationally recognized system like the 10-20 system, healthcare professionals can capture the continuous electrical impulses generated by firing cerebral neurons. These signals are amplified and translated into wave patterns displayed on a monitor or printed recording. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice and board examinations in Nigeria, understanding EEG is fundamental because it provides vital diagnostic insight without harming or surgically invading the patient. It aids in visualizing functional brain changes associated with various neurological conditions. The history and ongoing technological advancements of EEG have transformed it into a cornerstone of clinical neurology, epilepsy monitoring units, and intensive care settings, helping clinicians differentiate between primary cerebral disorders and secondary metabolic encephalopathies.

Indications and Clinical Uses of EEG in Neurologic Disorders

Electroencephalography plays a critical role in the diagnosis, classification, and management of various neurological disorders. The most common indication for an EEG is the evaluation and diagnosis of epilepsy and seizure disorders, where it helps pinpoint seizure foci and classify seizure types as generalized or focal. Additionally, nurses caring for clients in neurological or intensive care units will see EEGs utilized to diagnose and monitor encephalopathies, evaluate sleep disorders, assess cerebral function during medically induced comas, and confirm brain death. It is also valuable in investigating unexplained alterations in mental status, progressive dementias, focal neurological deficits, and central nervous system infections such as encephalitis. Recognizing these diverse clinical applications enables the nurse to anticipate provider orders, prepare the client adequately, and correlate clinical manifestations with electrophysiological findings observed on the diagnostic report.

Basic Neurophysiology and Brain Wave Patterns

To interpret EEG findings and understand nursing implications, students must master basic neurophysiology and the primary brain wave patterns. Brain waves are categorized by their frequency, measured in Hertz (Hz) or cycles per second, and their amplitude. The main wave categories include Alpha, Beta, Theta, and Delta waves. Alpha waves (8-13 Hz) predominate when an adult is awake, resting, and has their eyes closed. Beta waves (14-30 Hz) are faster, lower-amplitude waves associated with active thinking, alertness, concentration, or the use of certain medications like benzodiazepines. Theta waves (4-7 Hz) are normally seen during drowsiness, emotional stress, or light sleep in adults. Delta waves (under 4 Hz) are slow, high-amplitude waves characteristic of deep, non-REM sleep in healthy adults, but their presence in an awake adult signifies abnormal cerebral slowing or severe encephalopathy. Recognizing these distinct frequency bands helps nurses identify normal physiological states versus pathological disruptions.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Comprehensive pre-procedure nursing assessment and preparation are essential to ensure accurate EEG results and client comfort. The nurse must first verify informed consent and provide clear education, explaining that the procedure is painless, non-invasive, and does not involve electric shocks being sent into the brain. Preparation involves checking medication orders; healthcare providers may instruct the client to withhold certain CNS-active medications, such as sedatives or anticonvulsants, for 24 to 48 hours prior to the test, though the nurse must never alter medications without direct orders. The nurse should instruct the client to wash their hair thoroughly the night before and avoid applying oils, sprays, conditioners, or gels, as these substances impede electrode contact and electrical signal conduction. Furthermore, the client should be advised to eat a normal meal beforehand to prevent hypoglycemia, which can alter brain wave patterns, and to avoid caffeine, tea, and other stimulants for at least 8 hours prior to the examination.

Intra-Procedure Nursing Care and Monitoring

During the EEG procedure, the nurse's role focuses on client monitoring, managing provocative testing, and minimizing artifacts. The client typically reclines in a comfortable chair or bed. A technician or nurse applies a conductive paste or gel and attaches 16 to 25 electrodes to mapped areas of the scalp. The nurse must monitor the client for comfort and anxiety, as restlessness and muscle tension create movement artifacts that disrupt the recording. The procedure often incorporates activation methods designed to provoke latent abnormal brain wave patterns or seizures. These include hyperventilation (having the client breathe deeply and rapidly for 3 minutes, which induces cerebral vasoconstriction and hypocapnia) and photic stimulation (flashing a bright strobe light at varying frequencies into the client's eyes). Throughout these activation maneuvers, the nurse must closely observe the client for adverse reactions, respiratory distress, or seizure activity, remaining prepared to intervene immediately if a clinical seizure occurs.

Post-Procedure Care and Client Safety

Post-procedure care and client safety are paramount once the EEG recording is complete. The technician removes the electrodes and cleans the scalp using warm water, acetone, or a specialized solvent to remove residual conductive paste. The nurse should assist the client in washing their hair further if necessary and provide a towel. If the client's anticonvulsant or sedative medications were withheld prior to the test, the nurse must coordinate with the healthcare provider to restart these medications promptly, ensuring therapeutic blood levels are re-established to prevent rebound seizures. Because some clients experience fatigue, mild dizziness, or drowsiness—especially if sleep deprivation was used as an activation method or if sedatives were administered—safety precautions are necessary. The nurse should advise the client against driving or operating heavy machinery until they feel fully alert and recovered. Monitoring for any post-test seizures and ensuring safe ambulation complete the immediate post-procedure nursing responsibilities.

Interpretation of EEG Results and Normal vs. Abnormal Findings

The interpretation of EEG results requires distinguishing normal physiological rhythms from pathological, abnormal findings. A normal EEG demonstrates symmetrical background activity appropriate for the client's age and state of arousal, with expected alpha, beta, and sleep rhythms without focal or generalized abnormalities. Abnormal findings include epileptiform discharges, such as spike-and-wave patterns, sharp waves, and spike discharges, which strongly suggest epilepsy or a seizure disorder. Other abnormalities involve focal slowing, which points to localized structural lesions like tumors, abscesses, or infarcts, and generalized slowing, which indicates diffuse cerebral dysfunction like metabolic encephalopathy, drug toxicity, or anoxia. Asymmetry between the two cerebral hemispheres is also a key red flag for localized pathology. Nursing students must remember that an EEG is interpreted within the context of the client's overall clinical presentation, history, and physical examination, as an abnormal EEG can occasionally appear in asymptomatic individuals or normal EEGs can occur in patients with active epilepsy.

Complications, Limitations, and Nursing Considerations

While EEG is an invaluable diagnostic tool, nurses must understand its complications, limitations, and special clinical considerations. The procedure itself is extremely safe, non-invasive, and carries virtually no physical risk; electrical current does not enter the brain. However, potential complications include the provocation of a clinical seizure during activation techniques like hyperventilation or photic stimulation, requiring the nurse to be proficient in seizure precautions and emergency response. Limitations of standard surface EEGs include the inability to record electrical activity from deep brain structures, as surface electrodes only capture cortical activity. Additionally, some clients with true epilepsy may have completely normal interictal EEG recordings if they are not actively experiencing seizure activity during the test. Artifacts caused by eye blinking, muscle movement, sweating, or external electrical interference can also obscure results. Nursing considerations include providing reassurance to anxious clients, ensuring skin integrity around electrode sites, and communicating effectively with the multidisciplinary team to interpret findings accurately.
