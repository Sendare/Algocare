Nursing Process Management of Neurologic and Neurosurgical Problems

Introduction to Neurological and Neurosurgical Nursing

Neurological and neurosurgical nursing requires careful attention because the brain and spinal cord control nearly every function in the body. When a client experiences a brain injury, stroke, or spinal problem, small changes can lead to major health crises. Nurses working in this specialty must understand basic neuroanatomy to catch subtle shifts in a client's condition before permanent damage happens.

Comprehensive Neurological Assessment and Monitoring

Assessing a neurological client goes beyond taking standard vital signs. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is the universal tool nurses use to measure level of consciousness by evaluating eye opening, verbal response, and motor response. Cranial nerve checks, pupillary reaction tests, and limb strength testing give a complete picture of brain function. A sudden change in pupil size or a drop in the GCS score is often the first warning sign of rising brain pressure.

Nursing Diagnoses for Clients with Neurological Disorders

  • Ineffective Tissue Perfusion (Cerebral): Related to interrupted blood flow, brain swelling, or bleeding.
  • Ineffective Airway Clearance: Related to a depressed level of consciousness and inability to swallow or cough effectively.
  • Impaired Physical Mobility: Related to paralysis, muscle weakness, or prescribed bed rest.
  • Risk for Impaired Skin Integrity: Related to immobility, sensory loss, and moisture exposure.
  • Deficient Knowledge: Related to the new diagnosis, complex recovery process, and home care needs.

Managing Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

The skull is a rigid box containing brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid. When one of these components increases—such as blood from a hemorrhage or swelling from an injury—increased intracranial pressure (ICP) occurs. Normal ICP ranges from 5 to 15 mmHg.

To keep pressure down, nurses use specific positioning and care strategies:

  1. Head Elevation: Keep the head of the bed elevated at 30 degrees to help venous drainage from the brain.
  2. Neutral Alignment: Keep the client's neck straight; turning the head to the side kinks jugular veins and raises ICP.
  3. Avoid Straining: Prevent constipation and coughing, which spike pressure.
  4. Osmotic Diuretics: Administer medications like mannitol as ordered to draw fluid out of brain tissues.

Preoperative and Postoperative Neurosurgical Care

Before brain surgery, nurses prepare the client by completing baseline neurological assessments, explaining hair clipping procedures, and addressing intense anxiety. After surgery, the focus shifts to monitoring for surgical complications. Nurses check dressing sites for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage—which often tests positive for glucose and leaves a yellow halo around blood. Strict blood pressure control is vital because too much pressure can burst newly repaired blood vessels.

Maintaining Airway, Ventilation, and Hemodynamic Stability

Oxygen is the most critical requirement for an injured brain. Hypoxia and low blood pressure rapidly worsen brain damage. Nurses must ensure a patent airway, provide supplemental oxygen, and suction secretions gently while avoiding prolonged suctioning that triggers coughing and raises ICP. Monitoring heart rhythm and blood pressure ensures the brain receives adequate perfusion pressure.

Nutrition, Mobility, and Skin Integrity Management

Clients with long-term neurological problems face high risks for malnutrition, muscle contractures, and pressure ulcers. If a client cannot swallow safely due to cranial nerve damage, speech therapists perform evaluations, and enteral feeding tubes are placed. Nurses prevent joint contractures using splints and passive range-of-motion exercises. Frequent position changes and specialized mattresses protect fragile skin from breakdown.

Rehabilitation and Discharge Planning for Neurological Recovery

Recovery from neurological illness is a slow journey involving physical, occupational, and speech therapy. Discharge planning begins on the day of admission. Nurses coordinate with social workers and family caregivers to ensure the home environment is safe—adding ramps, bathroom grab bars, and organizing medication schedules. Educating family members on warning signs of complications ensures the client stays safe after leaving the hospital.
