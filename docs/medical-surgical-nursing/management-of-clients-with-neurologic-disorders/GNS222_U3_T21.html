Nursing Process Management of Neurologic and Neurosurgical Problems

Comprehensive Neurological Assessment

Assessing a client with a neurological condition requires a systematic approach to detect subtle shifts in cerebral function before catastrophic deterioration occurs. The foundational tool in this evaluation is the **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)**, which measures eye-opening, verbal response, and motor responsiveness. When evaluating motor function, clinicians test both upper and lower extremities for symmetry and strength, noting any asymmetry as a potential sign of focal pathology. Cranial nerve integrity is evaluated sequentially, paying close attention to pupillary light reflexes, which offer direct insight into brainstem function and intracranial pressure dynamics. Baseline data must be gathered immediately upon admission and tracked rigorously because trend analysis holds far greater clinical value than a single isolated observation.

Nursing Diagnoses and Care Prioritization

Caring for individuals with neurological deficits demands precise formulation of nursing diagnoses to guide urgent interventions. Common problems include **Ineffective Tissue Perfusion: Cerebral**, **Risk for Aspiration**, and **Impaired Physical Mobility**. Prioritization rests on the principle of airway, breathing, and circulation, elevated to account for central nervous system regulation. For instance, a client with altered consciousness faces an immediate threat to airway clearance, making airway management the primary focus over skin integrity concerns during initial resuscitation phases.

Managing Increased Intracranial Pressure

Intracranial pressure (ICP) represents the total pressure exerted by the brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid within the skull, with normal values ranging from 5 to 15 mmHg. Sustained elevations compromise cerebral perfusion pressure, triggering ischemia and potential brain herniation. - **Positioning**: Keep the head of the bed elevated 30 degrees and maintain the head in a neutral, midline alignment to promote venous drainage. - **Environmental Control**: Minimize suctioning, minimize startling noises, and space out nursing activities to prevent sudden spikes in pressure. - **Pharmacological Support**: Administer prescribed osmotic diuretics like mannitol or hypertonic saline to draw fluid out of the brain parenchyma. - **Temperature Regulation**: Prevent hyperthermia aggressively, as elevated body temperature increases metabolic demand and cerebral blood flow.

Airway, Ventilation, and Hemodynamic Stability

The central nervous system regulates autonomic cardiorespiratory functions, making neurological injury a direct threat to systemic oxygenation and blood pressure control. Clients with depressed conscious states lose protective laryngeal reflexes, requiring artificial airway placement and mechanical ventilation to prevent hypoxemia and hypercapnia—both of which cause cerebral vasodilation and worsen intracranial pressure. Hemodynamic monitoring ensures mean arterial pressure is maintained at levels capable of supporting adequate cerebral perfusion pressure. Vasopressors or volume expanders may be ordered to sustain perfusion when neurogenic shock or autonomic instability occurs.

Preoperative and Postoperative Neurosurgical Care

Neurosurgical intervention demands rigorous perioperative vigilance to safeguard brain tissue and prevent complications. Preoperative preparation centers on baseline neurological documentation, scalp preparation, and addressing client anxiety. Postoperative management focuses on monitoring for surgical site hemorrhage, managing pain without masking neurological changes, and watching for cerebrospinal fluid leaks through the nose or ears. Dressings must be inspected frequently for excessive drainage or halo signs. Any sudden headache, vomiting, or altered responsiveness must be reported immediately as a possible indicator of intracranial bleeding or acute edema.

Nutrition, Mobility, and Skin Integrity Management

Prolonged immobility and altered neurological status place clients at high risk for secondary systemic complications. Dysphagia is common, necessitating a formal swallow evaluation before any oral intake to prevent aspiration pneumonia. Enteral tube feeding may be required to meet metabolic demands. Skin breakdown occurs rapidly over bony prominences due to sensory deficits and poor peripheral perfusion, requiring scheduled turns and specialized pressure-relieving mattresses. Passive range-of-motion exercises and sequential compression devices protect against deep vein thrombosis and joint contractures while the client remains bedbound.

Rehabilitation and Discharge Planning

Recovery from a neurological or neurosurgical event extends far beyond acute hospital discharge. Multidisciplinary teams—including physical therapists, occupational therapists, speech-language pathologists, and social workers—collaborate to restore maximum functional independence. Discharge planning focuses on home safety adaptations, caregiver training, and medication management schedules. Families need thorough instruction on recognizing warning signs of neurological deterioration, managing assistive devices, and navigating community support resources to ensure long-term success and prevent unnecessary readmissions.
