Complications of Neurological Disorders

Introduction to Neurological Complications

Neurological disorders frequently trigger cascading systemic issues that extend far beyond primary brain or spinal cord pathology. When the central nervous system suffers acute injury or chronic progressive decline, regulatory mechanisms fail across multiple organ systems. A stroke, traumatic brain injury, or severe inflammatory condition does not exist in isolation; it rapidly endangers cerebral perfusion, autonomic stability, and basic metabolic functions. Recognizing these secondary threats early prevents irreversible brain tissue loss and dramatically alters patient outcomes. In Nigerian clinical practice, where resource constraints can delay advanced neuroimaging, astute bedside nursing observation remains the primary defense against devastating neurological deterioration.

Increased Intracranial Pressure and Brain Herniation

The skull is a rigid, fixed compartment housing brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid. According to the Monro-Kellie doctrine, an increase in volume of any one of these components must be offset by a decrease in the others, or intracranial pressure rises. Normal ICP ranges from 5 to 15 mmHg. When compensatory mechanisms exhaust, sustained high pressure reduces cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP), starving neurons of oxygen.

Unchecked ICP culminates in brain herniation, where brain tissue is displaced across structures:

  • Subfalcine herniation: Cingulate gyrus shifts under the falx cerebri.
  • Transtentorial (uncal) herniation: Medial temporal lobe squeezes through the tentorial notch, compressing cranial nerve III and causing a fixed, dilated pupil.
  • Tonsillar herniation: Cerebellar tonsils push downward through the foramen magnum, compressing the brainstem and arresting respiration.

Cerebral Edema and Ischemia

Cerebral edema is an abnormal accumulation of fluid within the brain parenchyma, driving up intracranial volume. It manifests primarily in two forms: vasogenic edema, caused by breakdown of the blood-brain barrier allowing serum proteins and fluid to leak into the extracellular space, and cytotoxic edema, resulting from cellular hypoxia that impairs sodium-potassium pumps, forcing water into neurons and glial cells.

As swelling intensifies, local blood vessels compress, precipitating cerebral ischemia. Deprived of glucose and oxygen, neurons undergo energy failure, anaerobic metabolism, and a toxic influx of calcium ions. This triggers a destructive cascade of free radical generation and enzymatic cell lysis. Managing edema requires meticulous control of blood pressure, osmotic agents like mannitol, and careful positioning to optimize venous drainage from the head.

Seizures and Status Epilepticus

Uncontrolled electrical discharges in the brain can emerge secondary to trauma, stroke, infection, or metabolic derangements. Acute symptomatic seizures can rapidly escalate cerebral metabolic demand far beyond oxygen supply, worsening ischemic injury.

When a seizure continues for longer than five minutes, or recurs without recovery of consciousness between episodes, it becomes status epilepticus. This is a medical emergency requiring rapid intervention to prevent permanent neuronal damage, hyperthermia, and cardiovascular collapse. First-line management typically involves rapid-acting intravenous benzodiazepines, followed by longer-acting antiepileptic drugs such as phenytoin or levetiracetam. Nursing priorities during an active seizure focus on maintaining a patent airway, protecting the patient from physical trauma, and timing the duration precisely.

Autonomic Dysreflexia and Hemodynamic Instability

Patients with spinal cord injuries at or above the T6 level are at high risk for autonomic dysreflexia, a life-threatening syndrome triggered by noxious stimuli below the level of the injury. Common triggers include a distended bladder, fecal impaction, or tight clothing.

When these visceral signals stimulate the sympathetic nervous system, blood vessels constrict below the injury, causing a dangerous surge in blood pressure. The baroreceptors detect this hypertension and signal the heart to slow down via the vagus nerve, but the brain cannot inhibit sympathetic outflow below the lesion. This results in paroxysmal hypertension, severe throbbing headache, flushing above the injury site, and profound pallor and piloerection below. Nursing management centers on immediately identifying and removing the triggering stimulus, sitting the patient upright to lower cerebral pressure, and administering rapid-acting antihypertensives if blood pressure remains critically elevated.

Impaired Airway Clearance and Aspiration Pneumonia

Neurological compromise frequently depresses the central drive to breathe and impairs cranial nerves IX and X, which coordinate swallowing and the gag reflex. Stroke, traumatic brain injury, and neuromuscular disorders like myasthenia gravis leave patients vulnerable to pooled oral secretions and food particles entering the lower respiratory tract.

When protective airway reflexes fail, aspiration pneumonia rapidly develops, marked by fever, cough, tachypnea, and worsening arterial blood gas parameters. Dysphagia screening should precede any oral intake in stroke survivors. Nurses must maintain suction equipment at the bedside, perform regular oral care to reduce bacterial load, and keep the head of the bed elevated at 30 to 45 degrees unless contraindicated.

Secondary Systemic Complications: DVT, Pressure Injuries, and Contractures

Immobility resulting from paralysis, profound sedation, or general debility triggers a cascade of mechanical and metabolic complications. Venous stasis combined with hypercoagulability creates a high risk for deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and subsequent pulmonary embolism. Skin integrity degrades rapidly over bony prominences due to sustained capillary compression, shearing forces, and moisture, leading to painful pressure injuries. Simultaneously, without passive range of motion, muscle fibers and tendons shorten, resulting in fixed joint contractures that complicate eventual rehabilitation.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring for Neurological Deterioration

Early detection of neurological decline requires systematic, repeatable evaluation tools. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) assesses eye opening, verbal response, and motor response, providing a standardized baseline for consciousness. Pupillary responsiveness, motor strength symmetry, and vital sign trends are equally critical. A classic sign of rising intracranial pressure is Cushing's triad, consisting of widening pulse pressure (rising systolic, falling diastolic blood pressure), bradycardia, and irregular respirations. This triad represents a late, ominous physiological sign that the brainstem is severely compromised. Nurses must track subtle trends rather than waiting for dramatic crashes to report changes to the medical team.

Comprehensive Nursing Management and Rehabilitation

Managing complex neurological complications requires an interprofessional approach involving nurses, physical therapists, speech-language pathologists, and physicians. Care plans must balance acute stabilization with early restorative intervention. Positioning the head in neutral alignment with the head of the bed elevated promotes cerebral venous outflow. Meticulous skin care, bowel regimens to prevent constipation, and scheduled bladder scanning or catheterization prevent secondary triggers. Rehabilitation begins at the bedside through passive movements, cognitive stimulation, and patient positioning. In Nigerian nursing wards, family caregivers often form an integral part of the care team, requiring structured education on positioning, feeding techniques, and recognizing early signs of infection or decline.
