Neoplasm of the Nervous System

Introduction and Classification of Nervous System Neoplasms

A neoplasm of the nervous system is an abnormal growth of tissue in the brain or spinal cord. These growths can be primary, meaning they start right inside the nervous system, or secondary, meaning they spread from cancer in another part of the body like the lungs or breast. In adults, secondary or metastatic tumors are actually more common than primary ones. Primary tumors are further grouped into benign, which grow slowly and do not spread, and malignant, which grow fast and invade surrounding healthy brain tissue. Even a benign brain tumor can be life-threatening because the skull is a rigid box that cannot expand. As the tumor grows, it crowds the brain and pushes against vital structures, causing dangerous pressure inside the head.

Pathophysiology and Common Tumor Types

Most primary nervous system tumors start from glial cells, which are the support cells of the brain, rather than from the nerve cells themselves. These are called gliomas.

  • Astrocytoma: Starts in star-shaped glial cells called astrocytes and can range from slow-growing to highly aggressive, like glioblastoma.
  • Meningioma: Grows from the meninges, the protective membranes covering the brain and spinal cord; these are usually slow-growing and benign.
  • Schwannoma: Affects the Schwann cells of the peripheral nerves, often impacting the auditory nerve and causing hearing loss.
  • Pituitary Adenoma: Develops in the pituitary gland, often disrupting hormone levels in the body.

As these tumors expand, they disrupt normal electrical impulses, destroy healthy brain parenchyma, and block the normal flow of cerebrospinal fluid, leading to fluid buildup known as hydrocephalus.

Clinical Manifestations and Increased Intracranial Pressure

Symptoms of a nervous system neoplasm depend heavily on where the tumor is located and how fast it grows. However, many symptoms are linked directly to increased intracranial pressure (ICP).

  • Headache: Typically worse in the morning and aggravated by straining, coughing, or changing position.
  • Nausea and Vomiting: Often projectile and not always related to food intake, caused by pressure on the brainstem.
  • Papilledema: Swelling of the optic disc seen during an eye exam due to high pressure.
  • Focal Deficits: Weakness on one side of the body, speech difficulties, or personality changes depending on the lobe affected.
  • Seizures: Common early signs as the tumor irritates surrounding cerebral cortex tissue.

A classic clinical warning sign is the Cushing triad, which consists of widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations. This triad indicates severe, life-threatening brain compression.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosing a brain or spinal tumor requires a combination of detailed neurological examinations and advanced imaging. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is the gold standard because it provides clear, high-resolution pictures of soft brain tissue, especially when paired with contrast dye. Computed Tomography (CT) scans are often used first in emergency settings to quickly check for bleeding, major swelling, or large masses. Electroencephalograms (EEGs) help check for abnormal brain wave activity if the patient has experienced seizures. Finally, a tissue biopsy, obtained either during surgery or through a needle guided by imaging, is necessary to determine the exact cellular type and grade of the tumor.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment goals for brain tumors include removing as much of the tumor as safely as possible, relieving symptoms, and improving the patient's quality of life. Surgical resection is often the first step, ranging from a formal craniotomy to open the skull, to minimally invasive stereotactic approaches. Radiation therapy uses high-energy rays to destroy remaining tumor cells or slow their growth. Chemotherapy may be given orally, intravenously, or directly into the cerebrospinal fluid, though some drugs struggle to cross the protective blood-brain barrier. Additionally, corticosteroid medications like dexamethasone are frequently prescribed to rapidly reduce brain swelling and ease pressure symptoms before and after surgery.

Nursing Care Plan and Postoperative Management

Postoperative nursing care for a patient who underwent brain surgery focuses on frequent neurological checks and preventing spikes in intracranial pressure. Nurses perform Glasgow Coma Scale assessments regularly to monitor changes in consciousness level. The patient's head should typically be elevated between 30 and 45 degrees to encourage venous drainage from the head, but flat positioning may be required depending on the specific surgical site. Nurses must monitor dressing sites closely for cerebrospinal fluid leakage, which appears as clear or pink fluid with a halo sign. Pain management must be balanced carefully so that strong analgesics do not mask changes in the patient's mental status or suppress breathing.

Complications and Rehabilitation

Survivors of nervous system neoplasms often face long-term physical, cognitive, and emotional challenges. Common complications include persistent seizures, motor weakness, sensory losses, and cognitive deficits involving memory, concentration, and executive function. Rehabilitation requires a multidisciplinary team. Physical therapists help patients regain balance, strength, and mobility. Occupational therapists assist with activities of daily living like dressing and eating. Speech therapists evaluate and treat swallowing difficulties, known as dysphagia, as well as speech and language problems. Emotional support is also vital, as depression and anxiety are prevalent among patients adjusting to life-altering neurological changes.
