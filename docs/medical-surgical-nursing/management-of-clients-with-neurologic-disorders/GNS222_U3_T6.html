Level of Consciousness Assessment

Introduction and Physiological Basis of Consciousness

Consciousness requires the integrated function of both the cerebral hemispheres and the reticular activating system (RAS) located within the brainstem. The cerebral hemispheres govern the content of consciousness, including awareness, thought, and sensory interpretation, while the RAS maintains wakefulness and arousal. Disruption anywhere along this anatomical axis results in an altered level of consciousness. In busy Nigerian teaching hospitals, sudden shifts in neurological status frequently stem from conditions like cerebrovascular accidents, severe malaria, or traumatic head injuries from road traffic accidents. Recognizing early subtle changes prevents secondary brain injury.

Terminology of Altered States of Consciousness

Clinical documentation demands precise terminology to describe a patient's exact mental state. Vague terms like "unconscious" or "drowsy" can mean different things to different shift members.

  • Confusion: Disorientation to time, place, or person, with impaired judgment and slower thinking.
  • Lethargy: Severe drowsiness where the patient responds appropriately to questions but falls asleep again if stimulation ceases.
  • Obtundation: A state resembling mild-to-moderate stupor; the client sleeps much of the time and, when awakened, responds slowly and confusedly.
  • Stupor: Unresponsiveness from which the client can be aroused only by vigorous, repeated, and often painful physical stimuli.
  • Coma: Complete unarousable unresponsiveness where the client fails to make purposeful movement even to deep pain.

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS): Structure and Scoring

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is the universal standard for quantifying level of consciousness based on three behavioral responses. Total scores range from 3 to 15. Always document the individual component scores alongside the total so providers know if a low score is due to eye opening or motor deficits.

Alternative Assessment Tools: AVPU and FOUR Score

While the GCS is robust, alternative tools serve specific clinical environments.

  • AVPU Scale: Frequently utilized in emergency triage and pre-hospital settings. It categorizes patients into Alert, responds to Verbal stimuli, responds to Painful stimuli, or Unresponsive. It is fast but lacks the granularity of GCS.
  • FOUR Score (Full Outline of UnResponsiveness): Assesses four domains: Eye response, Motor response, Brainstem reflexes, and Respiration. It evaluates intubated patients more effectively than GCS because it replaces the verbal score with brainstem assessments like corneal and cough reflexes, scoring each domain from 0 to 4.

Step-by-Step Clinical Assessment Technique

Perform neurological assessments in a systematic progression from least invasive to most invasive stimuli. Begin by calling the patient's name in a normal tone of voice. If there is no response, gently shake their shoulder. Should verbal and tactile cues fail, apply a standardized painful stimulus such as trapezius squeeze or supraorbital pressure, while avoiding sternal rubs which cause bruising. Always check for pre-existing physical barriers like severe facial trauma, periorbital edema, or language barriers that might falsely lower scores.

Interpreting and Documenting Assessment Findings

Accurate charting requires recording the exact GCS breakdown, such as E3V4M5 = 12, along with the date, time, and associated findings like pupil size and reactivity. A sudden drop of two or more points in the GCS score is a critical red flag indicating rising intracranial pressure or ongoing hemorrhage, requiring immediate notification of the medical team. Consistent serial checks reveal upward or downward trajectories far better than isolated single measurements.

Nursing Interventions for the Client with Altered Consciousness

Patients with depressed levels of consciousness face severe physiological vulnerabilities. Protecting the airway is paramount; position the client in a lateral recovery position or elevate the head of the bed to 30 degrees to minimize aspiration risk and promote venous drainage from the brain. Regular oral hygiene, passive range of motion exercises, and meticulous skin care prevent complications like pneumonia, contractures, and pressure ulcers during prolonged periods of immobility.
