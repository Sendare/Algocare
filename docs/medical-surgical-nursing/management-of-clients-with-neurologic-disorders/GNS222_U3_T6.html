Level of Consciousness Assessment

Introduction and Underlying Physiology of Consciousness

Consciousness relies on two main components: wakefulness and awareness. Wakefulness is the physical state of being open-eyed and responsive, while awareness involves higher thinking, memory, and interacting with the environment.

Inside the brain, these processes depend heavily on the Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS). The ARAS is a network of nerve pathways starting in the brainstem and shooting signals up into the cerebral cortex. When the ARAS functions properly, it keeps the whole brain alert. If damage occurs to the brainstem or to widespread areas of both cerebral hemispheres, the ARAS fails to send these vital arousal signals, and a person slips into an altered state of consciousness.

Terminology of Altered States of Consciousness

Nurses use specific terms to describe a patient's level of alertness. Vague phrases like 'unresponsive' are not enough; accurate terminology ensures clear communication among healthcare team members.

  • Alert: Fully awake, responsive, and oriented to person, place, and time.
  • Lethargic: Drowsy or sluggish, but can wake up and answer questions properly when spoken to, though they may drift back to sleep easily.
  • Obtunded: Very sleepy, difficult to awaken, and when awake, they act confused and respond slowly to questions.
  • Stupor: Unconscious most of the time; they only rouse briefly when subjected to strong, painful physical stimuli, making brief groaning or withdrawal movements.
  • Coma: Completely unresponsive to any stimulus, including severe pain. They cannot be awakened and show no purposeful movement.

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is the most widely used tool for assessing a patient's neurological state over time. It looks at three independent behavioral responses: Eye Opening, Verbal Response, and Motor Response.

  1. Eye Opening (E): Scored from 4 (spontaneous) to 1 (none).
  2. Verbal Response (V): Scored from 5 (oriented) to 1 (none). If a patient has a breathing tube or severe swelling preventing speech, the nurse notes this as 'V T' or 'V E'.
  3. Motor Response (M): Scored from 6 (obeys commands) to 1 (none). Testing motor response provides the most reliable indicator of brain survival and recovery.

The total score ranges from 3 (deep coma or brain death) to 15 (fully alert). A score of 8 or lower generally indicates a severe brain injury requiring advanced airway protection.

Alternative Assessment Tools: AVPU and FOUR Score

While the GCS is the gold standard for trauma and general neurology, other tools offer unique advantages in specific clinical settings.

The AVPU scale is a quick screening tool used primarily by first responders and in emergency rooms:

  • A: Alert
  • V: Responds to Voice
  • P: Responds to Pain
  • U: Unresponsive

Meanwhile, the FOUR Score (Full Outline of UnResponsiveness) overcomes some limitations of the GCS. It evaluates four components: Eye response, Motor response, Brainstem reflexes (pupil and corneal reflexes), and Respiration pattern. Because it tests brainstem reflexes and breathing patterns, the FOUR score is extremely helpful in intensive care units for patients who are intubated or suspected of having brain death.

Step-by-Step Clinical Assessment Technique

Performing a reliable neurological check requires a structured, gentle, yet firm approach. Always start with the least invasive method before moving to painful stimuli.

  1. Verbal approach: Enter the room and call the patient's name in a normal tone of voice. If there is no response, speak louder or gently touch their shoulder.
  2. Tactile stimulus: If the patient remains asleep, apply a gentle shake to the arm.
  3. Painful stimulus: If voice and touch fail, apply a safe, standardized painful stimulus. Acceptable techniques include trapezius squeeze, supraorbital pressure, or pencil pressure on the nail bed. Avoid sternal rubbing because it leaves visible bruising and is painful without clinical necessity.

Always document the exact stimulus used so that future assessments can be compared accurately.

Interpreting Findings and Nursing Interventions

A sudden drop in a patient's level of consciousness is often the very first sign of increased intracranial pressure (ICP), hypoxia, or hemorrhage. Recognizing these trends prevents catastrophic brain damage.

When caring for an unresponsive client, safety and airway maintenance are the absolute priorities:

  • Airway clearance: Keep the patient's head elevated to thirty degrees to encourage venous drainage from the brain. Suction airways carefully only when necessary, as suctioning can temporarily spike intracranial pressure.
  • Skin and eye care: Unconscious patients cannot blink, leading to dry, scratched corneas. Apply prescribed lubricating eye drops and tape eyes shut if needed. Turn the client regularly to prevent pressure injuries.
  • Communication: Never assume an unconscious client hears nothing. Always speak to the patient before touching them, explaining every care action you perform.
