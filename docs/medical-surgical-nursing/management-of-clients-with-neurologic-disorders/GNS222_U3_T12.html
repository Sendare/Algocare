Cerebrovascular Disorders

Introduction to Cerebral Circulation and Stroke

Cerebrovascular disorders disrupt blood flow to the brain, robbing neurons of essential oxygen and glucose. The brain relies on a constant, uninterrupted supply of blood delivered by two main pairs of vessels: the internal carotid arteries anteriorly and the vertebral arteries posteriorly. These systems meet at the base of the brain to form the Circle of Willis, a vital safety net that can reroute blood if one vessel becomes blocked. When blood flow stops even for a few minutes, brain cells begin to die, leading to the clinical syndrome known as a stroke or cerebrovascular accident (CVA). Understanding this circulatory highway helps nurses quickly figure out which part of the brain is damaged based on the patient's symptoms.

Pathophysiology of Ischemic and Hemorrhagic Strokes

Strokes fall into two main categories: ischemic and hemorrhagic. Ischemic strokes account for the vast majority of cases and happen when a blood clot blocks an artery supplying the brain. This blockage can be a thrombosis forming right inside a narrowed brain vessel, or an embolus traveling from somewhere else in the body, most commonly the heart. Deprived of blood, the local brain tissue enters an ischemic penumbra—a zone of threatened, underperfused tissue that can be saved if blood flow is restored quickly. Conversely, hemorrhagic strokes occur when a weakened blood vessel ruptures, spilling blood directly into the brain tissue or surrounding spaces. This sudden bleeding increases intracranial pressure (ICP), crushes delicate brain structures, and deprives distal areas of normal perfusion. While ischemic strokes starve the brain, hemorrhagic strokes drown and compress it.

Risk Factors in the Local Context

Stroke risk factors are divided into non-modifiable traits like age, sex, and family history, and modifiable conditions that can be treated or controlled. In West Africa and across Nigeria, hypertension remains the single most dangerous modifiable risk factor for both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes. Poorly controlled high blood pressure places chronic stress on fragile cerebral arteries, leading to micro-aneurysms or vessel wall thickening. Other major contributing factors growing in prevalence include type 2 diabetes mellitus, high cholesterol, cigarette smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, and sedentary lifestyles. Public health nurses play a vital role in community outreach by screening individuals for high blood pressure and educating populations on diet and lifestyle modifications.

Clinical Manifestations and Neurological Assessment

Stroke symptoms appear suddenly because brain tissue dies within minutes of losing blood. The specific signs depend entirely on which artery is blocked and which part of the brain is deprived of oxygen. Nurses use the FAST acronym—Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call—to quickly spot warning signs.

  • Motor deficits: Paralysis or weakness on one side of the body, known as hemiplegia or hemiparesis, typically occurs on the side opposite the brain lesion.
  • Communication disorders: Damage to the left hemisphere often causes aphasia, affecting speech production or comprehension.
  • Sensory changes: Patients may experience numbness, loss of position sense, or neglect, where they completely ignore one side of their body or environment.
  • Visual disturbances: Loss of vision in half of the visual field is common, known as homonymous hemianopsia.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging

Rapid diagnosis is critical because treatment for an ischemic stroke differs completely from treatment for a hemorrhagic stroke. The primary diagnostic tool in emergency settings is a non-contrast computed tomography (CT) scan of the brain. A CT scan quickly rules out bleeding, which must be done before doctors can consider giving clot-dissolving medications. If the CT scan shows no blood, medical teams may proceed with more detailed imaging like a CT angiogram or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to see the exact location of the blockage. Blood tests are also drawn immediately to check clotting times, blood sugar levels, and platelet counts, ensuring patient safety before any acute interventions begin.

Acute Medical and Surgical Management

Management in the hyper-acute phase focuses on saving living brain tissue in the penumbra and stabilizing vital signs. For ischemic strokes, treatment centers on restoring blood flow. Eligible patients arriving within a strict time window may receive thrombolytic therapy, commonly known as IV alteplase, to dissolve the clot. In cases involving large vessel blockages, surgeons may perform an endovascular thrombectomy, threading a catheter through an artery in the groin up to the brain to physically pull the clot out. For hemorrhagic strokes, management shifts toward lowering blood pressure carefully, reversing any blood-thinning medications, and sometimes performing emergency surgery to relieve dangerous intracranial pressure.

Nursing Care Plan and Rehabilitation Strategies

Once the acute danger passes, the nursing focus shifts to rehabilitation and preventing secondary complications. Nurses coordinate a multidisciplinary team including physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and speech-language pathologists.

  • Aspiration precautions: Swallowing difficulties (dysphagia) are common; nurses must perform bedside swallow screenings before offering any food or water.
  • Mobility and skin care: Paralyzed limbs need careful positioning, and patients must be turned regularly to prevent pressure ulcers.
  • Bladder management: Urinary retention or incontinence requires a structured toileting schedule rather than rushing to insert indwelling catheters.
  • Communication support: Nurses should use simple sentences, give patients extra time to respond, and use communication boards if needed.

Complications and Secondary Prevention

Survivors of a stroke face ongoing risks that require careful monitoring and lifestyle adjustments. Common long-term complications include spasticity in paralyzed muscles, deep vein thrombosis (DVT) from immobility, chronic shoulder pain, and post-stroke depression. Secondary prevention aims to stop another stroke from happening. This involves keeping blood pressure within target ranges, taking prescribed antiplatelet medications like aspirin or anticoagulants, managing blood sugar levels, and quitting smoking. Nurses provide crucial education to patients and their families, ensuring they understand medication schedules and recognize early warning signs of recurrent strokes.
