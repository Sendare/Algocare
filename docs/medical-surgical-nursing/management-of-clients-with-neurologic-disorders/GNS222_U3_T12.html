Cerebrovascular Disorders

Introduction to Cerebrovascular Disorders and Cerebral Circulation

Cerebrovascular disorders encompass a broad spectrum of neurological conditions that temporarily or permanently affect the blood supply to the brain. The brain is metabolically demanding, requiring a constant, uninterrupted delivery of oxygen and glucose to maintain normal cellular function. It receives roughly 15 to 20 percent of the resting cardiac output through two main arterial pairs: the internal carotid arteries anteriorly and the vertebral arteries posteriorly. These vessels converge at the base of the brain to form the Circle of Willis, a vital anastomotic network that provides collateral circulation should a primary vessel become occluded. When blood flow drops below critical thresholds—typically less than 18 milliliters per 100 grams of brain tissue per minute—neural cells rapidly depolarize, triggering a cascade of biochemical changes that lead to cell death if perfusion is not swiftly restored.

Pathophysiology and Classification: Ischemic vs. Hemorrhagic Strokes

Strokes are broadly categorized into two distinct etiological groups: ischemic and hemorrhagic. Ischemic strokes account for the vast majority of cases and occur when an arterial blockage limits blood flow to a region of the brain. This obstruction can be thrombotic, stemming from atherosclerotic plaque buildup within cerebral vessels, or embolic, originating from a distant site such as the heart in atrial fibrillation. The resulting tissue damage features a central core of irreversible necrosis surrounded by the ischemic penumbra, a metabolically sluggish zone of viable tissue that remains salvageable with prompt therapeutic intervention. Conversely, hemorrhagic strokes arise when a weakened cerebral blood vessel ruptures, spilling blood directly into the brain parenchyma or subarachnoid space. This extravasation increases intracranial pressure, causes direct mechanical compression of delicate neural tracts, and triggers secondary cellular injury through vasospasm and neurotoxicity.

Risk Factors and Etiology in the Nigerian Context

The epidemiological profile of stroke in Nigeria displays distinct characteristics, notably a high burden of disease presenting at younger ages compared to Western populations. Modifiable risk factors drive this trend heavily.

  • Hypertension: The single most powerful and prevalent precursor, frequently underdiagnosed or poorly controlled in local communities.
  • Diabetes Mellitus: Accelerates macrovascular and microvascular atherosclerosis throughout the cerebral circulation.
  • Cardiac Disorders: Rheumatic heart disease and non-valvular atrial fibrillation predispose patients to cardioembolic events.
  • Lifestyle Factors: Increasing urbanization has ushered in diets high in sodium and saturated fats, coupled with sedentary routines and tobacco use.

Non-modifiable factors such as advancing age, male gender, and a family history of vascular disease interact with these environmental pressures to elevate population vulnerability.

Clinical Manifestations and Neurological Assessment

Clinical presentations vary according to the specific cerebral artery involved and the exact size of the ischemic or hemorrhagic lesion. The anterior circulation, supplied by the internal carotid system, commonly produces classic signs like contralateral hemiparesis, sensory loss, and aphasia if the dominant hemisphere is affected. Posterior circulation strokes involve the vertebrobasilar system, presenting with vertigo, ataxia, cranial nerve deficits, and visual disturbances. A rapid bedside screening tool such as the FAST acronym (Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call for help) is essential for early identification. Formal neurological evaluation utilizes standardized scales like the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) to quantify deficit severity, monitor clinical trajectories, and guide acute management decisions.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging

Prompt diagnostic imaging is mandatory upon hospital arrival to differentiate ischemic stroke from intracranial hemorrhage, as administration of thrombolytic therapy in a hemorrhagic event would be catastrophic. Non-contrast computed tomography (CT) of the brain is the gold standard initial imaging modality because it is widely available, rapid, and highly sensitive in detecting acute blood. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) offers superior visualization of early ischemic changes and small posterior fossa strokes, though scan times are longer. Vascular imaging techniques, such as CT angiography or magnetic resonance angiography, help identify large vessel occlusions, aneurysms, or sites of stenosis. Ancillary laboratory investigations include complete blood counts, coagulation profiles, electrolyte panels, and random blood glucose to rule out stroke mimics like hypoglycemia.

Acute Medical and Surgical Management

Management in the acute phase focuses on preserving salvageable brain tissue and preventing secondary systemic complications. For eligible patients presenting within a strict therapeutic window—usually within 4.5 hours of symptom onset—intravenous thrombolytic therapy with alteplase is administered to dissolve the offending thrombus. Patients with large vessel occlusions may undergo endovascular mechanical thrombectomy to physically retrieve the clot. Blood pressure management requires extreme caution; permissive hypertension is often maintained initially to preserve collateral cerebral perfusion, unless levels exceed safe thresholds or thrombolysis is planned. Surgical interventions, such as decompressive craniectomy, may be life-saving for patients experiencing malignant cerebral edema or large cerebellar infarctions that threaten brainstem compression.

Nursing Care Plan and Rehabilitation Strategies

Comprehensive nursing care is pivotal in preventing complications and promoting functional recovery during the post-acute phase. Nurses conduct regular neuro-checks, monitor hemodynamic stability, and maintain strict glycemic control. A primary nursing priority is protecting airway integrity and preventing aspiration, which requires formal swallowing screenings before initiating oral intake. Mobility support involves frequent repositioning every two hours, early mobilization protocols, and proper joint positioning to prevent contractures and pressure injuries. Rehabilitation must begin as soon as the patient is medically stable, engaging a multidisciplinary team of physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and speech-language pathologists to retrain motor skills, cognitive function, and communication abilities.

Complications and Secondary Prevention

Survivors of cerebrovascular events face significant risks of short- and long-term complications, including aspiration pneumonia, deep vein thrombosis, contractures, depression, and recurrent stroke. Secondary prevention strategies target the underlying vascular etiologies to avert future events. Pharmacological interventions include antiplatelet agents like aspirin or clopidogrel for non-cardioembolic ischemic strokes, oral anticoagulants for atrial fibrillation, and aggressive statin therapy for dyslipidemia. Blood pressure control remains the cornerstone of long-term risk reduction. Lifestyle counseling focusing on smoking cessation, dietary modifications, and regular physical activity must be reinforced continuously, involving family caregivers in education to ensure sustained adherence upon discharge.
