Assessment of Neurological Function

Introduction and Health History in Neurological Assessment

A systematic neurological assessment begins long before you touch the patient. It starts the moment you walk into the room, observing their posture, gait, and facial symmetry. The health history focuses on uncovering current complaints alongside past medical events that could impact the nervous system. Common chief complaints include headaches, dizziness, weakness, numbness, and changes in vision or speech. When taking this history, inquire about the exact onset, duration, and triggering factors of these symptoms.

Explore past medical history for conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, previous stroke, head trauma, or central nervous system infections like meningitis, which is particularly relevant in sub-Saharan clinical practice. Ask about a family history of neurological disorders, as genetic predispositions play a major role in conditions like epilepsy and Huntington's disease. Finally, screen for environmental and lifestyle factors, including alcohol use, illicit substance exposure, and occupational toxins, all of which directly affect neurological integrity.

Assessment of Mental Status and Level of Consciousness

Evaluating mental status provides a window into cerebral hemisphere function. Begin with the level of consciousness (LOC), observing whether the patient is alert, lethargic, obtunded, stuporous, or comatose. The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is the gold standard for tracking LOC changes objectively across three distinct parameters:

  • Eye Opening: Renders a score from spontaneous opening (4) to no response (1).
  • Verbal Response: Renders a score from fully oriented (5) to incomprehensible sounds or none (1).
  • Motor Response: Renders a score from obeying commands (6) to no movement or flaccid (1).

Beyond consciousness, assess cognitive functions including orientation to person, place, and time, memory (immediate, recent, and remote), abstract thinking, and language capabilities. Note any signs of aphasia, where a patient struggles to produce or comprehend speech due to cortical damage.

Evaluation of Cranial Nerves

Systematic testing of the twelve cranial nerves helps localize lesions within the brainstem or peripheral pathways. Each nerve has specific clinical testing methods:

  1. CN I (Olfactory): Test one nostril at a time with familiar non-irritating scents like coffee or soap.
  2. CN II (Optic): Assess visual acuity using a Snellen chart and check visual fields by confrontation.
  3. CN III, IV, VI (Oculomotor, Trochlear, Abducens): Test extraocular movements in six cardinal directions of gaze and check direct and consensual pupillary light reflexes.
  4. CN V (Trigeminal): Assess facial sensation with a cotton wisp across ophthalmic, maxillary, and mandibular zones; test motor function by palpating temporal and masseter muscles while the patient clenches their teeth.
  5. CN VII (Facial): Observe symmetry while the patient smiles, frowns, puffs cheeks, and raises eyebrows.
  6. CN VIII (Acoustic): Perform the whispered voice test or Weber and Rinne tuning fork tests.
  7. CN IX and X (Glossopharyngeal and Vagus): Assess the gag reflex, soft palate elevation during phonation ('say ah'), and swallowing ability.
  8. CN XI (Spinal Accessory): Test strength by asking the patient to shrug shoulders against resistance and turn their head against applied hand pressure.
  9. CN XII (Hypoglossal): Inspect the tongue for fasciculations or deviation, asking the patient to stick it straight out.

Motor Function, Tone, and Coordination Assessment

Evaluating motor pathways involves observing muscle bulk, tone, and strength, alongside fine and gross motor coordination. Inspect muscle size for symmetry, looking for signs of atrophy that suggest lower motor neuron disease or disuse. Assess muscle tone by passively moving the limbs through a relaxed range of motion; expect a slight, smooth resistance. Abnormalities include flaccid muscles (hypotonia) or rigid, spastic muscles (hypertonia).

Test muscle strength on a standard 0 to 5 medical scale:

  • 0/5: No movement detected.
  • 1/5: Trace movement or flicker of contraction.
  • 2/5: Movement possible only with gravity eliminated.
  • 3/5: Movement against gravity, but not against resistance.
  • 4/5: Movement against some resistance, but weaker than normal.
  • 5/5: Normal strength against full resistance.

Coordination relies on the cerebellum, basal ganglia, and sensory systems. Test rapid alternating movements like palm-patting, point-to-point testing with finger-to-nose or heel-to-shin drills, and observe the patient's unassisted gait.

Sensory Function Examination

Testing sensory pathways requires assessing both superficial and deep sensation while the patient's eyes remain closed. Because sensory pathways cross over to the opposite side of the brain, a lesion in the cerebral cortex or spinal cord manifests deficits on the contralateral side of the body.

Superficial sensations include pain and temperature, carried by the spinothalamic tracts, and light touch, carried by the dorsal columns. Use a clean safety pin and a wisp of cotton, asking the patient to report if the stimulus feels sharp or dull, and whether they can feel the light touch. Deep and cortical sensations evaluate integrative brain processing:

  • Stereognosis: Place a familiar object like a coin or key in the patient's hand and ask them to identify it by touch alone.
  • Graphesthesia: Trace a number or letter on the palm of the hand and ask the patient to name it.
  • Proprioception: Gently move the patient's big toe up or down and ask them to state the direction without looking.
  • Extinction: Touch corresponding bilateral areas simultaneously to see if the patient perceives both.

Reflex Testing and Grading

Reflexes provide objective data on the integrity of both deep and superficial neural pathways. A deep tendon reflex (DTR) involves a monosynaptic arc where striking a tendon stretches the muscle, sending signals to the spinal cord and back to trigger contraction.

Standard deep tendon reflexes include the biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, patellar, and achilles reflexes. Use a reflex hammer with a brisk, direct stroke. Grade DTRs on a universal 0 to 4 scale:

  • 0: No response (absent).
  • 1+: Diminished or hyporeactive.
  • 2+: Average, normal response.
  • 3+: Brisk, possibly but not necessarily abnormal.
  • 4+: Hyperactive with clonus (rhythmic oscillations).

Superficial reflexes include the plantar reflex (Babinski test), elicited by stroking the lateral aspect of the sole from the heel toward the ball of the foot. In healthy adults, toes curl downward (plantar flexion). An abnormal upgoing big toe with fanning of the other toes is a positive Babinski sign, indicating an upper motor neuron lesion.

Diagnostic Tests and Advanced Neurological Monitoring

Physical assessment findings are confirmed and expanded through diagnostic imaging and electrophysiological testing. Computed Tomography (CT) scans provide rapid cross-sectional imaging of brain tissue, making it the primary tool for identifying acute hemorrhagic strokes, skull fractures, and space-occupying lesions. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) offers superior soft-tissue contrast, invaluable for detecting early ischemic stroke, demyelinating plaques in multiple sclerosis, and subtle brain tumors.

Electrophysiological studies like the Electroencephalogram (EEG) record spontaneous electrical activity of the cerebral cortex, essential for diagnosing seizure disorders and brain death. Lumbar punctures allow analysis of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for infection, bleeding, or elevated pressure. Finally, continuous intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring is critical in intensive care settings for managing traumatic brain injury and preventing secondary ischemic damage.

Nursing Responsibilities and Documentation in Care

Executing a neurological assessment requires clinical vigilance, accurate documentation, and proactive nursing intervention. Document baseline findings thoroughly so subsequent shifts can detect subtle neurological declines early. For patients with altered consciousness or intracranial pathology, perform serial neurological checks at regular intervals—every hour or every few hours as ordered.

Monitor vital signs closely, watching for Cushing's triad, a late and dangerous sign of increased intracranial pressure characterized by widening pulse pressure, bradycardia, and irregular respirations. Ensure patient safety by maintaining seizure precautions, keeping side rails padded and suction equipment functional. Communicate abrupt changes in GCS or focal motor deficits immediately to the attending physician to prevent permanent neurological injury.
