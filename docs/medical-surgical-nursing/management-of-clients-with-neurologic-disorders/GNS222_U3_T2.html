Assessment of Neurological Function

Introduction to Neurological Assessment

A neurological assessment evaluates the nervous system to detect early signs of dysfunction, track disease progression, or measure recovery after an injury. The process is systematic, moving from a broad overview of the patient's general appearance down to specific, localized testing of cranial nerves, reflexes, and sensory pathways.

Baseline data gathered during the initial admission assessment proves vital for comparison later. Subtle changes in a patient's responses often serve as the earliest warning signs of rising intracranial pressure or failing cerebral perfusion. Nurses perform focused neurological checks frequently in acute care settings, while chronic care environments may require less frequent monitoring unless a clinical status shift occurs.

Health History and Symptom Analysis

Taking a thorough health history uncovers clues about neurological conditions that physical testing alone might miss. Nurses must inquire about chief complaints such as headaches, dizziness, numbness, tingling, weakness, or sudden changes in vision and speech.

Exploring the timeline of symptoms helps differentiate acute events, like a stroke, from chronic progressive disorders, like multiple sclerosis. A complete history also covers past medical conditions, family history of neurological illnesses, and current medications, since many drugs can alter mental status or cause tremors.

Assessment of Mental Status and Level of Consciousness

Evaluating mental status begins the moment the nurse greets the patient. The assessment covers appearance, behavior, cognitive function, and thought processes. Orientation to person, place, time, and situation gives a quick snapshot of cortical function.

Level of consciousness is measured using standardized tools when a patient is lethargic, stuporous, or comatose.

  • Alert: Awake, responsive, and fully aware of surroundings.
  • Lethargic: Drowsy, drifts off to sleep when not actively stimulated.
  • Stuporous: Responds only to vigorous, painful physical stimuli, making minimal sounds.
  • Comatose: Unresponsive completely, failing to make purposeful movements even to pain.

Evaluation of Cranial Nerves

The twelve cranial nerves manage special senses, facial movements, and head sensations. Testing them requires specific, simple tools like a penlight, a tongue depressor, and aromatic substances.

  • CN I (Olfactory): Test the ability to identify familiar mild scents with each nostril separately.
  • CN II (Optic): Check visual acuity and visual fields using a chart or finger counting.
  • CN III, IV, VI (Oculomotor, Trochlear, Abducens): Assess pupillary reaction to light and extraocular eye movements.
  • CN V (Trigeminal): Test facial sensation to light touch and jaw clenching strength.
  • CN VII (Facial): Observe facial symmetry while the patient smiles, frowns, and puffs cheeks.
  • CN VIII (Vestibulocochlear): Evaluate hearing using the whisper test.
  • CN IX, X (Glossopharyngeal, Vagus): Assess swallowing, speech articulation, and the gag reflex.
  • CN XI (Accessory): Test shoulder shrug against resistance.
  • CN XII (Hypoglossal): Check tongue movement and midline alignment.

Motor Function and Coordination Assessment

Motor assessment looks at muscle size, tone, strength, and balance. Nurses test muscle strength by asking the patient to push and pull against resistance, grading the result on a standard scale from zero (no movement) to five (full strength against full resistance).

Coordination and cerebellar function rely on smooth, accurate movements. Common bedside tests include the finger-to-nose test, rapid alternating movements of the hands, and heel-to-shin sliding. Balance is evaluated through stance and gait observation, keeping safety measures like a gait belt nearby to prevent falls.

Sensory Function Examination

Sensory testing evaluates the integrity of peripheral nerves, spinal tracts, and sensory areas of the cerebral cortex. Testing is usually performed with the patient's eyes closed to prevent visual guidance.

Pain and temperature sensations travel along one set of pathways, while light touch, vibration, and proprioception travel along others. Cortical sensory functions include stereognosis (identifying a familiar object placed in the hand by touch) and graphaesthesia (identifying a number or letter traced on the palm). Comparing opposite sides of the body helps pinpoint areas of nerve injury or spinal cord damage.

Reflex Testing and Grading

Reflex testing measures the quick, involuntary response of the nervous system to a stimulus. Deep tendon reflexes are elicited using a reflex hammer, checking joints like the biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, patellar, and Achilles tendons.

Reflexes are graded on a scale where 2+ is considered normal. A grading guide helps document findings:

  • 0: Absent or no response.
  • 1+: Diminished or hypoactive.
  • 2+: Brisk and normal.
  • 3+: Brisk, possibly indicative of disease.
  • 4+: Hyperactive with clonus, indicating central nervous system disease.

Superficial reflexes, such as the plantar response, are also checked. A normal adult response to plantar stimulation is downward curling of the toes.

Diagnostic Tests and Advanced Neurological Monitoring

Physical assessments are often paired with diagnostic imaging and tests to confirm neurological diagnoses. Computed tomography (CT) scans and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide detailed structural views of brain tissue, identifying tumors, bleeding, or ischemia.

Electroencephalography (EEG) records electrical activity in the brain, which is crucial for diagnosing seizure disorders. For critically ill patients, advanced monitoring devices measure intracranial pressure directly inside the skull. Nurses caring for patients undergoing these tests must ensure safety, monitor equipment lines, and explain procedures to reduce anxiety.

Nursing Responsibilities and Documentation in Neurological Care

Accurate, timely documentation ensures safe continuity of care for patients with neurological conditions. Nurses must record exact observations rather than vague terms; writing that a patient opens eyes to speech is far more informative than simply writing 'patient is confused.'

Reporting sudden declines to the primary provider is a critical nursing duty. Because neurological status can fluctuate rapidly, structured handoff communication tools help relay accurate baseline data, current assessment scores, and recent trends between shifts without missing vital details.
