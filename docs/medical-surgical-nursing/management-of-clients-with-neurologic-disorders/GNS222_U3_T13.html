Trauma of the Nervous System

Anatomy and Etiology of Nervous System Trauma

Nervous system trauma encompasses injuries to the brain and spinal cord, structures rigidly protected by the skull and vertebral column. The central nervous system lacks significant regenerative capacity, meaning structural damage often carries permanent deficits. - **Road Traffic Collisions**: Motor vehicle and motorcycle crashes remain the leading driver of traumatic brain and spinal injuries across Nigerian tertiary hospitals. - **Falls**: High-altitude falls from buildings, trees, or construction sites frequently cause spinal compression fractures and severe closed head injuries. - **Violence**: Assaults involving blunt force or penetrating objects, such as stab and gunshot wounds, directly lacerate neural tissue. - **Sports and Occupational Hazards**: Industrial accidents in factories or poorly regulated mining sites contribute significantly to acute spinal trauma.

Pathophysiology: Primary Versus Secondary Injury

Understanding nervous system trauma requires dividing the pathological timeline into two distinct phases. **Primary injury** occurs at the exact moment of impact. Physical forces shear axons, contuse brain parenchyma, and fracture vertebrae. This initial damage is largely irreversible. Following the impact, **secondary injury** cascades begin. This destructive process evolves over hours and days, driven by ischemia, intracellular calcium overload, free radical generation, and localized inflammation. Cerebral edema swells the brain tissue within the fixed vault of the skull, driving up intracranial pressure (ICP) and choking off remaining blood flow. In spinal cord trauma, edema and hemorrhage compromise spinal perfusion, expanding the zone of tissue necrosis far beyond the initial mechanical tear.

Classification and Clinical Presentation of Injuries

Brain and spinal injuries present with diverse clinical pictures depending on the anatomical site and severity. Traumatic brain injuries are broadly categorized as **concussion** (brief disruption of mental status without structural imaging findings), **contusion** (bruising of the brain tissue), and **hemorrhage** (epidural, subdural, or intracerebral bleeding). Spinal cord injuries are classified as either **complete** (total loss of motor and sensory function below the lesion level) or **incomplete** (some preservation of sensory or motor fibers below the injury site). A classic clinical sign of expanding intracranial lesions is **Cushing's triad**, featuring bradycardia, widening pulse pressure, and irregular respirations—a late, ominous indicator of brainstem compression.

Neurological Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Rapid, systematic assessment dictates the survival and recovery trajectory of trauma clients. The **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** evaluates eye opening, verbal response, and motor performance, providing a standardized measure of consciousness depth. Pupil reactivity and symmetry offer quick clues regarding brainstem integrity and herniation risks. Diagnostic imaging confirms anatomical damage. **Computed Tomography (CT) scans** are the gold standard for acute head trauma, rapidly identifying skull fractures, blood collections, and midline shifts. **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)** delivers superior detail for soft tissues, making it invaluable for evaluating spinal cord edema and ligamentous stability once the client is stabilized.

Emergency Medical and Surgical Management

Initial emergency management follows the standard **ABCDE** trauma framework, placing immediate emphasis on airway protection and cervical spine immobilization. Hypoxia and systemic hypotension drastically worsen secondary brain injury and must be aggressively prevented. Surgical interventions aim to decompress neural structures and stop bleeding. **Craniotomy** or **craniectomy** allows evacuation of intracranial hematomas and provides room for swollen brain tissue to expand safely. For spinal injuries, surgical stabilization and decompression fix unstable vertebrae, relieving mechanical pressure on the spinal cord and enabling early mobilization.

Nursing Care Plan and Interventions

Comprehensive nursing care targets the prevention of secondary insults and the maintenance of physiological stability. - **Airway and Ventilation**: Maintain a patent airway, suction cautiously to prevent coughing spikes that raise ICP, and monitor oxygen saturation continuously. - **Positioning**: Elevate the head of the bed to 30 degrees to promote venous cerebral drainage, keeping the client's neck in neutral alignment without twisting. - **Thermoregulation**: Prevent fever aggressively, as elevated body metabolic rates increase cerebral oxygen demand and worsen ischemic injury. - **Skin and Joint Care**: Turn immobile clients regularly every two hours, apply pressure-relieving devices, and provide passive range-of-motion exercises to prevent contractures.

Complications and Long-Term Rehabilitation

Clients surviving acute nervous system trauma face significant long-term complications. **Autonomic dysreflexia** is a medical emergency occurring in clients with high spinal cord injuries, triggered by noxious stimuli like a distended bladder or bowel impaction, resulting in severe hypertension and pounding headache. **Post-traumatic epilepsy**, hydrocephalus, and chronic spasticity frequently complicate brain injury recovery. Rehabilitation requires a multidisciplinary team approach involving physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech pathologists, and nurses. The goal is to maximize residual function, foster psychological adaptation, and reintegrate the survivor into family and community life.
