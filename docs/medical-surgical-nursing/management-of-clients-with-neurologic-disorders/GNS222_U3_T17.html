Infections Affecting the Nervous System

Introduction and Routes of Entry

The central nervous system (CNS), comprising the brain and spinal cord, is normally well-protected from infection by specialized barriers. The blood-brain barrier and the bony structures of the skull and vertebral column form a formidable defense. However, pathogens manage to breach these defenses through distinct routes, leading to severe clinical conditions. Understanding how these microorganisms travel helps nurses anticipate complications and assess patient risk factors accurately.

  • Hematogenous spread: Pathogens enter the bloodstream from a primary infection elsewhere in the body, such as the lungs or skin, and cross the blood-brain barrier.
  • Direct extension: Infection spreads directly from nearby structures like the middle ear, mastoid air cells, or sinuses, or enters through penetrating head trauma and surgical procedures.
  • Perineural route: Certain viruses and toxins travel backward along peripheral nerves directly into the central nervous system, bypassing the bloodstream entirely.

Meningitis: Bacterial, Viral, and Fungal

Meningitis is an inflammation of the meninges, the protective membranes covering the brain and spinal cord. The disease profile varies significantly depending on the causative organism. Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency requiring rapid intervention, as it can cause death within hours. Viral meningitis, also called aseptic meningitis, is generally less severe and resolves on its own with supportive care. Fungal meningitis occurs less frequently and is typically seen in individuals with compromised immune systems, such as those living with untreated HIV.

Encephalitis and Brain Abscesses

While meningitis affects the outer membranes, encephalitis is an acute inflammation of the actual brain parenchyma itself. It is most often caused by viral infections, including herpes simplex virus, arboviruses spread by mosquitoes, and rabies. Patients often present with altered mental status, personality changes, and focal neurological deficits.

A brain abscess is a localized collection of pus within the brain tissue, usually resulting from a bacterial infection spreading from the sinuses, teeth, or lungs, or following head trauma. As the abscess grows, it acts like a space-occupying lesion, increasing intracranial pressure and causing localized deficits depending on the affected area of the brain.

Clinical Manifestations and Neurological Assessment

Infections of the nervous system present with classic signs that every nurse must recognize quickly. The classic triad of bacterial meningitis includes severe headache, high fever, and a stiff neck, known as nuchal rigidity. Patients also frequently exhibit photophobia, nausea, and vomiting.

During physical assessment, nurses check for specific meningeal irritation signs. Kernig's sign is present when the patient feels pain and resistance when the knee is straightened with the hip flexed at a right angle. Brudzinski's sign is positive when passive flexion of the patient's neck causes involuntary flexion of their hips and knees. In infants, signs may be much more subtle, showing up as irritability, poor feeding, and a bulging fontanelle.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Lumbar Puncture

Diagnosing a central nervous system infection requires a combination of laboratory tests and neuroimaging. Blood cultures are drawn to identify systemic bacteremia. Computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging of the head is often performed first to rule out a shift in brain tissue before any spinal procedure.

The definitive diagnostic test is a lumbar puncture to analyze cerebrospinal fluid. During this procedure, the patient is positioned in a lateral recumbent fetal position. The clinician inserts a needle into the subarachnoid space, usually between the third and fourth lumbar vertebrae, to measure opening pressure and collect fluid samples for cell counts, protein, glucose, Gram stain, and culture.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Treatment

Medical management relies on prompt administration of targeted antimicrobial therapy. For bacterial meningitis, broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics are started immediately after blood cultures are drawn, even before lumbar puncture results return if delays are anticipated. Once the specific organism is identified, therapy is tailored accordingly.

Corticosteroids, such as dexamethasone, are often given before or with the first dose of antibiotics to reduce neurological complications like hearing loss, especially in cases of pneumococcal meningitis. Antiviral drugs like acyclovir are standard for viral encephalitis caused by herpes simplex virus. Supportive care includes managing fever, controlling seizures with antiepileptic medications, and reducing elevated intracranial pressure.

Nursing Care Plan and Interventions

Nursing care for a patient with a nervous system infection centers on vigilant monitoring, protecting safety, and promoting recovery. Nurses must perform frequent neurological assessments using the Glasgow Coma Scale to detect subtle signs of increasing intracranial pressure or failing status early.

  • Infection control: Implement droplet precautions for suspected bacterial meningitis until the patient has received antibiotics for at least twenty-four hours.
  • Environment management: Keep the patient's room dark and quiet to minimize sensory stimulation and ease headaches caused by photophobia.
  • Hemodynamic monitoring: Track strict intake and output, monitor vital signs closely, and maintain safe positioning with the head of the bed elevated moderately to promote venous drainage from the brain.

Complications and Public Health in Nigeria

Nervous system infections can leave survivors with permanent disabilities, including hearing loss, intellectual impairment, paralysis, and seizure disorders. In severe cases, brain herniation leads rapidly to brain death.

In public health terms, particularly in regions like the meningitis belt of Nigeria, seasonal outbreaks of meningococcal meningitis pose a major challenge during the dry, dusty harmattan season. Overcrowding and dry mucous membranes facilitate transmission. Public health strategies rely heavily on mass vaccination campaigns with conjugate vaccines, public health education on early reporting, and prompt distribution of prophylactic antibiotics to close contacts during community outbreaks.
