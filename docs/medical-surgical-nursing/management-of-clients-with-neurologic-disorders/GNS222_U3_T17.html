Infections Affecting the Nervous System

Introduction and Overview of Nervous System Infections

The central nervous system, consisting of the brain and spinal cord, is normally protected from pathogens by the rigid skull, vertebral column, and the selective blood-brain barrier. Despite these barriers, various microorganisms can invade neural tissues, resulting in devastating clinical syndromes. Infections affecting the nervous system are classified anatomically based on the specific site of involvement. Meningitis involves inflammation of the protective membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord. Encephalitis represents an infection of the actual brain parenchyma, whereas a brain abscess is a localized, pus-filled collection within the brain tissue itself. These conditions constitute critical medical emergencies because the rigid cranial vault cannot accommodate swelling without causing dangerous increases in intracranial pressure. Prompt recognition and differentiation guide therapeutic success and prevent permanent neurological deficits or death.

Pathophysiology and Routes of Entry

Microorganisms breach the central nervous system through several distinct pathways. Hematogenous spread is the most frequent route, where pathogens enter the bloodstream from a primary site such as the respiratory tract, skin, or gastrointestinal system, eventually crossing the blood-brain barrier. Alternatively, pathogens can spread directly from adjacent structures through contiguous extension, such as severe otitis media, mastoiditis, sinusitis, or penetrating cranial trauma. Direct inoculation also occurs during neurosurgery or via infected medical devices like external ventricular drains. Once inside the subarachnoid space or brain parenchyma, the offending agent triggers a robust immune response. White blood cells release cytokines and inflammatory mediators, increasing vascular permeability, causing cerebral edema, and disrupting normal cerebrospinal fluid flow.

Bacterial, Viral, and Fungal Meningitis

  • Bacterial Meningitis: Often referred to as purulent meningitis, this is a rapidly progressive and highly lethal medical emergency. Common causative agents include *Streptococcus pneumoniae* and *Neisseria meningitidis*. It produces a thick exudate around the brain base, leading to cranial nerve palsies and obstructive hydrocephalus.
  • Viral Meningitis: Also known as aseptic meningitis, this form is generally less severe than bacterial meningitis and is frequently caused by enteroviruses. Patients experience full recovery with supportive care alone.
  • Fungal Meningitis: Subacute or chronic in presentation, fungal infections like *Cryptococcus neoformans* frequently affect immunocompromised individuals, such as patients with advanced HIV/AIDS, requiring prolonged antifungal therapy.

Encephalitis and Brain Abscesses

While meningitis affects the outer coverings of the brain, encephalitis and brain abscesses involve the neural parenchyma itself. Encephalitis is widespread inflammation of the brain tissue, most commonly caused by viruses such as herpes simplex virus, arboviruses, and rabies. Patients often present with altered mental status, personality changes, and focal neurological deficits like seizures. In contrast, a brain abscess is a focal, encapsulated collection of pus resulting from localized bacterial or fungal infection. Common sources include dental infections, endocarditis, or direct trauma. As the abscess enlarges, it acts as a space-occupying lesion, elevating intracranial pressure and causing focal deficits depending on the affected lobe. Surgical drainage combined with high-dose antimicrobial therapy is usually required for abscess resolution.

Clinical Manifestations and Neurological Assessment

The clinical presentation of nervous system infections reflects meningeal irritation and increased intracranial pressure. Classical signs include severe headache, projectile vomiting, photophobia, and fever. Physical examination relies on eliciting specific meningeal signs:

  • Kernig's sign: Resistance to knee extension when the patient's hip is flexed at a 90-degree angle.
  • Brudzinski's sign: Involuntary flexion of the hips and knees when the examiner passively flexes the patient's neck.
  • Nuchal rigidity: Stiff neck caused by involuntary muscle spasm of the paraspinal muscles, making passive or active forward bending of the neck extremely painful.

In addition to these signs, nurses must perform serial neurological assessments using the Glasgow Coma Scale to detect early signs of herniation or cognitive decline.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Lumbar Puncture

Definitive diagnosis of central nervous system infections requires a combination of laboratory and imaging studies. Lumbar puncture is the cornerstone diagnostic procedure, allowing analysis of cerebrospinal fluid for opening pressure, white blood cell counts, protein levels, and glucose concentrations. In bacterial infections, cerebrospinal fluid typically reveals elevated opening pressure, markedly elevated protein, depressed glucose, and a predominance of neutrophils. Viral infections show normal glucose with lymphocyte predominance. Before performing a lumbar puncture, neuroimaging such as a computed tomography scan or magnetic resonance imaging is mandatory if signs of elevated intracranial pressure or focal mass lesions are present, to prevent fatal brain herniation. Blood cultures and polymerase chain reaction assays are also essential to identify the exact causative pathogen.

Medical Management and Pharmacological Treatment

Medical management requires immediate intervention, particularly when bacterial etiology is suspected. Broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics capable of penetrating the blood-brain barrier must be initiated rapidly, often even before lumbar puncture results are finalized if diagnostic delays occur. Corticosteroids such as dexamethasone are frequently administered concurrently with or just before the first dose of antibiotics in bacterial meningitis to reduce the inflammatory response and decrease hearing loss and neurological sequelae. Antiviral medications like acyclovir are standard for suspected herpes simplex encephalitis. Supportive management focuses on maintaining airway, breathing, and circulation, controlling seizures with anticonvulsants, and managing fever and intracranial pressure.

Nursing Care Plan and Interventions

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with nervous system infections centers on continuous monitoring, symptom management, and complication prevention. Nurses must maintain strict neurological observations, documenting changes in the level of consciousness using standardized scoring tools. Given the risk of seizures, padded side rails, suction equipment, and oxygen therapy must be readily available at the bedside. Environmental stimuli should be minimized by maintaining a quiet, dimly lit room to reduce photophobia and irritability. Nurses manage strict intake and output records, monitor for signs of syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion, and ensure safe administration of intravenous antimicrobial therapy. Meticulous positioning—elevating the head of the bed to 30 degrees—promotes venous drainage and helps control intracranial pressure.

Complications, Prevention, and Public Health Considerations in Nigeria

Nervous system infections carry a high burden of long-term complications, including sensorineural hearing loss, cognitive impairment, hydrocephalus, and seizure disorders. In Nigeria, bacterial meningitis outbreaks pose a major public health challenge, particularly during the dry harmattan season in the country's meningitis belt, which spans the northern states. Transmission is facilitated by overcrowded living conditions and dry respiratory mucosa. Public health prevention relies heavily on vaccination campaigns targeting circulating strains such as *Neisseria meningitidis* serogroups A and C, alongside public health education regarding early reporting of symptoms, prompt treatment, and chemoprophylaxis for close household contacts of infected individuals.
