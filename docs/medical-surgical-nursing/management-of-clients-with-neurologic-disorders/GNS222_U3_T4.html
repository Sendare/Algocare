Physical Assessment

Introduction to Neurological Physical Assessment

The neurological physical assessment is a systematic component of a comprehensive nursing physical examination. For nursing students in Nigeria preparing for clinical rotations and professional board examinations, mastering this assessment is vital for detecting early signs of central and peripheral nervous system dysfunction. The neurological examination involves assessing several key areas: mental status, cranial nerves, motor system, cerebellar function, sensory system, and reflexes. A thorough baseline assessment allows the nurse to distinguish between normal physiological findings and acute pathological changes, such as increased intracranial pressure, stroke, or spinal cord injuries. Nurses must approach the patient systematically, ensuring a quiet, well-lit environment and utilizing appropriate assessment tools like penlights, cotton wisps, tuning forks, and percussion hammers. Effective communication is essential, especially when assessing patients from diverse cultural and linguistic backgrounds commonly found across Nigerian healthcare settings. By establishing a solid baseline, nurses play a critical role in interprofessional collaboration, ensuring timely medical interventions and improving patient outcomes in both acute care and community health settings.

Mental Status and Cognitive Function Examination

The evaluation of mental status and cognitive function provides vital insight into the cerebral cortex's integrity. For nursing students, this assessment begins the moment you interact with the patient. It encompasses evaluating the patient's appearance, behavior, level of consciousness, cognitive function, and speech. Orientation is typically assessed across four spheres: person, place, time, and situation. However, nurses must remain culturally sensitive; asking a patient their exact location or current political situation should be framed appropriately to avoid causing distress, especially in rural or older Nigerian populations who may use different frames of reference. Cognitive function testing includes evaluating immediate, recent, and remote memory. The nurse may ask the patient to recall three unrelated words after a few minutes or ask about historical events. Abstract thinking and judgment are evaluated by asking patients to interpret common proverbs or describe how they would handle a realistic scenario, such as a kitchen fire. Alterations in mental status often serve as the earliest indicator of neurological deterioration, hypoxia, metabolic imbalances, or drug toxicity, making sharp observational skills essential for safe nursing practice.

Assessment of Cranial Nerves I through XII

Assessing the twelve cranial nerves (CN I through XII) is a structured process that tests specific peripheral and central nerve pathways originating from the brainstem and cerebrum. CN I (Olfactory) is tested by asking the patient to identify familiar non-irritating odors with eyes closed. CN II (Optic) involves visual acuity testing using the Snellen chart and visual field confrontation. CN III, IV, and VI (Oculomotor, Trochlear, and Abducens) are evaluated together by checking pupillary reaction to light, accommodation, and extraocular muscle movements through the six cardinal fields of gaze. CN V (Trigeminal) has both sensory and motor components, tested by evaluating facial sensation with light touch and sharp objects, and palpating temporal and masseter muscles while the patient clenches their teeth. CN VII (Facial) is assessed by observing facial symmetry during smiling, frowning, and puffing cheeks. CN VIII (Acoustic) involves testing hearing via the whisper test, Weber, and Rinne tests. CN IX and X (Glossopharyngeal and Vagus) are evaluated together by observing the symmetrical rise of the uvula and soft palate during phonation (‘ah’), gag reflex, and swallowing. CN XI (Spinal Accessory) is tested by evaluating the strength of the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscles against resistance. Finally, CN XII (Hypoglossal) is assessed by observing tongue movement and asking the patient to repeat distinct speech sounds like 'light, dynamite'.

Motor System and Cerebellar Function Evaluation

Evaluating the motor system and cerebellar function requires examining muscle bulk, tone, strength, and coordination. For nursing students, understanding muscle tone involves feeling for passive resistance when a relaxed limb is moved through its range of motion, noting any hypotonia (flaccidity) or hypertonia (spasticity or rigidity). Muscle strength is systematically graded on a standard 0 to 5 scale, where 0 indicates no movement and 5 indicates normal power against full resistance. Testing should be performed bilaterally to compare sides. Cerebellar function is crucial for smooth, coordinated movements, balance, and posture. Tests for coordination include the finger-to-nose test and rapid alternating movements (such as patting knees with palms and backs of hands). Balance and gait are assessed through the Romberg test, where the patient stands with feet together and eyes closed for twenty seconds, observing for excessive swaying or loss of balance (a positive Romberg sign indicates sensory ataxia or cerebellar dysfunction). Additionally, asking the patient to walk in a heel-to-toe (tandem) fashion helps detect subtle gait abnormalities. Nurses must always ensure patient safety by standing close during balance testing to prevent falls.

Sensory System Testing and Pain Perception

Sensory system testing evaluates the integrity of peripheral nerves, spinal tracts, and the sensory cortex. The nurse assesses both superficial and deep sensations using various stimuli, ensuring the patient's eyes are closed throughout the examination. Superficial pain is tested using a disposable safety pin or sterile broken cotton-tipped applicator to lightly prick the skin on the face, arms, and legs, asking the patient to report if it feels sharp or dull. Light touch is assessed by gently stroking the skin with a wisp of cotton. Temperature sensation can be tested using tubes filled with warm and cold water if abnormalities are suspected. Cortical sensory functions, which require interpretation by the cerebral cortex, include stereognosis (the ability to recognize familiar objects placed in the hand by touch), graphesthesia (the ability to identify numbers or letters traced on the palm), and two-point discrimination. Vibration sense is tested by placing a vibrating tuning fork over bony prominences such as the medial malleolus or fingers, while position sense (proprioception) is assessed by passively moving the patient's finger or toe up and down and asking them to identify the direction. Accurate documentation of sensory deficits in specific dermatomal patterns helps localize spinal cord or peripheral nerve lesions.

Deep Tendon and Superficial Reflex Assessment

Reflex assessment is an objective way to evaluate the integrity of the spinal cord and peripheral nervous system pathways. Reflexes are categorized into deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) and superficial reflexes. DTRs are elicited by briskly tapping the tendon of a partially stretched muscle using a percussion hammer. Key reflexes tested include the biceps (C5-C6), triceps (C6-C7), brachioradialis (C5-C6), patellar (L2-L4), and Achilles (S1-S2) reflexes. Reflex responses are graded on a standardized 0 to 4+ scale, where 0 represents absent reflexes, 2+ is normal and active, and 4+ indicates hyperactive reflexes with clonus, which often points to an upper motor neuron lesion. Superficial reflexes include the abdominal reflexes and the plantar reflex (Babinski sign). The plantar reflex is tested by stroking the lateral aspect of the sole of the foot from the heel moving forward across the ball of the foot. In healthy adults, a normal response is plantar flexion of the toes (curling inward). A positive Babinski sign—characterized by dorsiflexion of the great toe and fanning of the other toes—is abnormal in adults and indicates an upper motor neuron lesion of the corticospinal tract, though it is a normal physiological finding in infants under one year of age.

Evaluating the Level of Consciousness Using the Glasgow Coma Scale

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is a universally accepted clinical tool used to reliably measure and document the level of consciousness in patients with acute brain injury, trauma, or altered mental status. For nursing students, mastering the GCS is a critical clinical competency. The scale evaluates three behavioral parameters: Eye Opening (E), Verbal Response (V), and Motor Response (M). Eye opening is scored from 4 (spontaneous) to 1 (none). Verbal response is scored from 5 (oriented) to 1 (none/no sound). Motor response is scored from 6 (obeys commands) to 1 (none/flaccid). The individual scores are summed to give a total GCS score ranging from 3 to 15. A score of 15 indicates a fully alert and oriented patient, while a score of 8 or less typically indicates severe neurological impairment, often necessitating immediate airway protection and advanced life support measures. Nurses must document each component separately alongside the total score (e.g., E4V5M6 = 15) to provide a clear clinical picture of the patient's exact deficits. Trend monitoring using the GCS is essential for detecting sudden neurological decline and communicating effectively with the medical team.

Documentation and Nursing Responsibilities in Neurological Findings

Accurate documentation and professional nursing responsibilities regarding neurological findings are paramount for patient safety and continuity of care. Following a comprehensive physical assessment, nurses must record their findings promptly, objectively, and using standardized terminology. Subjective descriptions like 'patient seems confused' should be replaced with specific, observable data such as 'patient disoriented to time and place, scores 13 on the GCS (E4, V4, M5).' Documentation must include baseline neurological vital signs, pupillary size and reactivity, motor strength grades, sensory status, and reflex responses. Any asymmetry, sudden changes, or signs of neurological decline must be reported immediately to the attending physician or healthcare provider using standardized communication tools like SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation). In Nigerian clinical environments, maintaining clear, legal nursing records protects both the patient and the practitioner. Furthermore, nurses bear the ethical responsibility of providing compassionate care, educating the patient and their family about ongoing monitoring, and ensuring a safe environment free from hazards for patients with altered sensory, motor, or cognitive function.
