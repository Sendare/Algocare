Physical Assessment

Introduction and Preparation for Neurological Assessment

A neurological physical assessment evaluates the central and peripheral nervous systems through a systematic examination. Preparation begins with establishing rapport and ensuring a quiet environment free from distractions. The examination follows a structured sequence: mental status, cranial nerves, motor system, sensory system, and reflexes. Comparing symmetrical body parts throughout the procedure helps identify focal deficits or unilateral abnormalities. Universal precautions and infection control must be maintained, especially when testing sensory modalities involving skin contact.

Mental Status and Higher Cognitive Function

Evaluating mental status provides insight into cerebral cortex functioning. Assessment parameters encompass appearance, behavior, cognition, and thought processes. Orientation is tested by asking about person, place, time, and situation. Memory is evaluated across immediate recall, recent events, and remote past. Abstract thinking and judgment give clues about frontal lobe integrity. In Nigerian clinical settings, adapting questions to the patient's primary language and cultural background prevents misinterpreting normal communication styles as cognitive impairment.

Systematic Examination of Cranial Nerves

The twelve cranial nerves are tested sequentially to assess brainstem and peripheral nerve pathways. Each nerve has specific clinical testing methods:

  • CN I (Olfactory): Test patency of nasal passages, then use non-irritating scents like coffee or soap with eyes closed.
  • CN II (Optic): Assess visual acuity using a Snellen chart and check visual fields by confrontation.
  • CN III, IV, VI (Oculomotor, Trochlear, Abducens): Inspect pupillary reaction to light, accommodation, and cardinal fields of gaze.
  • CN V (Trigeminal): Test facial sensation with light touch and sharp objects, and assess masseter muscle strength by clenching teeth.
  • CN VII (Facial): Observe symmetry during facial movements like smiling, frowning, and puffing cheeks.
  • CN VIII (Vestibulocochlear): Perform the whisper test or tuning fork tests for hearing.
  • CN IX and X (Glossopharyngeal, Vagus): Assess the gag reflex, swallow function, and symmetrical rise of the soft palate with phonation.
  • CN XI (Accessory): Test sternocleidomastoid and trapezius muscle strength against resistance.
  • CN XII (Hypoglossal): Observe tongue movement, symmetry, and midline protrusion.

Motor System and Cerebellar Function

Motor assessment evaluates muscle bulk, tone, and strength, graded on a standardized scale from 0 (no movement) to 5 (normal movement against full resistance). Cerebellar function governs balance, coordination, and smooth execution of movement. Key coordination tests include finger-to-nose, rapid alternating movements, and heel-to-shin testing. Balance is evaluated using the Romberg test, where the patient stands with feet together and eyes closed; a significant loss of balance is a positive sign indicating sensory ataxia or vestibular dysfunction. Always ensure safety by standing close to prevent falls during balance testing.

Sensory System Testing and Pain Perception

Sensory testing maps out the integrity of peripheral nerves, spinal tracts, and the sensory cortex. Assessment covers superficial pain, light touch, temperature, vibration, and position sense. Cortical sensory functions—such as stereognosis, graphesthesia, and two-point discrimination—evaluate the parietal lobe's ability to interpret complex sensory inputs. Patients should keep their eyes closed during all sensory trials to prevent visual cueing. Practitioners must use disposable instruments, such as sterile pins, for pain perception testing to prevent cross-contamination between patients.

Reflex Assessment: Deep Tendon and Superficial

Reflexes provide objective data regarding spinal reflex arcs and upper motor neuron integrity. Deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) are elicited by briskly striking a muscle tendon with a percussion hammer. Commonly tested DTRs include the biceps, triceps, brachioradialis, patellar, and achilles reflexes. Responses are graded on a 0 to 4+ scale, where 2+ represents normal. Superficial reflexes, such as the abdominal and plantar reflexes, test spinal cord pathways closer to the skin surface. A normal plantar response produces downward curling of the toes, while an abnormal Babinski sign—upward extension of the great toe and fanning of the others—indicates upper motor neuron disease in adults.

Evaluating Level of Consciousness with the Glasgow Coma Scale

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is a standardized tool used to objectively quantify a patient's level of consciousness after head trauma or acute neurological decline. It evaluates three distinct behavioral responses:

  1. Eye Opening Response (E): Spontaneous (4), to speech (3), to pain (2), or none (1).
  2. Best Verbal Response (V): Oriented (5), confused (4), inappropriate words (3), incomprehensible sounds (2), or none (1).
  3. Best Motor Response (M): Obeys commands (6), localizes pain (5), withdraws from pain (4), abnormal flexion (3), extensor response (2), or none (1).

The total score ranges from 3 to 15. A score of 8 or less typically indicates severe coma and warrants immediate advanced airway management.

Documentation and Clinical Responsibilities

Accurate documentation of neurological findings is critical for tracking patient progress and identifying early signs of neurological deterioration. Entries must be objective, specific, and clear, avoiding vague descriptors like 'normal' or 'fine' when precise measurements such as centimeter pupil sizes or exact GCS scores are available. Nurses must promptly report acute changes—such as a sudden drop in GCS score or unequal, sluggish pupils—to the attending physician. Maintaining consistency in documentation formatting across shifts ensures seamless continuity of care in busy clinical environments.
