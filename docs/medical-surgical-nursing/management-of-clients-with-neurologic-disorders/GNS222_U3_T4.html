Physical Assessment

Introduction to Neurological Physical Assessment

A neurological physical assessment evaluates the central and peripheral nervous systems to establish a baseline and detect acute abnormalities. Nurses perform this examination during admission, shift handovers, and whenever a patient's condition changes. The process requires a quiet environment, proper lighting, and active patient cooperation. A systematic approach ensures that subtle neurological deficits are not missed. The examination integrates several distinct components, moving from higher cognitive functions down to deep tendon reflexes.

Mental Status and Cognitive Function Examination

Assessing mental status begins the moment the nurse greets the patient. Evaluation covers appearance, behavior, mood, and cognitive abilities such as memory, attention, and abstract thinking.

  • Orientation: Assessing awareness of person, place, and time.
  • Attention and Memory: Testing immediate recall, recent memory, and remote memory using simple word lists or historical facts.
  • Thought Process: Evaluating whether speech is logical, coherent, and goal-directed.

Assessment of Cranial Nerves I through XII

Testing the twelve cranial nerves evaluates specific functions of the head, neck, and brainstem. Each nerve requires a targeted bedside test.

  • CN I (Olfactory): Test the ability to identify familiar non-irritating odors with eyes closed.
  • CN II (Optic): Assess visual acuity using a chart and visual fields by confrontation.
  • CN III, IV, VI (Oculomotor, Trochlear, Abducens): Check pupillary reaction to light and cardinal fields of gaze.
  • CN V (Trigeminal): Test facial sensation to light touch and clenching strength of jaw muscles.
  • CN VII (Facial): Observe symmetry during smiling, frowning, and puffing cheeks.
  • CN VIII (Acoustic): Test hearing with the whispered voice test.
  • CN IX, X (Glossopharyngeal, Vagus): Assess the gag reflex and symmetrical rise of the soft palate.
  • CN XI (Spinal Accessory): Test shoulder shrug and head turn against resistance.
  • CN XII (Hypoglossal): Observe tongue movement and symmetry.

Motor System and Cerebellar Function Evaluation

The motor examination evaluates muscle size, tone, strength, and involuntary movements. Strength is commonly graded on a standardized scale from 0 (no movement) to 5 (full strength against resistance).

Cerebellar function tests coordination, balance, and posture. Key tests include the finger-to-nose test, rapid alternating movements, and the heel-to-shin test. Balance is assessed using the Romberg test, where the nurse observes the patient standing with feet together and eyes closed for twenty seconds. A positive Romberg sign, characterized by severe swaying or loss of balance, indicates sensory ataxia or cerebellar dysfunction.

Sensory System Testing and Pain Perception

Sensory testing maps out the integrity of peripheral nerves, spinal tracts, and sensory cortex processing. Testing should be performed with the patient's eyes closed.

  • Light Touch: Tested using a wisp of cotton stroked lightly over the skin.
  • Pain and Temperature: Tested using a safe disposable pin to assess sharp versus dull sensation.
  • Vibration and Proprioception: Tested using a tuning fork placed over bony prominences or by moving the patient's fingers and toes up and down.
  • Cortical Sensory Functions: Includes stereognosis, the ability to identify a familiar object placed in the hand by touch alone.

Deep Tendon and Superficial Reflex Assessment

Reflex testing provides information on the integrity of the spinal cord pathways. Deep tendon reflexes (DTRs) are elicited using a percussion hammer and graded on a scale from 0 (absent) to 4+ (hyperactive with clonus), with 2+ being normal.

The plantar reflex is a superficial reflex tested by stroking the lateral sole of the foot. A normal adult response is downward curling of the toes. An upward response (Babinski sign) indicates upper motor neuron disease.

Evaluating the Level of Consciousness Using the Glasgow Coma Scale

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is a standardized tool used to objectively quantify a patient's level of consciousness based on three behavioral responses: eye opening, verbal performance, and motor responsiveness. The total score ranges from 3 (deep coma or unresponsive) to 15 (fully alert and oriented).

  • Eye Opening: Rated from spontaneous (4) to none (1).
  • Verbal Response: Rated from oriented conversation (5) to none (1).
  • Motor Response: Rated from obeys commands (6) to none (1).

A declining GCS score is often the earliest sign of increasing intracranial pressure or neurological deterioration.

Documentation and Nursing Responsibilities in Neurological Findings

Accurate and timely documentation of neurological assessments is a vital nursing responsibility. Notes must be clear, objective, and specific, avoiding vague phrases like 'patient is normal.' Findings should include exact GCS scores, pupil size and reactivity, limb strength grades, and orientation status. Any sudden change from the previous baseline must be communicated immediately to the primary healthcare provider using structured communication tools.
