Imaging Procedures

Introduction and Standard Radiography of the Central Nervous System

Diagnostic imaging allows visualization of intracranial and spinal structures without surgical intervention. Standard radiography, including plain skull and spinal X-rays, remains an initial tool in trauma settings or bone pathology assessment. - **Skull Radiography**: Visualizes fractures, erosion of the sella turcica, and calcified pineal glands, though largely superseded by advanced cross-sectional imaging for soft tissue injuries. - **Spine Radiography**: Evaluates vertebral alignment, disk space narrowing, fractures, and degenerative bony changes following trauma or chronic back pain. Examiners frequently contrast the rapid bone assessment of plain radiography with the superior soft-tissue detail offered by CT and MRI modalities.

Computed Tomography (CT) in Neurological Practice

Computed tomography utilizes rotating X-ray beams and computer processing to generate cross-sectional slices of the brain and spine. It is the frontline imaging modality in emergency neurological events due to its speed and wide availability across Nigerian tertiary hospitals. - **Non-Contrast CT (NCCT)**: The gold standard for rapidly ruling out acute intracranial hemorrhage in suspected stroke or acute head trauma. - **Contrast-Enhanced CT**: Involves administering an iodine-based radiopaque agent to highlight vascular lesions, tumors, or areas of blood-brain barrier breakdown. A critical exam pitfall involves missing subtle early signs of ischemic stroke on an unenhanced CT, as acute infarcts may appear normal within the first few hours of symptom onset.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and Advanced Techniques

Magnetic Resonance Imaging employs powerful magnetic fields and radiofrequency pulses to produce high-resolution anatomical images without ionizing radiation. It excels in visualizing posterior fossa structures, demyelinating plaques, and early ischemic changes. - **T1-Weighted Images**: Fluid appears dark (hypointense), making this sequence useful for anatomical structure delineation. - **T2-Weighted Images**: Fluid appears bright (hyperintense), effectively highlighting edema, inflammation, and pathology. - **Diffusion-Weighted Imaging (DWI)**: Highly sensitive for detecting acute cerebral ischemia within minutes of onset by measuring the random Brownian motion of water molecules. Clients with metallic implants, pacemakers, or severe claustrophobia present significant clinical challenges requiring alternative diagnostic pathways.

Vascular Imaging: Cerebral Angiography

Cerebral angiography provides a detailed roadmap of the intracranial and extracranial blood vessels. Traditional digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is an invasive procedure involving catheter insertion through the femoral or radial artery, guided into the carotid or vertebral arteries, followed by the injection of iodinated contrast. - **Indications**: Evaluation of aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), vasospasm, and vessel stenoses. - **Alternatives**: Non-invasive modalities such as Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA) and Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA) offer vascular visualization with lower procedural risks, though DSA remains the diagnostic gold standard for fine vessel architecture and endovascular interventions.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Comprehensive nursing preparation ensures patient safety, optimizes image quality, and minimizes procedural anxiety. - **Allergy Screening**: Inquire specifically about sensitivities to iodine, shellfish, or contrast media prior to CT and angiography. - **Renal Function Evaluation**: Check baseline serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) to prevent contrast-induced nephropathy. - **Metal and Device Screening**: Screen for pacemakers, cochlear implants, aneurysm clips, and shrapnel before MRI entry. Remove all jewelry, hairpins, and transdermal medication patches containing metallic foil. - **Informed Consent**: Ensure the client or legal surrogate understands the risks, benefits, and alternatives, particularly for invasive angiographic procedures.

Intra-Procedure Care and Client Safety

During neuroimaging procedures, the nurse maintains continuous vigilance over client positioning, comfort, and physiological stability. - **Immobilization**: Educate the client on the absolute necessity of remaining still. Movement causes motion artifacts, degrading image diagnostic quality and potentially necessitating repeat scans. - **Communication**: Utilize intercom systems and panic buttons during enclosed MRI scans to reassure claustrophobic individuals. - **Sedation Management**: Monitor vital signs and oxygen saturation continuously when procedural sedation is administered for uncooperative or anxious clients. - **Contrast Reactions**: Observe for acute allergic responses to contrast media, ranging from mild urticaria to severe anaphylaxis.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Complication Prevention

Post-imaging care focuses on monitoring for delayed complications associated with contrast administration and invasive vascular access. - **Hydration Promotion**: Encourage high oral fluid intake or administer intravenous fluids to flush contrast media rapidly through the renal system, reducing nephrotoxicity risks. - **Vascular Site Monitoring**: Following cerebral angiography, inspect the femoral or radial puncture site frequently for hematoma formation, active bleeding, or pseudoaneurysms. Palpate distal pulses and assess extremity perfusion regularly. - **Neurological Checks**: Perform serial neurological assessments to detect early signs of stroke or embolization resulting from vascular catheter manipulation.
