Imaging Procedures

Introduction and Basic Skull/Spine Radiography

Diagnostic imaging allows healthcare teams to visualize structures inside the skull and vertebral column without invasive surgery. Plain radiography, commonly known as an X-ray, serves as a quick, accessible first-line screening tool for structural bone changes.

  • Skull Radiography: Detects major fractures, bone erosion, or skull deformations caused by trauma or chronic pressure.
  • Spine Radiography: Evaluates vertebral alignment, bone spurs, joint space narrowing, and stability after injuries or in degenerative conditions.

While advanced scans provide much greater detail of soft tissues like the brain and spinal cord, plain films remain valuable for rapid assessment of bony integrity.

Computed Tomography (CT) Scans

A computed tomography, or CT scan, uses rotating X-ray beams and computer processing to create cross-sectional images of the head and spine. It is the gold standard for rapid emergency assessment because it is fast, widely available, and highly effective at identifying acute bleeding and bone fragments.

  • Non-contrast CT: Performed immediately when stroke or trauma is suspected to quickly rule out acute hemorrhage.
  • Contrast-Enhanced CT: Involves injecting an iodine-based dye to highlight blood vessels, tumors, or areas of inflammation.

A common misconception is that CT scans show all types of strokes right away; however, an ischemic stroke may not appear clearly on an early non-contrast scan until hours after symptom onset.

Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

Magnetic resonance imaging uses a powerful magnetic field and radio waves to generate remarkably detailed pictures of soft tissues, including the brainstem, white matter tracts, and spinal cord. Unlike CT scans, MRI does not use ionizing radiation.

Because of the strong magnet, every person entering the scanning room must be screened for embedded metallic objects, pacemakers, and certain surgical clips. The procedure also takes significantly longer than a CT scan and requires the client to lie completely still inside a narrow, noisy tube, which can provoke severe anxiety or claustrophobia.

Cerebral Angiography and Vascular Imaging

Cerebral angiography is a specialized fluoroscopic procedure used to visualize the blood vessels of the brain. A catheter is typically inserted into the femoral artery in the groin and threaded up through the aorta into the cerebral circulation, where a radiopaque contrast agent is injected.

This technique remains the gold standard for diagnosing aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, and vessel blockages. Because it involves arterial puncture and catheter manipulation, nurses must monitor the insertion site closely afterward for bleeding, hematoma formation, and distal pulse changes to ensure adequate limb perfusion.

Functional and Metabolic Imaging: PET and SPECT

While standard scans show anatomy and structure, functional imaging evaluates how the brain works and uses energy.

  • Positron Emission Tomography (PET): Uses radioactive tracers attached to glucose to measure metabolic activity in different regions of the brain. It helps map areas affected by epilepsy, Alzheimer's disease, or tumors.
  • Single-Photon Emission Computed Tomography (SPECT): Measures regional cerebral blood flow using gamma-emitting tracers, assisting in evaluating seizure foci and stroke recovery.

These tests provide vital functional insights that guide complex neurological treatments and surgical planning.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Thorough pre-procedure preparation ensures client safety and diagnostic accuracy. The nurse must verify informed consent, check for known allergies to iodine or shellfish if contrast is planned, and assess kidney function through blood tests like creatinine and blood urea nitrogen.

Clients should also be screened for any metal objects, pregnancy status, and claustrophobia. Clear communication explaining what the client will experience—such as the loud knocking sounds of an MRI or the warm flushing sensation when contrast is injected—helps reduce anxiety and promotes cooperation during the test.

Intra-Procedure Care and Client Safety

During the imaging procedure, maintaining client safety and comfort remains the primary nursing and technical focus. For scans requiring prolonged immobility, such as an MRI, positioning aids and padding help prevent pressure injuries and nerve compression.

If the client is sedated, continuous monitoring of vital signs, oxygen saturation, and airway patency is mandatory. For conscious clients, maintaining verbal communication through intercom systems reassures them and helps minimize sudden movements that could blur the diagnostic images.

Post-Procedure Monitoring and Complication Prevention

Care continues even after the imaging equipment is turned off. Nurses monitor clients for delayed allergic reactions to contrast media, which can include rash, hives, or breathing difficulty.

For clients who received intravenous contrast, encouraging robust fluid intake helps flush the dye safely through the kidneys. When an arterial puncture was performed, frequent neurological and vascular checks of the affected limb help detect complications like hematoma or ischemia before they cause permanent harm.
