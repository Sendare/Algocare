Glasgow Coma Scale

Introduction and Purpose of the Glasgow Coma Scale

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is a standardized tool used by nurses and doctors to measure a patient's level of consciousness. Developed in 1974 by neurosurgeons in Glasgow, Scotland, it provides a simple, reliable way to communicate brain function changes over time. You will see it used constantly in emergency rooms, intensive care units, and surgical wards. Instead of vague words like 'sleepy' or 'confused,' the GCS gives a clear numerical score that everyone on the healthcare team can understand instantly.

The Three Behavioral Components of GCS

The scale breaks neurological assessment down into three distinct areas of brain activity. Each area is tested separately and given its own sub-score.

  • Eye Opening (E): Tests the function of the brainstem and arousal mechanisms.
  • Verbal Response (V): Tests higher cerebral integration, language processing, and orientation.
  • Motor Response (M): Tests descending motor pathways and represents the single best predictor of patient outcome.

Assessing Eye Opening Response

The eye-opening score ranges from 4 down to 1. You must always start with the least invasive stimulus and move down if there is no reaction.

  1. Spontaneous (4): The patient's eyes are open naturally without any prompting.
  2. To Speech (3): The eyes open when you speak to the patient or call their name.
  3. To Pain (2): The eyes open only when a physical stimulus, like a trapezius squeeze, is applied.
  4. None (1): The eyes do not open at all, even to painful stimuli.

Assessing Verbal Response

Verbal scoring ranges from 5 down to 1. It measures whether the patient is awake, aware, and able to process and communicate information.

  • Oriented (5): Knows who they are, where they are, and the current month or year.
  • Confused (4): Can converse in sentences, but answers are out of touch regarding time or place.
  • Inappropriate Words (3): Utters random words or exclamations, but no real conversation takes place.
  • Incomprehensible Sounds (2): Makes moaning, groaning, or mumbling sounds without forming actual words.
  • None (1): Makes no vocal sounds at all.

Assessing Motor Response

Motor response is scored from 6 down to 1. You evaluate what the patient does when asked to move, or when a safe, standard pain stimulus is applied.

  • Obeys Commands (6): Follows simple instructions like 'squeeze my fingers' or 'wiggle your toes.'
  • Localizes to Pain (5): Reaches purposefully toward the source of pain to push it away.
  • Withdraws from Pain (4): Pulls the limb away quickly from a painful stimulus in a flexion motion.
  • Abnormal Flexion (3): Decorticate posturing; arms bend inward toward the chest with stiff legs.
  • Extension Response (2): Decerebrate posturing; arms straighten rigidly outward with pointed toes.
  • None (1): Flaccid, with no movement at all to pain.

Calculating and Documenting the Total Score

To report a GCS, you add the individual sub-scores together. The notation should always show the breakdown alongside the total so other clinicians know how the number was reached.

For example, a patient who opens eyes to speech (3), is confused (4), and obeys commands (6) has a total score of 13, written as E3V4M6 = 13. The absolute highest score is 15 (fully awake), and the lowest possible score is 3 (deep coma or brain death). A score of 8 or lower generally indicates a severe coma where the patient may need an artificial airway to protect breathing.

Special Considerations and Clinical Pitfalls

Nurses must remember that certain physical barriers can interfere with an accurate GCS assessment. If a patient has severe facial swelling, an endotracheal tube in place, or a language barrier, you cannot test every category fairly. Never guess or leave a blank space; instead, document the interference clearly. For example, write E1-C where 'C' denotes that the eye is closed due to swelling. Always check for baseline conditions, such as chronic deafness or speech impairments, before assuming a low score reflects acute brain damage.
