Glasgow Coma Scale

Introduction and Purpose of the Glasgow Coma Scale

The Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) is a standardized neurological tool designed to assess and record the level of consciousness in a person. Developed in 1974 by neurosurgeons Graham Teasdale and Bryan Jennett in Glasgow, it provides a reliable, objective method for evaluating neurological function following head trauma, stroke, or critical illness. In busy Nigerian emergency wards and intensive care units, this tool offers a common language for multidisciplinary healthcare teams, enabling rapid communication about subtle shifts in a patient's brain function. By tracking scores over time, clinicians can instantly recognize clinical improvement or deterioration without relying on vague descriptive terms like 'lethargic' or 'stuporous'.

The Three Core Components

The GCS evaluates neurological function across three distinct behavioral domains. Each category yields a separate subscore, which combines to create the total score.

  • Eye Opening (E): Assesses the mechanisms of arousal and brainstem integrity through visual responsiveness.
  • Verbal Response (V): Measures cerebral cortex integration, orientation, and speech capability.
  • Motor Response (M): Evaluates descending pathways and the highest level of motor control, serving as the strongest predictor of patient outcome.

Scores are typically written out as components—for example, E4V5M6—rather than just a single aggregate number, because a total score of 10 derived from different component breakdowns carries vastly different clinical implications.

Eye Opening Response Assessment

Assessing eye opening involves observing whether the patient opens their eyes spontaneously or requires stimulation. The scoring ranges from 4 down to 1:

  • 4 (Spontaneous): Eyes open open independently, indicating a baseline level of wakefulness.
  • 3 (To Speech): Eyes open in response to a spoken request, command, or normal voice.
  • 2 (To Pain): Eyes open only when a noxious stimulus, such as a sternal rub or trapezius squeeze, is applied.
  • 1 (None): Eyes remain closed even with maximum painful stimulation.

Always document if facial trauma, severe periorbital edema, or bandages prevent eye assessment by noting 'Eyes Closed by Swelling' (C) rather than defaulting to a score of 1.

Verbal Response Assessment

The verbal component evaluates orientation, confusion, and speech coherence. Scoring spans from 5 to 1:

  • 5 (Oriented): The patient knows their name, current location, and the date or time.
  • 4 (Confused): Converses in sentences, but is disoriented to time, place, or person.
  • 3 (Inappropriate words): Uses occasional intelligible words, but speech lacks conversational flow or context (e.g., shouting random expletives).
  • 2 (Incomprehensible sounds): Moans, groans, or makes unintelligible sounds without forming words.
  • 1 (None): Produces no verbal sounds whatsoever.

Language barriers, aphasia, or an endotracheal tube can severely skew this assessment. Never guess a verbal score for an intubated patient; always record 'Endotracheal tube' (ET) or 'Tracheostomy' (T) to reflect that verbal scoring is impossible.

Motor Response Assessment

Motor response is the most robust component of the GCS, reflecting brainstem integrity and cortical processing. Scores range from 6 down to 1:

  • 6 (Obeys commands): Performs multi-step or simple motor tasks on request (e.g., 'Hold up two fingers').
  • 5 (Localizes to pain): Reaches purposefully toward the source of a noxious stimulus above the clavicle (e.g., pushing away a sternal rub).
  • 4 (Withdraws from pain): Flexes an arm or leg rapidly away from a painful stimulus without a purposeful attempt to remove it.
  • 3 (Flexion posturing / Decorticate): Abnormal flexion where arms pull inward toward the core with wrists and fingers flexed.
  • 2 (Extension posturing / Decerebrate): Abnormal extension where arms stiffen straight out, elbows lock, and wrists flex outward.
  • 1 (None): Flaccid limbs with no movement to painful stimuli.

Always test motor response using peripheral stimulation (like a nail-bed squeeze) if central pain yields no movement, and document which side of the body demonstrated the highest response if asymmetry exists.

Scoring Interpretation and Clinical Significance

The aggregate GCS score ranges from a minimum of 3 to a maximum of 15. Clinicians generally group scores into three categories to guide immediate clinical urgency:

  • Severe (Score 3 to 8): Typically indicates a coma, severe traumatic brain injury, or critical neurological compromise requiring airway protection and intensive monitoring.
  • Moderate (Score 9 to 12): Suggests significant impairment, confusion, or depressed consciousness requiring close neurological observation.
  • Mild (Score 13 to 15): Reflects minor head injury or normal consciousness, though patients still warrant monitoring for delayed intracranial complications.

A drop of 2 or more points in the total GCS score is a critical red flag that demands immediate medical notification, as it frequently signals rising intracranial pressure, hemorrhage, or hypoxia.

Special Considerations and Limitations

While the GCS remains an essential pillar of critical care, nurses must recognize factors that can compromise its accuracy. Alcohol intoxication, drug overdoses, severe hypothermia, profound shock, and metabolic disturbances can depress neurological function independently of structural brain injury. Furthermore, spinal cord injuries can prevent valid motor assessment in limbs below the level of the lesion. When baseline deficits exist due to pre-existing conditions like stroke, learning disabilities, or deafness, documenting these baselines prevents misinterpretation. The scale should never replace a thorough cranial nerve and pupil examination, but rather complement a comprehensive neurological nursing assessment.
