Clinical Manifestations of Neurologic Disorders

Introduction to Neurologic Assessment and Symptomatology

Neurologic symptoms arise when disease, injury, or metabolic disturbances disrupt the central or peripheral nervous systems. Because the nervous system controls everything from basic breathing to complex thought, its dysfunction manifests in diverse ways. A thorough clinical assessment requires systematically evaluating mental status, cranial nerves, motor function, reflexes, sensory pathways, and cerebellar coordination. Spotting these signs early helps nurses locate the lesion and track disease progression. Every observation, from a slight facial asymmetry to an unusual gait, provides a vital clue about neural integrity.

Alterations in Level of Consciousness and Mentation

Consciousness has two main components: arousal (wakefulness) and awareness (content of thought). When the ascending reticular activating system or both cerebral hemispheres suffer damage, consciousness fades along a predictable spectrum.

  • Confusion: Inability to think clearly or rapidly; impaired judgment.
  • Lethargy: Severe drowsiness where the patient responds slowly and drops off back to sleep.
  • Obtundation: Dullness of consciousness with minimal response to the environment; falls asleep unless stimulated.
  • Stupor: A state of deep sleep; the patient only arouses to vigorous or painful physical stimuli.
  • Coma: Unarousable unresponsiveness; no voluntary movement or meaningful response to painful stimuli.

Motor Function Deficits and Movement Disorders

Motor pathways run from the motor cortex down the spinal cord to skeletal muscles. Damage anywhere along this journey alters strength, tone, and movement.

TermDescription
HemiparesisWeakness affecting one entire side of the body
HemiplegiaComplete paralysis of one entire side of the body
FlaccidityLoss of muscle tone, making limbs feel loose and heavy
SpasticityIncreased muscle tone with resistance to passive stretch

Involuntary movements also signal basal ganglia dysfunction. These include resting tremors seen in Parkinson's disease, rapid jerky chorea, and sustained twisting postures known as dystonia.

Sensory Disturbances and Pain Manifestations

Sensory pathways carry touch, temperature, vibration, and pain signals from peripheral receptors up to the sensory cortex. When these tracts fail, patients report abnormal sensations or lose perception entirely.

  • Paresthesia: Spontaneous, unpleasant prickling or tingling, often described as 'pins and needles'.
  • Hypesthesia: Reduced ability to feel touch or temperature.
  • Anesthesia: Complete loss of all tactile sensation in an area.
  • Neuropathic pain: Burning, shooting, or electric-shock pain originating from damaged nerve tissue rather than local injury.

Cranial Nerve Dysfunction and Visual Changes

The twelve cranial nerves manage facial sensation, special senses, eye movements, and swallowing. Damage to these pathways creates distinct focal deficits. For instance, oculomotor (CN III), trochlear (CN IV), and abducens (CN VI) lesions cause double vision (diplopia) or eyes that fail to move together. Facial nerve (CN VII) injury leads to drooping of the mouth and inability to close the eye on the affected side. Visual field cuts, such as homonymous hemianopsia, point to optic tract or occipital lobe damage.

Alterations in Coordination, Balance, and Gait

Smooth, purposeful movement requires seamless communication between the cerebellum, basal ganglia, and vestibular systems. When the cerebellum fails, patients develop ataxia, marked by clumsy, uncoordinated movements. Fine motor tasks like buttoning a shirt or touching a finger to the nose become erratic. Balance suffers, leading to a wide-based, staggering walk that resembles alcohol intoxication. Nurses test this with the Romberg test, watching to see if a standing patient loses balance when closing their eyes.

Signs of Increased Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

The skull is a rigid box containing brain tissue, blood, and cerebrospinal fluid. If any one component expands—such as from a tumor, bleeding, or swelling—pressure rises inside the cranium. Early signs include subtle confusion, restlessness, and headache. As pressure mounts, vital signs shift into Cushing's triad: widening pulse pressure (rising systolic, falling diastolic BP), bradycardia, and irregular respirations. A sluggish or fixed, dilated pupil signals brainstem compression and is a medical emergency.

Autonomic and Seizure Manifestations

Uncontrolled electrical discharges in the brain trigger seizures, altering movement, sensation, or consciousness depending on the focus area. A generalized tonic-clonic seizure features stiffening followed by rhythmic jerking and loss of consciousness. Meanwhile, autonomic dysfunction disrupts involuntary control systems, leading to dramatic swings in blood pressure, profuse sweating, high fevers, and abnormal heart rhythms. In spinal cord injuries, autonomic dysreflexia can cause dangerous hypertensive spikes triggered by bladder distension or skin irritation.
