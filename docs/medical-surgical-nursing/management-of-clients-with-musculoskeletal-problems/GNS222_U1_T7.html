Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

Introduction and Underlying Physics of MRI

Magnetic Resonance Imaging is a sophisticated diagnostic tool that uses powerful magnetic fields and radiofrequency waves to generate detailed cross-sectional images of internal body structures. Unlike X-rays or computed tomography scans, MRI does not utilize ionizing radiation, making it safer for repeated imaging.

  • Main Magnetic Field: Aligns the protons within the hydrogen atoms found abundantly in water and fat molecules throughout the body.
  • Radiofrequency Pulses: Temporarily knock the aligned protons out of position, causing them to emit energy as they return to their resting state.
  • Signal Detection: Receiver coils capture the emitted energy, and a computer translates these signals into grayscale anatomical images.

Water-rich tissues like cerebrospinal fluid appear bright on certain sequences, while dense structures like cortical bone appear dark due to low proton density.

Clinical Indications and Applications

Physicians order MRI scans to evaluate soft tissue injuries, neurological disorders, and cardiovascular conditions that require superior tissue contrast. In neurological nursing, MRI remains the gold standard for visualizing demyelinating plaques in multiple sclerosis and detecting early ischemic strokes.

Orthopedic surgeons rely on MRI to assess ligament tears, meniscus damage, and occult bone fractures missed by standard radiographs. Oncology teams use contrast-enhanced scans to stage tumors and monitor treatment responses across various organ systems.

Contraindications and Patient Safety

The intense magnetic field of the scanner exerts a constant force on ferromagnetic objects, creating severe projectile hazards and biological risks. Nurses must rigorously screen clients for implanted metallic devices before allowing entry into the scanner room.

  • Absolute Contraindications: Cardiac pacemakers, implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, cochlear implants, and intracranial aneurysm clips made of ferrous metals.
  • Relative Contraindications: Pregnancy, severe claustrophobia, metallic foreign bodies in the eye, and certain tattoos containing metallic ink.
  • Loose Metal Items: Jewelry, watches, hairpins, and oxygen tanks must be removed as they can become dangerous projectiles.

Pre-Procedure Nursing Assessment and Preparation

Thorough pre-procedure preparation minimizes client anxiety and prevents adverse events inside the scanning suite. The nurse is responsible for verifying informed consent, confirming pregnancy status in women of childbearing age, and administering premedication if ordered for severe claustrophobia.

Clients must change into a hospital gown to eliminate hidden metal fastners in clothing. Nurses should instruct the client to void right before the procedure because scans often last up to an hour. Providing thorough education about the loud knocking sounds produced by gradient coils helps reduce sudden panic during the imaging session.

Intra-Procedure Care and Client Monitoring

Maintaining absolute stillness during data acquisition is vital, as even minor movements create motion artifacts that ruin diagnostic clarity. For clients experiencing severe anxiety or panic attacks in the enclosed bore, healthcare providers may administer mild sedatives.

Communication is maintained through an intercom system, and clients receive a call button to squeeze if they feel overwhelmed. Nurses monitor vital signs and visual cues through observation windows or specialized monitoring equipment compatible with high-magnetic environments.

Post-Procedure Care and Potential Complications

Most clients require little recovery time after a standard MRI scan unless they received sedatives or intravenous contrast media. If gadolinium-based contrast was administered, nurses monitor the client for allergic reactions such as hives, itching, or respiratory distress.

Patients with impaired renal function face a rare but serious risk of nephrogenic systemic fibrosis when exposed to certain gadolinium agents. Nurses encourage adequate oral fluid intake to help flush contrast agents out of the circulatory system efficiently.
