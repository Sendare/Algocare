Review of Related Anatomy and Physiology of the Musculoskeletal System

Introduction to the Musculoskeletal System

The musculoskeletal system is a remarkable biological framework composed of bones, joints, muscles, cartilage, tendons, and ligaments. This intricate network provides the physical architecture that holds our bodies upright, protects vital internal organs, and allows us to move through our environment. Bones form the rigid structural scaffolding, while skeletal muscles supply the dynamic pulling power required for everything from a gentle blink to heavy lifting. Joints act as functional pivot points, and connecting tissues like tendons and ligaments anchor muscles to bones and bones to each other. Beyond simple movement and support, this system acts as a massive physiological storehouse. Bones hold over ninety percent of the body's calcium and phosphorus, releasing these vital minerals into the bloodstream whenever systemic levels dip. Furthermore, the soft interior of certain bones houses red bone marrow, the primary site for blood cell production. Nurses must grasp these foundational concepts because almost every acute illness or chronic condition ultimately impacts mobility, independence, or mineral balance in some way.

Anatomy and Structure of Bone Tissue

Bone is a specialized form of dense connective tissue that balances incredible strength with surprising lightness. Grossly, every bone features two distinct architectural regions. The outer shell is made of compact bone, a dense and smooth layer that provides structural strength and bears the brunt of mechanical stress. Inside lies spongy bone, sometimes called trabecular or cancellous bone, which consists of a porous web of bony struts called trabeculae that lighten the skeleton and house bone marrow.

Microscopically, compact bone is organized into cylindrical units called osteons or Haversian systems. Each osteon features concentric rings of hard matrix called lamellae, surrounding a central Haversian canal that carries blood vessels and nerves. Tiny spaces between the lamellae, known as lacunae, shelter mature bone cells called osteocytes. These cells communicate with one another through microscopic channels called canaliculi, ensuring that nutrients reach every corner of the rigid matrix.

Bone Growth, Remodeling, and Mineral Homeostasis

Bones are living, dynamic organs that continuously break down and rebuild themselves throughout an individual's lifetime. This ongoing renewal process relies on three primary cell types: osteoblasts, which build new bone matrix; osteoclasts, which break down or resorb old bone tissue; and osteocytes, which monitor mechanical stress. During childhood and adolescence, bone formation outpaces resorption, leading to skeletal growth. In adulthood, a delicate balance is maintained so that bone density remains stable.

When blood calcium levels drop, parathyroid hormone triggers osteoclasts to dissolve bone matrix, releasing stored calcium into the bloodstream. Conversely, when calcium levels run too high, calcitonin prompts osteoblasts to pull calcium out of the blood and lock it away inside new bone tissue. Regular weight-bearing exercise stimulates osteoblasts to lay down extra matrix, which is why active individuals develop stronger bones than those on prolonged bed rest.

Classification and Structure of Joints

Joints, or articulations, are the meeting points between bones. They are categorized either by how much movement they allow or by the type of connective tissue holding them together.

  1. Fibrous joints: Bones are bound tightly by dense fibrous connective tissue, allowing little to no movement, such as the sutures between skull bones.
  2. Cartilaginous joints: Bones are connected by cartilage, allowing slight movement, such as the pubic symphysis or the joints between vertebrae.
  3. Synovial joints: These are the most common and freely movable joints in the body. They feature a fluid-filled joint cavity enclosed by a fibrous capsule and lined with a synovial membrane that secretes lubricating synovial fluid to reduce friction during movement.

Physiology of Skeletal Muscle Contraction

Skeletal muscle movement relies on a beautifully orchestrated cellular event known as the sliding filament theory. Each muscle fiber contains microscopic strands called myofibrils, which are made of repeating contractile units called sarcomeres. These sarcomeres contain thin actin filaments and thick myosin filaments. When a nerve impulse reaches the muscle fiber, calcium ions rush into the cell and bind to regulatory proteins on the actin filament, exposing binding sites.

Once these sites are exposed, the heads of the myosin filaments attach to the actin, pulling the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere. This shortening action causes the entire muscle to contract. Adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, is required for the myosin head to detach from actin and reset for the next pull. Without adequate ATP, muscles cannot relax, leading to the stiffness seen in conditions like rigor mortis.

Supporting Structures: Cartilage, Ligaments, and Tendons

Movement would be impossible without the specialized connective tissues that support and stabilize the skeleton.

  • Cartilage: A firm yet flexible connective tissue that caps the ends of bones inside joints, acting as a shock absorber. Because it lacks a direct blood supply, damaged cartilage heals very slowly.
  • Tendons: Tough, fibrous cords of dense regular connective tissue that anchor skeletal muscles to bones, transmitting the force generated by muscle contraction to move the skeleton.
  • Ligaments: Similar bands of fibrous tissue that connect bone to bone across a joint, stabilizing the articulation and preventing excessive or abnormal range of motion.

A common clinical misconception is that torn ligaments and cartilage heal as quickly as skinned skin; in reality, their poor blood supply makes recovery sluggish and often requires surgical intervention.

Neurological Control of Movement and Posture

Muscles cannot contract on their own; they require precise commands from the nervous system. The junction where a motor neuron meets a skeletal muscle fiber is called the neuromuscular junction. When an electrical impulse travels down the nerve, it triggers the release of a chemical neurotransmitter called acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft. Acetylcholine binds to receptors on the muscle cell membrane, sparking an electrical wave that sweeps across the muscle and prompts a contraction.

To maintain posture and smooth coordination, the brain constantly receives feedback from proprioceptors—specialized sensory receptors located within muscles, tendons, and joints that report body position to the central nervous system. This continuous loop allows nurses to stand, walk, and balance without consciously thinking about every single muscle fiber involved.

Age-Related Changes in the Musculoskeletal System

As individuals age, natural physiological shifts alter the musculoskeletal system. Bone resorption gradually overtakes bone formation, leading to a steady loss of bone mineral density. This reduction in bone mass can progress to osteopenia or severe osteoporosis, leaving bones porous, fragile, and prone to fractures—especially in the hip, spine, and wrist.

Concurrently, skeletal muscle mass and strength decline, a process known as sarcopenia. Muscle fibers are often replaced by tougher fibrous tissue and fat, reducing overall strength and flexibility. Cartilage in joints thins and loses its water content, making joints stiff and increasing the risk of osteoarthritis. Nurses play a crucial role in promoting weight-bearing exercise, adequate dietary calcium, and fall prevention strategies for older adults to maintain independence and safety.
