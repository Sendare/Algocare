Review of Related Anatomy and Physiology of the Musculoskeletal System

Introduction to the Musculoskeletal System

The musculoskeletal system forms the structural framework of the human body, providing the physical architecture required to maintain posture, protect delicate internal organs, and execute coordinated movements. This integrated network consists primarily of bones, skeletal muscles, joints, tendons, ligaments, and cartilage, all working in unison under the precise command of the nervous system. Beyond mobility and support, bones act as a vital metabolic reservoir, storing essential minerals like calcium and phosphorus while housing bone marrow responsible for hematopoiesis. Skeletal muscles generate the mechanical force needed for locomotion, simultaneously producing thermal energy to help maintain core body temperature. Understanding these foundational structures is critical for assessing mobility impairments commonly encountered in Nigerian clinical practice, ranging from traumatic fractures to degenerative arthropathies.

Anatomy and Structure of Bone Tissue

Bone tissue is a specialized form of dense connective tissue characterized by an extracellular matrix that is heavily mineralized, giving it exceptional rigidity alongside a degree of flexibility. Structurally, bones are organized into two distinct architectural regions: compact bone and spongy bone. - **Compact (Cortical) Bone**: Forms the dense, hard outer shell of bones. It provides maximum protection and strength, structured in repeating microscopic units called **osteons** or Haversian systems. - **Spongy (Cancellous) Bone**: Located inside the compact bone, particularly at the expanded ends of long bones. It consists of a honeycomb-like network of trabeculae that reduces overall skeletal weight while effectively distributing mechanical stress. Microscopically, the matrix contains specialized cells: **osteoblasts** build new bone matrix, **osteocytes** maintain mature bone tissue, and **osteoclasts** resorb bone. In clinical settings, the balance between these cellular activities dictates fracture healing and structural integrity.

Bone Growth, Remodeling, and Mineral Homeostasis

Bone is a remarkably dynamic tissue that undergoes continuous restructuring throughout an individual's lifespan. **Bone remodeling** is the lifelong process where mature bone tissue is removed by osteoclasts and new bone tissue is formed by osteoblasts. This process allows the skeleton to adapt to changing mechanical loads, repair micro-damages from daily activities, and release calcium into the bloodstream when systemic levels drop. During childhood and adolescence, longitudinal bone growth occurs at the epiphyseal plates, where cartilage proliferation is continually replaced by bone tissue. Once adult stature is reached, these plates close and ossify. Calcium regulation is tightly coupled with this remodeling cycle; hormones such as parathyroid hormone and calcitonin act directly on bone cells to control calcium deposition and resorption, ensuring stable neuromuscular and cardiac function.

Classification and Structure of Joints

Joints, or articulations, are the functional junctions between bones that determine the degree and direction of movement possible in the skeletal system. Physiology of Skeletal Muscle Contraction

Skeletal muscle contraction transforms chemical energy into mechanical force through a highly organized molecular process known as the **sliding filament theory**. Each muscle fiber contains repeating functional units called **sarcomeres**, composed primarily of thin actin filaments and thick myosin filaments. When an action potential travels down a motor neuron, it triggers the release of acetylcholine across the neuromuscular junction. This electrical signal sweeps across the muscle cell membrane and stimulates the release of stored calcium ions from the sarcoplasmic reticulum. Calcium binds to troponin, shifting tropomyosin away and exposing binding sites on actin. Myosin heads then attach to actin, forming cross-bridges that pull the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere, shortening the muscle. Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) is required both to detach the myosin head and to pump calcium back into storage during relaxation.

Supporting Structures: Cartilage, Ligaments, and Tendons

Movement would be impossible or destructive without the specialized soft connective tissues that stabilize joints and transmit muscle forces. - **Tendons**: Dense fibrous connective cords that firmly attach skeletal muscle to bone, translating muscle tension into joint movement. - **Ligaments**: Tough bands of dense regular connective tissue connecting bone to bone, providing joint stability and preventing excessive or abnormal range of motion. - **Cartilage**: A resilient, smooth connective tissue that covers bone endings within joints, absorbing shock and reducing friction during movement. Because cartilage and tendons possess poor vascular supply, they heal very slowly following traumatic injuries or sports strains—a crucial clinical consideration when nursing patients undergoing orthopedic rehabilitation.

Neurological Control of Movement and Posture

Voluntary movement and postural stability require seamless coordination between the central nervous system and the musculoskeletal periphery. The cerebral cortex initiates conscious motor commands, which travel down spinal tracts to activate specific motor units. Meanwhile, specialized sensory receptors embedded within muscles and joints—known as **proprioceptors**—continuously monitor body position, muscle length, and tension. Muscle spindles detect changes in muscle length and trigger reflex contractions to prevent overstretching, while Golgi tendon organs monitor tension to protect muscles from excessive force. The cerebellum processes this incoming sensory feedback in real time, smoothing out movements and adjusting posture automatically. Disruption in this intricate feedback loop, such as from a stroke or spinal cord injury, leads to spasticity, flaccid paralysis, or ataxia.

Age-Related Changes in the Musculoskeletal System

As individuals age, progressive structural and physiological alterations occur throughout the musculoskeletal system. Bone resorption gradually outpaces bone formation, leading to a loss of bone mineral density and predisposing older adults to osteopenia and osteoporosis. Articular cartilage thins and wears down over decades, frequently resulting in degenerative joint changes such as osteoarthritis. Skeletal muscle mass and strength decline steadily—a generalized aging process known as **sarcopenia**. Muscle fibers decrease in both number and cross-sectional diameter, replaced progressively by fibrous connective tissue and adipose tissue. These cumulative changes reduce overall mobility, impair balance reflexes, and significantly increase the risk of falls and fragility fractures in geriatric patients.
