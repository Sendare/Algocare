Review of Related Anatomy and Physiology of the Musculoskeletal System

Introduction to the Musculoskeletal System

The musculoskeletal system is a complex, integrated network that provides form, support, stability, and movement to the human body. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice and exams in Nigeria, mastering this system is fundamental, as musculoskeletal disorders frequently present in various healthcare settings, from trauma wards to community health centers. The system is primarily composed of bones, skeletal muscles, joints, tendons, ligaments, and cartilage. Bones act as a rigid framework, protecting vital internal organs such as the brain, heart, and lungs, while also serving as a major reservoir for essential minerals like calcium and phosphorus. Skeletal muscles, attached to bones by tendons, generate the mechanical force required for locomotion and posture maintenance through coordinated contraction and relaxation. Joints provide the connection points between bones, allowing for varying degrees of movement depending on their structural classification. Meanwhile, supportive connective tissues like cartilage, ligaments, and tendons ensure smooth joint articulation, joint stability, and effective force transmission across the skeletal frame. The central and peripheral nervous systems tightly regulate every action, ensuring precision, balance, and controlled movement. Understanding how these individual components interact harmoniously provides the necessary baseline for assessing pathological conditions such as fractures, arthritis, and muscle strains. By grasping these foundational anatomical and physiological principles, student nurses can better anticipate patient needs, perform accurate physical assessments, and deliver holistic, evidence-based nursing care.

Anatomy and Structure of Bone Tissue

Bone tissue, or osseous tissue, is a specialized form of dense connective tissue characterized by a hard, mineralized extracellular matrix. Structurally, bones are divided into two main types: compact (cortical) bone and spongy (cancellous or trabecular) bone. Compact bone forms the dense, hard outer layer of bones, providing structural strength, protection, and support. Its microscopic functional unit is the osteon, or Haversian system, which consists of concentric rings of calcified matrix called lamellae surrounding a central Haversian canal that houses blood vessels and nerves. Nutrients and wastes travel through tiny channels called canaliculi, connecting osteocytes (mature bone cells) trapped within lacunae. In contrast, spongy bone is found inside bones, particularly at the epiphyses of long bones, and is composed of an irregular lattice-making network of trabeculae. Spaces between trabeculae often contain red bone marrow, which is responsible for hematopoiesis (blood cell production). Surrounding the external surface of bones is the periosteum, a dense, vascular fibrous membrane essential for bone nourishment, repair, and growth in width. The internal bone cavities are lined by the endosteum. Understanding this structural duality between compact and spongy bone is crucial for student nurses when studying conditions like osteoporosis, where trabecular bone density is significantly compromised, leading to increased fracture risks.

Bone Growth, Remodeling, and Mineral Homeostasis

Bone is a remarkably dynamic living tissue that undergoes continuous growth, remodeling, and mineral homeostasis throughout a person's lifespan. Bone growth in length occurs at the epiphyseal plate (growth plate) in long bones through endochondral ossification during childhood and adolescence, while growth in thickness (appositional growth) happens as osteoblasts deposit new bone matrix beneath the periosteum. Bone remodeling is a lifelong, ongoing process where mature bone tissue is continuously removed by osteoclasts (bone resorption) and replaced by new bone tissue synthesized by osteoblasts (bone formation). This balance is vital for repairing micro-damages from daily stress and shaping the skeleton. Furthermore, bones play a critical endocrine role in mineral homeostasis, specifically regulating calcium and phosphate levels in the blood. When serum calcium levels drop, parathyroid hormone (PTH) is released, stimulating osteoclast activity to break down bone matrix and release calcium into the bloodstream. Conversely, when calcium levels are elevated, the hormone calcitonin inhibits osteoclast activity, promoting calcium deposition into the bones. For nursing students, understanding these regulatory feedback loops is essential when caring for patients with metabolic bone diseases, calcium imbalances, or immobilization-induced bone loss, as prolonged bed rest significantly alters normal bone remodeling dynamics.

Classification and Structure of Joints

Joints, or articulations, are the points of contact between bones, between bone and cartilage, or between bone and teeth. They are structurally classified based on the type of connective tissue that binds them and whether a joint cavity is present, and functionally classified based on the degree of movement they permit. Structurally, joints can be fibrous, cartilaginous, or synovial. Fibrous joints are held together by dense regular connective tissue with no joint cavity, offering little to no movement, such as the sutures of the skull. Cartilaginous joints are connected by cartilage (either hyaline cartilage or fibrocartilage) and allow little movement, exemplified by the pubic symphysis or intervertebral discs. Synovial joints are characterized by the presence of a fluid-filled joint cavity, enclosed by a fibrous joint capsule lined with a synovial membrane that secretes lubricating synovial fluid. This structural arrangement allows synovial joints to be freely movable (diarthrosis). Examples include the knee, shoulder, and hip joints. Functionally, joints are categorized as synarthroses (immovable), amphiarthroses (slightly movable), or diarthroses (freely movable). Knowledge of joint structures helps nurses accurately assess orthopedic injuries, recognize signs of joint inflammation in conditions like osteoarthritis or rheumatoid arthritis, and assist in physical therapy exercises.

Physiology of Skeletal Muscle Contraction

The physiology of skeletal muscle contraction is driven by the sliding filament mechanism, a well-coordinated molecular process that turns chemical energy into mechanical work. Skeletal muscle fibers are excitable cells packed with cylindrical myofibrils, which are further composed of repeating functional units called sarcomeres. Each sarcomere contains thick filaments made of the protein myosin and thin filaments made of actin, along with regulatory proteins troponin and tropomyosin. The process begins when an action potential travels down a motor neuron to the neuromuscular junction, triggering the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (ACh) into the synaptic cleft. ACh binds to receptors on the muscle fiber membrane (sarcolemma), propagating an electrical impulse that travels down T-tubules and stimulates the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release stored calcium ions into the sarcoplasm. The released calcium ions bind to troponin, causing tropomyosin to shift and expose the myosin-binding sites on the actin filaments. Myosin heads then bind to actin, forming cross-bridges and pulling the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere, a step powered by ATP hydrolysis. This causes the sarcomere to shorten, resulting in muscle contraction. Relaxation occurs when nerve impulses cease, calcium is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, and cross-bridges detach. Understanding muscle contraction helps nursing students comprehend muscular disorders, electrolyte imbalances like hypocalcemia or hypokalemia, and the effects of neuromuscular blocking agents in clinical care.

Supporting Structures: Cartilage, Ligaments, and Tendons

Supporting structures of the musculoskeletal system—namely cartilage, ligaments, and tendons—are specialized forms of dense and specialized connective tissue vital for structural integrity, stability, and movement. Cartilage is an avascular, resilient connective tissue that provides flexible support and reduces friction at joint surfaces. The primary type found at articulating bone ends is hyaline cartilage, while fibrocartilage provides high tensile strength in places like the intervertebral discs and meniscus. Because cartilage lacks a direct blood supply, its healing and regenerative capacity are notoriously slow following injury. Ligaments are tough bands of dense regular connective tissue that connect bone to bone, providing joint stability and preventing excessive or abnormal range of motion. For example, the cruciate ligaments of the knee prevent anterior and posterior displacement. Tendons are similarly composed of dense regular connective tissue, but they connect skeletal muscle to bone, transmitting the force generated by muscle contraction to move the skeleton. Both tendons and ligaments have limited vascularity compared to muscle tissue, meaning rehabilitation following sprains (ligament injuries) or strains (tendon/muscle injuries) requires patience and careful management. Student nurses must understand these tissue characteristics to provide effective post-operative care, support mobility, and educate patients on injury prevention and healing times.

Neurological Control of Movement and Posture

The execution of smooth movement and the maintenance of upright posture rely entirely on precise neurological control from both the central and peripheral nervous systems. Voluntary movement begins in the motor cortex of the cerebrum, which plans and initiates motor commands. These electrical impulses travel down the corticospinal tracts of the spinal cord to the lower motor neurons, which innervate specific skeletal muscle fibers at the neuromuscular junction. However, movement is far more than simple commands; it requires continuous fine-tuning, balance, and coordination managed by the cerebellum and basal ganglia. The cerebellum compares intended movement with sensory feedback from proprioceptors—specialized receptors located in muscles, tendons, and joints that provide continuous data regarding body position and muscle tension. Muscle tone, the continuous and partial contraction of resting muscles, is maintained by spinal reflex arcs and controlled by higher brain centers to keep the body ready for action. Any disruption in this neurological pathway, whether from a stroke, spinal cord injury, or peripheral nerve damage, can result in clinical manifestations such as paralysis, spasticity, flaccidity, or ataxia. For nursing students, evaluating motor function, assessing deep tendon reflexes, and understanding proprioceptive feedback are core components of a comprehensive neurological and musculoskeletal physical assessment.

Age-Related Changes in the Musculoskeletal System

As individuals age, the musculoskeletal system undergoes progressive, natural physiological changes that can significantly impact mobility, strength, and overall functional independence. Bone mass gradually decreases as bone resorption by osteoclasts outpaces bone formation by osteoblasts, a process that accelerates in women following menopause due to declining estrogen levels. This loss of bone density can lead to osteopenia and eventually osteoporosis, greatly increasing the risk of fragility fractures. Articular cartilage thins and becomes less resilient with age, resulting in wear and tear on joints that frequently manifests as osteoarthritis. Ligaments, tendons, and joint capsules also lose their elasticity and flexibility, leading to joint stiffness and a decreased range of motion. Furthermore, skeletal muscle mass and strength decline progressively through a process known as sarcopenia, accompanied by a reduction in the number of muscle fibers and motor units. These age-related alterations collectively increase the risk of falls, postural instability, and frailty among older adults. Recognizing these physiological shifts is vital for nurses caring for geriatric populations in Nigeria and globally. It guides nursing interventions aimed at promoting bone health through adequate calcium and vitamin D intake, encouraging weight-bearing exercises, implementing rigorous fall-prevention strategies, and ensuring safe patient-handling techniques.
