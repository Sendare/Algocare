Degenerative Joint Diseases

Pathophysiology and Underlying Mechanisms

Degenerative joint disease, commonly known as osteoarthritis, is characterized by the progressive deterioration of articular cartilage and the underlying subchondral bone. Far from being a simple wear-and-tear process, it involves active, low-grade inflammation within the joint microenvironment. Chondrocytes undergo abnormal metabolic changes, releasing enzymes that break down the extracellular matrix. As the smooth cartilage wears away, the bone surfaces are exposed, rubbing against each other. This friction leads to the formation of bony outgrowths called osteophytes, along with synovial hypertrophy and thickening of the joint capsule. In busy Nigerian clinical settings, patients often present late in this disease trajectory, when structural changes are already advanced and joint space narrowing is clearly visible.

Etiology and Predisposing Risk Factors

The development of degenerative joint disease stems from a combination of systemic and biomechanical influences. Primary osteoarthritis typically arises idiopathically associated with aging, whereas secondary osteoarthritis results from a distinct predisposing event or condition.

  • Age: Cumulative joint use and cellular senescence reduce the regenerative capacity of cartilage.
  • Obesity: Excess body weight places chronic, excessive mechanical load on weight-bearing joints like the knees and hips.
  • Joint Trauma: Previous fractures, ligamentous tears, or repeated occupational stress alter joint mechanics permanently.
  • Genetics: Inherited variations in collagen structure can predispose individuals to early joint breakdown.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment Findings

Patients experiencing joint degeneration present with specific, progressive symptom patterns that impact their daily mobility. Pain is typically aggravated by weight-bearing activity and relieved by rest in the early stages, but can eventually become constant. Joint stiffness is most prominent after periods of inactivity, though this morning stiffness generally lasts less than thirty minutes, distinguishing it from inflammatory arthropathies.

Physical assessment often reveals crepitus—a grating sensation or sound during joint movement—along with bony enlargement, restricted range of motion, and varying degrees of joint effusion. In the hands, hard, non-tender nodules known as Heberden's nodes develop at the distal interphalangeal joints, while Bouchard's nodes appear at the proximal joints.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Confirming degenerative joint disease relies primarily on imaging studies supported by clinical history. Plain radiography is the gold standard for diagnosis and monitoring. Typical radiographic findings include asymmetric joint space narrowing, subchondral sclerosis, osteophyte formation, and bone cysts. Laboratory tests, including complete blood counts and inflammatory markers like erythrocyte sedimentation rate, are usually normal in osteoarthritis. They serve primarily to rule out inflammatory or infectious arthritides when joint effusions are present.

Pharmacological Management Strategies

Medication therapy focuses on pain relief and improving functional ability while minimizing adverse drug events. Topical nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs are recommended as first-line therapy for superficial joints because they offer localized relief with minimal systemic absorption. Oral analgesics, including paracetamol, are utilized cautiously, followed by oral NSAIDs for patients with greater pain intensity. Intra-articular corticosteroid injections provide rapid, short-term relief during acute flare-ups of joint inflammation. When administering oral NSAIDs, nurses must monitor for gastrointestinal irritation and renal impairment, educating older adults especially to take these medications with food.

Non-Pharmacological and Rehabilitative Care

Non-pharmacological modalities form the foundational bedrock of long-term joint preservation. Weight management programs reduce biomechanical stress on lower extremity joints. Physical therapy helps maintain joint mobility and strengthens periarticular muscles, particularly the quadriceps in knee osteoarthritis. Low-impact aerobic exercises, such as swimming and cycling, improve cardiovascular health without overloading joint structures. Assistive devices like canes, walkers, and knee braces help redistribute weight away from damaged compartments, enhancing patient safety and independence during ambulation.

Surgical Interventions and Perioperative Nursing Care

When conservative measures fail to control pain or restore function, surgical options are considered. Arthroscopic debridement may be performed to smooth cartilage or remove loose bodies, though its use is selective. Joint arthroplasty, or total joint replacement, is the definitive surgical treatment for end-stage hip and knee degeneration. Perioperative nursing care focuses on preventing common complications such as deep vein thrombosis, surgical site infection, and dislocation of the new joint prosthesis. Early postoperative mobilization, strict adherence to movement precautions, and neurovascular checks of the affected extremity are critical nursing responsibilities.

Nursing Diagnoses, Planning, and Self-Management Education

Comprehensive nursing care involves formulating targeted diagnoses such as Chronic Pain, Impaired Physical Mobility, and Deficient Knowledge regarding self-management strategies. The nursing care plan centers on setting realistic functional goals with the patient, balancing periods of rest with prescribed activity. Patient education empowers individuals to protect their joints through pacing daily tasks, utilizing proper body mechanics, and maintaining an ideal body weight. Nurses also teach patients how to safely manage assistive devices and recognize signs of worsening joint damage requiring medical follow-up.
