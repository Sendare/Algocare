Nursing Process Management of Musculoskeletal Problems

Introduction and Subjective Data Collection

Assessing the musculoskeletal system begins long before you touch the patient. Subjective data collection focuses on the patient's lived experience of pain, stiffness, weakness, or limited movement. During the health history, you must ask targeted questions about the onset, duration, and severity of symptoms. Common complaints like joint pain or backaches require careful exploration of aggravating and relieving factors.

Age, occupation, and lifestyle habits strongly influence musculoskeletal health. A market trader who stands for hours will present different risks than a bank teller who sits at a desk all day. Always inquire about past injuries, surgeries, and family history of conditions like osteoporosis or rheumatoid arthritis. Nutritional history is equally vital, particularly calcium and vitamin D intake, which directly affect bone density. Listening closely to how symptoms affect daily activities—such as bathing, cooking, or walking—gives you a clear picture of functional impairment.

Physical Examination Techniques

A systematic physical examination of the musculoskeletal system follows a strict clinical sequence: inspection, palpation, and range of motion testing.

  • Inspection: Observe posture, gait, body symmetry, and skin color over joints. Look for obvious deformities, swelling, or muscle wasting.
  • Palpation: Feel along muscles, bones, and joints to check for tenderness, warmth, swelling, or crepitus (a grating sound or feel during movement).
  • Range of Motion (ROM): Assess both active ROM (performed by the patient independently) and passive ROM (gently guided by the nurse). Never force a joint past the point of pain or resistance.

Muscle strength testing is another cornerstone. Grade muscle strength on a standard scale from zero (no movement) to five (normal movement against full resistance) to document baseline deficits accurately.

Diagnostic Tests and Lab Investigations

Diagnostic testing provides objective proof to confirm physical findings. X-rays remain the most common tool for visualizing bone fractures, joint dislocation, and degenerative changes like osteoarthritis. For soft tissue injuries involving tendons, ligaments, or cartilage, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides far superior detail.

Laboratory studies help detect systemic inflammation, infection, or metabolic bone disease.

Formulating Nursing Diagnoses

Once assessment data is gathered, you must analyze the cues to identify actual or potential health problems. Common NANDA nursing diagnoses related to musculoskeletal issues include Acute Pain, Impaired Physical Mobility, Risk for Peripheral Neurovascular Dysfunction, and Risk for Falls.

Be careful not to confuse a medical diagnosis with a nursing diagnosis. A doctor diagnoses a fractured femur; as a nurse, you diagnose the patient's response to that fracture, such as acute pain related to tissue trauma or impaired physical mobility related to skeletal traction. Your nursing diagnoses directly guide the rest of your care plan.

Interventions for Immobilization, Traction, and Cast Care

Immobilization devices are essential for healing broken bones, but they bring significant risks to skin and circulation.

When caring for a patient in traction, ensure that weights hang freely at all times and never rest on the floor. Ropes must remain seated securely within pulley grooves. For patients in casts, handle wet plaster casts with the palms of your hands—not your fingertips—to prevent indentations that can press into the skin and cause pressure ulcers.

Neurovascular checks (often called CMS checks: Circulation, Motion, and Sensation) are mandatory every few hours. Assess capillary refill, pedal or radial pulses, skin temperature, and the patient's ability to wiggle their fingers or toes. Report any signs of pallor, pulselessness, pain, or paresthesia immediately.

Pain Management and Mobility Promotion

Pain can severely hinder recovery by preventing deep breathing, movement, and sleep. Combine pharmacological treatments—such as analgesics and anti-inflammatory drugs—with non-pharmacological comfort measures like repositioning, gentle massage, heat, or cold therapy.

Promoting mobility requires careful planning and teamwork. Pre-medicate patients for pain about thirty minutes before scheduled physical therapy or ambulation sessions. Use assistive devices like walkers, crutches, or canes correctly, ensuring the device is fitted to the patient's height. Encourage early ambulation whenever medically cleared to prevent dangerous complications of immobility, such as deep vein thrombosis, pressure injuries, and muscle atrophy.

Rehabilitation, Education, and Outcome Evaluation

Long-term recovery from musculoskeletal trauma depends heavily on patient education and structured rehabilitation. Teach patients and their caregivers home safety measures, such as removing loose rugs and installing bathroom grab bars to prevent falls. Review proper use of assistive walking devices and emphasize the importance of keeping follow-up appointments with orthopedic surgeons and physical therapists.

Evaluation is the final step of the nursing process. You must continuously review whether your interventions met the set goals. Ask yourself: Is the patient's pain controlled? Is skin integrity maintained around casts or traction sites? Can the patient perform activities of daily living safely? If outcomes are not met, modify the care plan promptly.
