Complications of Musculoskeletal Problems

Introduction to Musculoskeletal Complications

Musculoskeletal injuries, particularly fractures and severe soft-tissue trauma, often trigger secondary systemic or local problems that threaten patient recovery. These adverse events can arise directly from the initial mechanical trauma or develop sequentially during treatment, casting, or surgical intervention. Recognizing these threats early prevents long-term disability and reduces mortality. In Nigerian clinical settings, where patients may present to healthcare facilities days after traditional bone setter manipulation, secondary complications like severe infection and vascular compromise are especially prevalent.

Hypovolemic Shock and Hemorrhage

Severe bone fractures, especially those involving the pelvis and femur, can result in massive internal blood loss. The pelvic ring can hold several liters of blood within the retroperitoneal space without obvious external signs. A fractured shaft of the femur can easily cause a loss of 1,500 mL of blood into the surrounding thigh tissues.

  • Tachycardia: Often the earliest physiological sign of significant blood volume depletion.
  • Hypotension: A late indicator, appearing only after compensatory mechanisms fail.
  • Cool, clammy skin: Caused by peripheral vasoconstriction shunting blood to core organs.

Prompt fluid resuscitation, blood typing, crossmatching, and stabilizing the fracture site to stop internal bleeding are immediate clinical priorities.

Fat Embolism Syndrome

Fat embolism syndrome occurs when fat globules enter the bloodstream from the yellow bone marrow of fractured long bones, lodging in the pulmonary microvasculature and other organ beds. This condition typically manifests twenty-four to seventy-two hours following trauma.

  1. Respiratory distress: Tachypnea, dyspnea, and hypoxia due to pulmonary capillary occlusion.
  2. Neurological changes: Confusion, restlessness, or coma resulting from cerebral micro-emboli.
  3. Petechial rash: A distinct, non-blanching rash appearing across the chest, neck, and axilla, caused by microvascular occlusion.

Management focuses primarily on supportive care, early fracture immobilization, and maintaining adequate arterial oxygenation.

Compartment Syndrome

Compartment syndrome is a limb-threatening surgical emergency characterized by increased pressure within a closed osteofascial muscle compartment. As pressure rises, microvascular perfusion drops, leading to cellular hypoxia and necrosis. This can be triggered by restrictive casts, tight circumferential bandages, or severe internal swelling from fractures and crush injuries.

The classic clinical indicators are known as the 5 Ps:

  • Pain: Out of proportion to the injury and unresponsive to standard narcotic analgesics.
  • Pallor: Pale skin tone distal to the affected compartment.
  • Pulselessness: A very late sign indicating complete arterial occlusion.
  • Paresthesia: Tingling, numbness, or abnormal nerve signaling.
  • Paralysis: Inability to move digits, signaling advanced nerve and muscle damage.

Immediate removal of constrictive dressings and potential surgical fasciotomy are required to save the limb.

Deep Vein Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism

Orthopedic trauma and prolonged immobilization foster the classic triad of Virchow: venous stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. Thrombi frequently form in the deep veins of the calf or pelvis following lower limb fractures or joint replacement surgeries.

When a thrombus dislodges, it travels through the venous system into the right side of the heart and lodges in the pulmonary arterial tree, becoming a pulmonary embolism. Patients present with sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachypnea, and unexplained apprehension. Prophylactic measures include early ambulation, sequential compression devices, and anticoagulant therapy such as low-molecular-weight heparin.

Osteomyelitis and Wound Infections

Bone infection can be introduced directly through open fractures, surgical incisions, or indirectly via hematogenous spread. Once bacteria colonize bone tissue, they form biofilms that resist host immune responses and standard antibiotic therapies.

Clinical manifestations include localized bone pain, erythema, warmth, draining sinus tracts, and systemic fevers. Treatment requires prolonged targeted intravenous antibiotics and aggressive surgical debridement of necrotic bone tissue, known as sequestrum. Left untreated, chronic osteomyelitis leads to persistent disability and recurrent flare-ups.

Avascular Necrosis

Avascular necrosis occurs when cellular death of bone components happens due to an interruption in blood supply. Common sites include the femoral head following displaced femoral neck fractures or hip dislocations that damage the delicate retinacular blood vessels.

Bone tissue without blood supply undergoes structural collapse over months to years, causing severe joint pain and loss of function. Early diagnosis via magnetic resonance imaging guides interventions like core decompression to relieve pressure and restore vascularity, though total joint arthroplasty is frequently required once joint architecture collapses.

Delayed Union, Malunion, and Nonunion

Fracture healing can deviate from the normal expected timeline due to systemic and local factors such as poor immobilization, inadequate blood supply, infection, or patient malnutrition.

Complication TypeDescription
Delayed UnionHealing takes significantly longer than the typical timeframe for that specific bone.
MalunionThe fracture heals in an unacceptable anatomical position, causing deformity or functional impairment.
NonunionHealing process completely ceases without achieving bony union, often forming a false joint or pseudoarthrosis.

Surgical intervention such as bone grafting, internal fixation correction, or electrical bone stimulation is often necessary to correct nonunion and malunion.

Nursing Assessment and Management of Musculoskeletal Complications

Comprehensive nursing care requires vigilant neurovascular monitoring and proactive prevention strategies. Nurses must perform serial checks of distal pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature, sensation, and motor movement every few hours for patients in casts or traction.

Pain management must be approached cautiously: if pain escalates despite adequate medication, evaluate immediately for compartment syndrome rather than simply increasing the narcotic dose. Encouraging deep breathing exercises, maintaining hydration, and monitoring surgical wound sites for signs of infection ensure holistic care and prompt detection of complications.
