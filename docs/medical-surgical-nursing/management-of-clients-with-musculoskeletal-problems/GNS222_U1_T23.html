Complications of Musculoskeletal Problems

Understanding Musculoskeletal Complications

Injuries to bones, joints, and soft tissues can sometimes trigger secondary health threats that become far more dangerous than the original break. When bones fracture, surrounding blood vessels, nerves, and soft tissues face immediate trauma. Nurses must look beyond the cast or the bandage to monitor for systemic changes. Spotting early warning signs prevents minor fractures from turning into life-threatening emergencies.

Hemorrhage and Hypovolemic Shock

Bones are highly vascular structures packed with blood-forming marrow. When a major bone like the pelvis or femur breaks, it can bleed massively into the surrounding tissue or body cavity. A patient can lose liters of blood internally without a single drop showing on the sheets. Nurses track vital signs closely, watching for a falling blood pressure and a rising heart rate as the body tries to compensate for lost volume.

Fat Embolism Syndrome

When long bones fracture, tiny globules of yellow bone marrow fat can escape into the venous bloodstream. These fat droplets travel to the lungs and brain, blocking small blood vessels. This condition usually strikes within twenty-four to seventy-two hours after injury.

  • Respiratory distress: Shortness of breath, rapid breathing, and low blood oxygen levels.
  • Neurological changes: Confusion, restlessness, or sudden drowsiness.
  • Petechiae rash: A characteristic pinpoint reddish-purple rash across the chest, neck, and shoulders caused by small vessel blockage.

Compartment Syndrome

Muscles are bundled inside tight, inelastic wrappers of fascia called compartments. When severe swelling or bleeding occurs inside a closed compartment, pressure rises dangerously. Because the fascia cannot stretch, blood vessels and nerves get squeezed, starving the tissue of oxygen.

The classic warning signs are known as the Ps:

  • Pain: Severe pain that gets worse and does not respond to strong pain medications.
  • Pressure: The affected area feels hard and tense to the touch.
  • Paresthesia: Numbness and tingling in the fingers or toes.
  • Pallor: Pale skin tone in the affected limb.
  • Pulselessness: A late and dangerous sign where pulses fade or disappear entirely.
  • Paralysis: Inability to move the digits, indicating severe nerve damage.

Deep Vein Thrombosis and Pulmonary Embolism

Immobility after a fracture or surgery slows down blood return from the lower limbs. When blood pools in the deep veins, a clot or thrombus can form, leading to deep vein thrombosis (DVT). If that clot breaks loose, it travels through the heart and lodges in the pulmonary arteries, causing a pulmonary embolism (PE). Nurses encourage early ambulation, leg exercises, and prescribed blood thinners to keep blood moving smoothly.

Osteomyelitis and Wound Infections

Open fractures expose bone directly to the outside environment, inviting bacteria into the wound. Infection can also spread to bone from nearby soft tissue or through the bloodstream. When bacteria invade bone tissue, it causes inflammation, swelling, and eventual bone death, a condition called osteomyelitis. Treating bone infections requires long-term intravenous antibiotics and sometimes surgical removal of dead bone tissue.

Avascular Necrosis

Bone is living tissue that depends on a steady supply of blood. Certain fractures, particularly femoral neck breaks or severe joint dislocations, can snap the tiny blood vessels feeding the bone head. Deprived of oxygen and nutrients, the bone tissue dies in a process called avascular necrosis (AVN). The bone gradually collapses over time, destroying joint function and causing chronic pain.

Healing Complications: Delayed Union, Malunion, and Nonunion

Sometimes bones fail to heal according to the normal timeline. Factors like poor blood supply, inadequate immobilization, or infection interfere with bone regeneration.

  • Delayed union: Healing takes significantly longer than the expected timeframe.
  • Malunion: The bone heals in an incorrect, crooked position, which can alter limb function.
  • Nonunion: The fracture fails to heal completely, leaving fibrous tissue instead of solid bone.

Nursing Assessment and Management

Comprehensive nursing care is vital to catching musculoskeletal complications early. The cornerstone of this care is the frequent neurovascular check, often called the '5 Ps' check, performed on any injured or casted limb. Nurses assess pain intensity, skin color, temperature, capillary refill, pulse strength, and movement. Maintaining proper traction alignment, encouraging deep breathing exercises, and administering ordered anticoagulants or antibiotics safeguard the patient throughout recovery.
