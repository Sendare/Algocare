Physical Assessment in Musculoskeletal Problems

Introduction to Musculoskeletal Physical Assessment

Musculoskeletal assessment evaluates the structural integrity and functional capability of the skeletal system, joints, and musculature. Clinical evaluation relies heavily on comparison between bilateral body parts. A systematic head-to-toe or proximal-to-distal sequence ensures no joint or muscle group is missed during the examination process. Physical findings are routinely correlated with patient history, occupational background, and presenting complaints such as pain, swelling, or limited mobility.

Preparation, Environment, and Patient Positioning

A reliable musculoskeletal examination requires adequate lighting, privacy, and an examination table that allows full patient access. The patient must be appropriately gowned to expose the specific body regions under inspection without causing undue embarrassment or chilling. Positioning shifts from standing to sitting and supine depending on the precise anatomical structures targeted. Clear communication before moving any limb helps minimize guarding and accurately reveals true functional limitations.

Inspection of Posture, Gait, and Alignment

Observation begins the moment the clinician encounters the patient. Posture is evaluated from multiple angles to detect spinal curvatures like lordosis, kyphosis, or scoliosis. Gait analysis focuses on the rhythm, base of support, and smoothness of the stride cycle. Limb alignment should follow natural anatomical axes without excessive varus or valgus deviation at the knees or elbows.

  • Lordosis: An exaggerated inward curvature of the lumbar spine.
  • Kyphosis: An excessive outward curvature of the thoracic spine, commonly called humpback.
  • Scoliosis: A lateral sideways curvature of the spinal column.
  • Varus alignment: Bow-legged appearance where knees diverge outward.

Palpation of Bones, Joints, and Soft Tissues

Palpation identifies localized tenderness, swelling, crepitus, and abnormal temperature changes. The examiner uses the pads of the fingers to systematically touch bony prominences, joint capsules, tendons, and surrounding musculature. Palpation should always proceed from the least painful area toward the site of reported discomfort to prevent premature patient withdrawal. Crepitus—a perceptible crunching sound or sensation during joint movement—indicates rough articular surfaces grinding together.

Assessment of Range of Motion

Range of motion measures the degree of movement possible at a specific joint. Active range of motion occurs when the patient independently moves the joint through its full capability, demonstrating both joint mobility and muscle power. Passive range of motion is performed when the examiner gently supports and moves the limb through the movement arc, which helps assess joint capsule integrity and ligamentous stability. Any restriction, pain, or locking observed during either phase must be carefully documented in degrees using a goniometer.

Muscle Strength Testing and Grading

Muscle strength testing evaluates the functional capacity of muscle groups against applied resistance. The standard Medical Research Council scale ranges from grade 0 (no movement) to grade 5 (normal strength against full resistance). Testing must be performed symmetrically, comparing the right side to the left. The examiner applies gradual, steady resistance rather than sudden force to avoid injury and accurately gauge true contractile power.

Neurovascular Assessment of the Extremities

Neurovascular checks are vital when evaluating fractures, splints, casts, or severe soft tissue trauma. This assessment monitors peripheral circulation, nerve function, and tissue perfusion. The traditional mnemonic involves checking pulse quality, skin color, temperature, capillary refill, sensory function, and motor movement.

Special Diagnostic Tests for Specific Conditions

Specialized orthopedic tests isolate specific ligaments, menisci, or nerve roots when routine inspection and palpation yield inconclusive results. For example, the Phalen and Tinel tests evaluate for carpal tunnel syndrome, while tests like the Lachman or McMurray maneuvers assess knee stability and meniscal tears. Each test applies mechanical stress designed to reproduce specific clinical symptoms. Interpreting these tests requires precise technique and thorough understanding of normal joint biomechanics.

Documentation and Abnormal Findings in Nursing Practice

Accurate documentation captures the baseline status of the musculoskeletal system and tracks changes over time. Clinical notes must avoid vague terminology, using precise measurements, standardized grading scales, and clear anatomical descriptors instead. Documenting abnormal findings such as asymmetry, crepitus, restricted range of motion, or diminished pulses ensures safe continuity of care across multidisciplinary healthcare teams. Timely reporting of acute deterioration prevents permanent neurological or vascular damage in vulnerable patients.
