Physical Assessment in Musculoskeletal Problems

Introduction to Musculoskeletal Physical Assessment

A thorough musculoskeletal assessment allows nurses to evaluate a patient's functional status, detect early signs of injury, and monitor chronic conditions like arthritis. This evaluation relies heavily on comparison: you must always compare the affected limb or joint with the unaffected side. The process follows a systematic head-to-toe or proximal-to-distal approach, integrating four primary physical examination techniques: inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation, though inspection and palpation form the absolute backbone of musculoskeletal exams. Nurses look at bones, joints, muscles, and surrounding neurovascular structures to determine how well the client can perform daily activities.

Preparation, Environment, and Patient Positioning

Before touching the patient, ensure the environment is warm, well-lit, and provides adequate privacy. Proper exposure is essential: ask the patient to remove clothing from the specific body parts being examined while keeping the rest of the body comfortably draped. Explain each step clearly to reduce anxiety, which can cause muscle guarding and alter physical findings. Position the patient comfortably—usually sitting or standing for upper body checks, and lying flat on an examination table for lower extremity evaluations. Ensure the examination surface is firm enough to support the patient safely during strength or movement tests.

Inspection of Posture, Gait, and Alignment

Inspection begins the moment you first see the patient enter the room. Observe their general posture, standing alignment, and symmetry of body parts.

  • Posture: Look for normal spinal curves, including cervical, thoracic, and lumbar curvatures. Exaggerated curves like kyphosis or lordosis indicate spinal problems.
  • Gait: Watch the patient walk a short distance, noting balance, stride length, and arm swing. A smooth, rhythmic stride signifies normal function, while a limp or shuffle points to pain, weakness, or neurological deficits.
  • Alignment: Check limb symmetry, looking closely for visible deformities, shortening, or abnormal rotation of the bones.

Palpation of Bones, Joints, and Surrounding Soft Tissues

Palpation requires a gentle, systematic touch using the pads of your fingers. Feel along the length of each bone, muscle group, and joint capsule.

  • Tenderness: Note if the patient flinches or reports pain during palpation.
  • Swelling: Identify whether swelling is soft, firm, or fluid-filled.
  • Temperature: Compare skin warmth across joints using the back of your hand; increased warmth often indicates inflammation or infection.
  • Crepitus: Feel and listen for a crunching or grating sensation when moving a joint, which suggests rough cartilage rubbing together.

Assessment of Range of Motion (Active and Passive)

Range of motion (ROM) measures the maximum movement possible for a specific joint.

  • Active ROM: The patient moves the joint independently without outside help. This tests both muscle strength and joint mobility.
  • Passive ROM: The nurse gently supports and moves the relaxed patient's joint through its full motion. This evaluates actual joint capsule flexibility and ligament integrity.

Never force a joint past the point of resistance or pain. If you notice limitation in either active or passive movement, use a tool called a goniometer to measure the exact angle of the movement in degrees.

Muscle Strength Testing and Grading

Muscle strength testing evaluates the power of specific muscle groups by asking the patient to move against resistance. Nurses grade muscle strength on a standardized scale from 0 to 5.

  • Grade 0: No movement at all (zero muscle contraction).
  • Grade 1: Trace movement, such as a slight flicker of muscle contraction without moving the joint.
  • Grade 2: Movement possible only with gravity eliminated (passive range of motion with support).
  • Grade 3: Movement against gravity alone, but unable to overcome resistance.
  • Grade 4: Movement against gravity and some resistance, but less than normal strength.
  • Grade 5: Normal strength against full resistance.

Neurovascular Assessment of the Extremities

Whenever bones or joints are injured, splinted, or casted, checking blood flow and nerve function is vital to prevent permanent damage. Nurses use the classic '5 Ps' framework during this evaluation:

  • Pain: Assess intensity, location, and whether it responds to medication.
  • Pulse: Check distal pulses (like dorsalis pedis or radial) to ensure good arterial blood flow.
  • Pallor: Look for pale or cyanotic skin color.
  • Paresthesia: Ask about numbness, tingling, or prickling sensations.
  • Paralysis: Test the patient's ability to wiggle fingers or toes independently.

Loss of pulses or severe, unmanaged pain often points toward compartment syndrome, a dangerous surgical emergency.

Special Diagnostic Tests for Specific Musculoskeletal Conditions

Beyond general movement checks, special physical maneuvers help isolate specific ligament, tendon, or meniscus tears.

  • Tinel's sign and Phalen's test are commonly used to assess carpal tunnel syndrome in the wrist.
  • McMurray's test checks for torn cartilage (meniscus) in the knee.
  • Straight leg raise tests for nerve root irritation or sciatica in the lower back.

Nurses should understand what these tests aim to find, even if advanced imaging like X-rays, MRIs, or CT scans ultimately confirms the final diagnosis.

Documentation and Abnormal Findings in Nursing Practice

Accurate, objective documentation is the final step of any musculoskeletal assessment. Avoid vague terms like 'normal' or 'extremities fine'; instead, record specific observations such as 'right knee active flexion to 120 degrees with mild crepitus, no swelling.' Note any abnormalities clearly, including asymmetry, muscle atrophy, localized redness, or diminished neurovascular status. Promptly reporting critical changes—such as a lost pulse or sudden unmanaged pain—to the healthcare provider ensures timely medical intervention and protects patient safety.
