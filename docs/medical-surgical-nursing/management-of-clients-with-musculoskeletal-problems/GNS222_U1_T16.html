Gout

Introduction and Definition of Gout

Gout is an ancient metabolic disorder characterized by recurrent attacks of acute inflammatory arthritis. It stems from the deposition of **monosodium urate (MSU) crystals** in joints and surrounding tissues. Throughout history, it earned the colloquial title 'the disease of kings' due to its historical association with rich diets and heavy alcohol consumption. In Nigerian clinical practice, healthcare providers increasingly encounter gout not only in affluent patients but across diverse socioeconomic backgrounds, often linked to rising rates of metabolic syndrome and dietary shifts.

Pathophysiology and Hyperuricemia

Uric acid is the final oxidation product of **purine metabolism** in humans. When purines break down, xanthine oxidase converts hypoxanthine to xanthine, and finally to uric acid. **Hyperuricemia** occurs when serum urate levels exceed the saturation point, typically above 6.8 mg/dL, though not everyone with elevated uric acid develops clinical gout. When these levels remain persistently high, precipitation occurs. The body's immune system recognizes these deposited crystals as foreign invaders. Neutrophils engulf the crystals, releasing inflammatory cytokines like **interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β)**, which drives the excruciating pain, intense heat, swelling, and redness characteristic of an acute flare.

Risk Factors and Dietary Triggers

- **Dietary factors**: High intake of purine-rich foods, particularly red meat, shellfish, organ meats, and beverages sweetened with high-fructose corn syrup. - **Alcohol consumption**: Beer and spirits strongly precipitate flares by increasing purine production and decreasing renal uric acid excretion. - **Genetics and sex**: Being male or having a family history of gout significantly raises baseline risk, as estrogen promotes renal urate excretion in premenopausal women. - **Comorbidities**: Conditions such as hypertension, chronic kidney disease, obesity, and metabolic syndrome impair the body's ability to clear uric acid efficiently. - **Medications**: Loop and thiazide diuretics, commonly prescribed for hypertension, elevate serum urate by reducing renal clearance.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

The classic presentation of an acute gout attack is dramatic, sudden, and intensely painful. Patients often awaken in the middle of the night with excruciating joint agony. The **first metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint**—the base of the big toe—is involved in over half of initial presentations, a condition specifically known as **podagra**. During physical assessment, the affected joint appears bright red, shiny, swollen, and exquisitely tender. Even the light weight of a bedsheet can prove intolerable. Fever and leukocytosis may accompany the attack, mimicking a septic joint. Left untreated, early attacks typically resolve spontaneously within a week to ten days, returning the patient to an asymptomatic intercritical period.

Stages of Gout and Chronic Complications

| Stage | Clinical Characteristics | | --- | --- | | **Asymptomatic Hyperuricemia** | Elevated serum urate with no clinical symptoms or crystal deposition | | **Acute Gouty Arthritis** | Sudden, painful inflammatory flares separated by symptom-free periods | | **Intercritical Gout** | The calm intervals between acute attacks, where low-grade inflammation may persist | | **Chronic Tophaceous Gout** | Advanced stage featuring visible chalky nodules (**tophi**) and joint destruction | Chronic tophaceous gout develops after years of poorly controlled hyperuricemia. **Tophi** appear as firm, yellowish-white nodules under the skin, commonly found on the ears, fingers, toes, and olecranon bursae. Chronic crystal deposition erodes bone and cartilage, leading to permanent joint deformity, reduced range of motion, and an increased risk of uric acid kidney stones (**nephrolithiasis**).

Diagnostic Evaluation

Definitive diagnosis of gout requires identifying monosodium urate crystals in synovial fluid or tophus aspirate using **polarized light microscopy**. Under a polarized microscope, these needle-shaped crystals exhibit **strong negative birefringence**. When joint aspiration is impractical or dry, clinicians rely on clinical criteria combined with laboratory and imaging findings. Serum uric acid levels alone are unreliable during an acute attack, as levels can drop due to an inflammatory stress response. **Ultrasound** and dual-energy computed tomography (**DECT**) scanning are increasingly utilized to detect subclinical crystal deposition, such as the characteristic 'double contour sign' on articular cartilage.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacotherapy for gout is divided into treating acute inflammatory flares and long-term urate-lowering therapy (**ULT**). - **Acute management**: Options include nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (**NSAIDs** like indomethacin), **colchicine** (best initiated within 24 hours of symptom onset), and corticosteroids (oral or intra-articular) for patients with renal impairment. - **Urate-lowering therapy**: Indicated for patients with frequent flares, tophi, or joint damage. Xanthine oxidase inhibitors such as **allopurinol** and **febuxostat** reduce uric acid production. Uricosuric agents like probenecid enhance renal excretion. An essential clinical caveat: never initiate urate-lowering therapy during an acute attack, as sudden fluctuations in serum urate can mobilize existing crystals and prolong or worsen the flare.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nursing care during an acute gout flare focuses on pain management, joint protection, and inflammation reduction. Elevate the affected limb and apply cold compresses if tolerated, though many patients find cold increases their discomfort. Handle the affected joint with extreme care and avoid pressure from bed linens by utilizing a cradling arch. Long-term education is paramount for preventing recurrent attacks. Counsel patients to maintain adequate hydration—aiming for high fluid intake to promote urinary excretion and prevent kidney stones. Encourage dietary modifications, emphasizing weight management, moderation of alcohol, and limitation of high-purine meats and sugary beverages. Emphasize that urate-lowering drugs must be taken consistently even when pain-free, as stopping medication allows crystals to reform.
