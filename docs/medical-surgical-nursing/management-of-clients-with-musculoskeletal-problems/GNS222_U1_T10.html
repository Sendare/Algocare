Fracture

Introduction and Definition of Fractures

A fracture is a break or disruption in the continuity of a bone. This structural failure happens when the physical force exerted on the bone is stronger than the bone itself can withstand. While many people use the words fracture and break interchangeably in everyday speech, medical professionals treat them as the exact same condition. Bone is a living, dynamic tissue with a tough outer cortex and a spongy inner marrow. When an impact, twisting force, or pathological disease overwhelms this structure, the bone tissue yields, resulting in localized injury, cellular disruption, and immediate loss of mechanical support.

Classification and Types of Fractures

Fractures are grouped into various types based on how the bone breaks and the condition of the surrounding skin. Understanding these categories helps nurses anticipate treatment needs and monitor for specific risks.

  • Closed (Simple): The bone breaks, but the overlying skin remains intact.
  • Open (Compound): The broken bone punctures through the skin, creating a direct pathway for bacteria to enter the bone and soft tissue.
  • Transverse: The break goes straight across the bone shaft at a right angle.
  • Oblique: The fracture line runs diagonally across the bone.
  • Spiral: The break twists around the bone shaft, often caused by a rotational injury.
  • Comminuted: The bone shatters into three or more pieces.
  • Greenstick: The bone bends and cracks on one side only, commonly seen in young children because their bones are softer and more flexible.

Pathophysiology and the Bone Healing Process

When a bone breaks, the body immediately begins a remarkable healing cascade divided into distinct biological phases.

  1. Hematoma Formation: Blood vessels tear at the fracture site, forming a clotting blood mass within the first 24 to 72 hours. This clot provides a framework for incoming repair cells.
  2. Fibrocartilaginous Callus Formation: Granulation tissue invades the area. Fibroblasts and chondroblasts create a soft bridge of cartilage and collagen that spans the break.
  3. Bony Callus Formation: Osteoblasts deposit spongy bone, replacing the soft cartilage callus with a harder bony bridge over several weeks.
  4. Remodeling: Osteoclasts absorb excess bone while osteoblasts lay down new compact bone along stress lines, gradually restoring the original shape and strength of the bone over months or years.

Causes and Risk Factors

Most fractures happen due to acute trauma, such as motor vehicle accidents, heavy falls, or sports injuries where the impact overwhelms skeletal strength. However, many breaks occur because underlying conditions weaken the bone structure. Osteoporosis is the leading culprit, stealing bone density and leaving vertebrae, wrists, and hips vulnerable to low-impact breaks. Nutritional deficiencies, especially in calcium and vitamin D, impair bone mineralization. Additionally, long-term corticosteroid use, sedentary lifestyles, and endocrine disorders increase a patient's risk of sustaining a fracture from everyday movements.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Recognizing a fracture requires a careful physical examination and an understanding of classic signs. Patients typically report sudden, severe pain that worsens with any movement as surrounding muscles spasm to protect the site. Swelling and bruising appear quickly due to local bleeding from torn vessels. Deformity, abnormal mobility, and a grinding sensation called crepitus may also be present. Nurses must always perform a thorough neurovascular assessment checking pulse, color, temperature, capillary refill, and movement distal to the injury site to ensure blood and nerve supplies are intact.

Diagnostic Investigations

While clinical signs point strongly toward a broken bone, diagnostic tests confirm the exact diagnosis. Standard X-rays remain the primary tool, capturing two views of the bone to reveal the precise location, angle, and type of fracture. If an X-ray is unclear or if the break involves complex joints like the spine or pelvis, a CT scan provides detailed cross-sectional images. Magnetic resonance imaging is helpful when doctors suspect soft tissue damage, ligament tears, or hidden stress fractures that do not show up on conventional films.

Medical and Surgical Management

Managing a fracture focuses on returning the bone fragments to their normal alignment and holding them steady until healing occurs. Realignment is known as reduction. Closed reduction uses manual manipulation and traction without surgery, followed by a plaster cast or splint. Open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) requires surgery to expose the bone and secure it with metal plates, screws, or rods. External fixation uses pins placed through the skin into the bone attached to an external metal frame, often utilized for complex open fractures with severe soft tissue damage.

Nursing Care and Patient Management

Comprehensive nursing care ensures safe healing and prevents secondary complications for immobilized patients. Nurses must regularly monitor casted or splinted limbs for signs of tight pressure, numbness, or loss of circulation. Pain control using prescribed analgesics is vital, alongside encouraging deep breathing exercises to prevent respiratory complications from bed rest. For patients in traction, nurses ensure weights hang freely and ropes remain unobstructed. Nutrition rich in protein, calcium, and vitamin C supports tissue repair, while early mobilization exercises prevent muscle wasting and deep vein thrombosis.

Complications of Fractures

While many fractures heal without trouble, nurses must remain vigilant for serious complications. Compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency where swelling inside enclosed muscle fascia cuts off blood flow, causing severe pain and potential tissue death. Fat embolism syndrome can occur when fat globules from bone marrow enter the bloodstream and lodge in the lungs, typically presenting with respiratory distress and a petechial rash. Other risks include infection, especially with open fractures, delayed union where healing takes longer than normal, and nonunion where the bone fails to heal altogether.
