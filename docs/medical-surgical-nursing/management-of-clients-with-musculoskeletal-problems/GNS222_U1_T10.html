Fracture

Introduction and Definition of Fractures

A fracture is defined as a complete or incomplete disruption in the continuity of bone structure. In nursing practice, understanding fractures is fundamental for orthopedic care, acute pain management, and preventing long-term physical disabilities. When an external mechanical force is applied to a bone that exceeds its structural strength and elasticity, the bone tissue fails, resulting in a break. Fractures can occur in isolation as a result of trauma, or they can be pathological, arising from underlying disease processes that weaken the skeletal system. As nursing students preparing for clinical practice in Nigeria and beyond, you must recognize that bone is a dynamic, highly vascularized living tissue capable of remarkable regeneration. However, a fractured bone disrupts not only structural integrity but also damages surrounding soft tissues, periosteum, blood vessels, and nerves. Consequently, assessment of neurovascular status distal to the injury site is a critical competency. The severity of a fracture depends on the magnitude of the force, the specific bone involved, and the general health status of the patient. Comprehensive patient assessment involves understanding how the structural disruption affects overall mobility, systemic homeostasis, and psychological well-being. Recognizing the immediate consequences of a fracture enables the healthcare team to institute timely interventions, minimize secondary tissue damage, and set the foundation for optimal bone healing and rehabilitation. Effective patient education begins at this foundational level, ensuring patients comprehend why immobilization and proper alignment are necessary for their recovery.

Pathophysiology and the Bone Healing Process

The bone healing process is a sophisticated biological cascade divided into distinct yet overlapping phases: inflammatory, reparative, and remodeling. Immediately following a fracture, bleeding from broken bone ends and damaged periosteum forms a fracture hematoma within the injury site. This initiates the inflammatory phase, where localized hypoxia, cell death, and an influx of inflammatory cells occur, clearing away cellular debris and releasing growth factors. Within days, the reparative phase begins. Fibroblasts, chondroblasts, and osteoblasts migrate into the hematoma, transforming it into a fibrocartilaginous callus that provides soft stabilization. Vascular ingrowth occurs simultaneously, supplying necessary nutrients. Gradually, this soft callus is ossified through endochondral ossification, converting the fibrocartilaginous bridge into a hard, woven bone callus. Finally, the remodeling phase can take months to years. Osteoclasts resorb excess and structurally unnecessary bone, while osteoblasts lay down compact lamellar bone along lines of mechanical stress. By the end of this phase, the bone regains its original shape, strength, and structural integrity. Nursing students must understand these stages because interventions such as immobilization, adequate nutrition, and weight-bearing progression directly support or hinder specific physiological healing phases. Disrupting the healing microenvironment through premature movement can delay union or lead to non-union, extending the patient's recovery period.

Classification and Types of Fractures

Fractures are classified using several criteria to guide medical diagnosis, surgical planning, and nursing care. Based on skin integrity, fractures are categorized as closed (simple), where the skin remains intact over the break, or open (compound), where a broken bone fragment punctures the skin, exposing the bone to the external environment and high infection risks. Structural configuration further defines fractures: transverse fractures break straight across the bone shaft; oblique fractures occur at an angle; spiral fractures twist around the bone shaft, often indicating rotational trauma or abuse; greenstick fractures are incomplete breaks seen primarily in children whose bones are pliable; and comminuted fractures feature multiple bone fragments. Additionally, fractures are described by their displacement status, noting whether the bone ends are aligned or displaced. Pathological fractures occur in bones weakened by disease such as osteoporosis, bone tumors, or osteogenesis imperfecta, requiring minimal trauma to break. Stress fractures result from repeated, cumulative mechanical overload, frequently seen in athletes or military recruits. Recognizing these classifications allows nurses to anticipate specific medical interventions, such as emergency antibiotic administration and surgical debridement for open fractures, or simple closed reduction and casting for stable closed transverse fractures.

Causes and Risk Factors

The etiology and risk factors associated with fractures span environmental, physical, and systemic variables. Traumatic causes are the most prevalent, including motor vehicle accidents, falls from height, occupational injuries, and sports-related trauma. These high-energy impacts deliver immense mechanical force that exceeds bone tolerance. Conversely, low-energy or fragility fractures occur due to systemic conditions that compromise bone mineral density and structural architecture. Osteoporosis is a primary risk factor, particularly in postmenopausal women due to estrogen deficiency, leading to porous, fragile bones susceptible to fractures from routine activities like bending or mild falls. Other medical conditions contributing to bone weakness include osteomalacia, renal osteodystrophy, hyperparathyroidism, and bone malignancies like multiple myeloma or metastatic lesions. Nutritional deficiencies, notably inadequate calcium and vitamin D intake during developmental years or later in life, impair bone mineralization. Lifestyle risk factors such as sedentary behavior, chronic smoking, and excessive alcohol consumption negatively impact calcium absorption and bone remodeling dynamics. Pharmaceutical agents like long-term corticosteroid therapy can also induce secondary osteoporosis. Understanding these causes helps nurses identify vulnerable patient populations, implement targeted health education regarding fall prevention, and advocate for dietary modifications and bone density screening programs.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Clinical manifestations of a fracture are distinct and require careful nursing assessment to differentiate from soft tissue injuries like sprains and strains. Classic signs and symptoms include localized pain and tenderness directly over the fracture site, which worsens with movement or weight-bearing due to muscle spasm and irritation of periosteal nerve endings. Loss of function or abnormal mobility of the affected limb is a hallmark sign. Deformity of the extremity may be visible, caused by displacement of bone fragments, muscle pull, or swelling. Edema and ecchymosis (bruising) develop rapidly due to extravasation of blood into surrounding soft tissues from ruptured blood vessels. Muscle spasms surrounding the break often pull bone fragments past each other, resulting in limb shortening. Crepitus, a crackling or grating sensation caused by bone fragments rubbing together, may be felt or heard upon gentle movement, though nurses should avoid purposely eliciting crepitus to prevent further tissue damage. Neurovascular compromise must be systematically assessed by evaluating the five Ps: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, and paralysis. Recognizing these signs promptly allows nurses to implement immediate immobilization measures and report critical changes, such as impending compartment syndrome, to the attending physician without delay.

Diagnostic Investigations

Diagnostic investigations are essential to confirm the presence, exact location, type, and displacement of a fracture. Plain radiography (X-rays) remains the primary diagnostic tool. Standard orthogonal views—typically anterior-posterior (AP) and lateral views—are obtained to visualize the entire bone and the joints proximal and distal to the injury site. X-rays reveal the fracture line, degree of displacement, and presence of comminution. In complex injuries, such as fractures involving weight-bearing joints, the spine, or the pelvis, advanced imaging modalities like Computed Tomography (CT) scans are utilized to provide detailed, cross-sectional three-dimensional views of the bone architecture. Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is exceptionally useful for detecting occult (hidden) fractures, stress fractures, and associated soft tissue injuries, including ligament tears, meniscal damage, and bone marrow edema. Laboratory investigations are also ordered as part of the comprehensive diagnostic workup. Complete blood counts (CBC) help assess blood loss and monitor for signs of infection in open fractures. Serum calcium, phosphorus, alkaline phosphatase, and vitamin D levels may be evaluated if a metabolic bone disorder or pathological fracture is suspected. Coagulation profiles are obtained prior to any planned surgical intervention to ensure safe operative management.

Medical and Surgical Management

Medical and surgical management of fractures focuses on achieving reduction (realignment of bone fragments), immobilization to maintain alignment, and restoration of function. Reduction can be closed, where manual traction and manipulation are applied to realign the bone without surgical incision, often followed by the application of plaster casts or splints. Alternatively, open reduction requires surgical exposure of the fracture site to align the bone fragments accurately under direct visualization. Once reduced, fractures must be immobilized. Internal fixation (ORIF - Open Reduction Internal Fixation) utilizes hardware such as metal plates, screws, intramedullary nails, and wires placed directly onto or inside the bone to hold fragments stable while healing occurs. External fixation involves placing percutaneous pins through the skin and bone above and below the fracture site, attaching them to an external metal frame; this method is frequently chosen for open fractures with severe soft tissue damage, unstable pelvic fractures, or infected non-unions. Traction—either skin traction or skeletal traction—may be used temporarily to reduce muscle spasms, realign bone ends, and immobilize extremities before definitive surgery. Pharmacological management is integral, incorporating analgesics for pain relief, antibiotics for open fractures to prevent osteomyelitis, and anticoagulant prophylaxis to guard against deep vein thrombosis during periods of immobilization.

Nursing Care and Patient Management

Nursing care and patient management require a holistic approach focused on monitoring recovery, preventing complications, promoting healing, and encouraging early rehabilitation. A cornerstone of nursing responsibility is performing frequent neurovascular checks (checking capillary refill, peripheral pulses, skin color, temperature, motor function, and sensation) on the affected extremity to detect ischemia or nerve damage early. For patients in casts or traction, nurses must inspect the skin around cast edges for pressure ulcers, ensure casts remain clean and dry, and maintain proper traction alignment with weights hanging freely off the bed. Pain management is paramount; nurses administer prescribed analgesics and implement non-pharmacological comfort measures such as elevation and cold therapy to reduce swelling. Mobility support involves assisting with safe transfers, teaching the correct use of assistive devices like crutches or walkers, and coordinating with physical therapists for progressive exercises. Nutritional support is vital for bone regeneration; nurses encourage high-protein diets rich in calcium, vitamin D, and Vitamin C while ensuring adequate fluid intake to prevent constipation associated with immobility and opioid use. Psychological support is equally important, as prolonged recovery and temporary loss of independence can lead to anxiety or depression. Comprehensive patient education ensures compliance with activity restrictions and follow-up care.

Complications of Fractures

Complications of fractures can be local or systemic, immediate or delayed, and pose significant threats to patient recovery if not identified and treated promptly. Compartment syndrome is a severe, limb-threatening local emergency characterized by increased pressure within a confined muscle compartment, compromising circulation and nerve function. Symptoms include severe pain out of proportion to the injury that is unrelieved by opioids, along with absent distal pulses and paresthesia, requiring emergent fasciotomy. Fat embolism syndrome (FES) is a serious systemic complication where fat globules from the yellow bone marrow are released into the bloodstream, frequently following long bone fractures; it manifests as a classic triad of respiratory distress, neurological changes (confusion), and a petechial rash. Venous thromboembolism (VTE), including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), can occur due to venous stasis from immobility and endothelial injury. Infection (osteomyelitis) is a major risk, particularly in open fractures or following surgical fixation, requiring aggressive antibiotic therapy and surgical debridement. Delayed union, malunion, or non-union are healing complications where the bone fails to unite correctly or in a timely fashion due to inadequate immobilization, poor blood supply, or infection. Nurses must maintain high vigilance for these complications to ensure immediate multidisciplinary intervention.
