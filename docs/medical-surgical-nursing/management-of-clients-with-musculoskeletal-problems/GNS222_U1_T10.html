Fracture

Introduction and Definition of Fractures

A fracture is a complete or partial disruption in the structural continuity of bone tissue. When the physical force applied to a bone exceeds its tensile strength, it breaks. While healthy bones withstand considerable impact, pathologic fractures occur in bones weakened by underlying disease states like osteoporosis, osteomyelitis, or metastatic bone lesions. In busy Nigerian emergency wards, road traffic crashes involving commercial motorcycles remain a leading driver of traumatic skeletal injuries, often presenting as severe open breaks complicated by soft tissue loss and contamination.

Classification and Types of Fractures

Skeletal injuries are categorized based on their fracture line pattern, relationship to the external environment, and stability. Recognizing these patterns guides orthopaedic management. - **Closed (Simple)**: The skin overlying the fracture site remains intact, minimizing the risk of external bacterial contamination. - **Open (Compound)**: The broken bone ends pierce through the skin or a deep wound extends to the bone, creating a direct pathway for infection. - **Transverse**: The break line runs straight across the bone shaft at a right angle. - **Oblique**: The fracture line extends across the bone at an angle. - **Spiral**: Encircling the bone shaft, this pattern typically results from a twisting rotational force. - **Comminuted**: The bone shatters into three or more distinct fragments. - **Greenstick**: An incomplete fracture where one side of the bone breaks while the other bends, common in pediatric patients due to softer, more pliable bones.

Pathophysiology and the Bone Healing Process

Bone healing is a remarkable biological process of regeneration rather than simple scar formation. It proceeds through four overlapping phases: 1. **Hematoma Formation**: Immediately following the break, blood vessels rupture, bleeding into the injury site and forming a localized clot within hours. Bone cells deprived of blood supply at the fracture ends die. 2. **Fibrocartilaginous Callus Formation**: Within days, capillaries grow into the hematoma, and phagocytic cells clear debris. Fibroblasts and osteoblasts migrate from the periosteum and endosteum, producing fibrocartilage that bridges the broken ends. 3. **Bony Callus Formation**: Osteoblasts deposit spongy bone trabeculae, converting the soft fibrocartilaginous callus into a hard bony callus over three to four weeks. 4. **Remodeling**: Over months to years, osteoclasts resorb excess bony callus while osteoblasts lay down compact bone along lines of physical stress, eventually restoring the original anatomical contour.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

Detecting a skeletal injury requires systematic physical assessment. Classic signs include localized pain, swelling, bruising, and loss of functional use. Deformity and abnormal mobility are diagnostic hallmarks. Crepitus—a grating sensation caused by bone ends rubbing together—may be felt or heard, though clinicians should avoid deliberately eliciting it to prevent further soft tissue damage. Neurovascular assessment distal to the injury site is mandatory: check pulses, capillary refill, skin temperature, sensation, and motor movement to rule out acute ischemia or nerve compression.

Diagnostic Investigations

Clinical suspicion must be confirmed through objective imaging. Standard plain radiography (X-rays) taken in at least two planes—anteroposterior and lateral—is the primary diagnostic tool, capturing the exact location, alignment, and pattern of the break. Computed tomography scans provide detailed multi-planar views for complex intra-articular or spinal fractures. Magnetic resonance imaging helps identify occult hairline fractures and associated soft tissue injuries like ligamentous tears or bone bruising that X-rays miss.

Medical and Surgical Management

Management focuses on reduction, retention, and rehabilitation. Reduction realigns displaced bone fragments, which can be achieved through closed manipulation or open surgical exposure. Once aligned, the bone must be immobilized (retained) to allow healing. - **Closed Reduction and External Immobilization**: Manual manipulation followed by plaster casts, functional braces, or traction. - **Open Reduction and Internal Fixation (ORIF)**: Surgical incision to realign fragments, secured using plates, screws, intramedullary nails, or wires. - **External Fixation**: Pins are inserted through the skin into the bone and attached to an external metal frame, frequently utilized in open fractures with extensive soft tissue damage or contamination common in high-impact trauma settings.

Nursing Care and Patient Management

Comprehensive nursing care revolves around promoting bone healing, preventing complications, and supporting functional independence. For patients in casts or traction, perform frequent neurovascular checks and inspect skin integrity around device edges. Maintain correct traction alignment; weights must hang freely and never rest on the floor. Encourage deep breathing exercises to prevent atelectasis from immobility. Nutritional support rich in protein, calcium, and vitamin D is vital for cellular repair. Pain management incorporates prescribed analgesics balanced with non-pharmacological comfort measures.

Complications of Fractures

Both early and late complications can threaten limb and life. Acute compartment syndrome occurs when increased pressure within a closed fascial space compromises circulation and nerve function, marked by severe pain unrelieved by opioids and pain on passive stretch. Fat embolism syndrome typically follows long bone fractures when fat globules occlude microvessels in the lungs and brain, presenting with respiratory distress, petechiae, and altered mental status. Delayed union, nonunion, and malunion represent healing failures, while osteomyelitis poses a severe risk in open fractures due to bacterial seeding.
