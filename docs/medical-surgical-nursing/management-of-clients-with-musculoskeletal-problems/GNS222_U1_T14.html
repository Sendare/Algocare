Osteoarthritis

Pathophysiology and Joint Changes

Osteoarthritis is fundamentally a disease of whole joints, characterized primarily by the progressive breakdown of articular cartilage. Under normal physiological conditions, chondrocytes maintain a delicate balance between the synthesis and degradation of the extracellular matrix. In osteoarthritis, this balance shifts heavily toward degradation, mediated by inflammatory cytokines and enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases.

As the smooth, glistening articular cartilage thins, softens, and ultimately forms vertical clefts or fissures—a process termed fibrillation—the underlying subchondral bone loses its protective cushion. In response to altered biomechanical stress and micro-trauma, the subchondral bone undergoes sclerosis, becoming dense and ivory-like. Concurrently, bone outgrowths known as osteophytes develop at the joint margins, and the synovial membrane frequently exhibits mild to moderate chronic inflammation. Joint space narrowing observed on imaging reflects this extensive loss of articular cartilage.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Joint degeneration arises from a complex interplay of systemic and mechanical determinants. Primary osteoarthritis has no identifiable single cause, though it correlates strongly with chronological aging and cumulative joint wear. Secondary osteoarthritis stems from a predisposing event or condition, such as significant joint trauma, congenital abnormalities, metabolic disorders, or prolonged occupational stress.

  • Age: The prevalence climbs steeply after middle age as chondrocytes lose their regenerative capacity and cumulative microtrauma adds up.
  • Obesity: Excess body weight places disproportionate mechanical loads on weight-bearing joints like the knees and hips, while adipose tissue also secretes systemic inflammatory adipokines.
  • Genetics: A positive family history, particularly involving hereditary hand or knee osteoarthritis, suggests genetic susceptibility influencing collagen structure.
  • Gender and Hormones: Post-menopausal women experience a higher incidence of severe knee osteoarthritis, pointing to the protective role of estrogen.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

The clinical presentation of osteoarthritis develops insidiously, often leading clients to ignore early symptoms until functional limitation sets in. Joint pain is the hallmark complaint, typically exacerbated by physical activity and relieved by rest. As the disease advances, pain may occur even at rest or disturb sleep.

Stiffness is another prominent feature, characteristically lasting less than thirty minutes upon awakening or following a period of inactivity—distinguishing it from the prolonged morning stiffness seen in inflammatory arthropathies like rheumatoid arthritis. On physical examination, crepitus—a grating or cracking sensation during joint movement—may be palpable or audible. Bony enlargements at the distal interphalangeal joints, known as Heberden's nodes, and at the proximal interphalangeal joints, called Bouchard's nodes, are classic signs in hand involvement. Range of motion gradually diminishes, and joint instability or misalignment can appear in later stages.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosing osteoarthritis relies heavily on a thorough clinical history and physical examination, corroborated by targeted investigations. Plain radiography remains the primary imaging modality. Radiographic hallmarks include joint space narrowing resulting from cartilage loss, subchondral sclerosis, subchondral cyst formation, and marginal osteophytes.

Laboratory tests are generally normal in primary osteoarthritis and are primarily utilized to rule out inflammatory, crystal-induced, or infectious arthropathies. For instance, erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein levels remain within normal limits, distinguishing degenerative joint disease from systemic inflammatory conditions. Synovial fluid analysis, if performed, typically reveals a clear to pale yellow fluid with good viscosity and a low white blood cell count, confirming a non-inflammatory effusion.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Management strategies focus on relieving pain, maintaining or improving joint mobility, and minimizing functional impairment. Treatment begins with non-pharmacological modalities, including structured exercise programs, weight reduction, physical therapy, and the use of assistive devices like walking frames or knee braces.

Pharmacological interventions follow a stepwise approach. Acetaminophen is often considered a first-line oral analgesic for mild to moderate pain, though non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs provide superior relief when inflammation is present. Topical NSAIDs are favored for superficial joints like the knee due to a lower systemic side-effect profile. Intra-articular corticosteroid injections offer temporary relief during acute inflammatory flares, whereas viscosupplementation with hyaluronic acid derivatives aims to restore joint lubrication. Long-term opioid therapy is generally reserved for severe cases refractory to other measures.

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Nursing care for individuals with osteoarthritis centers on promoting functional independence, managing pain, and supporting lifestyle modifications. Nurses assess the client's activities of daily living, home environment, and pain coping mechanisms.

Rehabilitation relies heavily on balanced exercise prescription. Low-impact aerobic activities, such as swimming and cycling, combined with quadriceps-strengthening exercises, help stabilize the knee joint and reduce stress on the articular surfaces. Heat therapy applied prior to exercise can relax surrounding muscles and ease stiffness, whereas cold packs are useful for mitigating acute post-activity swelling. Education on energy conservation techniques and proper body mechanics prevents undue joint strain during daily routines.

Surgical Interventions

When conservative measures fail to control pain or restore acceptable function, surgical options are considered. Arthroscopic debridement and lavage may be performed to remove loose bodies, torn menisci, or osteophytes, though its benefits are often limited in advanced degenerative disease.

Joint realignment procedures, such as osteotomies, shift weight away from damaged compartments of the knee toward healthier cartilage, particularly in younger clients. For end-stage disease, total joint arthroplasty—joint replacement—is the definitive intervention. Total knee and total hip replacements involve removing the diseased bone and cartilage and resurfacing the joint with metal and high-grade plastic components, successfully eliminating pain and restoring mobility.

Complications and Client Education

Living with a chronic degenerative condition introduces various risks that demand proactive education and monitoring. Chronic pain and limited mobility frequently lead to sedentary lifestyles, weight gain, social isolation, and depressive symptoms. Falls are a significant hazard, especially when lower extremity joints lack stability or pain impairs gait.

Client education must emphasize self-management strategies. Topics include weight management, proper footwear, utilization of assistive devices, and pacing daily activities. Clients taking long-term pharmacological therapies need instruction on recognizing adverse effects, such as gastrointestinal bleeding from NSAIDs. Empowering individuals with realistic expectations and coping tools promotes long-term independence and quality of life.
