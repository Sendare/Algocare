Osteoarthritis

Introduction and Pathophysiology

Osteoarthritis is the most common form of joint disease, often called wear-and-tear arthritis, though it is actually a whole-joint disorder. The primary issue begins with the breakdown of articular cartilage, the smooth, slippery tissue that covers the ends of bones and allows joints to glide easily.

As the cartilage wears away, it becomes rough, pitted, and eventually thins out completely. This exposes the underlying bone ends, which begin to rub directly against each other during movement. To compensate for the lost cartilage and increased friction, the bone reacts by remodeling itself.

  1. Cartilage degeneration: The smooth protective cushion breaks down and loses its elasticity.
  2. Subchondral bone thickening: The bone beneath the cartilage hardens and becomes dense.
  3. Osteophyte formation: Bony spurs grow out at the edges of the joint as the bone tries to spread out weight load.
  4. Synovial inflammation: The joint lining becomes mildly irritated, producing excess joint fluid that causes swelling.

Over time, these changes lead to chronic pain, joint stiffness, and a gradual loss of normal joint movement. Unlike rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis is non-systemic, meaning it does not cause widespread body fatigue or organ inflammation.

Causes and Risk Factors

Developing osteoarthritis depends on a mix of systemic traits and mechanical stressors on the joints. While aging is the single biggest risk factor because cartilage naturally loses water content and becomes more brittle over time, aging alone does not tell the whole story.

  • Excess body weight: Puts continuous, heavy mechanical stress on weight-bearing joints like the knees and hips, and fat tissue also releases inflammatory chemicals.
  • Joint trauma: Previous sports injuries, fractures, or major ligament tears can damage cartilage and set the stage for early arthritis years later.
  • Repetitive occupational stress: Jobs that require kneeling, heavy lifting, or constant bending wear down specific joints prematurely.
  • Genetics: Inherited flaws in cartilage proteins or slight bone alignment issues can make some families more prone to the condition.
  • Gender: Women are more likely than men to develop osteoarthritis, especially after menopause when protective hormones drop.

A common misconception is that cracking your knuckles causes osteoarthritis. Research shows that knuckle cracking does not damage joint cartilage or increase your risk of getting arthritis.

Signs and Symptoms

Clients with osteoarthritis usually report a slow, gradual onset of joint discomfort that worsens over months or years. Pain is typically aggravated by activity and relieved by rest in the early stages, but as the disease advances, pain can become constant and even disrupt sleep.

Joint stiffness is another hallmark sign, most notable first thing in the morning or after a long period of sitting still. Unlike the prolonged stiffness of inflammatory arthritis, osteoarthritis stiffness usually eases up within thirty minutes once movement begins.

Physical examination often reveals crepitus, a grating or cracking sound felt or heard when moving the joint. Joints may look enlarged or misshapen due to bone spurs. In the hands, hard bony lumps commonly form at the distal finger joints, known as Heberden's nodes, or at the proximal joints, known as Bouchard's nodes. Range of motion gradually decreases, and clients may develop a limp if weight-bearing joints are badly affected.

Diagnostic Evaluation

Diagnosing osteoarthritis relies heavily on a careful health history and a physical examination of the affected joints. Healthcare providers look for the classic pattern of activity-related pain, brief morning stiffness, and physical signs like crepitus or bony enlargement.

Imaging studies are primarily used to confirm the diagnosis and rule out other joint problems. X-rays are the standard imaging tool because they are inexpensive and readily available. An X-ray of an arthritic joint typically shows:

  • Joint space narrowing: Caused by the loss of protective cartilage between the bones.
  • Subchondral sclerosis: Whitish, hardened patches of bone right under the missing cartilage.
  • Osteophyte development: Visible bony spurs jutting out from the joint margins.
  • Bone cysts: Small fluid-filled holes that can form in the bone beneath the joint surface.

Blood tests are not used to diagnose osteoarthritis because inflammatory markers like ESR and CRP remain normal. Blood work is mainly ordered to rule out rheumatoid arthritis, gout, or joint infections if the clinical picture is unclear.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Management of osteoarthritis focuses on relieving pain, maintaining joint mobility, and minimizing functional disability. Because joint damage cannot be reversed, treatment is tailored to the severity of symptoms and the client's overall health.

Initial pharmacological treatment often begins with acetaminophen for mild to moderate pain. If inflammation is present, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen or diclofenac are prescribed topically or orally. Topical NSAIDs are especially favored in older adults because they carry fewer systemic side effects, such as stomach ulcers or kidney strain, compared to pills.

For persistent pain, intra-articular injections may be used:

  • Corticosteroid injections: Provide quick, temporary relief during severe inflammatory flare-ups, though they are usually limited to a few times a year.
  • Hyaluronic acid injections: Act as a joint lubricant and shock absorber, though results vary widely among clients.

Assistive devices such as canes, walkers, and knee braces help take physical pressure off painful weight-bearing joints during daily activities.

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation

Nurses play a central role in helping clients manage chronic osteoarthritis pain and maintain their independence. Exercise is one of the most effective non-drug therapies, though clients often fear that movement will damage their joints further.

Nurses must teach clients that low-impact exercises—such as swimming, cycling, and walking—strengthen the muscles supporting the joint, improve flexibility, and nourish cartilage through joint fluid movement. Physical therapy referrals are essential for creating a safe, personalized exercise routine.

Weight management guidance is equally vital. Losing even a modest amount of weight significantly reduces the mechanical load on knees and hips, translating to noticeable pain relief. Application of heat and cold provides cheap, accessible comfort: heat relaxes stiff muscles before exercise, while cold packs reduce acute swelling after activity.

Nurses also assess home safety, suggesting modifications like grab bars in the bathroom, raised toilet seats, and removing tripping hazards so clients can move around safely despite joint stiffness.

Surgical Interventions

When conservative treatments and lifestyle changes no longer control pain or maintain function, surgical options are considered. Surgery is generally reserved for clients whose quality of life is severely impaired by joint pain and stiffness.

  • Arthroscopy: A minimally invasive procedure using a tiny camera and small instruments to clean out loose cartilage fragments or smooth torn surfaces. It is mostly helpful if the joint is locking up, but it does not cure underlying arthritis.
  • Osteotomy: Involves cutting and reshaping the bone to shift body weight away from the damaged part of the joint, often used in younger clients to delay total joint replacement.
  • Arthrodesis: Surgical fusion of the bones making up a joint. While it eliminates pain, it also eliminates all movement in that joint, so it is rarely used today except for small joints in the spine, hands, or feet.
  • Arthroplasty (Joint Replacement): The gold standard for advanced hip and knee osteoarthritis. Damaged bone and cartilage are removed and replaced with artificial metal and plastic components, restoring smooth movement and drastically reducing pain.

Complications and Client Education

Living with chronic osteoarthritis brings several potential complications that nurses must help clients navigate. Chronic pain and limited mobility often lead to a sedentary lifestyle, which increases the risk of weight gain, muscle weakness, depression, and cardiovascular deconditioning.

Clients taking long-term NSAIDs for pain relief are at high risk for gastrointestinal bleeding and kidney dysfunction. Nurses must educate clients to report warning signs like black tarry stools, stomach pain, or unusual swelling in the feet.

Comprehensive client education focuses on self-management strategies:

  • Pacing daily activities to avoid overworking painful joints.
  • Maintaining correct posture and using proper body mechanics when lifting or standing.
  • Sticking to a prescribed weight-loss diet if overweight.
  • Keeping up with assistive devices and home safety modifications to prevent dangerous falls.

Empowering clients with knowledge ensures they remain active partners in managing their chronic condition and preserving their independence.
