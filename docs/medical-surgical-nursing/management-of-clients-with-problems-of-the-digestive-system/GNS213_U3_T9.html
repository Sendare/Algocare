Disorders of the Large Intestine

Anatomy and Physiology of the Large Intestine

The large intestine, spanning roughly 1.5 meters, forms the final terminal portion of the gastrointestinal tract. It comprises the cecum, appendix, colon, rectum, and anal canal. Unlike the small intestine, the colon features unique longitudinal muscular bands called taeniae coli that gather the bowel wall into distinct sacculations known as haustra.

Primary physiological functions center on fluid absorption, electrolyte regulation, and waste compaction. Approximately one to two liters of chyme enter the cecum daily, rich in water and unabsorbed nutrients. The mucosa extracts the vast majority of this fluid, transforming liquid residue into solid or semi-solid feces. Microscopic resident flora synthesize essential vitamins, particularly vitamin K and certain B vitamins. Peristaltic movements are generally sluggish, featuring mass movements a few times daily to propel fecal contents toward the rectum for elimination.

Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures

Clinical evaluation of large intestine pathology requires a systematic approach combining physical examination and advanced visualization tools.

  • Abdominal Inspection and Palpation: Auscultate bowel sounds in all four quadrants before palpating to avoid altering peristaltic activity. Note any tenderness, masses, or abdominal distension.
  • Digital Rectal Examination (DRE): Essential for detecting palpable masses, rectal bleeding, or tenderness near the anal verge.
  • Colonoscopy: The gold standard diagnostic procedure allowing direct visualization of the entire colonic mucosa and biopsy collection.
  • Stool Examination: Evaluates for occult blood, ova, parasites, and pathogenic bacterial cultures.

Inflammatory Bowel Diseases: Crohn's Disease and Ulcerative Colitis

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) encompasses chronic, relapsing immune-mediated disorders of the gastrointestinal tract.

  • Crohn's Disease: Can affect any segment of the GI tract from mouth to anus, typically characterized by transmural inflammation (involving the entire bowel wall thickness) and skip lesions. This discontinuous pattern often leads to strictures, fistulas, and deep fissures.
  • Ulcerative Colitis: Restricted exclusively to the colon and rectum, presenting continuous mucosal and submucosal inflammation starting at the rectum and extending proximally. Patients frequently experience bloody diarrhea and pus-filled ulcerations.

Management focuses on suppressing the inflammatory cascade using aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and biologic agents, alongside nutritional support during acute flares.

Diverticular Disease and Diverticulitis

Diverticulosis refers to the presence of multiple small outpouchings (diverticula) in the colonic wall, formed where blood vessels penetrate the circular muscle layer. This condition correlates strongly with chronic constipation and low dietary fiber intake, which increase intraluminal pressure.

When these pouches become inflamed or micro-perforated, the condition transitions to diverticulitis. Clinical manifestations typically include acute left lower quadrant abdominal pain, fever, nausea, and an elevated white blood cell count. Uncomplicated cases respond well to clear liquid diets, oral broad-spectrum antibiotics, and bowel rest. Severe complications like abscess formation, perforation, or peritonitis necessitate surgical resection and temporary or permanent colostomy creation.

Irritable Bowel Syndrome and Functional Disorders

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) is a common functional gastrointestinal disorder diagnosed based on clinical criteria rather than structural abnormalities. Patients experience chronic abdominal pain linked with alterations in bowel habits, categorized by predominant stool consistency into diarrhea-dominant, constipation-dominant, or mixed types.

Pathophysiology involves visceral hypersensitivity, dysregulated gut-brain interactions, and altered GI motility. Diagnosis is one of exclusion, requiring thorough investigation to rule out organic pathologies such as IBD, celiac disease, or malignancy. Management emphasizes lifestyle modification, dietary adjustments (such as low-FODMAP diets), stress management, and pharmacotherapy targeting specific symptoms like antispasmodics or laxatives.

Colorectal Polyps and Colorectal Cancer

Colorectal cancer is a major global health concern, frequently developing from benign mucosal growths known as adenomatous polyps. The transformation from a normal epithelium to an invasive adenocarcinoma typically follows a multi-step genetic pathway over several years, making screening highly effective.

Risk factors include advanced age, family history, inflammatory bowel disease, and lifestyle factors such as high red meat consumption, smoking, and physical inactivity. Early-stage colorectal cancer is often asymptomatic, which underscores the importance of routine screening via fecal occult blood tests and colonoscopy. Symptoms of advanced disease include rectal bleeding, unexplained weight loss, change in bowel habits, and iron-deficiency anemia.

Intestinal Obstruction of the Large Bowel

Large bowel obstruction (LBO) represents a critical surgical emergency where the normal forward flow of intestinal contents is mechanically blocked. Common causes include colorectal cancer, volvulus (twisting of the bowel), and severe diverticular strictures.

Pathophysiologically, obstruction leads to significant fluid and gas accumulation proximal to the blockage, increasing intraluminal pressure. This pressure compromises mucosal blood supply, risking ischemia, necrosis, and bowel perforation. Clinical signs include progressive abdominal distension, severe cramping pain, absolute constipation, and feculent vomiting in late stages. Management requires rapid fluid resuscitation, nasogastric decompression, and prompt surgical intervention.

Nursing Management and Patient Care

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with large intestine disorders requires vigilant monitoring, symptom management, and supportive education. For patients undergoing bowel surgery or experiencing acute inflammation, nurses must closely monitor fluid and electrolyte balance, vital signs, and abdominal status.

When caring for a patient with a new ostomy, the nurse assesses stoma color and viability—a healthy stoma should appear pink to red and moist. Pale, dusky, or black coloration indicates compromised blood flow and must be reported immediately. Postoperative care also includes pain management, early ambulation to prevent venous thromboembolism, and progressive advancement of diet as bowel motility returns.
