Disorders of the Large Intestine

Anatomy and Physiology of the Large Intestine

The large intestine, or colon, plays a vital role in our digestive system. It starts at the cecum and ends at the rectum and anal canal. Its main jobs are absorbing water and electrolytes, manufacturing certain vitamins through normal gut bacteria, and forming and storing stool until it is ready to leave the body.

  • Cecum and Appendix: The pouch-like beginning of the colon where the small intestine empties its contents.
  • Colon Segments: Divided into the ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid colons, each making sharp turns called flexures.
  • Rectum and Anus: The final holding area for waste before the anal sphincters control its release.
  • Normal Flora: Friendly bacteria living inside that help break down leftover food and produce vitamin K.

Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures

Checking the health of the large intestine requires a mix of physical exams, laboratory tests, and direct visualization tools. Nurses play a major role in preparing patients for these tests and monitoring them afterward.

  • Colonoscopy: A flexible tube with a camera is passed through the rectum to view the entire colon lining and remove polyps.
  • Stool Tests: Checked for occult blood, parasites, or signs of inflammation and infection.
  • Abdominal Imaging: X-rays and CT scans help spot blockages, free air, or signs of bowel wall thickening.
  • Barium Enema: A special liquid contrast is introduced via the rectum to highlight the large bowel on X-rays.

Inflammatory Bowel Diseases: Crohn's and Colitis

Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) covers chronic, relapsing conditions marked by severe inflammation in the digestive tract. It includes Crohn's disease and Ulcerative Colitis. While both cause abdominal pain and diarrhea, they behave differently in the body.

Crohn's disease can affect any part of the digestive tract from mouth to anus, and inflammation often goes through the entire thickness of the bowel wall, creating patchy 'skip lesions'. Ulcerative Colitis is limited strictly to the colon and rectum, starting at the rectum and moving upward in a continuous line, affecting only the inner mucosal layer.

Diverticular Disease and Diverticulitis

Diverticulosis happens when small, bulging pouches called diverticula push outward through weak spots in the colon wall, usually in the sigmoid colon. This is often linked to a low-fiber diet and chronic straining during bowel movements.

When these pouches become blocked, irritated, and inflamed, the condition turns into diverticulitis. Patients experience sharp left lower quadrant abdominal pain, fever, and nausea. Management includes clear liquids, antibiotics, and avoiding high-fiber foods during the acute painful flare-up, followed by a high-fiber diet once healing occurs to prevent future episodes.

Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

Irritable Bowel Syndrome is a common functional bowel disorder where the structure of the bowel looks completely normal, but how it works is disturbed. Patients experience recurrent abdominal pain linked with changes in bowel habits, such as diarrhea, constipation, or alternating between both.

Triggers include stress, certain foods, and hormonal shifts. Management focuses on lifestyle changes, stress management, identifying trigger foods, and using specific medications to control cramping, constipation, or diarrhea.

Colorectal Polyps and Colorectal Cancer

Colorectal cancer is one of the most common malignancies of the digestive system. It almost always begins as a small, non-cancerous growth on the inner lining of the colon called a polyp. Over several years, certain types of polyps, particularly adenomas, can undergo dysplasia and turn into cancer.

Screening tests like colonoscopies save lives because doctors can find and remove pre-cancerous polyps before they turn malignant. Risk factors include age, a family history of colon cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, and diets high in red meat and low in fiber.

Intestinal Obstruction of the Large Bowel

A large bowel obstruction happens when something blocks the normal flow of digested contents through the colon. Common causes include colorectal cancer, twisted bowel loops called volvulus, and severe scar tissue from past surgeries.

Signs and symptoms develop more slowly than small bowel blockages, but include progressive abdominal distension, cramping pain, absolute constipation, and failure to pass gas. Vomiting occurs late in the process. A complete blockage is a surgical emergency because pressure can build up and cause the bowel wall to rupture, leading to peritonitis.

Nursing Management and Patient Care

Caring for patients with large intestine disorders requires careful monitoring, pain relief, skin care, and nutritional support. Nurses watch closely for signs of complications such as bleeding, dehydration, bowel perforation, or electrolyte imbalances.

For patients with a new ostomy, teaching is vital. The nurse helps the patient and family learn how to empty and change the pouch, inspect the stoma color—which should be pink and moist—and cope with body image changes. Dietary counseling helps patients avoid foods that cause excessive gas or blockages.
