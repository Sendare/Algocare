Disorders of the Stomach

Anatomy and Physiology of the Stomach

The stomach is a muscular, J-shaped organ situated in the upper left part of the abdomen, right below the diaphragm. It acts as a temporary reservoir for food, mixing it with powerful gastric juices to break it down into a semi-liquid paste called chyme before it enters the small intestine.

The stomach wall features several specialized cell types that keep digestion running smoothly:

  • Mucous neck cells: Produce protective mucus and bicarbonate to line the stomach and shield it from self-digestion.
  • Parietal cells: Secrete hydrochloric acid, creating the acidic environment needed to activate digestive enzymes, and intrinsic factor, which is vital for absorbing vitamin B12.
  • Chief cells: Release pepsinogen, the inactive precursor to pepsin, which starts breaking down proteins.
  • G cells: Release the hormone gastrin into the bloodstream to stimulate acid secretion.

A common misconception is that the stomach is the primary site of nutrient absorption. In reality, very little absorption happens here, with the exception of water, alcohol, and certain medications like aspirin.

Gastritis: Acute and Chronic Forms

Gastritis is an inflammation of the gastric mucosa. It can manifest swiftly as an acute condition or develop slowly over time as a chronic issue.

Acute gastritis is often triggered by local irritants. Common culprits include regular use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, heavy alcohol consumption, severe physical stress from burns or trauma, and bacterial infections. Patients typically complain of sudden, sharp epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting.

Chronic gastritis involves long-term mucosal inflammation and is heavily linked to *Helicobacter pylori* bacterial colonization or autoimmune destruction of the gastric parietal cells. Over many years, chronic inflammation can cause the stomach lining to thin out, leading to a condition called atrophic gastritis. This process destroys the cells that produce acid and intrinsic factor, ultimately preventing the proper absorption of vitamin B12 and resulting in pernicious anemia.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

Peptic ulcer disease involves erosive lesions in the mucosal lining of the stomach or duodenum where the acidic digestive juices eat away at the unprotected tissue. The two most frequent causes are chronic *H. pylori* infection and the routine use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, which block the protective prostaglandins in the stomach wall.

Gastric ulcers and duodenal ulcers present slightly differently. Gastric ulcer pain often flares up shortly after eating because food stimulates acid production while the ulcer is directly exposed. Conversely, duodenal ulcer pain typically improves temporarily when eating, only to return a few hours later when the stomach empties and acid flows into the duodenum.

Left untreated, ulcers can erode deeply into the muscle wall, leading to serious complications like hemorrhage, gastric outlet obstruction, or sudden perforation, which causes acute, severe abdominal rigidity and board-like pain.

Gastric Cancer and Neoplasms

Gastric cancer usually begins in the glandular cells of the inner stomach lining, developing slowly over many years. Risk factors include a diet high in salted, smoked, or pickled foods, smoking, chronic *H. pylori* infection, and a family history of stomach malignancies.

In its early stages, gastric cancer is notoriously silent, often producing no symptoms at all or mimicking simple gastritis or indigestion. As the tumor grows, patients may notice vague upper abdominal discomfort, early satiety, unexplained weight loss, fatigue, and difficulty swallowing if the mass blocks the upper GI tract.

Diagnostic confirmation requires an upper endoscopy with tissue biopsies. Because symptoms usually appear only after the cancer has advanced into deeper layers or spread to nearby lymph nodes, the overall prognosis remains guarded compared to other gastrointestinal malignancies.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Assessing a patient with a suspected gastric disorder starts with a detailed medical history. The nurse must ask about dietary habits, alcohol and tobacco use, pain characteristics, and any regular use of pain medications like ibuprofen or aspirin. Physical examination of the abdomen is usually unremarkable unless a complication like obstruction or perforation has occurred, though mild epigastric tenderness may be noted.

Diagnostic tests help pinpoint the exact cause of discomfort:

  • Upper Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (EGD): The gold standard test that lets the clinician directly visualize the mucosa and take tissue samples for biopsy or *H. pylori* testing.
  • Stool Tests: Used to check for hidden blood, which indicates active gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Breath or Blood Tests: Detect antibodies or active antigens related to *H. pylori* infection.
  • Complete Blood Count: Checks for anemia caused by chronic blood loss or vitamin deficiency.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical treatment for stomach disorders focuses on reducing gastric acidity, clearing out bacterial infections, and protecting the vulnerable mucosal lining.

Several medication classes work together to achieve these goals:

  • Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs): Drugs like omeprazole block the enzyme system that produces acid, providing powerful, long-lasting acid suppression.
  • H2-Receptor Antagonists: Medications like famotidine reduce the amount of acid released into the stomach, helping ulcers heal more slowly.
  • Antacids: Over-the-counter options like magnesium hydroxide neutralize existing stomach acid for rapid, temporary pain relief.
  • Antibiotic Regimens: A combination of specific antibiotics is prescribed alongside a PPI to completely eradicate *H. pylori* infections.
  • Mucosal Protectants: Agents like sucralfate form a thick, protective paste that coats an ulcer site, shielding it from further acid damage.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with gastric disorders revolves around managing pain, ensuring proper nutrition, preventing complications, and educating the patient on lifestyle changes.

Pain management involves administering prescribed medications on time and monitoring the patient for any adverse drug reactions. The nurse should also encourage lifestyle adjustments, such as eating smaller, more frequent meals to prevent gastric distension, avoiding late-night eating, and cutting out irritants like caffeine, spicy foods, alcohol, and nicotine.

Patient education is vital for long-term recovery. The nurse must teach the patient to recognize red-flag symptoms that require immediate medical attention, such as black tarry stools, persistent vomiting, or sudden, sharp abdominal pain. Finally, if the patient has had a partial gastrectomy or atrophic gastritis, education must cover the lifelong need for vitamin B12 injections to prevent pernicious anemia.
