Disorders of the Stomach

Anatomy and Physiology of the Stomach

The stomach is a J-shaped muscular organ located in the upper left quadrant of the abdomen, serving as a temporary reservoir for ingested food while initiating mechanical and chemical breakdown. Anatomically, it is divided into four main regions: the cardia, fundus, body, and pylorus. The internal lining is thrown into folds known as **rugae**, which allow the organ to expand significantly following a meal. Gastric secretions are produced by specialized cells within the gastric glands: - **Mucous neck cells**: Secrete alkaline mucus to protect the gastric epithelium from autodigestion by acid and enzymes. - **Parietal cells**: Produce **hydrochloric acid (HCl)**, creating a highly acidic environment essential for activating digestive enzymes, and intrinsic factor, which is required for vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum. - **Chief cells**: Secrete **pepsinogen**, the inactive precursor to pepsin, which begins protein digestion. - **G cells**: Release the hormone **gastrin** into the bloodstream, stimulating parietal cells to increase acid secretion and promoting gastric motility.

Gastritis: Acute and Chronic Forms

Gastritis refers to the inflammation of the gastric mucosa, manifesting in either acute or chronic forms. **Acute gastritis** typically develops suddenly due to local irritants such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), excessive alcohol consumption, or acute stress from severe trauma or burns. This form causes mucosal redness, edema, and superficial surface erosion. **Chronic gastritis** involves prolonged, persistent inflammation leading to mucosal atrophy and loss of functional gastric glands. It is frequently categorized into two main types: 1. **Type A (Autoimmune)**: Antibodies attack parietal cells, predominantly affecting the fundus and body, leading to pernicious anemia due to a lack of intrinsic factor. 2. **Type B (Helicobacter pylori-associated)**: Bacterial colonization primarily affects the antrum, strongly linked to chronic inflammation, peptic ulcer disease, and an increased risk of gastric malignancy.

Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

Peptic Ulcer Disease is characterized by mucosal erosion in the gastrointestinal tract exposed to gastric acid and pepsin, most commonly found in the proximal duodenum or the lesser curvature of the stomach. The two primary etiologies are chronic **Helicobacter pylori** infection and the regular use of **NSAIDs**, both of which compromise the mucosal defense barrier by reducing bicarbonate and prostaglandin production. Clients typically present with a gnawing or burning epigastric pain. A key clinical differentiator is the relationship between pain and food intake: - **Duodenal ulcers**: Pain often occurs 2 to 3 hours after meals and is frequently relieved by eating food or taking antacids. - **Gastric ulcers**: Pain is commonly exacerbated by eating shortly after ingestion, leading to food avoidance and potential weight loss. Complications can be life-threatening and require prompt intervention. **Hemorrhage** manifests as hematemesis or melena, **perforation** causes sudden, severe generalized abdominal pain with a rigid board-like abdomen, and **gastric outlet obstruction** results from edema or scarring at the pylorus, causing persistent vomiting.

Gastric Cancer and Neoplasms

Gastric cancer is a malignant neoplasm arising most frequently from the glandular epithelium of the mucosal lining, with **adenocarcinoma** accounting for over ninety percent of all cases. Risk factors include chronic Helicobacter pylori infection, diets high in salted, smoked, or preserved foods containing nitrates, and a family history of gastric malignancies. The lesion commonly develops in the pylorus or antrum. Early-stage gastric cancer is notoriously asymptomatic, leading to late diagnosis when metastases have already occurred. As the tumor enlarges, manifestations include persistent indigestion, early satiety, unexplained weight loss, iron deficiency anemia from chronic occult bleeding, and a palpable epigastric mass. Spread occurs through direct extension into adjacent organs, lymphatic drainage to regional nodes, and hematogenous dissemination to the liver and peritoneum.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Thorough clinical assessment begins with a detailed history focusing on pain patterns, dietary habits, NSAID use, and family history. Physical examination may reveal epigastric tenderness, abdominal distension, or signs of chronic blood loss such as pallor. When gastrointestinal pathology is suspected, several diagnostic modalities confirm the diagnosis: - **Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD)**: The gold standard test allowing direct visualization of the mucosal lining and targeted tissue biopsy for H. pylori or malignancy. - **Barium Swallow**: Fluoroscopic imaging using contrast media to outline structural abnormalities, strictures, or large ulcers. - **Urea Breath Test**: A non-invasive test detecting active H. pylori infection by measuring labeled carbon isotopes in exhaled breath after ingesting urea. - **Fecal Occult Blood Test**: Detects microscopic, hidden blood in stool samples, indicating gastrointestinal bleeding from ulcers or tumors.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Medical therapy for gastric disorders focuses on neutralizing or reducing acid secretion, eradicating infectious agents, and protecting compromised mucosal tissue. Pharmacological agents are selected based on the specific underlying pathology: - **Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs)**: Drugs such as omeprazole block the final hydrogen-potassium ATPase enzyme system, providing profound and long-lasting acid suppression. - **H2-Receptor Antagonists**: Medications like famotidine decrease acid production by blocking histamine receptors on parietal cells. - **Antacids**: Fast-acting agents like magnesium hydroxide neutralize existing gastric acid for immediate symptom relief. - **Mucosal Protective Agents**: Sucralfate forms a viscous, adhesive coating over the ulcer bed to shield it from acid and pepsin. - **Triple or Quadruple Therapy**: A combination of at least two antibiotics (such as amoxicillin and clarithromycin) paired with a PPI and sometimes bismuth subsalicylate to successfully eradicate **Helicobacter pylori** infection.

Surgical Interventions and Postoperative Care

Surgical management becomes necessary when gastric disorders fail medical therapy, or when complications like massive hemorrhage, obstruction, perforation, or malignancy arise. Common procedures include **partial gastrectomy**, **vagotomy** to reduce vagal stimulation of acid secretion, and **pyloroplasty** to widen the pyloric sphincter. Postoperative monitoring requires vigilance for complications. A major specific concern after gastric surgery is **Dumping Syndrome**, which occurs when undigested food dumps rapidly into the jejunum. This triggers an osmotic fluid shift into the bowel lumen and a subsequent reactive hypoglycemia. Manifestations include early vasomotor symptoms like dizziness, tachycardia, diaphoresis, and diarrhea shortly after eating. Nursing care involves advising the client to consume small, frequent meals, avoid drinking fluids with meals, and lie down briefly after eating to slow gastric emptying.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Holistic nursing care for clients with stomach disorders centers on managing acute pain, maintaining nutritional status, preventing complications, and promoting lifestyle modifications. The nurse monitors vital signs for signs of bleeding or infection, administers prescribed medications accurately, and evaluates pain relief. Patient education is critical for long-term disease control and prevention of recurrence. Clients must be instructed to: - Avoid known gastric irritants, including NSAIDs, spicy foods, caffeine, and alcohol. - Complete the full course of prescribed antibiotic therapy if treated for H. pylori, even if symptoms resolve early. - Recognize red flag symptoms that require immediate medical evaluation, such as persistent vomiting, black tarry stools, or sudden severe abdominal pain.
