Disorders of the Oesophagus

Anatomy and Physiology of the Oesophagus

The oesophagus is a muscular, collapsible tube measuring approximately twenty-five centimetres in length, extending from the pharynx down to the stomach. Its primary function is the transit of ingested food and liquids via coordinated muscular contractions known as peristalsis. Structurally, the wall consists of four layers: mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and adventitia. The muscular layer transitions from striated skeletal muscle in the upper third to smooth muscle in the lower third.

Two critical physiological sphincters regulate the unidirectional flow of gastrointestinal contents:

  • Upper Oesophageal Sphincter (UOS): A high-pressure zone preventing air entry into the digestive tract during respiration.
  • Lower Oesophageal Sphincter (LOS): A physiological sphincter acting as a mechanical barrier against the retrograde movement of highly acidic gastric contents into the lower oesophagus.

Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures

Accurate evaluation of oesophageal pathology requires a thorough clinical history focusing on dysphagia, odynophagia, heartburn, and regurgitation. Diagnostic modalities bridge the gap between subjective symptoms and objective anatomical visualization.

  • Oesophagogastroduodenoscopy (OGD): Direct visualization of the mucosal lining, enabling biopsy acquisition and therapeutic intervention.
  • Barium Swallow: Fluoroscopic imaging utilizing a contrast medium to evaluate structural narrowing, motility, and mucosal irregularities.
  • Oesophageal Manometry: Measures intraluminal pressure and muscular coordination, vital for diagnosing motility disorders.
  • 24-Hour pH Monitoring: Tracks acid exposure episodes over a full day to confirm gastro-oesophageal reflux disease.

Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD) and Hiatus Hernia

Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease occurs when chronic retrograde flow of gastric contents causes troublesome symptoms or mucosal damage. The frequent culprit is transient lower oesophageal sphincter relaxation. A hiatus hernia, where part of the stomach herniates upward through the diaphragmatic hiatus into the thorax, frequently coexists and exacerbates reflux by compromising the mechanical barrier of the crural diaphragm.

Patients report retrosternal burning pain known as pyrosis, often worsening when lying flat or bending forward. Long-term mucosal irritation can induce metaplasia, transforming normal squamous epithelium into specialized columnar epithelium—a precancerous condition termed Barrett's oesophagus. Management combines lifestyle modification, proton pump inhibitors, and surgical fundoplication for refractory cases.

Oesophagitis and Oesophageal Ulceration

Oesophagitis represents inflammation of the mucosal lining, triggered by various insults including gastric acid, infections (such as Candida in immunosuppressed individuals), and caustic ingestion. Chemical oesophagitis from swallowed corrosive substances or pill retention (pill-induced oesophagitis from drugs like doxycycline or iron supplements) can lead to severe mucosal erosion and ulceration.

Patients experience odynophagia, retrosternal pain, and dysphagia. Deep ulceration carries significant risks of stricture formation, haemorrhage, and perforation. Nursing care focuses on pain management, monitoring for signs of gastrointestinal bleeding or mediastinitis, and ensuring adequate nutritional support through modified diets or enteral feeding tubes.

Oesophageal Motility Disorders: Achalasia and Spasms

Motility disorders disrupt the coordinated peristaltic contractions required to propel boluses downward.

  • Achalasia: Characterized by the degeneration of ganglion cells in the myenteric plexus, leading to failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter to relax during swallowing and complete absence of normal peristalsis in the smooth muscle oesophagus. Food accumulates, causing gross dilation (megaoesophagus).
  • Diffuse Oesophageal Spasm: Features uncoordinated, simultaneous, non-peristaltic contractions causing severe chest pain that can mimic myocardial infarction.

Treatment aims to reduce lower oesophageal sphincter pressure via pneumatic dilation, surgical myotomy (Heller myotomy), or smooth muscle relaxants.

Oesophageal Varices and Bleeding Management

Oesophageal varices are dilated submucosal veins developing secondary to portal hypertension, most commonly caused by liver cirrhosis. The elevated pressure in the portal system forces blood to collateral channels, including the fragile veins of the lower oesophagus, which possess thin walls prone to catastrophic rupture.

Bleeding varices present as acute, painless haematemesis and melaena, frequently precipitating hypovolaemic shock. Emergency nursing management focuses on hemodynamic stabilization, establishing large-bore intravenous access, fluid resuscitation, and administering vasoactive drugs like octreotide to reduce splanchnic blood flow. Endoscopic band ligation and sclerotherapy are definitive acute interventions, while Sengstaken-Blakemore tubes serve as a temporary mechanical tamponade measure when endoscopy fails.

Oesophageal Tumours and Carcinoma

Oesophageal malignancy manifests primarily in two histological forms: squamous cell carcinoma, linked to tobacco and alcohol use, and adenocarcinoma, heavily driven by chronic GORD and Barrett's oesophagus. The disease often progresses insidiously, with early stages remaining asymptomatic.

Progressive dysphagia is the hallmark symptom, starting with solids and eventually advancing to semi-solids and liquids. Accompanying features include significant unexplained weight loss, odynophagia, hoarseness due to recurrent laryngeal nerve involvement, and cachexia. Diagnostic confirmation relies on endoscopy with biopsy. Treatment modalities encompass surgical resection (oesophagectomy), chemotherapy, and radiation therapy, often utilized in palliative combinations for advanced disease.

Nursing Care and Patient Management

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with oesophageal disorders spans diagnostic preparation, acute intervention, and postoperative recovery. For patients undergoing surgical procedures such as oesophagectomy, vigilant respiratory monitoring is paramount due to the risk of pulmonary complications, atelectasis, and anastomotic leak.

Nutritional support requires tailored interventions, transitioning from clear fluids to soft diets or managing enteral feeding tubes. Pain management protocols must balance patient comfort with the avoidance of respiratory depression. Nurses also provide invaluable education regarding lifestyle adjustments, such as elevating the head of the bed, avoiding meals close to bedtime, and eliminating dietary triggers that relax the lower oesophageal sphincter.
