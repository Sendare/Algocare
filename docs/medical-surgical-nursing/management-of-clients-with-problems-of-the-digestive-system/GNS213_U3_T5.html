Disorders of the Oesophagus

Anatomy and Physiology of the Oesophagus

The oesophagus is a muscular tube, roughly twenty-five centimeters long, connecting the pharynx to the stomach. Its primary role is moving swallowed food and liquids downward using coordinated muscle contractions known as peristalsis.

At the upper and lower ends of this tube sit specialized circular muscle bands called sphincters. The upper oesophageal sphincter stops air from entering the digestive tract during breathing. Meanwhile, the lower oesophageal sphincter acts as a crucial one-way valve, opening to let food into the stomach while preventing acidic stomach contents from splashing back upward. The inner lining consists of stratified squamous epithelium, which protects the delicate underlying tissue from abrasion during swallowing.

Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD) and Hiatus Hernia

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease happens when stomach contents repeatedly flow back into the oesophagus, irritating the mucosal lining. Patients frequently report a burning sensation behind the breastbone, known as heartburn, alongside sour regurgitation.

A hiatus hernia often contributes to this problem. This anatomical defect occurs when the upper part of the stomach pushes upward through the diaphragm into the chest cavity. Because this displacement disrupts the normal barrier function of the lower oesophageal sphincter, acid reflux becomes much more frequent and severe.

Oesophagitis and Oesophageal Ulceration

Persistent exposure to harsh gastric acid causes inflammation of the oesophageal lining, a condition called oesophagitis. Beyond acid reflux, infections, certain medications taken without enough water, and caustic ingestions can also trigger this inflammation.

If the mucosal damage continues unchecked, the tissue breaks down, forming oesophageal ulcers. These open sores can cause sharp pain during swallowing, known as odynophagia, and occasionally lead to chronic bleeding, iron-deficiency anaemia, or deep tissue scarring.

Oesophageal Motility Disorders: Achalasia and Spasms

  • Achalasia: A rare disorder where the lower oesophageal sphincter fails to relax during swallowing, and normal peristalsis in the lower tube is absent. Food accumulates, causing progressive difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids.
  • Diffuse Oesophageal Spasm: Characterized by uncoordinated, painful contractions that squeeze the tube tightly without moving food effectively downward, often mimicking cardiac chest pain.

Oesophageal Varices and Management of Bleeding

Oesophageal varices are abnormally dilated, tortuous veins located in the lower part of the oesophagus. They develop secondary to portal hypertension, most commonly caused by advanced liver cirrhosis, which forces blood to seek alternative pathways through smaller vessels.

Because these vessel walls are extremely thin and fragile, they carry a severe risk of rupture, leading to massive, life-threatening upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage. Patients often present with sudden vomiting of bright red blood, known as haematemesis, or passage of black, tarry stools called melaena. Management focuses on stabilizing vital signs, restoring blood volume, and using pharmacological agents or endoscopic banding to stop the bleeding.

Oesophageal Tumours and Carcinoma

Malignant tumours of the oesophagus are aggressive cancers with two main histological types: squamous cell carcinoma, linked to smoking and heavy alcohol use, and adenocarcinoma, strongly associated with chronic acid reflux and Barrett's oesophagus.

The earliest and most common symptom is progressive difficulty swallowing solids, termed dysphagia, which eventually progresses to liquids. Weight loss, fatigue, and pain behind the sternum develop as the tumour grows and narrows the lumen. Diagnosis relies heavily on endoscopy with tissue biopsy, and treatment often involves a combination of surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Management

Nurses play a vital role in monitoring patients with oesophageal disorders by assessing nutritional status, pain levels, and swallowing ability. For patients experiencing dysphagia, nurses ensure proper dietary modifications, upright positioning during meals, and thorough swallow evaluations before oral intake.

In emergency situations like bleeding varices, nursing care centers on strict hemodynamic monitoring, maintaining large-bore intravenous access, and preparing equipment for emergency procedures. Post-procedure care requires close observation for respiratory distress, signs of infection, and monitoring of drainage tubes.
