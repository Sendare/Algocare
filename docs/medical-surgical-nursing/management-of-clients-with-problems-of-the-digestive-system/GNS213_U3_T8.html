Disorders of the Small Intestine

Anatomy and Physiology of the Small Intestine

The small intestine is the longest segment of the gastrointestinal tract, spanning approximately six meters in adults, and is anatomically divided into the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. Its primary physiological functions include the completion of chemical digestion and the absorption of nutrients, electrolytes, and water. Digestion is facilitated by the secretion of enzymes from the pancreas and bile from the liver, which enter the duodenum via the hepatopancreatic ampulla. The internal lining of the small intestine is uniquely adapted for maximal surface area absorption through circular folds known as plicae circulares, microscopic villi, and even finer microvilli that form the brush border. Nutrient molecules, including monosaccharides, amino acids, fatty acids, and vitamins, cross the mucosal epithelial barrier via active transport or passive diffusion into the capillary network or lacteals for systemic distribution. Additionally, the small intestine plays a critical role in fluid balance, secreting and reabsorbing significant volumes of fluid daily. Understanding this intricate anatomy and physiology is foundational for nursing students, as disruptions in any of these segments or mechanisms can rapidly lead to severe nutritional deficits, fluid and electrolyte imbalances, and systemic complications that require prompt clinical intervention in diverse healthcare settings across Nigeria and globally.

Pathophysiology and Classification of Small Intestinal Disorders

Pathophysiologically, disorders of the small intestine disrupt its normal digestive, absorptive, and motor functions. These conditions can be broadly classified into inflammatory and infectious diseases, mechanical and functional obstructions, vascular insufficiencies, and malabsorptive syndromes. Inflammatory conditions like Crohn's disease cause transmural inflammation, ulceration, and fibrosis, impairing both motility and nutrient uptake. Obstructions can be mechanical, caused by physical barriers such as adhesions, tumors, or hernias, or functional, such as paralytic ileus resulting from decreased smooth muscle peristalsis after abdominal surgery. Vascular disorders, including acute mesenteric ischemia, compromise blood flow, leading rapidly to tissue hypoxia, necrosis, and bowel perforation if untreated. Malabsorptive disorders stem from mucosal damage, enzyme deficiencies, or a reduction in functional bowel length, preventing adequate transfer of nutrients into the bloodstream. For nursing students, categorizing these pathologies helps in anticipating specific clinical presentations, such as severe colicky pain, vomiting, electrolyte derangements, or chronic diarrhea. Recognizing these underlying mechanisms is crucial for accurate triage, monitoring, and executing targeted nursing interventions that mitigate life-threatening complications.

Inflammatory and Infectious Conditions: Crohn's Disease and Enteritis

Inflammatory and infectious conditions of the small intestine, such as Crohn's disease and infectious enteritis, present significant clinical challenges. Crohn's disease is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory bowel disease that can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract but frequently involves the terminal ileum. It is characterized by skip lesions, cobblestone appearance of the mucosa, and deep ulcerations that can lead to fistula formation, strictures, and bowel perforation. Patients typically experience chronic abdominal pain, weight loss, and non-bloody diarrhea. Infectious enteritis, caused by bacterial agents like Salmonella, Campylobacter, or viral pathogens like Rotavirus, often presents with acute watery or bloody diarrhea, fever, and cramping abdominal pain resulting from mucosal invasion and toxin production. Nursing care focuses heavily on managing acute fluid volume deficits, monitoring for signs of peritonitis or bowel obstruction, and administering prescribed anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, or anti-infective therapies. Patient education on dietary modifications, avoidance of trigger foods, and strict hand hygiene is vital to prevent recurrences and transmission, especially in resource-limited or community settings where infectious gastroenteritis is prevalent.

Intestinal Obstruction: Types, Causes, and Clinical Manifestations

Intestinal obstruction occurs when the normal forward flow of luminal contents is impaired, categorized mainly into mechanical obstruction and paralytic ileus (non-mechanical). Mechanical obstructions account for the majority of small bowel obstructions (SBOs), with postoperative adhesions being the leading cause, followed by incarcerated hernias, neoplasms, and Crohn's strictures. Paralytic ileus involves a lack of intestinal peristalsis without any physical obstruction, commonly triggered by abdominal surgery, electrolyte imbalances, or peritonitis. Clinical manifestations of SBO include acute, colicky, crampy abdominal pain, nausea, and early profuse vomiting that may progress from gastric contents to bilious and eventually feculent material. Patients also exhibit abdominal distention, high-pitched or tinkling bowel sounds early on, which eventually become absent if the bowel becomes silent or ischemic. Nurses must closely monitor vital signs for tachycardia and hypotension, which indicate dehydration or impending hypovolemic shock. Prompt recognition of strangulated obstruction—manifested by severe, constant pain, fever, and localized tenderness—is critical because it constitutes a surgical emergency to prevent bowel gangrene and perforation.

Malabsorption Syndromes: Celiac Disease and Short Bowel Syndrome

Malabsorption syndromes occur when the small intestine fails to adequately absorb nutrients, vitamins, and minerals, leading to systemic nutritional deficiencies. Celiac disease (gluten-sensitive enteropathy) is a chronic immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by the ingestion of dietary gluten found in wheat, barley, and rye. This immune response causes flattening and atrophy of the small intestinal villi, severely reducing the absorptive surface area and leading to symptoms such as chronic diarrhea, steatorrhea (fatty, foul-smelling stools), weight loss, and fatigue. Short bowel syndrome (SBS), on the other hand, results from surgical resection of a significant portion of the small intestine due to conditions like Crohn's disease, mesenteric infarction, or trauma. In SBS, the remaining bowel undergoes structural and functional adaptation, but patients often experience severe diarrhea, dehydration, and malnutrition requiring long-term specialized nutritional support such as parenteral nutrition. Nurses caring for patients with malabsorption must assess for signs of specific vitamin deficiencies, such as pallor from iron or vitamin B12 deficiency, bruising from vitamin K deficiency, or tetany from calcium deficiency, and educate patients thoroughly on lifelong dietary restrictions.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluations

Comprehensive nursing assessment of patients with small intestinal disorders requires a systematic health history and focused physical examination. Nurses should inquire about abdominal pain characteristics, bowel movement frequency and consistency, presence of nausea, vomiting, or blood in the stool, and previous abdominal surgeries. Physical examination involves inspection for distention or surgical scars, auscultation of bowel sounds (noting hyperactive, hypoactive, or absent sounds), percussion for tympany or dullness, and gentle palpation to identify tenderness, guarding, or masses. Diagnostic evaluations are crucial to confirm clinical suspicions and guide therapy. Laboratory tests include complete blood counts to check for anemia or leukocytosis, electrolyte panels to assess hydration status and acid-base balance, and specific serologic markers like anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies for celiac disease. Imaging studies such as abdominal plain radiographs can reveal distended bowel loops and air-fluid levels indicative of obstruction. Computed tomography (CT) scans provide detailed cross-sectional views of the bowel wall and peritoneal cavity. Endoscopic procedures, including upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsy or capsule endoscopy, allow direct visualization of the mucosal lining and histological confirmation of inflammatory or malabsorptive diseases.

Medical and Surgical Management of Small Intestinal Disorders

Medical and surgical management of small intestinal disorders aims to restore bowel patency, correct fluid and electrolyte imbalances, control inflammation, and ensure adequate nutrition. For acute conditions like mechanical small bowel obstruction, initial medical management centers on bowel rest via gastrointestinal decompression using a nasogastric (NG) tube, aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation, and electrolyte correction. If the obstruction is complete, strangulated, or fails to resolve with conservative management, emergency surgical exploration—such as laparoscopy or exploratory laparotomy—is performed to lysis adhesions, reduce hernias, or resect necrotic bowel. In inflammatory conditions like Crohn's disease, medical therapy utilizes anti-inflammatory drugs (corticosteroids, 5-ASA agents), immunomodulators, and biologic therapies to induce and maintain remission. Nutritional support is paramount; elemental diets, enteral nutrition via feeding tubes, or total parenteral nutrition (TPN) may be initiated depending on the functional capacity of the gastrointestinal tract. Nurses play an indispensable role in monitoring patients receiving these complex medical and surgical therapies, ensuring accurate administration of intravenous fluids and medications, and maintaining patency of drainage and feeding tubes.

Nursing Care Plan, Complications, and Patient Education

Developing a comprehensive nursing care plan for patients with small intestinal disorders involves prioritizing interventions around fluid balance, pain management, nutritional support, and complication prevention. Key nursing diagnoses often include Deficient Fluid Volume, Acute Pain, Imbalanced Nutrition: Less Than Body Requirements, and Risk for Deficient Knowledge. Nurses must diligently monitor intake and output, assess daily weights, and observe for clinical signs of dehydration or electrolyte imbalance. Pain management includes administering prescribed analgesics while avoiding medications that significantly slow gut motility when obstruction is a risk. Postoperative nursing care requires monitoring abdominal incision sites, encouraging early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis and promote the return of peristalsis, and assessing for the return of bowel sounds and flatus. Patient education is vital for long-term health maintenance; nurses must instruct patients on strict dietary compliance—such as a lifelong gluten-free diet for celiac disease or low-residue diets for Crohn's disease—recognizing warning signs of recurrent obstruction or infection (such as severe abdominal pain, vomiting, or fever), and knowing when to seek immediate medical attention.
