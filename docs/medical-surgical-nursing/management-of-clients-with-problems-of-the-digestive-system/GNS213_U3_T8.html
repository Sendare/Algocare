Disorders of the Small Intestine

Anatomy and Physiology of the Small Intestine

The small intestine forms the longest segment of the gastrointestinal tract, stretching from the pyloric sphincter of the stomach to the ileocecal valve. It is divided anatomically into three continuous regions: the duodenum, the jejunum, and the ileum. Each segment possesses distinct structural features tailored to its specific digestive responsibilities. The duodenum receives acidic chyme from the stomach along with alkaline secretions from the pancreas and bile from the liver, neutralizing the mixture and initiating enzymatic breakdown. The jejunum represents the primary site for active nutrient absorption, boasting a thick mucosal wall lined with dense circular folds known as plicae circulares. The ileum specializes in absorbing remaining nutrients, vitamin B12, and bile salts before the luminal contents pass into the large intestine.

Microscopically, the inner lining of the small intestine maximizes surface area through millions of finger-like projections called villi, which are further covered by microscopic microvilli forming a brush border. This massive surface area ensures efficient transfer of digested carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, and minerals directly into the capillary networks and lacteals of the lamina propria. Crypts of Lieberkühn located at the base of the villi secrete intestinal juices and house stem cells responsible for continuous mucosal regeneration. Any disruption to this delicate, rapidly dividing epithelial lining—whether from ischemia, inflammation, or infection—rapidly impairs digestion and leads to profound nutritional deficits.

Pathophysiology and Classification of Small Intestinal Disorders

Disorders affecting the small intestine disrupt normal digestion, motility, secretion, and absorption. Clinically, these pathologies are generally classified into major categories based on their underlying mechanisms:

  • Inflammatory and Autoimmune Disorders: Conditions like Crohn's disease cause chronic, transmural inflammation, leading to ulceration, strictures, and fistulae.
  • Malabsorptive Syndromes: Conditions such as celiac disease destroy mucosal villi, preventing proper nutrient uptake despite adequate food intake.
  • Mechanical and Functional Obstructions: Physical blockages (adhesions, tumors, hernias) or motility failures (ileus) halt the forward propulsion of bowel contents.
  • Infectious Enteritis: Bacterial, viral, or parasitic invasions damage the epithelial barrier, causing acute secretory or inflammatory diarrhea and fluid losses.

Understanding these classifications helps nurses anticipate clinical instability, fluid and electrolyte imbalances, and nutritional collapse. The shared endpoint of these diverse diseases is mucosal dysfunction, which triggers systemic manifestations such as weight loss, dehydration, and vitamin deficiencies.

Inflammatory and Infectious Conditions: Crohn's Disease and Enteritis

Inflammatory conditions of the small intestine severely compromise mucosal integrity and systemic health. Crohn's disease is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease that can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract from mouth to anus, though it most commonly targets the terminal ileum. It features skip lesions—patches of healthy tissue alternating with severely diseased areas—and transmural inflammation that penetrates deep into the muscularis and serosa. This deep inflammation forms granulomas, fibrotic strictures, and abnormal passages called fistulae between bowel loops or adjacent organs.

Infectious enteritis, caused by pathogens like *Campylobacter*, *Salmonella*, *E. coli*, or viral agents like rotavirus, triggers acute mucosal injury. Toxins or direct cellular invasion destroy enterocytes, causing sudden watery or bloody diarrhea, fever, and severe cramping. Unlike chronic conditions, acute infectious enteritis is usually self-limiting in healthy individuals, though vulnerable patients can quickly develop dangerous hypovolemic shock from massive fluid shifts into the gut lumen.

Intestinal Obstruction: Types, Causes, and Clinical Manifestations

An intestinal obstruction occurs when the normal forward movement of bowel contents is physically blocked or functionally impaired. Obstructions are broadly split into two types: mechanical and non-mechanical (functional). Mechanical obstructions involve a physical barrier inside or outside the lumen. Common culprits include post-operative peritoneal adhesions, hernias, tumors, volvulus (twisting of the bowel), and intussusception (telescoping of one bowel segment into another). Non-mechanical obstructions, such as paralytic ileus, stem from a failure of normal intestinal peristalsis, frequently following abdominal surgery, severe electrolyte disturbances, or peritonitis.

When an obstruction takes hold, gas, fluid, and secretions accumulate proximal to the blockage point, causing bowel distension. This distension increases intraluminal pressure, compromising mucosal blood flow and leading rapidly to edema, ischemia, necrosis, and potentially bowel perforation.

  1. Colicky abdominal pain: Cramping pain as peristalsis tries to force content past the blockage.
  2. Vomiting: Often containing bilious or fecal material depending on the obstruction's level.
  3. Abdominal distension: Visible and palpable swelling due to trapped gas and fluid.
  4. Alteration in stool passage: Early passage of stool distal to the block, followed by absolute constipation and failure to pass flatus.

Malabsorption Syndromes: Celiac Disease and Short Bowel Syndrome

Malabsorption syndromes occur when the small intestine fails to adequately absorb nutrients, vitamins, and minerals into the bloodstream. Celiac disease is an immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by the ingestion of dietary gluten—a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye. In susceptible individuals, gluten triggers an autoimmune response that flattens the mucosal villi in the proximal small intestine, destroying the brush border and halting nutrient absorption. Patients experience chronic diarrhea, steatorrhea (fatty, foul-smelling stools), abdominal bloating, weight loss, and signs of nutrient deficiencies such as anemia from iron malabsorption.

Short bowel syndrome (SBS) arises structurally or functionally from surgical resection of significant portions of the small intestine, often due to Crohn's disease, trauma, or mesenteric ischemia. With a severely reduced surface area, the remaining bowel cannot absorb enough water, electrolytes, and macronutrients. Management focuses on intestinal adaptation, specialized nutritional support, and medications to slow gut motility and control gastric acid hypersecretion.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluations

A thorough nursing assessment for small intestinal disorders begins with a detailed health history focusing on pain characteristics, bowel habits, weight changes, and dietary intake. During the physical examination, the nurse inspects the abdomen for distension or surgical scars, auscultates bowel sounds in all four quadrants, and gently palpates for tenderness, masses, or guarding. High-pitched, tinkling bowel sounds often indicate an early mechanical obstruction, whereas absent bowel sounds point toward a paralytic ileus or advanced peritonitis.

Diagnostic testing confirms structural and functional abnormalities:

  • Laboratory Studies: Complete blood count (CBC) to check for infection or anemia, electrolyte panels to monitor for dehydration and imbalances, and serum albumin to assess nutritional status.
  • Serologic Tests: Anti-tissue transglutaminase (tTG) antibodies to screen for celiac disease.
  • Imaging Studies: Abdominal X-rays show dilated loops of bowel and air-fluid levels in obstructions. CT scans provide detailed cross-sectional views of wall thickening, inflammation, and strictures.
  • Endoscopy and Biopsy: EGD (esophagogastroduodenoscopy) with small bowel biopsy allows direct visualization and histological confirmation of mucosal diseases like celiac disease or enteritis.

Medical and Surgical Management of Small Intestinal Disorders

Management strategies for small intestinal disorders aim to restore fluid balance, reduce inflammation, relieve obstructions, and correct nutritional deficits. For acute conditions like bowel obstructions or severe enteritis, initial medical stabilization involves bowel rest via nil per os (NPO) status, placement of a nasogastric (NG) tube for gastric decompression, and aggressive IV fluid and electrolyte resuscitation. Anti-inflammatory drugs, corticosteroids, and immunosuppressants are pivotal in managing chronic inflammatory conditions like Crohn's disease, while strict lifelong gluten avoidance remains the definitive medical treatment for celiac disease.

When medical management fails or surgical emergencies arise—such as bowel strangulation, perforation, ischemia, or complete mechanical blockages—surgical intervention becomes mandatory. Surgeons may perform an exploratory laparotomy to lyse adhesions, resect necrotic or strictured bowel segments (bowel resection), or create an ostomy when primary anastomosis is unsafe. Post-operative nursing vigilance centers on monitoring for return of bowel function, preventing surgical site infections, and managing pain.

Nursing Care Plan, Complications, and Patient Education

Comprehensive nursing care for patients with small intestinal disorders requires vigilant monitoring for life-threatening complications, including hypovolemic shock, electrolyte derangements, bowel perforation, and sepsis. Nurses must track accurate intake and output, monitor vital signs for trends indicating worsening infection or fluid loss, and assess abdominal girth regularly. Pain management and emotional support are essential, as chronic gastrointestinal illness significantly impacts a patient's quality of life.

Patient education bridges acute hospital treatment and safe home management:

  • Dietary Modification: Educating celiac patients on strict gluten avoidance, reading food labels carefully, and avoiding cross-contamination. For Crohn's disease, teaching patients to identify and avoid individual trigger foods.
  • Ostomy Care: If a bowel resection required an ostomy, nurses must teach stoma care, skin protection, and appliance management before discharge.
  • Medication Adherence: Ensuring patients understand how to take anti-inflammatories, nutritional supplements, or enzyme replacements correctly.
  • Red Flag Symptoms: Instructing patients to report severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, fever, or bleeding immediately.
