Disorders of the Small Intestine

Anatomy and Functional Overview of the Small Intestine

The small intestine serves as the primary site for chemical digestion and nutrient absorption within the gastrointestinal tract. Spanning approximately six meters in adults, it is divided sequentially into the **duodenum**, **jejunum**, and **ileum**. - **Duodenum**: Receiving chyme from the stomach, it combines acidic contents with alkaline pancreatic secretions and bile to neutralize pH and initiate enzymatic breakdown. - **Jejunum**: Highly vascularized with prominent circular folds, known as **plicae circulares**, specialized for absorbing carbohydrates and amino acids. - **Ileum**: Features aggregated lymphoid follicles, called **Peyer's patches**, and handles the uptake of vitamin B12 bound to intrinsic factor, alongside remaining bile salts. Microscopically, the mucosal surface features millions of finger-like projections named **villi**, which are further covered by microvilli, collectively forming the **brush border**. This structural adaptation multiplies the internal surface area exponentially, ensuring maximum transfer of nutrients into the bloodstream and lymphatic vessels.

Inflammatory and Infectious Enteropathies

Inflammatory conditions of the small intestine disrupt mucosal integrity, impairing absorption and motility. **Crohn's disease** stands out as a chronic, transmural inflammatory bowel disease that can affect any segment of the gastrointestinal tract from mouth to anus, though the terminal ileum is a frequent site. Pathologically, it presents with skip lesions, deep ulcerations, and a characteristic **cobblestone appearance** of the mucosa. Infectious enteritis, caused by bacterial agents like *Salmonella* or viral pathogens such as rotavirus, triggers acute mucosal inflammation, secretory diarrhea, and electrolyte imbalances. In resource-constrained settings across Nigeria, infectious enteropathies remain a leading cause of pediatric morbidity, requiring prompt fluid resuscitation and stool microscopy. Unlike chronic autoimmune conditions, infectious enteritis is typically self-limiting unless complicated by severe dehydration or systemic sepsis.

Intestinal Obstruction: Mechanics and Manifestations

An intestinal obstruction occurs when the normal forward flow of luminal contents is physically blocked or functionally impaired. Obstructions are broadly classified into two categories: - **Mechanical obstruction**: Physical barriers block the lumen. Common culprits include **adhesions** from previous abdominal surgeries, incarcerated hernias, foreign bodies, and tumors. - **Functional (adynamic) obstruction**: Also known as **ileus**, this arises from a failure of peristalsis, frequently occurring after abdominal surgery, severe electrolyte disturbances, or peritonitis. Clinical manifestations vary depending on the level of the blockage. High small bowel obstructions present early with profuse, projectile **bilious vomiting**, upper abdominal distension, and rapid electrolyte depletion. Conversely, low obstructions feature more delayed vomiting that may eventually become fecaloid, accompanied by pronounced abdominal distension and absolute constipation.

Malabsorption Syndromes: Celiac Disease and Short Bowel

Malabsorption syndromes occur when the small intestine fails to adequately absorb macronutrients, micronutrients, or water. **Celiac disease** is an immune-mediated enteropathy triggered by dietary **gluten**, a protein found in wheat, barley, and rye. Ingestion leads to an autoimmune response that flattens the mucosal villi, destroying the brush border enzymes necessary for digestion. Patients present with chronic diarrhea, steatorrhea, weight loss, and signs of nutrient deficiencies such as iron-deficiency anemia. **Short bowel syndrome** develops following massive surgical resection of the small intestine, often due to mesenteric ischemia, trauma, or Crohn's disease complications. The remaining bowel lacks sufficient surface area to absorb adequate fluids, electrolytes, and nutrients, leaving patients dependent on parenteral nutrition while the remaining intestine undergoes structural adaptation.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluations

Comprehensive nursing assessment for small intestinal disorders begins with a detailed history focusing on bowel habits, pain characteristics, surgical history, and dietary patterns. Physical examination relies on inspection, auscultation, and palpation of the abdomen. Nurses must listen carefully for **hyperactive bowel sounds** proximal to an early mechanical obstruction or **hypoactive to absent bowel sounds** in paralytic ileus or peritonitis. Diagnostic investigations help pinpoint structural and functional abnormalities: - **Abdominal X-ray (AXR)**: Identifies dilated loops of bowel, air-fluid levels indicative of obstruction, or free air signaling perforation. - **Computed Tomography (CT) scan**: Provides cross-sectional imaging to visualize bowel wall thickening, strictures, masses, or abscesses. - **Endoscopy and Biopsy**: EGD (esophagogastroduodenoscopy) allows direct visualization of the duodenal mucosa and procurement of tissue samples to confirm celiac disease or mucosal inflammation. - **Stool Studies**: Evaluates for occult blood, ova, parasites, or fecal fat content.

Medical and Surgical Management Strategies

Management of small intestinal disorders depends entirely on the underlying etiology and acuity of the condition. For acute obstructions or severe inflammatory flares, conservative medical management is initiated first. This involves placing a **nasogastric (NG) tube** for gastric decompression to relieve nausea, vomiting, and internal pressure, alongside aggressive intravenous fluid resuscitation to correct electrolyte imbalances and dehydration. When conservative measures fail, or when complications such as strangulation, perforation, or ischemia occur, emergency surgical intervention becomes necessary. Procedures range from **adhesiolysis** to cut scar tissue bands, to bowel resection and anastomosis for necrotic segments, strictures, or tumors. In cases of extensive tissue loss, a temporary or permanent ostomy may be created to allow the remaining bowel to heal.

Nursing Care Plan, Complications, and Recovery

Holistic nursing care focuses on stabilizing physiological parameters, managing intense pain, preventing life-threatening complications, and preparing the patient for discharge. Critical nursing interventions include monitoring intake and output strictly, assessing surgical wound sites for signs of infection, and tracking the return of bowel sounds and passing of flatus as indicators of resolving ileus. Severe complications of small intestinal disorders include **hypovolemic shock** from third-space fluid loss, **bowel perforation** leading to generalized peritonitis, and sepsis. The nurse plays a pivotal role in patient education, particularly for individuals managing chronic conditions like celiac disease or short bowel syndrome. Dietary counseling must emphasize strict adherence to a gluten-free lifestyle or small, frequent, nutrient-dense meals paired with appropriate vitamin supplementation to prevent long-term deficiency states.
