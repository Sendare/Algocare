Disorders of the Mouth

Anatomy and Physiology of the Oral Cavity

The oral cavity serves as the entry point to the digestive and respiratory systems. It is bounded by the lips anteriorly, the cheeks laterally, the hard and soft palates superiorly, and the muscular floor of the mouth inferiorly. The mucosal lining consists of stratified squamous epithelium, which is either keratinized in areas subject to heavy friction, such as the hard palate and gingiva, or non-keratinized in the buccal mucosa, soft palate, and floor of the mouth. This epithelial barrier protects underlying tissues from mechanical trauma and microbial invasion. Saliva plays a critical role in maintaining oral health. Produced majorly by the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands, saliva moistens food to facilitate swallowing, initiates carbohydrate digestion via salivary amylase, and provides antimicrobial defense through immunoglobulins and lysozymes. The tongue, a mass of intrinsic and extrinsic skeletal muscles, manipulates food during mastication and houses taste buds that innervate via the facial and glossopharyngeal nerves.

Common Infectious and Inflammatory Conditions

Infections and inflammatory lesions of the oral cavity are frequently encountered in clinical practice, ranging from self-limiting ulcers to severe fungal overgrowths. - **Stomatitis**: Generalized inflammation of the oral mucosa involving the cheeks, tongue, and palate, often triggered by chemotherapy, radiation, or systemic infections. - **Herpes Labialis**: Caused by herpes simplex virus type 1, manifesting as painful fluid-filled vesicles around the lips that rupture and crust over. - **Oral Candidiasis (Thrush)**: A fungal infection caused by *Candida albicans*, presenting as creamy white plaques on the buccal mucosa or tongue that leave a bleeding surface when scraped. - **Aphthous Ulcers**: Commonly known as Canker sores, these are painful, non-contagious shallow sores with a white or yellowish base surrounded by a red border.

Premalignant Lesions and Oral Cancers

Early identification of abnormal mucosal changes prevents progression to invasive oral malignancies. Tobacco use, heavy alcohol consumption, and chronic irritation are primary risk factors. 1. **Leukoplakia**: Defined as adherent white patches or plaques on the mucous membranes that cannot be wiped off or classified clinically as any other disease. A significant percentage show dysplasia and potential malignant transformation. 2. **Erythroplakia**: A fiery red mucosal plaque that carries a much higher risk of dysplasia and progression to squamous cell carcinoma than its white counterpart. 3. **Squamous Cell Carcinoma**: The most common histological type of oral cancer, frequently developing on the lateral borders of the tongue or the floor of the mouth as a persistent ulcer or indurated mass.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Thorough assessment of the oral cavity requires adequate lighting, a tongue depressor, and clean gloves. Inspect the lips, buccal mucosa, teeth, gingiva, hard and soft palates, floor of the mouth, and pharynx. Document the size, shape, location, and color of any lesions, along with patient-reported pain levels. Diagnostic tools range from simple visual inspections to advanced laboratory and imaging procedures. A biopsy remains the definitive gold standard for confirming malignancy in suspicious, persistent lesions. Cultures may be obtained to identify specific bacterial or fungal pathogens when infection is suspected. In resource-constrained settings in Nigeria, careful visual inspection combined with a detailed history remains the cornerstone of primary screening, ensuring patients with suspicious signs are promptly referred to tertiary centers.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological interventions target the underlying etiology of oral disorders, focusing on pain relief, infection eradication, and inflammation reduction. Antifungal agents such as nystatin oral suspension or fluconazole treat candidiasis. For viral outbreaks like herpes labialis, topical or systemic acyclovir reduces viral replication and lesion duration. Topical anesthetics, including viscous lidocaine, provide temporary relief for painful aphthous ulcers or stomatitis, allowing patients to maintain adequate nutritional intake. Chlorhexidine mouthwashes are frequently prescribed to reduce bacterial load and promote gingival healing after procedures. Corticosteroids may be used sparingly in severe inflammatory or autoimmune conditions to suppress localized immune responses.

Surgical Interventions and Postoperative Care

Surgical management becomes necessary when treating advanced oral cancers, severe jaw trauma, or deep abscesses requiring drainage. Procedures range from wide local excisions of tumors to partial mandibulectomies and neck dissections. Postoperative nursing care prioritizes maintaining a patent airway. Edema, surgical manipulation, and bleeding can quickly compromise the upper airway, making bedside suction and emergency tracheostomy equipment mandatory. Nurses must monitor hemodynamic stability, assess surgical flaps for adequate perfusion indicated by color and temperature, and manage pain effectively without causing excessive respiratory depression.

Oral Hygiene, Nutrition, and Patient Education

Preventive care and health promotion are essential components of nursing practice. Patients should be guided on proper toothbrushing techniques using soft-bristled brushes and daily flossing to prevent plaque accumulation and gingivitis. Nutritional support must be tailored for individuals experiencing painful oral lesions. Recommend soft, bland, and lukewarm foods while advising avoidance of citrus, spicy, or rough-textured items that exacerbate mucosal irritation. Educate patients to quit smoking and limit alcohol consumption as primary measures to reduce oral cancer risks. Encourage regular dental checkups for early detection of abnormalities.
