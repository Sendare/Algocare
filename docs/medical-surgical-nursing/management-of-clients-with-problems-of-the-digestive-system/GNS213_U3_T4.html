Disorders of the Mouth

Anatomy and Physiology of the Oral Cavity

The oral cavity serves as the entry point to the digestive and respiratory systems. It is lined by a moist mucous membrane that protects underlying tissues from mechanical stress and pathogens. Major structures include the lips, tongue, teeth, hard and soft palates, and the salivary glands.

Saliva plays a critical role in oral health by lubricating food for swallowing, initiating starch digestion via amylase, and providing antimicrobial protection through lysozymes and immunoglobulins. Any disruption in saliva production, such as dry mouth or xerostomia, quickly compromises oral tissue integrity and increases vulnerability to decay and infection.

Common Infectious and Inflammatory Conditions

Infections and inflammations of the mouth are frequent encounters in clinical practice, often causing severe discomfort and interfering with nutrition.

  • Stomatitis: A general term for inflammation of the oral mucosa, causing painful ulcers and redness.
  • Oral Candidiasis (Thrush): A fungal infection caused by *Candida albicans*, presenting as creamy white patches that can be scraped off, leaving a bleeding base. It is common in infants, denture wearers, and immunocompromised patients.
  • Herpes Simplex Labialis (Cold Sores): Viral vesicles caused by HSV-1 that rupture and crust over on the lips.
  • Gingivitis and Periodontitis: Bacterial infections affecting the gums and supporting tooth structures, driven by poor plaque control.

Premalignant Lesions and Oral Cancers

Early detection of oral premalignant lesions is vital to prevent progression to squamous cell carcinoma, which accounts for the vast majority of oral cancers.

  • Leukoplakia: Appears as firmly attached white patches or plaques that cannot be scraped off. It is strongly linked to tobacco use.
  • Erythroplakia: Appears as bright red, velvety plaques. It carries a much higher risk of malignant transformation than leukoplakia.

Risk factors include chronic tobacco use, heavy alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, and human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Lesions that persist for more than two weeks without a clear cause require urgent biopsy.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough oral assessment requires adequate lighting, gloves, gauze, and a tongue depressor. The nurse inspects the lips, buccal mucosa, gums, palate, tongue floor, and oropharynx for color, texture, moisture, and lesions.

Palpation of the floor of the mouth and neck lymph nodes helps detect hidden masses or metastasis. When a suspicious lesion is found, diagnostic workups include tissue biopsies, brush cytology, and imaging studies like CT or MRI to determine the depth of invasion. Documenting the exact size, location, and characteristics of any finding establishes a baseline for medical treatment.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Pharmacological therapy for oral disorders targets the underlying cause, whether infectious, inflammatory, or autoimmune.

  • Antifungals: Nystatin suspension or oral fluconazole for candidiasis.
  • Antivirals: Acyclovir or valacyclovir for herpes simplex outbreaks.
  • Topical Corticosteroids: Triamcinolone dental paste to reduce inflammation in painful ulcers like aphthous stomatitis.
  • Analgesics: Viscous lidocaine or benzocaine rinses for pain relief before meals, though care must be taken with numbing agents to prevent choking or tongue biting.

Surgical Interventions and Postoperative Care

Surgical management ranges from simple excision of benign lesions to extensive radical resections for advanced oral cancers, sometimes involving partial glossectomy, mandibulectomy, or neck dissection.

The highest priority in postoperative care is maintaining a patent airway. Edema, bleeding, and surgical swelling can rapidly obstruct breathing. Nurses must keep suction equipment and emergency airway supplies at the bedside. Nutrition is managed via enteral tubes until safe swallowing returns, and communication methods must be established before surgery if speech will be temporarily or permanently altered.

Oral Hygiene, Nutrition, and Patient Education

Preventative care and supportive education reduce the incidence and severity of oral disorders. Nurses teach patients proper tooth brushing techniques with soft-bristled brushes, daily flossing, and the use of alcohol-free mouthwashes to avoid mucosal irritation.

For patients with painful oral lesions, nutrition is often compromised. Recommendations include offering soft, bland, room-temperature foods, avoiding citrus and spicy items, and using straws to bypass painful areas. Emphasizing smoking cessation and limiting alcohol intake remains the single most effective strategy for preventing long-term oral malignancies.
