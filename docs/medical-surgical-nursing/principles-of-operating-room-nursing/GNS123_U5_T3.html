Perioperative Asepsis

Principles of Perioperative Asepsis and Terminology

Perioperative asepsis refers to the collective practices used to protect the surgical patient from infectious microbes before, during, and immediately after surgery. The core goal is zero contamination. While medical asepsis focuses on reducing the total number of microorganisms and preventing their spread, surgical asepsis—also known as sterile technique—aims to eliminate every single microorganism, spore, and virus from an object or area.

  • Medical Asepsis: Clean technique used for daily care, medication administration, and general ward routines.
  • Surgical Asepsis: Sterile technique required for invasive procedures, entering body cavities, and handling sterile instruments.

A common misconception among beginners is that washing hands with regular soap achieves surgical asepsis. True surgical asepsis requires specialized physical scrubbing with antimicrobial agents and strict adherence to defined physical boundaries within the hospital environment.

Zoned Areas of the Surgical Suite

The surgical suite is divided into distinct zones to control traffic flow and minimize the introduction of airborne and contact contaminants. Each zone has specific clothing and behavioral requirements for staff and patients.

  • Unrestricted Zone: Street clothes are permitted here. This area includes the main reception desk, holding areas, and locker rooms where staff change into hospital scrubs.
  • Semi-restricted Zone: Surrounds the operating rooms and sterile storage areas. Personnel must wear surgical scrubs, head coverings that cover all hair, and shoe covers.
  • Restricted Zone: The operating room itself and scrub sink areas. Full surgical attire is mandatory, including a surgical mask and eye protection when sterile supplies or open wounds are exposed.

Surgical Scrubbing, Gowning, and Gloving

Before touching sterile instruments or assisting at the operating table, members of the surgical team must perform a surgical hand scrub. Unlike standard hand hygiene, a surgical scrub removes transient flora and significantly reduces resident skin flora for the duration of the procedure.

The scrub starts at the fingertips, cleaning under the nails with a pick under running water, and proceeds methodically up the forearms to two inches above the elbows. Hands must be kept higher than the elbows at all times so that dirty water drips downward away from the clean hands.

After scrubbing, staff enter the operating room and use an aseptic technique to put on sterile gowns and gloves. The closed-glove technique is standard: hands remain inside the gown sleeves while pulling the glove cuffs over the wrists, ensuring bare skin never touches the outside of the sterile glove.

Maintaining a Sterile Field: Core Rules

A sterile field is a specified area considered free of all microorganisms. Once established, maintaining this boundary is the shared responsibility of every team member in the operating room.

RulePractical Application
Sterile to SterileOnly sterile items and surfaces may contact other sterile items.
Border AwarenessThe outer edges of any sterile wrapper or table drape are considered unsterile (usually a one-inch margin).
Line of SightSterile team members face each other and never turn their backs on a sterile field.
Level CheckAnything dropping below the level of the operating table top is considered contaminated.

If a sterile item touches an unsterile surface or wrapper edge, it is immediately discarded. There is no guesswork in sterile technique: when doubt exists about sterility, consider the item contaminated.

Prevention and Management of Surgical Site Infections

Surgical site infections (SSIs) represent one of the most common and costly healthcare-associated complications. Prevention begins well before the skin incision and continues long after closure. Key preventative measures include administering prophylactic antibiotics within one hour before the incision, maintaining strict normothermia, and controlling blood glucose levels in diabetic patients.

Skin preparation in the operating room uses an antiseptic agent like chlorhexidine gluconate in alcohol. The scrub must start at the planned incision site and move outward in concentric circles toward the periphery, never bringing a contaminated sponge back to the clean center.

When an SSI does develop, early signs include localized redness, increasing pain, purulent drainage, and fever. Treatment involves wound cultures, targeted antibiotic therapy, and sometimes reopening the incision at the bedside to allow proper drainage.

The Perioperative Nurse's Role in Quality Assurance

The perioperative nurse acts as the patient's primary advocate and safety monitor throughout the surgical experience. In quality assurance, this role extends to rigorous counting protocols, continuous environmental monitoring, and prompt reporting of any aseptic breaks.

Surgical counts of sponges, instruments, and sharp items are performed collaboratively by the circulator and scrub nurse before the procedure begins, before closing cavities, and during skin closure. Missing items trigger an immediate X-ray and delayed closure of the wound.

Beyond counting, nurses monitor traffic flow in the operating room, ensure that sterilization indicators on instrument trays have changed properly, and intervene immediately if any team member compromises the sterile field, regardless of hierarchy.
