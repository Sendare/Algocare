Perioperative Asepsis

Introduction and Principles of Perioperative Asepsis

Perioperative asepsis encompasses the collective practices used to maintain a micro-organism-free environment during surgical interventions. The primary goal is safeguarding the patient from exogenous and endogenous sources of contamination that cause severe postoperative complications. The foundational tenet dictates that sterile areas must only come into contact with other sterile areas, while non-sterile items must remain strictly isolated. Any violation of this boundary compromises the entire procedure and increases morbidity risks. Clinical environments in Nigerian tertiary hospitals demand strict adherence to these principles despite resource constraints, as environmental dust and power fluctuations present unique challenges to maintaining sterile corridors.

Medical Asepsis versus Surgical Asepsis

Differentiating between clean and sterile techniques prevents cross-contamination across various levels of patient care.

  • Medical Asepsis: Known as clean technique, it focuses on reducing the total number of micro-organisms and preventing their spread. Examples include hand hygiene before medication administration and routine surface cleaning.
  • Surgical Asepsis: Known as sterile technique, it eliminates all micro-organisms, including spores and viruses, from an object or area. Examples include insertion of an indwelling urinary catheter and performing open laparotomy incisions.

Confusing these two practices in the operating theatre leads directly to preventable wound colonization.

Classification of Zoned Areas in the Surgical Suite

Surgical suites are strategically zoned to control traffic flow and progressively decrease microbial counts as personnel move closer to the operating room.

  • Unrestricted Zone: Street clothes are permitted; this area includes reception desks, holding areas, and locker rooms where administrative tasks occur.
  • Semi-Restricted Zone: Scrub attire, head covers, and shoe covers are mandatory. This zone houses corridors, equipment storage rooms, and instrument processing spaces.
  • Restricted Zone: Complete personal protective gear including masks is required. This encompasses the actual operating theatres and sterile core areas where sterile supplies are opened.

Standard Precautions and Personal Protective Equipment in Surgery

Every patient in the perioperative setting must be treated as potentially infectious. Personal protective equipment (PPE) acts as a physical barrier against bloodborne pathogens and bodily fluids. The correct sequence for donning PPE is gown, mask, eye protection, and gloves, while removal follows a specific decontamination sequence to avoid auto-contamination. Surgical masks must cover both the nose and mouth entirely, and protective eyewear is non-negotiable during procedures with anticipated splashing or bone-sawing activities. Neglecting these safeguards exposes clinicians to hepatitis B, C, and HIV.

Surgical Hand Scrubbing, Gowning, and Gloving Techniques

The surgical hand scrub reduces the resident microbial count and temporarily suppresses transient flora on the hands and forearms. Scrubbing proceeds from the fingertips to the elbows, keeping hands elevated above the elbows at all times to allow water to run downward toward the waist. Once scrubbed, the nurse enters the operating room and dons a sterile gown using the closed-glove technique, which prevents bare skin from touching the exterior of the gown or gloves. An assistant then secures the back of the gown. Thorough drying and proper friction during the scrub phase are far more critical than water temperature.

Maintaining a Sterile Field: Rules and Practices

A sterile field is established to create a safe zone where instruments and supplies can be handled without microbial contamination.

  • Only sterile items are placed within the sterile field.
  • Sterile persons are gowned and gloved; unsterile persons must never reach over the sterile field.
  • The edges of a sterile wrapper or container are considered unsterile once opened.
  • A sterile barrier that has been penetrated, punctured, or dampened by moisture is immediately considered contaminated.

When doubt exists regarding the sterility of an item, the golden rule applies: consider it contaminated and replace it immediately.

Prevention and Management of Surgical Site Infections

Surgical site infections (SSIs) represent a major healthcare challenge, prolonging hospital stays and increasing treatment costs. Prevention begins preoperatively with appropriate patient skin preparation, timely administration of prophylactic antibiotics within one hour before incision, and maintaining normothermia and normal blood glucose levels during surgery. Intraoperatively, gentle tissue handling, meticulous hemostasis, and minimizing operating room traffic reduce bacterial contamination. Postoperatively, wound dressings must be assessed using aseptic techniques. Recognizing early signs like localized erythema, purulent discharge, and increasing pain allows for prompt wound cultures and targeted antimicrobial therapy.

The Role of the Perioperative Nurse in Quality Assurance

Quality assurance in the surgical suite requires continuous monitoring, auditing, and enforcing of aseptic protocols by the perioperative nurse. As patient advocates, scrub and circulating nurses maintain unyielding vigilance over every team member's aseptic technique, regardless of hierarchy. They document instrument sterilization cycles, monitor environmental parameters such as theater temperature and humidity, and participate in safety checklists like the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist. Incident reporting of near-misses helps cultivate a safety culture that systematically targets institutional weaknesses and improves patient outcomes across surgical units.
