Roles of the Nurse in Peri-operative Care

Introduction and Phases of Peri-operative Nursing

Peri-operative nursing encompasses the care provided to patients before, during, and after surgical procedures. This continuum is traditionally divided into three distinct phases: pre-operative, intra-operative, and post-operative. Each phase demands specific clinical competencies, ranging from psychological preparation and physical assessment to sterile technique maintenance and complex post-anaesthetic monitoring. The nurse acts as the central coordinator, bridging communication between the surgical team, the anaesthetist, the patient, and their relatives.

The Pre-operative Phase: Assessment and Preparation

Before any incision is made, the pre-operative nurse establishes a baseline dataset through comprehensive physical assessment and detailed history-taking. Key areas evaluated include previous anaesthetic reactions, current medication use, chronic medical conditions, and known allergies. Psychological preparation is equally vital; addressing patient anxieties fosters trust and aids physiological stability. Informed consent verification is a critical nursing duty, ensuring the patient understands the procedure, its risks, and alternatives without feeling coerced. In busy Nigerian surgical wards, this phase also involves confirming fasting status, ensuring pre-op investigations like packed cell volume and urinalysis are attached to the chart, and confirming that baseline vital signs are documented.

Intra-operative Nursing Roles: Scrub and Circulating Duties

Inside the operating theatre, nursing responsibilities split into two distinct specialized roles: the scrub nurse and the circulating nurse.

  • Scrub Nurse: Operates within the sterile field, donning sterile gown and gloves. Prepares and manages sterile instruments, sutures, and supplies, anticipating the surgeon's needs and actively participating in formal instrument, needle, and sponge counts.
  • Circulating Nurse: Remains in the unsterile zone, managing the overall environment of the operating room. Facilitates patient positioning, manages specimen collection, documents intra-operative events, and assists the scrub team while maintaining strict adherence to aseptic principles.

Post-operative Care in the Recovery Room

Immediately following surgery, the patient is transferred to the Post-Anaesthesia Care Unit or Recovery Room. The primary nursing focus shifts toward monitoring respiratory function, maintaining a patent airway, managing pain, and stabilizing haemodynamic parameters. Because emergence from anaesthesia carries high risks of airway obstruction, laryngospasm, and haemorrhage, vigilant observation is mandatory. Nurses assess surgical sites for excessive drainage, monitor urine output, and ensure adequate oxygenation through pulse oximetry. Discharge from the recovery unit depends on standardized criteria, such as the Aldrete score, which evaluates activity, respiration, circulation, consciousness, and colour.

Patient Safety and Surgical Checklists

Surgical safety relies heavily on standardized verification tools. The World Health Organization surgical safety checklist provides a reliable framework that bridges communication across the pre-operative, intra-operative, and post-operative stages. This checklist is executed in three distinct milestones: Sign In before induction of anaesthesia, Time Out before skin incision, and Sign Out before the patient leaves the operating room. The nurse plays an active role in initiating or verifying these pause points, ensuring the correct patient, correct site, and correct procedure are confirmed collectively by the entire surgical team.

Legal, Ethical, and Documentation Considerations

Peri-operative nursing practice is bound by strict legal and ethical obligations. Informed consent is not merely a signed piece of paper, but an ongoing communication process safeguarding patient autonomy. Nurses must ensure the consent is voluntary, informed, and obtained by the medical practitioner before any sedative medications alter cognition. Documentation must be contemporaneous, objective, and accurate, reflecting every intervention, medication administration, and status change. Failing to record accurate sponge counts, misidentifying specimens, or breaching patient confidentiality exposes both the nurse and the institution to severe legal liability.
