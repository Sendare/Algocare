Scrubbing

Introduction to Surgical Scrubbing

Surgical scrubbing is a fundamental infection control procedure performed by the surgical team prior to entering the operating theatre. Unlike routine handwashing, which aims to remove transient microorganisms and visible dirt, a surgical scrub is designed to significantly reduce the resident microbial flora on the hands and forearms for the duration of the surgical procedure. Furthermore, surgical scrubbing has a cumulative antimicrobial effect, inhibiting the rapid proliferation of bacteria should gloves become compromised during surgery. As nursing students training in Nigeria, understanding the physiological and microbiological rationale behind scrubbing is vital to safeguarding patients from surgical site infections (SSIs), which remain a significant cause of postoperative morbidity and mortality in resource-constrained healthcare settings. The process requires meticulous attention to detail, adherence to institutional protocols, and a thorough comprehension of hand anatomy, particularly the subungual spaces under the fingernails where bacteria heavily congregate. By mastering the surgical scrub, student nurses build the foundation for sterile consciousness, ensuring that patient safety remains paramount throughout every phase of the perioperative journey. This practice establishes a critical barrier between the patient's vulnerable internal tissues and the external environment, embodying the core nursing tenet of doing no harm.

Principles of Asepsis and Infection Control in the OR

Principles of asepsis form the bedrock of infection control in the operating room (OR), dictating every action and movement of the surgical team. Asepsis refers to the absence of pathogenic microorganisms, and surgical asepsis, or sterile technique, aims to eliminate all microorganisms from an object or area. In the OR environment, maintaining this sterile field requires constant vigilance regarding what is clean, what is contaminated, and what is sterile. Members of the scrub team must understand that the sterile field is established immediately before surgery and only sterile items are permitted within it. Gowns are considered sterile only from the chest to the level of the sterile field, and sleeves from two inches above the elbow to the cuffs. Personnel must always face the sterile field when passing it, never turn their backs on it, and maintain a safe distance when moving around unsterile areas. In Nigerian hospitals, where environmental vectors and institutional challenges can sometimes test infection control protocols, strict adherence to these aseptic boundaries prevents exogenous and endogenous wound contamination. Any doubt regarding the sterility of an item or surface mandates that it be treated as contaminated. Through rigorous application of these principles, nurses actively mitigate the risk of healthcare-associated infections and ensure optimal patient outcomes.

Preparation and Attire Before Scrubbing

Before commencing the surgical scrub, proper preparation and appropriate attire are mandatory to minimize shedding of skin squames, hair, and microbes into the theatre environment. All personnel entering the semi-restricted and restricted zones of the OR suite must don clean surgical scrubs provided by the facility, with scrub tops securely tucked into trousers to prevent air currents from carrying skin particles outward. Head coverings, such as caps or hoods, must completely contain all head and facial hair, including sideburns and beards, because hair is a major vehicle for harboring and dispersing microorganisms. Protective eyewear and face masks must be worn prior to scrubbing, ensuring the mask fits snugly over both the nose and mouth to prevent droplet contamination from the respiratory tract. Furthermore, all jewelry, including rings, watches, bracelets, and earrings, must be removed. Jewelry harbors high concentrations of bacteria that cannot be effectively washed away, and rings increase the risk of glove puncture. Fingernails must be kept short, clean, and natural; artificial nails, extenders, and nail polish are strictly prohibited because they significantly increase the fungal and bacterial load on the hands. Ensuring compliance with these pre-scrubbing standards protects both the surgical patient and the healthcare worker from avoidable infectious complications.

Selection and Use of Antiseptic Agents

The choice of antiseptic agent used during a surgical scrub is critical for achieving maximum microbial reduction with minimal skin irritation. An ideal antiseptic agent must possess a broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, act rapidly, have a persistent or residual effect that inhibits microbial growth over time, and remain effective in the presence of organic matter like blood or body fluids. Common agents utilized in clinical practice include chlorhexidine gluconate (typically 4%) and povidone-iodine (typically 7.5% or 10%). Chlorhexidine gluconate is widely favored in modern nursing practice due to its rapid action, significant residual activity that binds to the stratum corneum, and sustained reduction of skin flora even hours after application. Povidone-iodine releases free iodine to kill microorganisms, but its residual effect is shorter and can be inactivated by blood or serum proteins. Alcohol-based rub formulations containing chlorhexidine or other active compounds are also increasingly recognized for their superior antimicrobial efficacy and skin-conditioning properties, provided the hands are visibly clean. When selecting an agent, nurses must also consider patient and staff allergies, skin integrity, and institutional guidelines. Understanding the pharmacological properties of these agents allows the nurse to execute an evidence-based scrub that offers maximum protection against surgical site infections.

Step-by-Step Surgical Scrub Techniques

The step-by-step surgical scrub technique is a structured ritual designed to ensure every square centimeter of the hands, fingers, and forearms is thoroughly decontaminated. Depending on the institution and the agent used, a scrub can be time-based (typically 3 to 5 minutes) or stroke-based. Before applying the antiseptic, hands and forearms are thoroughly washed with plain soap and water, and subungual debris is removed under running water using a sterile, disposable nail cleaner. Once the hands are wet, the antiseptic agent is dispensed, and the mechanical scrubbing begins. The standard anatomical approach involves scrubbing each finger systematically, addressing all four sides (lateral, medial, dorsal, palmar), followed by the web spaces between the fingers. Next, the palm and back of the hand are scrubbed in a circular motion. The scrub then progresses up the forearm in distinct anatomical increments toward the elbow, ensuring that the elbows are also scrubbed rigorously because they harbor high bacterial counts. Throughout the process, hands must be kept higher than the elbows at all times to ensure that contaminated water and suds flow downward from the clean hands toward the dirty elbows, never backward. Rinsing follows the same directional rule: hands are passed through the water in one direction only, from fingertips to elbows, avoiding any back-and-forth motion that could recontaminate the clean areas.

Post-Scrub Protocol and Entering the Sterile Field

Transitioning from the scrub sink into the sterile field requires meticulous execution to avoid accidental contamination of the freshly scrubbed hands, arms, and surgical attire. After the thorough rinse, the scrubbed nurse or surgeon must back away from the sink, ensuring their hands do not touch the sink, walls, or scrub suit. Hands must remain elevated in front of the body between the chest and waist level at all times. Drying is accomplished using a sterile towel provided in a sterile gown pack or wrap. The proper technique involves picking up the sterile towel, bending slightly forward to prevent touching the scrub top, and drying one arm and hand thoroughly using a blotting, non-friction motion, moving from fingers toward the elbow. Once one hand/arm is dry, the unsoiled end of the towel is used to dry the opposite arm, taking care never to retrace a dried area back over a wet area (the one-way rule). The damp towel is then carefully discarded away from the sterile field without letting arms drop below waist level. Following drying, the sterile gown is donned using an aseptic technique, usually via a closed-glove method where hands do not emerge from the gown cuffs until gloves are secured. This careful protocol ensures that the sterile barrier remains uncompromised as the nurse steps into the operating theatre.

Common Errors and Troubleshooting in Surgical Scrubbing

Even with comprehensive training, nursing students and clinical staff frequently encounter errors and challenges during the surgical scrub process. One of the most common mistakes is allowing the hands to drop below waist level or letting scrubbed arms brush against unsterile surfaces, such as the scrub sink, doors, or colleagues. When this happens, the scrub is immediately compromised, requiring either re-scrubbing or appropriate remedial action according to institutional policy. Another frequent error is improper timing or rushed scrubbing, where personnel skip the anatomical web spaces, nails, or elbows to save time. Furthermore, splashing water onto the surgical attire during rinsing can create a bridge for microbial migration through a damp gown, as moisture wicks bacteria from the skin through fabric. Using contaminated or expired antiseptic agents, improper storage of nail cleaners, or wearing damaged gloves immediately after scrubbing also undermine infection control efforts. Troubleshooting these issues involves fostering a culture of mutual accountability in the OR, where team members politely point out contamination errors without blame. By recognizing these pitfalls, student nurses can cultivate situational awareness, correct their technique proactively, and uphold the highest standards of patient safety in Nigerian surgical suites.
