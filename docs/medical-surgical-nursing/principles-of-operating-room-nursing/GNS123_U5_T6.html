Scrubbing

Introduction and Aseptic Principles in Surgical Scrubbing

Surgical scrubbing is a specialized, timed mechanical washing and chemical disinfection process performed by the surgical team before entering the sterile operating environment. Unlike routine handwashing which removes transient microorganisms and visible dirt, the surgical scrub aims to significantly reduce the resident microbial count on the skin for the duration of the procedure. Skin can never be completely sterilized because resident flora live deep within the sebaceous glands and hair follicles. Therefore, the goal is to suppress microbial multiplication rapidly and maintain that low count using an antimicrobial agent. Understanding this distinction prevents the false belief that hands become permanently sterile. Every member of the surgical team must execute this protocol meticulously to protect vulnerable surgical incisions from potential contamination.

Preparation and Attire Before the Scrub

Before stepping up to the scrub sink, proper preparation of the body and attire is mandatory to minimize shedding of skin squames, hair, and microbes.

  • Surgical Attire: Scrub suits must fit closely to the body with trousers tucked in or snug at the waist, and all head coverings must completely contain hair, sideburns, and scalp hair.
  • Personal Items: All jewelry including rings, watches, bracelets, and earrings must be removed. Rings trap moisture and heavy bacterial loads underneath them, making adequate skin disinfection impossible.
  • Nails and Hands: Fingernails must be short, clean, and free of artificial nails, extenders, or nail polish. Artificial nails harbor fungal and bacterial pathogens that resist standard scrubbing agents.
  • Physical Inspection: Inspect hands and forearms for any open cuts, abrasions, or weeping lesions. Personnel with broken skin must not scrub or participate in sterile procedures due to the high risk of wound contamination.

Antiseptic Agents Used in Surgical Scrubbing

The choice of antiseptic agent dictates how effectively microbial populations are managed during the scrub. An ideal agent has a broad antimicrobial spectrum, rapid action, and a persistent residual effect that prevents bacterial regrowth under surgical gloves.

Chlorhexidine remains a favorite due to its lasting binding property, while povidone-iodine requires time to dry and release its active component.

Step-by-Step Surgical Scrub Techniques

Surgical scrubbing follows either a timed method or a counted-brushstroke method. Whichever facility protocol is adopted, the fundamental rule is unidirectional movement: scrubbing always proceeds from the cleanest area (fingertips) toward the dirtier area (elbows).

  1. Pre-Wash: Turn on water with foot or knee controls. Wet hands and forearms, apply soap, and wash thoroughly up to two inches above the elbows before rinsing.
  2. Nail Cleaning: Clean under fingernails using a disposable pick under running water, discarding the pick immediately.
  3. Scrubbing Sequence: Apply the chosen antiseptic agent. Scrub each finger, the web spaces between fingers, the palm, and the back of the hand. Move systematically up the forearm to the elbow, dividing the arm into four distinct sides.
  4. Maintaining Position: Keep hands elevated higher than the elbows at all times during and after the scrub. This ensures dirty water and soap drip downward toward the elbow, away from the surgically clean hands.
  5. Rinsing: Rinse thoroughly by passing hands and arms through the water in one direction only—fingertips first, never moving elbows back through the water stream.

Drying and Entering the Sterile Field

Transitioning from the scrub sink into the operating room requires extreme vigilance to avoid contaminating the newly disinfected skin.

  • Entering the Room: Back into the operating room through the door or push it open with your back, keeping hands safely in front of you and away from your scrub suit.
  • Picking up the Towel: Reach down to pick up the sterile towel from the pack, bending slightly forward without letting your body or uniform touch the surrounding sterile tray table.
  • Drying Technique: Open the towel carefully. Dry one hand and arm thoroughly using a blotting, rotating motion, moving from fingers to elbow. Grasp the clean end of the towel with the already dried hand to dry the opposite arm, never moving backward over a dried area.
  • Gown and Glove Application: Drop the used towel into a hamper without letting your hand dip below waist level. Proceed immediately to don the sterile surgical gown and gloves using closed-gloving or assisted-gloving techniques.

Common Errors and Troubleshooting in Surgical Scrubbing

Even experienced personnel can make mistakes that break aseptic technique during the scrub process. Recognizing these pitfalls prevents accidental patient infections.

  • Recontamination by Uniform Contact: Leaning against the scrub sink during the process transfers microorganisms from the scrub suit onto the washed area. Always maintain a safe distance from the sink basin.
  • Dropping Hands Below the Waist: Allowing hands to drop below waist level renders them unsterile because the lower body is considered contaminated. If hands drop below the waist, the scrub must be repeated.
  • Inadequate Contact Time: Rushing through the required scrub duration decreases the chemical and mechanical destruction of microbes. Facilities specify exact minutes or brush strokes for valid reasons.
  • Splashing Water: Splashing water onto the scrub suit creates a wet bridge through which bacteria from the uniform can wick outward toward sterile gowns.
