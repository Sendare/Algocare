Scrubbing

Introduction to Surgical Scrubbing

Surgical scrubbing is a specialized, timed mechanical washing and chemical antisepsis process performed before any surgical intervention. The primary objective is to significantly reduce the transient microbial flora and drastically decrease resident microbial counts on the skin of the surgical team's hands and forearms. Unlike routine handwashing meant for social hygiene, a surgical scrub aims to achieve a high level of skin decontamination that persists throughout the duration of the operation, should a sterile glove become punctured.

In Nigerian tertiary hospitals and operating suites across the globe, adherence to this protocol forms the first line of defense against surgical site infections. Even though skin cannot be rendered completely sterile, rigorous scrubbing minimizes the risk of introducing endogenous and exogenous pathogens into an open surgical wound. Every member of the sterile team—including the surgeon, assistant, and scrub nurse—must perform this ritual meticulously before donning sterile gowns and gloves.

Preparation and Attire Before Scrubbing

Before approaching the scrub sink, the nurse must ensure proper operating theatre attire is worn to prevent shedding skin cells, hair, and microbes into the sterile environment.

  • Surgical Attire: Scrub suits must be freshly laundered, with scrub tops securely tucked into trousers to prevent drafts from shedding skin.
  • Head Coverings: Caps or hoods must completely enclose all scalp hair and sideburns. Facial hair requires a snug-fitting surgical mask and beard cover.
  • Footwear: Dedicated theatre shoes or protective shoe covers must provide firm support and be easy to clean.
  • Jewelry Removal: Rings, watches, bracelets, and earrings harbor massive bacterial colonies and compromise glove integrity. All must be removed prior to scrubbing.
  • Nail Care: Fingernails must be short, clean, and free of artificial nails, extenders, or polish, which trap moisture and microbes.

Selection and Use of Antiseptic Agents

An effective surgical scrub agent must possess a broad antimicrobial spectrum, act rapidly, have a persistent cumulative effect, and be non-irritating to skin tissues.

When waterless alcohol rubs are used, hands must first be washed with soap and water if visibly soiled. For traditional water-based scrubs, chlorhexidine remains a frequent choice in many Nigerian hospitals due to its sustained residual activity.

Step-by-Step Surgical Scrub Techniques

Two primary methodologies govern the physical scrub process: the timed scrub method and the counted-stroke method. Both methods require strict attention to anatomical zones, beginning at the fingertips and progressing systematically up the arms.

  1. Pre-wash: Wet hands and forearms under running water, apply antimicrobial soap, and wash thoroughly up to five centimeters above the elbows. Clean under fingernails using a disposable nail cleaner under running water.
  2. Hand and Arm Division: Mentally divide the arm into distinct sections: fingers, hand (palms and backs), wrist, forearm, and the region just above the elbow.
  3. Scrubbing Motion: Use firm, circular strokes. Scrub every surface of each finger, moving from the thumb to the little finger, followed by the palm and back of the hand.
  4. Forearm Progression: Progress up the forearm in sections toward the elbow, ensuring every square centimeter is scrubbed.
  5. Rinsing: Always hold hands higher than the elbows at all times during and after the scrub. Rinse thoroughly under running water, letting water flow from fingertips down to the elbows, never allowing water to run backward toward the clean hands.

Post-Scrub Protocol and Entering the Sterile Field

Completing the physical scrub does not end the preparation process; transitioning safely into the sterile operating environment is critical. The nurse must walk backward or sideways when opening doors to avoid touching non-sterile surfaces with the body or scrub attire.

Upon entering the theatre, hands and arms must be dried using a sterile towel provided in the sterile pack. Bend slightly forward at the waist so the towel does not touch the scrub suit. Use one half of the towel for one hand and arm, moving in a continuous motion from fingers to elbow without retracing. Carefully drop the used towel into a designated receptacle without lowering the hands below waist level.

Once dried, the scrub nurse adopts the sterile gown and gloving technique, keeping hands within the chest area and away from unsterile surfaces at all times.

Common Errors and Troubleshooting in Surgical Scrubbing

Even experienced personnel can commit technique lapses that compromise sterility. Recognizing these errors promptly ensures patient safety.

  • Re-tracing Areas: Moving the brush back down the arm to a previously scrubbed area transfers microbes back onto clean skin.
  • Lowering the Hands: Dropping hands below waist level immediately contaminates the field of vision and requires a complete re-scrub.
  • Inadequate Time: Rushing through the prescribed minutes or strokes fails to reduce resident flora to safe surgical levels.
  • Splashing Water: Allowing water to drip onto the scrub suit and then touch the hands creates a bridge for strike-through contamination.
  • Touching Unsterile Surfaces: Bumping against doors, instrument trolleys, or other team members mandates immediate re-scrubbing or re-gloving depending on the point of contact.
