Personnel in the Operating Theatre

Introduction to the Operating Theatre Team

The operating theatre (OT) is a specialized, highly controlled environment within a hospital where surgical procedures are performed safely. Because patient vulnerability is at its highest during surgery, success relies heavily on a coordinated, multi-disciplinary team. Every member of the OT personnel has distinct, well-defined roles and responsibilities that overlap to ensure seamless patient care, safety, and infection control. Clear communication and strict adherence to aseptic techniques are paramount for everyone working in this environment. The team is broadly categorized into sterile personnel, who scrub in and work within the sterile field, and non-sterile personnel, who manage the overall environment, monitor the patient, and support the sterile members. Understanding these roles is essential for student nurses in Nigeria as they transition from classroom theory to practical clinical placements. Familiarity with the chain of command and the specific duties of each healthcare professional promotes teamwork, reduces the risk of surgical errors, and ultimately improves patient outcomes. By respecting each other's expertise, the OT team functions as a single, cohesive unit dedicated to the welfare of the surgical patient from the pre-operative phase through to post-operative recovery.

The Surgeon

The surgeon is the licensed medical practitioner who has ultimate medical and legal responsibility for the performance of the surgical procedure and the overall care of the patient in the operating theatre. Before entering the theatre, the surgeon evaluates the patient, confirms the diagnosis, obtains informed consent, and plans the surgical approach. During the operation, the surgeon leads the sterile team, performs the primary incisions, manipulates tissues, achieves hemostasis, and completes the corrective or therapeutic intervention. The surgeon must possess exceptional manual dexterity, sound clinical judgment, and the ability to manage unexpected intraoperative complications calmly and decisively. In a teaching hospital setting, the surgeon also acts as an educator, guiding surgical residents and nursing students through procedures. Postoperatively, the surgeon writes the surgical notes, prescribes necessary medications such as analgesics and antibiotics, and monitors the patient for immediate surgical complications. Clear communication between the surgeon, the scrub nurse, and the anaesthetist is vital throughout the entire perioperative continuum to prevent errors such as retained surgical items or wrong-site surgery.

The Anaesthetist

The anaesthetist is a qualified medical doctor specializing in anaesthesiology who is responsible for the safety, comfort, and physiological monitoring of the patient before, during, and immediately after surgery. Their primary role begins in the pre-operative assessment clinic, where they review the patient's medical history, airway status, and previous anesthetic experiences to formulate a safe anesthetic plan. Inside the operating theatre, the anaesthetist induces anesthesia, secures the airway through intubation or other means, and maintains an appropriate depth of anesthesia using pharmacological agents. Throughout the procedure, they continuously monitor the patient's vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, end-tidal carbon dioxide, and urine output, managing any hemodynamic instability that arises. They work in close collaboration with the nurse anaesthetist and the rest of the surgical team. At the end of the operation, the anaesthetist reverses anesthesia, ensures the patient has regained protective airway reflexes, and safely transfers them to the post-anaesthesia care unit (PACU) or recovery room while providing a comprehensive handover report.

Nurse Anaesthetist

A nurse anaesthetist is a specialized registered nurse who has completed advanced training in nurse anesthesia and works alongside or under the supervision of a physician anaesthetist, or independently in settings with limited medical specialists. In many Nigerian healthcare facilities, nurse anaesthetists play a crucial workforce role in delivering safe anesthesia care, particularly in rural or resource-constrained hospitals. Their responsibilities include conducting pre-anesthetic evaluations, assisting with the administration and maintenance of general, regional, or local anesthesia, and closely monitoring the patient's physiological status during surgery. They are adept at managing airways, administering intravenous fluids and blood products, and recognizing early signs of anesthetic complications such as malignant hyperthermia or severe hypotension. Like their medical counterparts, nurse anaesthetists participate in the safe emergence of the patient from anesthesia and accompany them to the recovery room. They ensure meticulous documentation of all drugs administered, fluid balances, and vital sign trends throughout the perioperative period, maintaining high standards of patient safety and professional nursing accountability.

Scrub Nurse

The scrub nurse is an essential sterile member of the operating theatre team who works directly within the sterile field. Before the procedure begins, the scrub nurse performs a rigorous surgical hand scrub, dons sterile gown and gloves, and sets up the sterile instrument table, sutures, and supplies in an organized, efficient manner. During the surgery, the scrub nurse anticipates the needs of the surgeon and assistant, handing instruments, sponges, and sutures promptly and safely without taking their eyes off the sterile field. Maintaining strict aseptic technique is a core duty; the scrub nurse immediately identifies and corrects any breaks in sterility made by any member of the team. Along with the circulating nurse, the scrub nurse is strictly responsible for conducting accurate, thorough counts of all instruments, needles, and sponges before the incision, during closure, and before skin closure to prevent retained surgical items. After the surgery, the scrub nurse assists with dressing application, helps clean the patient's wound site, and participates in safely dismantling and decontaminating delicate surgical instruments.

Circulating Nurse

The circulating nurse is a registered professional nurse who functions in the non-sterile zone of the operating theatre. Unlike the scrub nurse, the circulating nurse does not wear sterile gowns or gloves, allowing them freedom of movement to manage the overall environment of the theatre and assist the entire team. Their duties begin before the patient arrives by ensuring the operating room is clean, safe, and fully equipped with functioning lights, suction apparatus, and electrosurgical units. The circulating nurse greets the patient upon arrival, verifies identity, checks surgical consent, performs safety checklists, and provides psychological support to reduce anxiety. During the operation, the circulating nurse manages specimen collection and labeling, adjusts lighting, connects electrical equipment, retrieves extra supplies, and documents all intraoperative events accurately in the nursing notes. They also assist the anaesthetist during induction and emergence from anesthesia. Critically, the circulating nurse works alongside the scrub nurse to perform mandatory sponge, sharp, and instrument counts, maintaining constant vigilance to uphold patient safety standards throughout the entire perioperative journey.

Health Orderlies

Health orderlies, also known as nursing aides, ward attendants, or operational support personnel, are vital non-professional members of the operating theatre team who provide crucial logistical and environmental support. Though they do not perform clinical nursing procedures, surgery cannot run smoothly without their contributions. Their responsibilities include transporting patients safely to and from the operating theatre on trolleys or stretchers, maintaining the physical cleanliness of the theatre between cases, and assisting with terminal cleaning at the end of the operating list. Health orderlies help in positioning heavy equipment, disposing of biomedical waste and hazardous materials in accordance with infection control protocols, and running errands such as delivering laboratory specimens or blood products from the blood bank. They work under the direct supervision of qualified nurses and are expected to understand basic safety principles, standard precautions, and the importance of maintaining a sterile environment. Their supportive role reduces the workload of professional nurses, allowing scrub and circulating nurses to focus entirely on direct patient care and intraoperative safety.

Nursing Student

Nursing students in the operating theatre are learners gaining hands-on clinical exposure to perioperative nursing care as part of their professional education in Nigeria. While in the OT, student nurses must adhere strictly to hospital policies, professional codes of conduct, and universal safety precautions. Their primary objective is to observe surgical procedures, understand aseptic techniques, learn the anatomy and physiology relevant to surgeries, and comprehend the specific roles of scrub and circulating nurses. Depending on their level of study and under the direct, close supervision of a qualified clinical preceptor, instructor, or experienced staff nurse, students may assist with circulating duties, help position patients, or occasionally scrub in as a secondary assistant. Student nurses must recognize their scope of practice and never attempt independent clinical tasks beyond their training level. Maintaining patient confidentiality, showing professional empathy, and demonstrating a respectful, eager attitude toward learning are essential attributes for any student in this specialized environment. By actively engaging in pre-conference briefings, intraoperative learning moments, and post-operative debriefings, nursing students bridge the gap between theoretical classroom knowledge and real-world clinical practice.
