Personnel in the Operating Theatre

Introduction to the Operating Theatre Team

The operating theatre is a high-stress environment where human lives depend on absolute coordination, precision, and clear communication. No single individual can perform a surgical procedure alone; patient safety relies entirely on a multidisciplinary team. Every member has a defined role, strict boundaries, and shared responsibilities.

Team members are divided into two main categories based on where they stand during the operation:

  • Sterile team: Members who scrub their hands, wear sterile gowns and gloves, and handle sterile instruments directly within the sterile field. This includes the surgeon, surgical assistants, and the scrub nurse.
  • Non-sterile team: Members who remain outside the sterile field, manage overall room operations, and assist the sterile team without touching sterile equipment. This includes the anaesthetist, circulating nurse, and health orderlies.

The Surgeon and Surgical Assistants

The surgeon is the licensed medical practitioner who takes ultimate legal and clinical responsibility for the entire surgical procedure. Before entering the theatre, the surgeon reviews the patient's history, performs the diagnostic checks, obtains informed consent, and decides the exact surgical technique. During the operation, the surgeon leads the team and performs the physical intervention.

Surgical assistants—who may be resident doctors, other surgeons, or specialized nurses—stand opposite or beside the primary surgeon. Their core duties include retracting tissues to expose the surgical site, sponging blood, suctioning fluids, and occasionally tying knots or suturing incisions under the surgeon's direction. Clear, calm communication between the surgeon and assistants prevents tissue trauma and shortens the time the patient spends under anaesthesia.

The Anaesthetist and Anaesthetic Assistant

The anaesthetist is a specialist doctor responsible for keeping the patient pain-free, unconscious, and physiologically stable throughout the surgery. This professional evaluates the patient preoperatively, chooses the appropriate anaesthetic agent, manages the airway, and continuously monitors vital signs such as blood pressure, heart rate, oxygen saturation, and breathing depth.

An anaesthetic assistant, who is often a specialized nurse or technician, supports the anaesthetist by checking machine functions, preparing emergency drugs, monitoring intravenous lines, and helping position the patient safely on the operating table.

The Scrub Nurse

The scrub nurse is a registered nurse who works within the sterile field. Before the procedure begins, this nurse performs a thorough surgical scrub, puts on a sterile gown and gloves, and arranges all surgical instruments, needles, and sutures on a sterile back table according to a strict mental map of the upcoming procedure.

During surgery, the scrub nurse anticipates the surgeon's needs, handing instruments promptly without taking their eyes off the sterile field. Along with the circulating nurse, the scrub nurse is legally responsible for conducting accurate, continuous counts of all sponges, instruments, and needles before, during, and at the end of the operation to prevent leaving foreign items inside the patient.

The Circulating Nurse

The circulating nurse is an unsterile registered nurse who manages the overall environment of the operating theatre. Unlike the scrub nurse, this professional moves freely around the room, opening sterile packs without contaminating them, connecting suction and electrical equipment, and ensuring supplies are readily available.

The circulating nurse acts as the patient's advocate while the patient is unconscious. They verify patient identity, check surgical site markings, manage specimen labeling and dispatch to the laboratory, and document all events in the theatre records. If a sterile item is accidentally contaminated, the circulating nurse immediately halts its use and provides a sterile replacement.

Health Orderlies and Support Staff

Health orderlies, sometimes called theatre attendants or porters, form the backbone of physical logistics within the surgical suite. They are responsible for transporting patients safely on trolleys between the ward and the operating theatre, moving heavy furniture, and fetching blood products or urgent supplies from the laboratory or pharmacy.

Following a procedure, orderlies assist with terminal cleaning, waste disposal, and preparing the theatre for the next case. Although they do not perform clinical procedures or touch sterile equipment, their efficiency directly impacts theatre turnaround time and overall workflow safety.

Nursing Students in the Theatre

Nursing students enter the operating theatre to bridge classroom theory with clinical practice. Their role is primarily educational and observational, though under direct supervision by a clinical instructor or assigned preceptor, they may practice basic skills such as proper hand scrubbing, donning sterile gowns, or observing surgical anatomy.

Students must maintain strict vigilance regarding sterile technique. A common mistake among beginners is breaking sterility by accidentally touching a non-sterile surface or lowering their hands below waist level. Students must ask questions, remain aware of spatial boundaries, and prioritize patient dignity and safety at all times.
