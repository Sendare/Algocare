Instrumentation

Introduction to Surgical Instrumentation

Surgical instruments are precision tools designed specifically to perform various actions during an operation, such as modifying biological tissue or providing access to a surgical site. The history of surgical tools spans thousands of years, evolving from crude bronze and flint implements to the sophisticated, high-grade stainless steel, titanium, and vessel-sealing devices used in modern operating theatres today. During any surgical procedure in Nigeria, from a routine appendectomy in a district hospital to complex cardiothoracic surgery in a teaching hospital, the scrub nurse acts as the vital gatekeeper for these tools. Understanding the anatomy of an instrument—including the tips, jaws, box lock, shanks, and ratchets—forms the foundation of safe perioperative practice.

Classification of Surgical Instruments

To maintain order on the instrument table and ensure efficient communication between the surgeon and the scrub nurse, tools are categorized into functional families. Grouping them by their primary intraoperative purpose prevents frantic searching during critical moments of an operation.

  • Cutting and Dissecting: Blades, scalpels, and scissors used for incising tissue or separating planes.
  • Grasping and Holding: Forceps and clamps designed to manipulate tissues or secure drapes.
  • Clamping and Occluding: Hemostats and bulldog clamps engineered to obstruct the flow of blood or tubular structures.
  • Retracting and Exposing: Retractors that hold back tissue layers to maintain a clear visual field.
  • Suturing and Stapling: Needle holders and staplers required for wound closure.

Cutting and Dissecting Instruments

Cutting instruments possess sharp edges or blades designed to divide flesh, cut suture material, or sharply dissect adherent tissue planes. Scalpels consist of a reusable handle, typically a Bard-Parker No. 3 or No. 4, paired with a disposable blade like the No. 10, 11, or 15. Scissors vary from delicate Metzenbaum scissors, designed for fine tissue dissection, to heavier Mayo scissors, which are robust enough to cut thick fascia or suture materials. Care must be taken never to use delicate tissue scissors for cutting wire or heavy drains, as this ruins the alignment of the blades.

Grasping and Holding Instruments

These instruments are engineered to manipulate tissues, pick up dressings, or stabilize structures without causing undue trauma. They are subdivided into traumatic instruments, which feature teeth for a secure grip on tough structures like fascia, and atraumatic instruments, which rely on smooth jaws or delicate fenestrations to handle fragile viscera like the bowel.

  • Toothed dissecting forceps (tissue forceps): Provide a firm grip on skin and fascia using 1x2 teeth.
  • Adson forceps: Delicate forceps often used in plastic or neurological procedures.
  • Allis clamps: Feature interlocking teeth that provide a secure, somewhat traumatic grip on heavy tissue.
  • Babcock forceps: Feature a smooth, rounded, fenestrated tip ideal for grasping the ureter, intestine, or fallopian tubes safely.

Retracting and Exposing Instruments

Adequate visualization is the cornerstone of safe surgery. Retracting instruments are deployed to hold back wound edges, organs, or major blood vessels, giving the surgical team a clear pathway to the operative site. Retractors can be manual (requiring an assistant or scrub nurse to hold them constantly) or self-retaining (equipped with a ratchet, screw, or crank mechanism to maintain position independently).

Manual retractors include the Senn, Richardson, and Langenbeck, which are staples of general surgical trays. Self-retaining retractors, such as the Gelpi, Weitlaner, or Balfour, are invaluable during deep cavity or orthopedic exposures because they free up human hands for other tasks.

Clamping and Occluding Instruments

Hemostats and clamps are designed to control bleeding by compressing the lumen of bleeding vessels until they can be ligated with suture or cauterized. These instruments feature opposing ring handles, a locking ratchet, and jaws that may be straight or curved, with transverse serrations running partway or all the way down the blade. Common examples include the small Halsted mosquito forceps for delicate vessels, the Kelly or Crile forceps for medium vessels, and heavier clamps like the Kocher or Ochsner for dense tissues and major bleeding points.

Suturing and Stapling Instruments

Closing wounds securely requires needle holders (also called needle drivers) and mechanical stapling devices. Needle holders resemble hemostats but feature shorter, sturdier jaws designed to prevent the surgical needle from bending or rotating during tissue penetration. The jaws often contain tungsten carbide inserts for a superior grip. Mechanical staplers, increasingly common in Nigerian tertiary hospitals for gastrointestinal anastomoses and skin closure, speed up operative times and provide consistent, airtight staple lines.

Instrument Care, Handling, and Sterilization

The lifecycle of a surgical instrument involves meticulous cleaning, inspection, packaging, and sterilization to prevent hospital-acquired infections. Following surgery, gross bioburden must be removed promptly via rinsing and enzymatic soaking to prevent pitting and corrosion. Instruments are inspected for structural integrity, particularly checking box locks, ratchets, and cutting edges. They are then arranged in perforated trays, wrapped in appropriate sterilization grade material, and subjected to autoclaving or ethylene oxide sterilization. Proper handling protects patient safety and preserves the financial investment of the health institution.
