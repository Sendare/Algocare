Instrumentation

Introduction to Surgical Instrumentation

Surgical instruments are precision tools designed to perform specific actions during an operation, such as cutting tissue, controlling bleeding, or holding organs out of the way. Understanding these tools is a fundamental requirement for every nurse working in the operating theatre. Instruments are traditionally manufactured from high-grade metals, most commonly stainless steel, which resists corrosion and withstands repeated heat sterilization. Some specialized micro-instruments are made of titanium or coated with ceramic to reduce light reflection under surgical lamps. Every item on the instrument trolley has a designated name and purpose. Knowing your tools protects the patient from prolonged surgical time and prevents accidental tissue trauma caused by using an instrument for an unintended job.

Classification of Surgical Instruments

To make sense of the hundreds of tools found in a surgical tray, nurses group them into functional categories based on what they do during an operation. This system keeps the instrument trolley organized and helps the scrub nurse pass the correct tool without hesitation.

  • Cutting and Dissecting: Blades and scissors used to incise skin or separate tissues.
  • Grasping and Holding: Forceps and clamps used to hold tissues, dressings, or needles.
  • Clamping and Occluding: Hemostats and vascular clamps used to compress blood vessels and stop bleeding.
  • Retracting and Exposing: Retractors used to hold back wound edges for a clear view of the surgical site.
  • Suturing and Stapling: Needle holders and staplers used to close wounds at the end of surgery.

Cutting and Dissecting Instruments

Cutting instruments include scalpels, surgical scissors, and bone cutters. The scalpel typically consists of a reusable metal handle and a disposable blade, such as a number 10 or number 15 blade, which is attached using a needle holder or forceps to prevent finger injuries. Scissors come in various designs, including Mayo scissors for cutting heavy fascia and delicate Metzenbaum scissors for dissecting fine, delicate tissues. A common mistake among junior nurses is using heavy cutting scissors on delicate structures or sutures, which blunts the blades prematurely. Always match the tool to the tissue density.

Grasping and Holding Instruments

These instruments are designed to pick up, hold, or pull tissues, organs, or sterile drapes. They are broadly divided into toothed and non-toothed varieties.

  • Toothed forceps (e.g., Adson with teeth): Feature small interlocking teeth at the tip that provide a secure grip on tough structures like skin or fascia without slipping. They should never be used on delicate organs like the bowel.
  • Non-toothed forceps (e.g., plain dissecting forceps): Have smooth or ridged tips used for handling delicate structures, blood vessels, or moist dressings to prevent crushing injury.
  • Tissue holding clamps (e.g., Allis or Babcock forceps): Feature ratcheted finger rings that lock into place to hold heavy tissue securely.

Clamping and Occluding Instruments

Clamping and occluding instruments are designed to control bleeding by compressing blood vessels until they can be tied off with a suture ligature. The most common example is the hemostat, which features a ratcheted handle and serrated jaws. Hemostats can be straight or curved; curved hemostats are more frequently used because the curve allows the surgeon to see around the tip and place a tie accurately. Vascular clamps feature atraumatic serrations that close tightly enough to stop blood flow without crushing the delicate inner walls of major blood vessels.

Retracting and Exposing Instruments

Retractors are essential for pulling back skin, fat, muscle, and organs to maintain a clear, well-lit view of the deep surgical site. They can be hand-held by an assistant or self-retaining using a mechanical frame.

  • Hand-held retractors (e.g., Senn, Richardson, or Army-Navy): Require a member of the surgical team to hold them in position continuously.
  • Self-retaining retractors (e.g., Weitlaner or Gelpi): Feature a ratchet mechanism that locks the blades open, freeing up human hands for other tasks during deep cavity operations.

Nurses must ensure retractors are adjusted gently and periodically released during long procedures to prevent tissue ischemia caused by constant heavy pressure.

Suturing, Stapling, and Closure Instruments

At the conclusion of a surgical procedure, the wound must be closed securely to promote healing. Needle holders, also known as needle drivers, are specialized locking clamps with short, sturdy jaws designed to hold surgical needles firmly without bending them. The nurse must load the needle securely into the tip of the needle holder, grasped approximately one-third to one-half of the distance from the eye or swaged end. Surgical staplers are also frequently used for rapid skin and bowel closure, offering a consistent and secure seal with minimal tissue reaction.

Instrument Care, Handling, and Sterilization

Proper maintenance ensures that expensive surgical instruments remain functional and safe for patient use. After surgery, instruments must be sorted, soaked in enzymatic detergent to remove organic debris, and thoroughly washed before sterilization. Box locks and ratchets must be kept open during cleaning and autoclaving to allow steam to reach all surfaces. Instruments should be inspected regularly for cracks, rust, or loose joints. Damaged tools must be taken out of service immediately, as a broken jaw or a loose screw inside a patient's wound constitutes a severe surgical emergency.
