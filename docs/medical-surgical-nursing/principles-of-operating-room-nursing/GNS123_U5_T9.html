Instrumentation

Introduction to Surgical Instrumentation

Surgical instrumentation forms the backbone of any surgical procedure, requiring nursing students in Nigeria to master both identification and function. Proper understanding of surgical tools ensures patient safety, optimizes the efficiency of the operating theatre team, and maintains the sterile field. Instruments are generally manufactured from high-grade stainless steel, titanium, or other durable metals designed to withstand repeated sterilization cycles without corrosion. As a nursing professional, you must be intimately familiar with the anatomy of an instrument, which includes the tips, jaws, box lock, shank, ratchets, and finger rings. The box lock is a critical hinge joint that allows the instrument to open and close, while ratchets provide locking mechanisms to maintain tension without continuous manual pressure. Handling these delicate and expensive tools with care prevents unnecessary damage and financial loss to the health facility. Furthermore, effective communication and passing of instruments using the proper technique—such as the one-step or neutral zone method—reduces the risk of sharps injuries and promotes seamless surgical teamwork. Mastering instrumentation is foundational for transitioning from the classroom to the scrub and circulating roles in clinical practice across Nigerian hospitals.

Classification of Surgical Instruments

To streamline learning and clinical practice, surgical instruments are classified into functional categories based on their primary use during an operation. These major categories include cutting and dissecting, grasping and holding, clamping and occluding, retracting and exposing, and suturing and stapling. Each category is specifically designed to perform mechanical tasks on human tissues or support the operative process. For example, cutting instruments like scalpels and scissors are engineered with sharp edges to divide tissue, whereas clamping instruments like hemostats feature serrated jaws designed to occlude blood vessels and achieve hemostasis. Understanding these classifications helps student nurses anticipate the surgeon's needs during intraoperative phases, ensuring rapid and safe instrument delivery. Moreover, proper categorization aids in efficient instrument tray assembly, counting procedures, and post-operative cleaning protocols. In the fast-paced operating room environment of Nigerian healthcare facilities, quick recognition of an instrument's functional class directly impacts operative time and patient outcomes. Consequently, regular practice with instrument trays and flashcards is an essential study habit for nursing students.

Cutting and Dissecting Instruments

Cutting and dissecting instruments are designed to incise, separate, or excise tissues. This group includes scalpels, surgical scissors, osteotomes, curettes, and saws. The scalpel is typically composed of a reusable handle (such as a Bard-Parker handle, commonly size 3 or 4) and a disposable blade (such as number 10, 11, 15, or 20) attached using a needle holder or forceps to prevent finger injuries. Surgical scissors vary in shape and purpose; Mayo scissors are heavy and used for cutting dense tissues like fascia, while Metzenbaum scissors are finer and designed for delicate tissue dissection. When handling these sharp items, nursing students must practice absolute precision and safety. Scalpel blades should never be attached or removed with bare fingers; instead, a needle holder or specialized instrument must be used to snap the blade on and off. Furthermore, cutting instruments must be protected from colliding with other hard metal instruments during processing to avoid dulling their fine edges. Keeping cutting edges sharp is vital to ensure clean tissue planes and minimize trauma to the patient.

Grasping and Holding Instruments

Grasping and holding instruments are utilized to safely handle tissues, organs, sponges, or dressings without causing unnecessary trauma. These instruments can be traumatic, featuring sharp teeth (like the Adson forceps with teeth or Kocher clamps), or atraumatic, featuring smooth or fenestrated jaws (like plain dissecting forceps or Babcock forceps). Tissue forceps, commonly called thumb forceps, are held like a pencil between the thumb and index finger, allowing for delicate manipulation during dissection or suturing. Babcock and Allis forceps are ring-handled instruments equipped with ratchets; Allis forceps have inward-curving teeth providing a secure grip on tough tissues, whereas Babcock forceps feature a smooth, rounded loop that gently holds delicate structures like the bowel or fallopian tubes without crushing them. Sponge holding forceps, such as Foerster sponges, are designed with circular fenestrations to securely grasp surgical sponges for skin preparation or intraoperative hemostasis. As a scrub nurse, selecting the appropriate grasping instrument based on tissue fragility is a crucial skill that prevents iatrogenic injury and ensures optimal surgical exposure.

Retracting and Exposing Instruments

Retracting and exposing instruments are essential for maintaining a clear, unobstructed view of the surgical site by holding back tissues, skin, fat, and organs. Retractors are broadly divided into two categories: manual (hand-held) and self-retaining. Manual retractors, such as Senn, Richardson, Army-Navy, and Deaver retractors, require an assistant or scrub nurse to hold them actively throughout the procedure. Deaver retractors are large and curved, frequently used for deep abdominal cavity retraction, whereas Army-Navy and Senn retractors are smaller and used for superficial wound exposures. Self-retaining retractors, such as the Balfour, Weitlaner, and Gelpi retractors, feature mechanical mechanisms like ratchets or screw adjustments that lock them in place, freeing up human hands for other tasks. For instance, the Weitlaner and Gelpi retractors are commonly used in orthopedic and neurological procedures to hold back skin edges and muscle layers. When applying retractors, nursing personnel must ensure that pressure is applied evenly and intermittently to prevent tissue ischemia, necrosis, or nerve damage from prolonged retraction.

Clamping and Occluding Instruments

Clamping and occluding instruments are designed to compress blood vessels or hollow organs to obstruct the flow of blood or contents, thereby achieving hemostasis. The most recognizable instruments in this group are hemostats, which include Kelly, Crile, and Halsted (mosquito) clamps. Mosquito clamps are small and delicate, ideal for superficial or small vessels, while Kelly and Crile clamps are slightly larger with transverse serrations running either partially or fully along the jaws. Heavier vascular clamps, such as the Right Angle or Doyen clamps, are used for deeper or larger vessels and intestinal occlusion. These instruments feature ratchets that allow the surgeon to lock the clamp onto a vessel securely, leaving their hands free to tie off the vessel with suture ligature. When passing a clamp to the surgeon, the scrub nurse should firmly place the instrument into the surgeon's outstretched hand in the position of function, usually with the ratchets partially engaged. Proper maintenance of the box locks and ratchets on these instruments is mandatory to ensure they do not slip or fail during critical moments of intraoperative bleeding.

Suturing and Stapling Instruments

Suturing and stapling instruments are utilized to close wounds, approximate tissue edges, and ligate vessels at the conclusion of or during a surgical intervention. This category includes needle holders (such as Hegar or Mayo-Hebgar needle holders), suture scissors, and mechanical staplers. Needle holders are sturdy instruments with short, thick jaws designed to grasp curved surgical needles securely without bending them; unlike hemostats, their jaws are reinforced and lack transverse serrations that could damage suture material. Suture materials are categorized into absorbable (e.g., Catgut, Vicryl) and non-absorbable (e.g., Nylon, Silk), and the scrub nurse must verify needle types and sizes with the surgeon before opening packages. In addition to manual suturing, surgical staplers have become increasingly popular for skin closure, bowel anastomoses, and vascular ligation because they decrease operative time and provide uniform tissue approximation. As part of safety protocols in Nigerian surgical suites, nurses must perform rigorous instrument and sponge counts, including needles and staples, before closure begins and before the patient leaves the theatre to prevent retained foreign objects.

Instrument Care, Handling, and Sterilization

Proper care, handling, and sterilization of surgical instruments are vital responsibilities of perioperative nursing staff to prevent healthcare-associated infections. The reprocessing cycle begins immediately after surgery with pre-cleaning and decontamination, where organic debris and blood are removed using enzymatic detergents. Instruments must never be soaked in saline, as salt causes pitting and rapid corrosion of stainless steel. Following manual or automated washing (using ultrasonic cleaners and washer-disinfectors), instruments are thoroughly inspected for functionality, cleanliness, and structural integrity. They are then assembled into specialized instrument trays, ensuring box locks are open and delicate tips are protected. Packaging is performed using sterilization pouches, wrappers, or rigid containers that allow sterilizing agents to penetrate effectively. The gold standard for sterilization in most Nigerian clinical settings is steam sterilization via an autoclave, which uses high pressure and temperature to destroy all microbial life, including bacterial spores. Alternative methods include ethylene oxide gas or chemical sterilants for heat-sensitive equipment. Documenting biological and chemical indicator results is mandatory to verify sterilization efficacy before any instrument tray is cleared for surgical use.
