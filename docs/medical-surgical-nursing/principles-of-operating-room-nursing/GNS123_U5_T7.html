Swab Counting

Definition and Types of Surgical Swabs

Surgical swabs and sponges are essential fabric items used during operations to maintain a clear visual field, absorb blood and bodily fluids, and protect delicate tissues. Unlike standard cotton wool used in dressing changes, surgical swabs possess specific manufacturing traits, most notably an attached radio-opaque marker. This radiopaque strip allows the item to be identified easily under X-ray imaging if an instrument count discrepancy arises post-operatively. - **Laparotomy sponges**: Large, highly absorbent cotton pads utilized in major abdominal or thoracic surgeries to pack away bowels and control deep pooling of blood. - **Gauze swabs**: Smaller, folded square sponges deployed for superficial wound absorption, blunt dissection, and applying localized pressure. - **Tonsil sponges**: Tiny, cord-attached cotton balls designated for restricted spaces like the oropharynx to prevent accidental aspiration during ear, nose, and throat procedures. - **Pledgets and neuro patties**: Specialized, diminutive felt or cotton pieces moistened before application on fragile neural or vascular tissue structures.

Legal Responsibilities and Accountability in Theatre

Surgical count procedures carry profound professional, ethical, and legal weight. Leaving a foreign item inside a patient constitutes a preventable medical error and a direct breach of the duty of care. In Nigerian courts, a retained surgical item (RSI) is widely viewed as res ipsa loquitur—the thing speaks for itself—making defense challenging if counts were omitted or improperly recorded. Both the scrub nurse and the circulating nurse share joint accountability for maintaining accurate tallies from skin incision to skin closure. Institutional policies mandate that all counts must be vocalized clearly and documented contemporaneously in the theatre register. Failure to adhere to established counting protocols can lead to malpractice litigation, loss of operating theater privileges, and disciplinary action by the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria.

Roles of the Scrub Nurse and Circulating Nurse

Teamwork between the scrub nurse and the circulating nurse forms the bedrock of safe surgical counting. Their duties are distinct yet complementary, designed to create a fail-safe dual-checking mechanism. - **Scrub nurse**: Manages the sterile field, tracks items currently on the operative table, handles items directly passed to the surgeon, and keeps discarded bloody swabs organized in a designated holding area. - **Circulating nurse**: Operates outside the sterile boundary, records every number called out on the theatre white board or safety checklist, manages discarded swabs in impermeable bags, and counts extra items introduced mid-procedure. Communication must be unambiguous. When counts are performed, both nurses must visualize the items together, ensuring numbers are spoken aloud rather than mumbled or assumed.

Standard Protocols for Counting Procedures

Every soft good entering the sterile field must undergo a rigorous, standardized counting sequence. Skipping any phase of this protocol invites catastrophic error. 1. **Initial count**: Performed before the operation begins, while all items are still outside the patient, establishing a baseline inventory. 2. **First closing count**: Initiated when the surgeon begins to close a cavity, such as the peritoneum or fascia. 3. **Second closing count**: Carried out when subcutaneous tissue layers are being approximated. 4. **Final skin closure count**: Executed as the skin edges are being sutured or stapled shut. If a cavity is added mid-procedure, such as opening the stomach or bladder, separate counts must be done for instruments and swabs entering those hollow organs.

Managing Count Discrepancies and Missing Items

A count discrepancy occurs when the number of swabs recovered at closure does not match the baseline inventory. Immediate action is required. The scrub nurse must announce the discrepancy to the lead surgeon at once, halting all wound closure. Both nurses recount all items on the sterile field, in the kick buckets, and inside disposal bags. The surgeon inspects the operative field thoroughly, running fingers through gutters, pelvis, and subdiaphragmatic spaces. If the missing item remains unaccounted for, the circulating nurse must arrange for an intraoperative X-ray machine to be brought into the theatre. Under no circumstances should the surgical team rush or falsely sign off on a discordant count.

Strategies for Preventing Retained Surgical Items

Preventing retained surgical items relies on strict adherence to organizational safety cultures and standardized routines. Operating room staff must minimize distractions during count sequences; radio playing and unnecessary chatter should be paused during critical counts. Standardized sorting practices ensure that used swabs are never balled up or piled randomly, but rather kept visible in graded quantities, such as packs of five. Adopting barcoding or electronic sponge-tracking systems can further reduce human error in high-volume trauma or obstetric centers where rapid blood loss complicates counting. Ultimately, vigilance, mutual accountability, and respect for protocol protect patients from avoidable harm.
