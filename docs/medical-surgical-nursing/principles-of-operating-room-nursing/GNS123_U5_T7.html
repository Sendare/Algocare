Swab Counting

Introduction and Definition of Surgical Swabs

Surgical swabs, often called surgical sponges, are essential textile items used inside the operating theater. Their primary jobs are soaking up blood and body fluids, clearing away tissue debris, and gently holding back organs to keep the surgical field clear for the surgeon. These items come in different shapes and sizes depending on the type of operation taking place. Because they go inside sterile body cavities, every single swab must be accounted for meticulously before the surgical wound is closed. Leaving even one behind leads to severe, life-threatening complications for the patient.

Types of Surgical Swabs and Sponges

Different surgical procedures require distinct types of swabs. Knowing them helps the nursing team count accurately and prevent mix-ups.

  • Lap sponges (Laparotomy packs): Large, highly absorbent cotton pads used in major abdominal or chest surgeries to retract organs and soak up large amounts of blood.
  • Gauze swabs (Swabs / Swab-gazes): Smaller square pieces of gauze used for light wiping and minor bleeding control in superficial wounds.
  • Tonsil sponges: Small, rounded cotton balls attached to strings, used specifically in throat, nose, or mouth surgeries.
  • Neurological patties (Patties / Booties): Tiny, delicate cotton strips used to protect sensitive brain or spinal cord tissues.

Roles and Responsibilities of the Scrub and Circulating Nurses

Swab counting is a team effort requiring clear communication between the scrub nurse and the circulating nurse. They work together using a dual-check system where neither nurse counts alone.

  • Scrub Nurse: Manages the sterile field, handles swabs directly, keeps track of items currently in use, and sorts used swabs systematically.
  • Circulating Nurse: Manages the unsterile environment, records every count on the white board or safety sheet as it happens, and inspects discarded swabs.

Both nurses must voice their numbers clearly out loud so the entire surgical team hears the final tally.

Standard Protocols for Swab Counting Procedures

A standard surgical procedure requires counts at specific, mandatory intervals. Skipping any of these checkpoints puts the patient at immediate risk.

  1. Initial count: Performed before the surgery starts, when items are unsealed, to establish a baseline number.
  2. First addition count: Conducted whenever new packs of swabs are added to the sterile field mid-surgery.
  3. Closing count (First closure): Performed when the surgeon begins closing deep body cavities or peritoneal layers.
  4. Final count: Conducted as the skin and superficial tissue layers are being closed.

Handling Discrepancies and Missing Swabs

If a count does not match, panic is not an option, but immediate action is mandatory. The scrub and circulating nurses must inform the surgeon immediately before wound closure proceeds further. The entire theater team stops and conducts a systematic search of the wound, the floor, the linen laundry hampers, and the trash bins. If the missing item still cannot be located, an urgent X-ray is performed inside the theater using a portable machine to check the patient's body for the radiopaque strip embedded in modern surgical sponges.

Legal and Ethical Implications in Nigerian Practice

In Nigerian healthcare settings, failing to account for surgical items can lead to severe legal and professional consequences. A retained surgical item is legally classified as negligence. Nurses and surgeons can face malpractice lawsuits, suspension of their practicing license by the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN), and loss of public trust. Maintaining rigorous count records protects the patient from harm and shields the healthcare professionals from legal liability. Documentation in the patient's chart must be clear, honest, and signed by those who performed the counts.

Prevention of Retained Surgical Items (RSIs)

Preventing retained surgical items relies on strict discipline and adherence to safety checklists. Hospitals must enforce standardized counting routines without taking shortcuts, even during emergency surgeries where time is short. Team communication must remain open, allowing any junior nurse to speak up if a count is unverified. Avoiding distractions in the operating room during count times is equally vital. By treating every count as a critical safety barrier, the nursing team ensures optimal patient outcomes.
