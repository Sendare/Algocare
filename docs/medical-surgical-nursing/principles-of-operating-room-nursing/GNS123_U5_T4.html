Operating Room Protocols

Introduction to the Operating Room Environment

The operating room (OR) environment is a highly specialized, controlled, and restricted area within a healthcare facility designed specifically for surgical interventions. As student nurses preparing for clinical rotations and professional practice in Nigeria, understanding this environment is paramount for patient safety. The primary goal of the OR ecosystem is to minimize microbial contamination, prevent healthcare-associated infections, and provide a seamless, efficient workflow for the multidisciplinary surgical team. The OR layout is structurally organized into distinct zones of increasing cleanliness to restrict access and control air quality. Environmental parameters such as temperature, humidity, and positive pressure ventilation are meticulously regulated to reduce airborne pathogens. Positive pressure ensures that when doors are opened, air flows outward from the clean core to less clean areas, preventing contaminated air from entering. Additionally, lighting, electrical safety, and specialized medical gas supply systems are integral components that must be regularly inspected and maintained. Every member of the surgical team—including surgeons, anaesthetists, perioperative nurses, and surgical technicians—must strictly adhere to these environmental protocols. Failure to respect these boundaries compromises the sterile field and directly endangers the surgical patient's life. Navigating this high-stress environment requires situational awareness, clear communication, and unwavering discipline from all nursing personnel involved in perioperative care.

Surgical Zoning and Attire Regulations

Surgical zoning is a critical infection control measure implemented in the operating department to segregate areas based on cleanliness requirements and required attire. Typically, the OR suite is divided into three distinct zones: the unrestricted, semi-restricted, and restricted zones. The unrestricted zone, such as the reception desk and changing rooms, allows street clothes, and personnel do not require special surgical scrubs. The semi-restricted zone includes corridors, storage areas for sterile supplies, and utility rooms; personnel entering this area must wear authorized surgical scrubs, hair covers that completely contain scalp hair, and shoe covers where applicable. The restricted zone encompasses the actual operating suites and sterile core, where surgical procedures take place. In this zone, complete surgical attire including scrubs, caps, masks that cover both nose and mouth, and protective eyewear is strictly mandatory. Attire regulations serve to trap skin squames, hair, and respiratory droplets, which are major vehicles for bacterial shedding. Compliance with these protocols is non-negotiable for all nursing staff and visitors. Wearing surgical attire outside the hospital or wearing jewelry and artificial nails inside the restricted zones violates aseptic protocols and increases the risk of surgical site infections. Proper donning and doffing sequences must be rigorously followed to maintain the integrity of the zoning system.

Aseptic Technique and Sterile Field Maintenance

Aseptic technique encompasses all practices intended to prevent microbial contamination and maintain a sterile field during surgical interventions. For student nurses, mastering this concept is vital because a break in technique can lead to devastating surgical site infections (SSIs) and prolonged patient morbidity. A sterile field is defined as a specified area, considered free of all microorganisms, usually represented by draped surfaces and scrubbed personnel. To maintain this environment, only sterile items and supplies are allowed within the boundaries of the sterile field. When sterile packages are opened, the edges are considered unsterile, and contents must be lifted out rather than dragged across edges. Scrubbed personnel must remain within the sterile area, keep their hands in sight between waist and chest level, and pass back-to-back if they need to change positions. Unscrubbed personnel must never reach over a sterile field and must maintain a safe distance of at least one foot from it to avoid accidental contamination. Fluid containers should be poured carefully without splashing, as moisture can bridge microbes from unsterile surfaces to sterile drapes through a process known as strike-through contamination. If any doubt exists regarding the sterility of an item or surface, it must be considered contaminated and discarded immediately. Vigilance and uncompromising honesty in reporting accidental contamination are hallmark traits of an ethical professional nurse.

Surgical Scrubbing, Gowning, and Gloving

Surgical scrubbing, gowning, and gloving are foundational procedural skills designed to remove transient microorganisms and significantly reduce resident flora on the hands and arms of the surgical team. The surgical scrub is performed using an antiseptic agent and running water, following a strict anatomical timed or stroke-based method from the fingertips up to the elbows, always keeping the hands higher than the elbows to prevent contaminated water from running back onto cleaned areas. Once scrubbed, the nurse enters the OR and carefully dries their hands and arms using a sterile towel, moving from clean (fingers) to dirty (elbows) without returning to a previously dried area. Gowning follows immediately; the scrub nurse picks up the sterile gown by the neckline, steps back, allows it to unfold without touching unsterile surfaces, and slides their arms into the sleeves while a circulating nurse ties the back securely. Gloving is then performed using either the closed-gloving or open-gloving technique, with closed-gloving being preferred in the OR because the bare hands never touch the outside of the sterile gloves. Throughout this entire sequence, meticulous attention must be paid to prevent cuffs from rolling down and gloves from touching non-sterile ambient surfaces. Any breach in glove integrity, such as a puncture or tear, requires immediate replacement using established aseptic replacement protocols to protect both the patient and the nurse.

Patient Safety and Surgical Safety Checklists

Patient safety in the operating room relies heavily on structured communication, systematic verification processes, and universal safety checklists. The World Health Organization (WHO) Surgical Safety Checklist is a globally recognized tool implemented to reduce surgical errors, complications, and mortality. The checklist is divided into three distinct phases: Sign In (before induction of anaesthesia), Time Out (before skin incision), and Sign Out (before the patient leaves the operating room). During the 'Sign In', the circulating nurse and anaesthetist verify patient identity, surgical site, procedure, and consent, alongside checking allergy history and airway risks. The 'Time Out' is a mandatory pause where all team members—surgeon, anaesthetist, and nurses—stop all activities to verbally confirm the patient's identity, exact site, procedure, and specific critical concerns, such as antibiotic prophylaxis administration and expected blood loss. The 'Sign Out' occurs near the end of the procedure, involving a verbal confirmation of the completed procedure name, correct count of instruments, needles, and sponges, labeling of any specimens, and discussion of key recovery concerns. Nursing students must actively participate in these briefings, advocating for the patient and ensuring that no step is bypassed, as open communication bridges hierarchical divides and prevents catastrophic medical errors.

Intraoperative Positioning and Skin Preparation

Intraoperative positioning and surgical skin preparation are vital preparatory steps executed after anesthesia induction to optimize surgical access while safeguarding the patient's physiological integrity. Common surgical positions include supine, prone, lithotomy, Trendelenburg, and lateral decubitus, each carrying specific physiological challenges. Improper positioning can cause severe complications, such as peripheral nerve damage, pressure ulcers, compromised respiratory excursion, and venous stasis leading to deep vein thrombosis. Perioperative nurses must utilize adequate padding for bony prominences, ensure joints are never hyperextended, and maintain neutral spinal alignment. Once the patient is safely positioned, surgical skin preparation (prep) is performed to eliminate resident and transient skin flora, minimizing the risk of surgical site infections. The skin prep is conducted using approved antiseptic solutions (such as chlorhexidine gluconate or povidone-iodine) applied in a concentric circle starting from the planned incision site and moving outward toward the periphery. Once the periphery is reached, the sponge is discarded to prevent bringing contaminants back to the clean incision zone. Draping follows immediately after skin prep dries, isolating the operative site with sterile drapes while leaving only the surgical window exposed. Nursing diligence during positioning and prep directly prevents long-term postoperative musculoskeletal and integumentary morbidities.

Instrument, Sponge, and Needle Accountability

Instrument, sponge, and needle accountability is a critical safety responsibility shared between the scrub nurse and the circulating nurse during every surgical procedure. Retained surgical items (RSIs)—such as surgical sponges, gauze, clamps, and needles left inside a patient's body cavity—constitute a preventable medical error that causes severe physical harm, sepsis, legal liabilities, and psychological trauma. To prevent this, comprehensive counts must be performed according to strict institutional policies, typically occurring at baseline (before surgery begins), when closure of a cavity begins, when skin closure starts, and during final skin closure. All items must be counted audibly and concurrently by both the scrub nurse and circulating nurse. Sponges must be separated and counted individually; they should also possess radiopaque markers to allow identification via X-ray if a count discrepancy occurs. If any count is incorrect, the surgical team must be immediately notified, and a systematic search of the wound, instrument table, and surrounding floor must be conducted before the patient is closed. Student nurses must understand that bypassing or rushing through count protocols under time pressure is unacceptable. Diligence, meticulous record-keeping, and clear verbal communication are essential skills required of every nurse maintaining patient safety in the operating theater.

Infection Control and Hazard Management in the OR

Infection control and hazard management within the operating room encompass protocols designed to protect patients and healthcare workers from biological, chemical, physical, and environmental hazards. Biological hazards include exposure to bloodborne pathogens such as HIV, Hepatitis B, and Hepatitis C through needlestick injuries or splashes of body fluids. To mitigate this, nurses must strictly practice universal precautions, utilize personal protective equipment (PPE) such as face shields, waterproof gowns, and double gloves, and employ safe sharps disposal techniques without recapping needles. Chemical hazards involve exposure to anesthetic gases, toxic sterilants like formaldehyde or glutaraldehyde, and hazardous vapors from bone cement; these require scavenging systems, proper ventilation, and appropriate handling guidelines. Physical hazards include risks from electrosurgical units (diathermy), lasers, ionizing radiation from C-arm fluoroscopy machines, and heavy lifting. For instance, electrosurgical units require the correct placement of a grounding pad on the patient's muscle mass to prevent electrical burns, while radiation safety requires lead aprons and thyroid collars. Hazard management is a continuous duty requiring regular safety audits, proper maintenance of biomedical equipment, and strict adherence to hospital waste segregation protocols (such as using color-coded bins for infectious and sharp waste). Student nurses must remain vigilant, prioritize self-protection, and report occupational hazards immediately.
