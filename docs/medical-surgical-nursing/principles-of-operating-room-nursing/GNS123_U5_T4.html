Operating Room Protocols

Introduction to the Operating Room Environment

The operating room is a highly controlled, high-stress clinical area designed specifically for invasive surgical procedures. Maintaining strict environmental standards is essential to protect vulnerable surgical patients from hospital-acquired infections. Air handling systems in the operating theatre use positive pressure ventilation to prevent unfiltered outside air from rushing in when doors open. Air is continuously filtered through high-efficiency particulate air filters, which trap microscopic dust and airborne microorganisms. Temperature and humidity are also strictly regulated to discourage bacterial growth and to keep surgical patients from getting too cold on the operating table. Strict traffic control limits entry only to authorized personnel who wear proper hospital-approved scrubs.

Surgical Zoning and Attire Regulations

The surgical suite is divided into distinct zones to gradually decrease the risk of contamination as staff move closer to the actual operating room.

  • Unrestricted Zone: This area includes dressing rooms and the main reception desk where street clothes are still permitted and patients enter the facility.
  • Semi-restricted Zone: Surrounds the core areas and requires all personnel to wear clean surgical attire, head covers that completely tuck in hair, and scrub suits.
  • Restricted Zone: Includes the actual operating rooms and sterile core storage areas. Here, masks must be worn over both the nose and mouth at all times in addition to standard scrubs and headwear.

Aseptic Technique and Sterile Field Maintenance

Asepsis forms the foundation of safe surgical nursing practice. A sterile field is created specifically to keep microbes away from the open surgical wound. Once a sterile drape is placed, only items that are certified sterile may touch that surface.

  • Sterile personnel must pass each other face-to-face or back-to-back to prevent accidental contamination of sterile gowns.
  • Unsterile personnel must never reach across a sterile field and should always maintain a safe distance of at least one foot from it.
  • Tables are considered sterile only at the level of the tabletop; anything hanging below that edge is contaminated.
  • If there is any doubt about whether an item is sterile, nurses must consider it contaminated and discard it immediately.

Surgical Scrubbing, Gowning, and Gloving

Before touching sterile items, members of the surgical team must perform a thorough surgical scrub. Unlike routine handwashing, a surgical scrub removes transient flora and reduces resident skin flora for the duration of the operation using an antimicrobial soap and a specialized sponge. Hands and arms are washed thoroughly, always keeping the hands higher than the elbows so dirty water drains down toward the arms, never back toward clean hands. After scrubbing, personnel enter the room and don sterile gowns and gloves using closed-glove techniques. Gowns are considered sterile only from the chest to the level of the sterile field, and the sleeves from two inches above the elbow to the cuffs.

Patient Safety and Surgical Safety Checklists

Surgical safety checklists save lives by creating mandatory pause points for the entire multidisciplinary team. These checks follow the World Health Organization framework, divided into three crucial phases:

  1. Sign In: Performed before anesthesia induction. The nurse, anesthetist, and patient verify the patient's identity, the correct surgical site, the procedure, and known allergies.
  2. Time Out: Performed immediately before the skin incision. All team members stop what they are doing to introduce themselves by name and role, and verbally confirm the patient, site, and procedure one last time.
  3. Sign Out: Performed before the patient leaves the operating room. The scrub nurse or circulator confirms sponge and instrument counts, specimen labeling, and recovery priorities.

Intraoperative Positioning and Skin Preparation

Proper patient positioning protects nerves, joints, and skin integrity while giving the surgeon optimal access to the surgical site. Common positions include supine, prone, lithotomy, and lateral, each carrying unique risks of pressure injuries and nerve damage. Nurses must pad bony prominences carefully and secure limbs without causing restricted circulation. Once positioned, the skin prep is performed to reduce the microbial count on the patient's skin. Prep solutions such as chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine are applied in a concentric circle starting directly over the planned incision site and moving outward toward contaminated peripheral areas, never returning a used sponge to the center.

Instrument, Sponge, and Needle Accountability

Retained surgical items represent a serious, preventable medical error. Accountability relies on rigorous, standardized counting protocols performed jointly by the scrub nurse and the circulating nurse. Counts are conducted at baseline before the procedure begins, again when closure of a cavity begins, and finally during skin closure. All sponges, sharps, and instruments must be accounted for before the surgical wound is permanently closed. If any count is discrepant, the surgical team must immediately halt closure, search the wound, and inspect the surrounding drapes and trash receptacles until every item is located.

Infection Control and Hazard Management in the OR

Operating rooms present multiple physical and biological hazards that require constant vigilance. Universal precautions and standard precautions protect staff from bloodborne pathogens like HIV and Hepatitis B through the safe handling of sharps and the use of protective eye gear. Electrosurgical units create surgical smoke plumes containing toxic chemicals and viable viral particles, necessitating the use of smoke evacuation systems. Chemical disinfectants used on surfaces require specific contact times to be effective. Finally, proper disposal of biohazardous waste in red bags and sharps in puncture-resistant containers prevents accidental needlestick injuries for housekeeping and nursing staff alike.
