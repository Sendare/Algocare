Pathophysiology of Male Reproductive Disorders

Normal Anatomy and Hormonal Regulation

The male reproductive system depends on a finely tuned hormonal loop known as the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis. The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which signals the anterior pituitary gland to secrete luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH). LH stimulates the Leydig cells in the testes to produce testosterone, while FSH acts on the Sertoli cells to support spermatogenesis. Any disruption along this chemical pathway can impair both hormone balance and sperm production.

Testicular Inflammation and Vascular Disorders

  • Orchitis: An acute inflammation of the testes, frequently caused by viral infections like mumps or bacterial infections spreading from the epididymis. It presents with severe pain, swelling, and potential atrophy.
  • Testicular Torsion: A surgical emergency caused by the twisting of the spermatic cord, which cuts off the blood supply to the testicle. Immediate detorsion is required within hours to prevent permanent tissue necrosis.
  • Varicocele: An abnormal dilation of the pampiniform venous plexus within the scrotum, which raises scrotal temperature and frequently contributes to male infertility.

Prostate Disorders: BPH and Malignancy

The prostate gland undergoes distinct pathological changes as men age. Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) involves the non-malignant overgrowth of epithelial and stromal cells in the transition zone of the gland. This enlargement compresses the urethra, causing lower urinary tract symptoms such as weak urinary stream, hesitancy, and nocturia. Conversely, prostate carcinoma typically develops in the peripheral zone of the gland. Early prostate cancer is usually asymptomatic, which makes routine screening with prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood tests and digital rectal exams vital for early detection before metastasis to the bones occurs.

Testicular Malignancy and Cryptorchidism

Testicular cancer is one of the most common solid malignancies in young adult males between the ages of 15 and 35. The majority of these tumors originate from germ cells and are classified as either seminomas or non-seminomas. A major risk factor for developing testicular cancer is cryptorchidism, a condition where one or both testes fail to descend properly into the scrotum during fetal development. The higher ambient temperature of the abdominal cavity damages the germinal epithelium over time, increasing the risk of malignant transformation even if the testicle is later brought down surgically.

Mechanisms of Male Infertility

Male infertility stems from pre-testicular, testicular, or post-testicular causes. Pre-testicular factors involve endocrine disorders like hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, where low GnRH or gonadotropins fail to stimulate testosterone production. Testicular factors include genetic abnormalities such as Klinefelter syndrome (XXY), direct trauma, chemotherapy, or infections that destroy seminiferous tubules. Post-testicular factors involve physical obstructions in the reproductive tract, such as congenital absence of the vas deferens or scarring from sexually transmitted infections like chlamydia or gonorrhea, which block sperm transport.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Clinical Manifestations

Diagnosing male reproductive disorders requires a careful clinical history, physical examination, and targeted laboratory and imaging studies. The primary diagnostic tools include:

  • Semen Analysis: Evaluates sperm count, motility, and morphology to assess fertility potential.
  • Hormone Panel: Measures serum testosterone, LH, FSH, and prolactin to differentiate between primary and secondary hypogonadism.
  • Scrotal Ultrasound: Uses sound waves to visualize testicular masses, hydroceles, varicoceles, and blood flow during torsion.
  • PSA Testing and Biopsy: Used alongside digital rectal exams to screen for and confirm prostate malignancy.
