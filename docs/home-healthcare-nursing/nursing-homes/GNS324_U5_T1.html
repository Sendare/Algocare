Nursing Homes

Introduction to Nursing Homes in Nigeria

Traditionally, elderly care in Nigeria has been family-centered. Extended family networks traditionally absorbed and cared for aging relatives as a cultural norm of respect and reciprocity. However, rapid urbanization, economic shifts, and the migration of younger family members to urban centers or abroad have strained these traditional support systems. This shift has created an emerging demand for formal long-term care facilities, commonly known as nursing homes or residential care homes.

Nursing homes in Nigeria occupy a unique socio-cultural space. Many families face deep-seated societal stigma when placing an older relative in a facility, often viewing it as a sign of abandonment or failure in filial duty. Nurses working in these settings must navigate this emotional landscape, reassuring families while providing compassionate, professional care. Facilities range from modest private homes converted into care centers to more structured, purpose-built residential care communities catering to middle- and upper-income retirees.

The Role of the Nurse in Nursing Home Settings

The nurse in a long-term care facility functions as a direct caregiver, care coordinator, patient advocate, and educator. Unlike acute hospital settings where the goal is rapid stabilization and discharge, the nursing home is the resident's permanent home. Consequently, care focuses on maintaining quality of life, preserving residual functional abilities, and providing dignified palliative support.

Nurses collaborate closely with care assistants, visiting physicians, physiotherapists, and families. A significant part of the daily routine involves supervising support staff who perform most of the hands-on personal care. The nurse must ensure that basic care standards are consistently met, signs of physical or cognitive decline are detected early, and emotional needs are addressed with patience and empathy.

Comprehensive Resident Assessment and Care Planning

Admitting a new resident requires a systematic, multi-dimensional assessment. This evaluation covers physical health, functional status, cognitive ability, psychological well-being, nutritional state, and social history. The goal is to establish a baseline and identify individual risks, such as the potential for falls, pressure injuries, or malnutrition.

Following the assessment, an individualized care plan is developed through an interdisciplinary team conference. This document outlines specific, measurable goals for the resident's care and daily routines. The care plan is not a static document; it must be regularly reviewed and updated whenever the resident experiences a significant change in health status or functional ability.

Promoting Activities of Daily Living and Mobility

  • Bathing and Hygiene: Assistance should encourage maximum self-care to preserve dignity and muscle tone.
  • Feeding and Hydration: Monitor intake, provide adaptive utensils, and position residents upright to prevent aspiration.
  • Mobility Maintenance: Regular assisted walking, passive range-of-motion exercises, and timely repositioning prevent contractures and stiffness.
  • Continence Management: Establish regular toileting schedules rather than relying solely on absorbent pads.

Medication Management in Long-Term Care

Medication administration in older adults is complex due to age-related changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Reduced kidney and liver function alters drug clearance, increasing the risk of toxicity. Polypharmacy—taking multiple medications simultaneously—is common and raises the likelihood of adverse drug interactions and falls.

Nurses must rigorously verify the five rights of medication administration, monitor for unexpected side effects, and collaborate with physicians to periodically review and simplify medication regimens. Crushing pills or opening capsules should only be done after confirming safety with a pharmacist, as doing so can alter the pharmacologic action of sustained-release drugs.

Prevention and Management of Geriatric Complications

Residents in long-term care are highly vulnerable to specific syndromes collectively known as geriatric giants: immobility, instability, incontinence, and impaired intellect (dementia). In addition, pressure injuries and healthcare-associated infections pose constant threats.

Nursing interventions focus on proactive prevention. Regular turning schedules, adequate hydration, skin barrier creams, and early mobilization reduce pressure injury risks. Fall prevention involves clearing walkways, ensuring proper lighting, and assessing assistive device usage. Recognizing subtle changes in behavior or vital signs often serves as the earliest warning sign of infection in an older adult.

Ethical, Legal, and Cultural Considerations in Nigerian Nursing Homes

Practicing in a Nigerian nursing home requires a delicate balance of professional ethics, legal standards, and cultural sensitivity. Nurses must respect resident autonomy and obtain informed consent for care procedures whenever possible. When cognitive impairment limits decision-making, designated family surrogate decision-makers are consulted.

Culturally, respect for elders is paramount. Nurses must maintain respectful communication styles, addressing older adults with appropriate titles of respect rather than first names. Furthermore, spiritual and religious practices are deeply important to many Nigerian seniors; facilitating access to prayer, worship services, or spiritual leaders is an essential component of holistic, culturally competent care.
