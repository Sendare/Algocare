Nursing Homes

Introduction and Evolution of Long-Term Care in Nigeria

Traditionally, eldercare in Nigeria has been a deeply familial responsibility rooted in the extended family system, where older adults reside with their children or relatives. However, rapid urbanization, changing economic dynamics, and the migration of younger generations to urban centers or abroad have strained this traditional safety net. Consequently, structured nursing homes and residential eldercare facilities are gradually emerging, particularly in major cities like Lagos, Ibadan, and Abuja.

Setting up and operating these facilities requires balancing modern institutional care with cultural expectations. Many families still view placing an aging relative in a facility as a breach of cultural duty, which places an extra communication burden on healthcare staff. Facilities range from basic assisted living houses to specialized skilled nursing centers designed for individuals requiring 24-hour medical supervision, chronic disease management, or palliative care.

The Role and Scope of the Nurse in Residential Facilities

Nurses in nursing homes function as clinical coordinators, direct care providers, educators, and resident advocates. Unlike acute hospital wards where patient turnover is rapid, long-term care emphasizes stability, functional maintenance, and quality of life over curative interventions.

  • Clinical Monitoring: Detecting subtle baseline changes in chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, or osteoarthritis before they manifest as acute emergencies.
  • Supervision of Caregivers: Directing and delegating tasks to auxiliary nurses, care assistants, and support staff while maintaining accountability for clinical outcomes.
  • Psychosocial Support: Alleviating loneliness, managing depression, and fostering a sense of community among residents who may feel isolated from their families.
  • Liaison Role: Communicating changes in health status to visiting physicians, therapists, and resident family members.

Comprehensive Resident Assessment and Individualized Care Planning

Holistic care begins the moment a resident is admitted. Comprehensive assessment goes beyond recording vital signs; it incorporates functional capacity, cognitive status, nutritional risk, skin integrity, and psychosocial history.

Care plans must be dynamic documents developed by an interdisciplinary team comprising nurses, physiotherapists, nutritionists, and where possible, social workers. In resource-constrained settings where specialized multidisciplinary teams are rare, the nurse frequently leads the assessment process, identifying priority problems, setting measurable goals, and updating interventions regularly. Involving the resident and their family in setting these goals fosters compliance and respects personal dignity.

Promoting Activities of Daily Living, Mobility, and Rehabilitation

Maintaining functional independence is a central objective in eldercare. When older adults stop moving or performing tasks for themselves, physical decline accelerates rapidly through disuse syndrome.

Nurses encourage residents to perform as much of their self-care as possible—such as feeding, grooming, and light walking—even if it takes more time. Assistive devices like walkers, canes, and grab bars should be readily available and properly fitted. Collaborating with rehabilitation professionals ensures that exercise regimens are safe for individuals with cardiac or joint limitations. Preserving mobility not only prevents contractures and muscle atrophy but also protects psychological well-being by fostering personal autonomy.

Medication Management and Pharmacokinetics in Aging

Administering medications to older adults requires specialized knowledge due to age-related physiological changes in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. Reduced hepatic blood flow, lower glomerular filtration rates, and altered body fat distribution mean drugs remain in the system longer and can accumulate to toxic levels.

Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—is a significant hazard, increasing the risk of adverse drug reactions, confusion, and falls. Nurses play a frontline role by conducting regular medication reviews, watching for atypical signs of drug toxicity (such as sudden delirium instead of classic side effects), and educating residents and caregivers on correct administration schedules. Crushing pills or opening capsules should only be done after consulting a pharmacist, as doing so can destroy sustained-release mechanisms.

Prevention and Management of Common Geriatric Complications

Residents in long-term care are vulnerable to specific secondary complications that can rapidly diminish their quality of life or prove fatal if neglected. Nurses must implement proactive prevention protocols for these high-risk areas.

  • Pressure Injuries: Caused by unrelieved pressure over bony prominences. Prevented through repositioning schedules every two hours, rigorous skin assessments, and maintaining adequate nutrition and hydration.
  • Falls: Precipitated by muscle weakness, poor lighting, unstable footwear, or orthostatic hypotension. Mitigation involves environmental audits, non-slip flooring, and targeted balance exercises.
  • Incontinence: Often multifactorial, stemming from mobility issues, urinary tract infections, or cognitive impairment. Managed through scheduled voiding regimens and skin protection rather than automatic use of indwelling catheters, which carry high infection risks.

Ethical, Legal, and Cultural Dimensions in Nigerian Nursing Homes

Providing care within Nigerian residential facilities requires navigating complex intersections of modern bioethics, prevailing legal frameworks, and deep-seated cultural norms. Issues surrounding autonomy, informed consent, and end-of-life decisions often generate friction between westernized institutional policies and traditional communal values.

While bioethics emphasizes individual patient autonomy, Nigerian family structures frequently operate on collective decision-making where adult children make medical choices for their aging parents. Nurses must skillfully mediate these dynamics, respecting family involvement while safeguarding the expressed wishes of competent residents. Furthermore, navigating end-of-life care requires sensitivity to spiritual and religious beliefs common in Nigeria, ensuring that palliative measures respect both clinical standards and cultural dignity.
