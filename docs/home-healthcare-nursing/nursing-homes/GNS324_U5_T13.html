Geriatric Homes

Introduction to Geriatric Homes and Residential Care

Geriatric homes provide specialized residential care for older adults who can no longer live completely independently. These facilities bridge the gap between acute hospital care and independent living, offering structured support tailored to the unique physical, emotional, and social needs of aging individuals. In West Africa, traditional family-based care remains the cultural bedrock; however, rapid urbanization, changing family structures, and the demands of modern employment have increased the need for formal residential facilities. Nurses working in these environments must understand that moving into a geriatric home is a major life transition. Residents often experience feelings of loss of autonomy, separation from family, and anxiety about their new surroundings. A successful transition requires a welcoming environment, active family involvement where possible, and staff who prioritize person-centered care over rigid institutional routines.

The Role of the Home Healthcare Nurse in Geriatric Facilities

The nurse in a geriatric facility wears many hats: caregiver, advocate, educator, and care coordinator. Unlike acute care settings where interventions are short and episodic, residential care involves building long-term relationships with residents and their families. Nurses monitor chronic illnesses, administer medications safely, manage wounds, and supervise healthcare assistants and support staff. A critical nursing function is advocacy—ensuring that vulnerable residents retain their dignity, rights, and a voice in their daily care plans. Effective communication is essential, requiring patience, active listening, and an awareness of sensory deficits like hearing loss or visual impairment common in older age.

Comprehensive Health Assessment of the Older Adult

Assessing an older adult requires a holistic approach known as a Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA). This evaluation goes beyond simple vital signs to examine physical, functional, psychological, and social well-being.

  • Functional Status: Evaluation of Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) such as bathing, dressing, and eating, alongside Instrumental ADLs like managing money and housekeeping.
  • Cognitive and Mental Health: Screening for common conditions like dementia, delirium, and depression using validated tools such as the Mini-Mental State Examination.
  • Environmental and Social Factors: Reviewing living spaces for safety hazards and evaluating social support systems.

A common clinical mistake is attributing new confusion or behavioral changes simply to normal aging or dementia, when it may actually signal an acute delirium caused by a urinary tract infection or dehydration.

Promoting Mobility and Preventing Falls in Geriatric Homes

Falls are a leading cause of morbidity, loss of independence, and mortality among older adults. In a residential setting, prevention requires identifying individual risk factors and maintaining environmental safety. Factors contributing to falls include muscle weakness, balance deficits, visual impairment, poor footwear, and side effects from medications like sedatives or antihypertensives.

  1. Environmental Adjustments: Ensure adequate lighting in hallways and bathrooms, keep pathways clear of clutter, and install secure grab bars.
  2. Mobility Encouragement: Promote regular, supervised physical activity to maintain muscle mass and joint flexibility rather than enforcing strict bed rest out of fear.
  3. Assistive Devices: Ensure residents correctly use walkers, canes, or eyeglasses, and check that devices are well-maintained.

Managing Chronic Conditions and Polypharmacy

Most residents in geriatric homes live with multiple chronic illnesses such as hypertension, diabetes, arthritis, and heart failure. Managing these conditions requires careful coordination to prevent disease progression while minimizing the burden of treatment. Polypharmacy—the concurrent use of multiple medications—is a major hazard in older adults due to age-related changes in drug metabolism and excretion. Kidneys and livers process drugs more slowly in old age, increasing the risk of adverse drug reactions, drug interactions, and toxicity. Nurses must routinely review medication charts with the medical team, watch for subtle signs of drug toxicity (such as confusion, lethargy, or loss of appetite), and educate residents about taking their medications correctly.

Nutrition, Hydration, and Dietary Management for Residents

Proper nutrition and hydration are vital for maintaining skin integrity, immune function, and overall strength in older adults. However, physiological changes such as reduced sense of taste and smell, poor dentition, dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), and depression often lead to malnutrition and dehydration. Older adults also have a blunted thirst response, meaning they frequently do not feel thirsty even when their bodies need water. Nurses should monitor meal intake, collaborate with dietitians to provide texture-modified diets when needed, and encourage fluid intake throughout the day. Meals should be served in a pleasant, social environment to stimulate appetite and prevent feelings of isolation.

Palliative Care and End-of-Life Considerations

Palliative care in geriatric homes focuses on improving the quality of life for residents facing serious, life-limiting illnesses. The goal shifts from curative treatment to comfort, pain management, symptom control, and emotional and spiritual support. Nurses play a central role in recognizing the dying phase, managing distressing symptoms like breathlessness or pain, and supporting grieving family members. Advance care planning—discussing and documenting the resident's wishes regarding resuscitation, artificial feeding, and hospitalization before a crisis occurs—is essential to honor the resident's autonomy and dignity at the end of life.

Ethical, Legal, and Quality Improvement Standards in Nigeria

Operating and working within geriatric homes requires strict adherence to ethical principles and professional standards. Nurses must respect resident autonomy, maintain confidentiality, and protect vulnerable individuals from physical, emotional, or financial abuse. In Nigeria, regulatory frameworks and guidelines from bodies like the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Nigeria (NMCN) guide professional practice and accountability. Quality improvement involves regular audits of care practices, infection control measures, staff training, and incident reporting to ensure residents receive safe, compassionate, and evidence-based care.
