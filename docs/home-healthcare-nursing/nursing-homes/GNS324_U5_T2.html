Long-Term Care

Introduction and Populations Served in Long-Term Care

Long-term care (LTC) encompasses a wide range of health, personal, and social services provided over an extended period to individuals who have lost independent self-care abilities. Unlike acute care, which focuses on short-term healing and stabilization, LTC centers on maintaining quality of life, preserving remaining function, and managing chronic conditions safely.

The population residing in these facilities is diverse and has unique care needs:

  • Older adults: The largest demographic group, frequently dealing with multiple chronic illnesses, frailty, and age-related sensory declines.
  • Individuals with physical disabilities: Younger adults who require ongoing assistance due to traumatic injuries, spinal cord damage, or congenital neuromuscular conditions.
  • Persons with cognitive impairments: Residents living with Alzheimer's disease, vascular dementia, or other neurodegenerative disorders needing specialized supervision and behavioral support.
  • Post-acute or transitional care clients: Individuals recovering from complex surgeries, strokes, or severe infections who require extended rehabilitation before returning home.

Goals and Objectives of Long-Term Care

The fundamental mission of long-term care goes far beyond basic medical treatment. The primary goal is to promote the highest possible level of physical, mental, and social well-being for every resident. Nurses help achieve this by maximizing functional independence, delaying further decline, and offering a homelike environment that fosters dignity and personal autonomy. Another critical objective is providing emotional and physical support to residents and their families, especially when navigating chronic, progressive illnesses or end-of-life decisions.

Comprehensive Nursing Assessment and ADLs

Assessing a long-term care resident requires a holistic approach that looks at physical health, emotional status, mental clarity, and social history. Because residents live in the facility, nurses monitor subtle baseline changes rather than just single-moment episodic issues.

A cornerstone of this assessment evaluates Activities of Daily Living (ADLs), which are fundamental self-care tasks:

  • Bathing: Assessing the resident's ability to wash their body safely.
  • Dressing: Determining if they can select and put on appropriate clothes.
  • Toileting: Checking continence control and transfer abilities to the bathroom.
  • Transferring: Evaluating strength and balance when moving from a bed to a chair.
  • Continence: Monitoring bowel and bladder control.
  • Feeding: Observing the physical ability to bring food from a plate to the mouth.

Person-Centered Care Planning

Person-centered care shifts the focus away from rigid institutional routines and places the individual resident at the center of all planning. Nurses collaborate with the resident and their family to identify personal preferences, past life routines, and individual goals. Care plans are tailored living documents that respect how a resident likes to wake up, what foods they prefer, and how they wish to spend their time. When care is personalized, behavioral symptoms in dementia often decrease, and resident satisfaction and engagement significantly improve.

Interprofessional Team Collaboration

No single healthcare provider can manage the complex needs of a long-term care resident alone. Successful care relies heavily on an interprofessional healthcare team working together toward common goals.

  • Registered Nurses (RNs) and Licensed Practical Nurses (LPNs): Coordinate daily care, administer medications, and monitor clinical status.
  • Certified Nursing Assistants (CNAs): Provide direct, hands-on assistance with hygiene, mobility, and daily meals, often noticing subtle health changes first.
  • Physicians and Nurse Practitioners: Diagnose medical conditions, manage chronic disease regimens, and oversee treatment plans.
  • Physical and Occupational Therapists: Help residents regain or maintain mobility, strength, and safe functional movement.
  • Registered Dietitians: Evaluate nutritional needs, manage therapeutic diets, and monitor unintended weight loss.
  • Social Workers: Assist with psychosocial support, family counseling, and discharge or placement planning.

Preventing Common Complications: Falls, Pressure Injuries, and Infections

Residents in long-term care are vulnerable to specific hospital-acquired or facility-acquired complications that can severely impact their health. Nurses play a frontline role in proactive prevention.

  • Falls: Prevented through regular risk screening, environmental adjustments like clearing clutter, proper footwear, and individualized exercise or balance programs.
  • Pressure injuries: Prevented by frequent repositioning schedules, maintaining skin dryness and cleanliness, using pressure-relieving mattress pads, and ensuring adequate nutrition and hydration.
  • Infections: Prevented through strict hand hygiene, proper catheter care, routine vaccination programs, and prompt isolation precautions when symptoms appear.

Ethical, Legal, and Palliative Considerations

Ethical dilemmas frequently arise in long-term care, particularly regarding resident autonomy, informed consent, and life-sustaining treatments. Nurses must advocate for the resident's expressed wishes, documented through advance directives and durable powers of attorney. When residents face advanced, incurable illnesses, palliative and hospice care principles become central. The focus shifts entirely from curative interventions to aggressive comfort management, pain relief, and emotional support, ensuring the resident experiences a peaceful, dignified end of life.
