Ambulation and Use of Wheelchair

Physiological and Psychological Benefits of Early Ambulation

Getting a patient out of bed and moving early in their recovery process is one of the most powerful interventions a nurse can champion. When a patient lies flat for too long, their body systems begin to slow down and decondition rapidly.

  • Cardiovascular system: Regular movement prevents blood from pooling in the lower extremities, significantly reducing the risk of deep vein thrombosis and orthostatic dizziness.
  • Respiratory system: Upright posture allows the lungs to expand fully, preventing atelectasis and the pooling of secretions that lead to pneumonia.
  • Gastrointestinal system: Walking stimulates peristalsis, helping to prevent painful constipation and abdominal distension.
  • Musculoskeletal system: Muscle mass can waste away at an alarming rate during bedrest; gentle ambulation preserves strength and joint mobility.

Beyond the physical healing, moving under one's own power provides a massive psychological boost. Patients often feel helpless in a hospital gown or home bed; taking steps restores a sense of independence and dignity, easing symptoms of anxiety and depression.

Pre-Ambulation Assessment and Patient Readiness

Before you ask a patient to stand or walk, you must perform a thorough safety check. Never assume a patient is ready just because the doctor wrote an order for out-of-bed activity.

Start by checking the patient's vital signs at rest. Look closely at their level of consciousness, neurological status, and ability to follow commands. Assess their muscle strength, particularly in the lower limbs, and ask if they feel dizzy, weak, or in severe pain.

A common mistake is rushing straight from a lying position to a standing position. Always have the patient sit on the edge of the bed with their legs dangling for a few minutes. This allows their circulatory system to adjust and helps prevent orthostatic hypotension—a sudden drop in blood pressure that causes lightheadedness and falls. If the patient feels dizzy while dangling, lay them back down and postpone ambulation.

Assisted Ambulation Techniques and Devices

When a patient needs help walking, the nurse or caregiver acts as a steady physical anchor. Always use a gait belt secured snugly around the patient's waist—never grab them by their bare arms or clothing, which can cause bruising or skin tears. Stand on the patient's weaker side, holding the gait belt with an underhand grip, and stay slightly behind them.

Different assistive devices require specific techniques to ensure stability:

  • Walkers: The patient moves the walker forward a short distance, steps into it with the weaker leg first, and then follows with the stronger leg.
  • Canes: The cane is always held in the hand on the *stronger* side of the body. The cane moves forward first, followed by the weaker leg, and then the stronger leg.
  • Crutches: Require significant upper body strength, and the nurse must check that there is a two-to-three-finger-width space between the top of the crutch and the armpit to prevent nerve damage.

Safe Wheelchair Transfers: Bed, Chair, and Toilet

Transfers are high-risk moments in nursing care where falls frequently happen. Before moving a patient between a bed, a wheelchair, or a toilet, preparation is everything. Lock the wheels of both the bed and the wheelchair securely. Remove or swing away the footrests of the wheelchair so the patient's feet can plant firmly on the floor.

Position the wheelchair at a 45-degree angle to the bed on the patient's stronger side whenever possible. Ask the patient to scoot to the edge of the mat or mattress, lean their trunk forward, and push off with their hands. Use the pivot technique: once standing, the patient pivots on their feet to turn their back toward the target seat, reaches back for the armrests, and lowers themselves down smoothly. Always bend your knees and keep your back straight during the transfer to protect your own spine from injury.

Wheelchair Selection, Fitting, and Navigation

A wheelchair is not a one-size-fits-all piece of equipment. An improperly fitted chair can cause poor posture, severe fatigue, and painful pressure sores. When a patient sits in the chair, their hips should be at a 90-degree angle, with their feet resting flat on the footplates. The seat width should allow about two inches of clearance on either side of the patient's hips to avoid pinching, while the seat depth should leave a two-finger space between the edge of the seat and the back of the knees.

When propelling the chair, teach patients to grip the push rims at the ten o'clock position and push forward smoothly down to two o'clock. Navigating obstacles requires specific techniques: to go up a curb or threshold, tip the wheelchair back slightly on its rear wheels to lift the front casters clear of the bump. When going down a steep ramp or curb, back the wheelchair down slowly so the patient does not pitch forward out of the seat.

Preventing Complications: Falls, Fatigue, and Skin Breakdown

Mobility devices and ambulation programs come with inherent risks that nurses must actively manage. Falls remain the most common hazard; always ensure pathways in the home are free of loose rugs, electrical cords, and clutter. Proper lighting is non-negotiable, especially along the path to the bathroom at night.

Fatigue can sneak up on recovering patients quickly. Monitor for signs of exhaustion, such as shortness of breath, pale skin, or sweating, and build planned rest breaks into every activity. Finally, protect the skin of patients who use wheelchairs for long periods. Sitting puts constant pressure over bony prominences like the ischial tuberosities and the sacrum. Teach the patient to perform weight-shifting push-ups every fifteen to thirty minutes, or tilt the chair back to relieve pressure and keep blood flowing to the skin tissues.
