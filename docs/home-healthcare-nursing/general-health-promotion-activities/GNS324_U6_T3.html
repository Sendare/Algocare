Ambulation and Use of Wheelchair

Physiological and Psychological Benefits of Early Ambulation

Early ambulation is a cornerstone of restorative nursing care. When patients get out of bed soon after surgery or an acute illness, multiple body systems experience immediate positive shifts. Respiratory function improves as upright posture expands the lung bases, promoting better gas exchange and clearing retained secretions. Circulation benefits significantly; skeletal muscle contraction in the legs acts as a natural pump, driving venous return and drastically lowering the risk of deep vein thrombosis. Gastrointestinal motility wakes up, reducing postoperative ileus and constipation. Beyond physical metrics, regaining the ability to walk restores a sense of independence and dignity, combating hospital-induced depression and anxiety.

Pre-Ambulation Assessment and Patient Readiness

Before taking any steps, a thorough patient evaluation prevents catastrophic falls. Start by reviewing the medical chart for weight-bearing restrictions, cardiac stability, and recent lab results such as hemoglobin levels. Next, perform a physical check at the bedside: - **Orthostatic Vital Signs**: Measure blood pressure and pulse while the patient lies down, then after they sit up, checking for a drop that causes dizziness. - **Cognitive Status**: Ensure the patient can follow multi-step commands and understands safety instructions. - **Muscle Strength**: Test bilateral handgrip and lower extremity extension against resistance. - **Balance**: Observe sitting balance at the edge of the bed without back support.

Assisted Ambulation Techniques and Assistive Devices

When a patient needs physical support to walk, the nurse acts as a dynamic stabilizer. Always apply a **gait belt** securely around the patient's waist over clothing—never on bare skin—to provide a firm handle for control. Walk slightly behind and to the weaker side of the patient, grasping the gait belt with an underhand grip. Assistive devices must match the patient's specific functional deficit. Canines, walkers, and crutches alter the base of support and weight distribution. Ensure the device is adjusted to the correct height, usually with the elbow flexed at an angle of roughly 20 to 30 degrees. Remind patients to look ahead, not down at their feet, to maintain proper cervical alignment and balance.

Wheelchair Selection, Sizing, and Safe Transfers

A wheelchair is not a universal vehicle; proper sizing prevents pressure injuries, spinal deformities, and falls. Seat width should allow about two fingers of clearance on each side of the user's hips. Seat depth must leave a three-finger gap between the front edge of the seat and the back of the user's knees to protect popliteal blood vessels and nerves. Transfers require meticulous mechanics. Position the wheelchair at a 45-degree angle to the bed or chair, lock both wheels, and swing the footrests out of the way. Instruct the patient to place their feet firmly on the floor, lean forward from the hips—often called nose over toes—and push off the mattress or armrests using their strongest muscles.

Propelling the Chair and Navigating Environmental Obstacles

Propelling a wheelchair safely requires mastering both flat surfaces and architectural barriers. When moving forward on flat ground, grip the handrims at the ten o'clock position and push forward to the two o'clock position, using long, smooth strokes. Navigating architectural hurdles demands specific techniques: - **Ramps**: Lean the upper body forward when ascending to keep the center of gravity stable. When descending, face forward but control the descent by applying gentle, constant friction to the handrims, or reverse down steep inclines. - **Curbs and Thresholds**: Approach thresholds head-on. To mount a curb, tilt the chair back slightly using the tipping lever to lift the front casters, push forward until the large wheels touch the curb, and roll up smoothly.

Preventing Mobility-Related Complications

Mobility interventions carry inherent risks that demand vigilant nursing oversight. Falls remain the most critical hazard during ambulation and transfers; always keep pathways clear of clutter and ensure non-slip footwear is worn. Fatigue can set in rapidly, especially in deconditioned individuals. Monitor for subtle signs of overexertion such as dyspnea, pallor, or changes in conversational ability. Furthermore, prolonged sitting in a wheelchair places immense pressure on the ischial tuberosities, accelerating tissue breakdown. Establish a strict schedule for weight shifts every fifteen to thirty minutes, encouraging patients to push up on the armrests or lean side to side.
