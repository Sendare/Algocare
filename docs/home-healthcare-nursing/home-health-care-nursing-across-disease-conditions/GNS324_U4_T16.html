Hypertension

Understanding Blood Pressure and Hypertension

Hypertension, commonly called high blood pressure, is a chronic medical condition where the force of blood against the artery walls is consistently too high. Healthcare providers measure this force using two numbers: systolic pressure when the heart beats, and diastolic pressure when the heart rests between beats. A normal reading typically falls below 120/80 mmHg, while a consistent reading of 140/90 mmHg or higher indicates hypertension.

In home care and community nursing, you will encounter patients who feel completely fine despite very high readings. This silent nature of hypertension makes routine screening vital. If left unchecked, the relentless pressure damages delicate blood vessels throughout the body, setting the stage for heart attacks, strokes, and kidney failure.

Causes and Risk Factors

Most cases of high blood pressure are classified as primary or essential hypertension, meaning there is no single identifiable medical cause. Instead, it develops gradually over many years due to a combination of genetics and daily habits. Secondary hypertension, by contrast, is caused by an underlying condition such as kidney disease or hormonal disorders, and it usually appears suddenly.

Several modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors increase a person's chances of developing the condition:

  • Age: Blood vessels naturally lose elasticity and stiffen as people grow older.
  • Genetics: A family history of high blood pressure significantly raises personal risk.
  • Dietary habits: High sodium intake from processed foods and traditional seasonings contributes heavily to fluid retention.
  • Lifestyle factors: Physical inactivity, chronic stress, excessive alcohol consumption, and smoking damage vascular health.

Accurate Blood Pressure Measurement at Home

Getting an accurate blood pressure reading is the foundation of effective home management. Many patients experience 'white coat syndrome,' where their blood pressure spikes simply because a healthcare worker is taking it in a clinic. Teaching patients and family caregivers how to measure blood pressure correctly at home provides a true picture of their daily cardiovascular status.

To ensure reliable readings, instruct the patient to sit quietly with their feet flat on the floor and back supported for at least five minutes before starting. The arm must be bare, supported at heart level, and fitted with a cuff of the correct size. Using a cuff that is too small falsely elevates the reading, while an oversized cuff can yield falsely low numbers. Emphasize that they should record both numbers in a logbook alongside the time of day.

Lifestyle Modifications and Dietary Approaches

Medication alone cannot control hypertension if a patient's daily habits work against treatment. Lifestyle modifications are powerful tools that can lower blood pressure and enhance the effectiveness of prescribed drugs.

The DASH diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension) focuses on eating whole foods rich in potassium, calcium, and magnesium while sharply limiting sodium, saturated fats, and added sugars. Patients should load their plates with fresh fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and lean proteins.

Regular aerobic exercise, such as brisk walking, swimming, or cycling for thirty minutes most days of the week, strengthens the heart so it pumps with less effort. Weight management, quitting smoking, and reducing alcohol intake are equally critical pillars of non-pharmacological care.

Pharmacological Management and Adherence

When lifestyle changes are not enough to bring blood pressure into a safe range, healthcare providers prescribe antihypertensive medications. Common classes include diuretics (water pills) that reduce fluid volume, ACE inhibitors and ARBs that relax blood vessels, and calcium channel blockers that keep blood vessels from tightening.

A major challenge in home care is medication non-adherence. Because hypertension often causes no symptoms, patients frequently stop taking their pills when they feel well, believing they are cured. Nurses must counsel patients that these medications control the condition rather than cure it, and sudden discontinuation can trigger a dangerous rebound in blood pressure.

Recognizing and Responding to Hypertensive Crises

A hypertensive crisis occurs when blood pressure spikes dangerously high, usually above 180/120 mmHg. It is vital to distinguish between hypertensive urgency, where blood pressure is severely elevated without acute target organ damage, and hypertensive emergency, where dangerously high pressure causes active damage to organs such as the brain, heart, or kidneys.

Signs of a hypertensive emergency include severe headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, confusion, visual disturbances, or neurological deficits resembling a stroke. This is a medical emergency requiring immediate hospital transfer. Never attempt to drop the blood pressure too rapidly at home, as sudden drops can compromise blood flow to vital organs.

Long-term Complications and Local Nursing Care

Persistent uncontrolled hypertension places a heavy toll on the cardiovascular system and target organs over time. Chronic high pressure forces the left ventricle of the heart to work harder, leading to left ventricular hypertrophy and eventual heart failure. It also accelerates atherosclerosis, increasing the risk of myocardial infarction and cerebrovascular accidents. The kidneys suffer as well, as delicate filtering units become scarred, leading to chronic kidney disease.

In the Nigerian context, nurses face unique challenges including economic constraints on medication purchase, reliance on herbal concoctions alongside orthodox drugs, and traditional dietary preferences high in sodium and palm oil. Effective nursing care requires culturally sensitive education, encouraging affordable local alternatives for the DASH diet, and fostering open dialogue about traditional remedies without judgment.
