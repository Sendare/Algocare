Hypertension

Introduction and Pathophysiology of Hypertension

Hypertension is a chronic medical condition characterized by a sustained elevation of systemic arterial blood pressure. Clinically, it is diagnosed when a patient's resting blood pressure consistently reads at or above 140/90 mmHg. The cardiovascular system relies on a precise balance between cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance to maintain perfusion. When systemic resistance increases due to arteriolar remodeling or persistent vasoconstriction, the heart must generate higher pressures to circulate blood. Over time, this mechanical strain damages the delicate endothelial lining of blood vessels, accelerating atherosclerosis and increasing the workload on the left ventricle. In many Nigerian clinics, patients often present late in the disease progression due to the asymptomatic nature of early elevation, earning hypertension its clinical moniker as the silent killer.

Etiology and Risk Factors

The vast majority of diagnosed cases fall under **essential (primary) hypertension**, meaning there is no single identifiable organic cause. Instead, it arises from a complex interaction of genetic predisposition, aging, and environmental triggers. - **Age and Genetics**: Arteries naturally lose elasticity and stiffen over time, while a positive family history significantly raises baseline vulnerability. - **Dietary Sodium**: Excessive salt intake expands extracellular fluid volume, augmenting cardiac output and vascular resistance. - **Metabolic Factors**: Conditions such as obesity, insulin resistance, and dyslipidemia frequently cluster with high blood pressure. - **Secondary Hypertension**: A smaller percentage of cases stem from an identifiable underlying pathology, most commonly chronic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis, or endocrine disorders like primary aldosteronism.

Accurate Blood Pressure Measurement

Obtaining a reliable blood pressure reading requires strict adherence to standardized technique. Errors in measurement can easily lead to misdiagnosis or inappropriate medication adjustments. | Parameter | Correct Clinical Procedure | Common Pitfall | |---|---|---| | **Patient Position** | Seated quietly for 5 minutes, feet flat on the floor, arm supported at heart level | Measuring immediately after the patient walked briskly into the clinic | | **Cuff Sizing** | Bladder length covers 80% and width covers 40% of the upper arm circumference | Using a standard adult cuff on a patient with a large arm circumference | | **Inflation & Deflation** | Inflate 30 mmHg above estimated systolic, deflate at a steady rate of 2 mmHg per second | Deflating the cuff too rapidly, which compresses Korotkoff sounds |

Pharmacological Management and Adherence

When lifestyle modifications are insufficient to control blood pressure, pharmacotherapy becomes necessary. First-line antihypertensive drug classes include thiazide diuretics, calcium channel blockers, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and angiotensin receptor blockers. These agents lower blood pressure through varied mechanisms, such as promoting renal sodium excretion, inducing vasodilation, or blocking vasoconstrictive hormonal pathways. A major hurdle in long-term management is medication non-adherence. Because hypertension is largely asymptomatic, patients often discontinue their medications when they feel well, assuming they are cured. Nurses play a crucial role in educating patients that antihypertensives control blood pressure rather than cure the underlying disease, emphasizing the danger of abrupt cessation which can trigger rebound hypertension.

Lifestyle Modifications and the DASH Diet

Non-pharmacological interventions form the cornerstone of both prevention and management. Adopting a healthy lifestyle can significantly reduce blood pressure and enhance the efficacy of prescribed medications. - **DASH Diet**: Emphasizes high consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy products while minimizing saturated fats and refined sugars. - **Sodium Restriction**: Limiting daily salt intake decreases intravascular volume load and vascular reactivity. - **Regular Aerobic Exercise**: Engaging in activities like brisk walking for at least 30 minutes most days of the week improves endothelial function. - **Weight and Alcohol Control**: Maintaining a healthy body mass index and moderating alcohol consumption prevents sympathetic nervous system overactivation.

Identifying and Managing Hypertensive Crises

A hypertensive crisis occurs when blood pressure spikes dramatically, typically exceeding 180/120 mmHg. This clinical emergency is categorized into two distinct states based on target organ damage: 1. **Hypertensive Urgency**: Blood pressure is severely elevated, but there is no clinical evidence of acute target organ damage. Management involves gradual blood pressure reduction over 24 to 48 hours using oral medications in an outpatient or observational setting. 2. **Hypertensive Emergency**: Severe blood pressure elevation is accompanied by acute, life-threatening damage to target organs such as the brain, heart, kidneys, or retina (e.g., encephalopathy, acute myocardial infarction, or intracranial hemorrhage). This requires immediate admission to an intensive care unit for continuous monitoring and rapid blood pressure reduction using intravenous vasodilators.

Long-Term Complications and Patient Education

Uncontrolled chronic hypertension exerts relentless mechanical stress on the circulatory system, leading to profound long-term systemic complications. Target organ damage manifests across several major body systems: - **Cardiovascular**: Left ventricular hypertrophy, heart failure, and coronary artery disease. - **Neurological**: Transient ischemic attacks, ischemic stroke, and hypertensive encephalopathy. - **Renal**: Nephriosclerosis progressing to chronic kidney disease and eventual renal failure. - **Ophthalmological**: Retinal hemorrhages, exudates, and papilledema that can impair vision. Patient education must focus on cultivating self-management skills. Nurses should teach clients how to keep an accurate home blood pressure diary, recognize early warning signs of complications, and maintain open communication with their healthcare providers regarding medication side effects.
