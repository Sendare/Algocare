Anxiety Disorders

Pathophysiology and Neurobiology of Anxiety

Anxiety involves complex dysregulations within the central nervous system, particularly centered around the limbic system, amygdala, and prefrontal cortex. The amygdala acts as the brain's emotional threat detector, triggering the fight-or-flight response when danger is perceived. In individuals with anxiety disorders, this alarm system becomes hyperactive, firing inappropriately even in the absence of actual physical threat. Key neurotransmitters heavily implicated in this pathology include: - **Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)**: The primary inhibitory neurotransmitter. Deficits or reduced receptor sensitivity diminish the brain's ability to calm neural excitation, leading to persistent worry. - **Norepinephrine**: Often overactive in anxiety states, driving physical symptoms like tachycardia, sweating, and hypervigilance. - **Serotonin**: Modulates mood, sleep, and emotional processing; dysregulation contributes significantly to generalized distress.

Classification and Clinical Presentation

Anxiety disorders manifest in distinct clinical syndromes, each characterized by excessive fear or behavioral disturbances. Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) involves chronic, uncontrollable worry about everyday events lasting six months or more. Panic Disorder features sudden, unexpected surges of intense terror accompanied by physical symptoms like chest pain, palpitations, and a sense of impending doom. Phobias represent intense, irrational fears directed at specific objects or social situations, leading to profound avoidance behavior. Clinically, patients present with a mix of somatic complaints—such as gastrointestinal distress, muscle tension, and chronic fatigue—alongside cognitive distortions like catastrophic thinking.

Etiology and Sociocultural Risk Factors in Nigeria

The development of anxiety disorders stems from a multifactorial interplay of genetic predisposition, childhood trauma, and environmental stressors. In the Nigerian context, unique sociocultural pressures significantly shape vulnerability and presentation. Economic hardships, unemployment, and financial instability create chronic stress environments that elevate baseline anxiety. Additionally, security challenges across various regions, community displacement, and high societal expectations regarding family provision act as powerful chronic stressors. Cultural explanatory models often attribute psychiatric symptoms to spiritual attacks or somatic complaints, which can delay professional mental health seeking and lead patients to first consult traditional or religious healers.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Evaluation

Thorough assessment requires screening for both physical manifestations and psychological distress. Because anxiety frequently mimics somatic illness, nurses must first rule out organic causes such as hyperthyroidism, hypoglycemia, or cardiac arrhythmias. Standardized rating scales, such as the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) or the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A), assist in quantifying symptom severity. During clinical interviews, the nurse evaluates coping mechanisms, substance use history, and suicidal ideation, as chronic demoralization can co-occur with severe anxiety.

Pharmacological and Psychosocial Management

Effective treatment typically combines pharmacotherapy with evidence-based psychotherapy. Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) such as sertraline or escitalopram serve as first-line pharmacological agents due to their favorable safety profile. Benzodiazepines may be utilized for short-term relief during acute crises, but their long-term use is restricted due to risks of tolerance, dependence, and sedation. Psychosocially, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) remains the gold standard, helping patients identify, challenge, and restructure distorted thought patterns while gradually confronting avoided situations.

Home Care Interventions and Coping Strategies

Nurses empowering patients and families in home settings focus on practical, actionable strategies to manage daily stress. Teaching controlled diaphragmatic breathing helps interrupt the hyperventilation cycle during rising anxiety. Establishing predictable daily routines provides a sense of structure and control. Lifestyle modifications—such as reducing caffeine intake, ensuring adequate sleep hygiene, and incorporating regular physical exercise—naturally regulate neurotransmitter activity. Family education is equally crucial, teaching relatives how to offer supportive listening without inadvertently enabling avoidance behaviors.

Complications and Crisis Management

Untreated or severe anxiety can lead to significant functional impairment, social isolation, and secondary depression. In acute panic states or severe distress, crisis management takes precedence. The nurse must maintain a calm, reassuring demeanor, use clear and concise instructions, and reduce environmental stimuli to prevent further sensory overload. If self-harm or suicidal ideation is expressed, immediate safety measures and psychiatric referral are mandatory. In Nigerian community practice, collaborating with local primary health care teams ensures that patients experiencing severe psychiatric crises are safely bridged to specialist care.
