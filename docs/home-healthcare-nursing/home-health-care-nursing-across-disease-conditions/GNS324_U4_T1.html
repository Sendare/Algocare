Anxiety Disorders

Understanding Anxiety Disorders and Biological Roots

Anxiety is a normal human response to stress, but when it becomes overwhelming, persistent, and interferes with daily functioning, it crosses into an anxiety disorder. The biological basis of these conditions involves an imbalance in brain chemicals known as neurotransmitters. Specifically, low levels of serotonin, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), and norepinephrine dysregulation keep the central nervous system in a constant state of hyperarousal. The amygdala, the brain's emotional processing center, triggers the fight-or-flight response even when no actual physical danger is present, flooding the body with adrenaline and cortisol.

Types of Anxiety Disorders

Anxiety disorders manifest in several distinct ways, each with unique diagnostic criteria:

  • Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD): Characterized by chronic, uncontrollable worry about everyday issues like finances, health, or family, lasting for at least six months.
  • Panic Disorder: Marked by sudden, repeated attacks of intense fear and physical symptoms such as chest pain and palpitations, often accompanied by a fear of future attacks.
  • Social Anxiety Disorder: Involves an intense, persistent fear of being watched, judged, or humiliated in social or performance situations.
  • Specific Phobias: Out-of-proportion fear triggered by a specific object or situation, such as heights, enclosed spaces, or certain animals.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Home Context

Developing an anxiety disorder is rarely caused by a single factor. A combination of genetics, childhood trauma, chronic medical illnesses, and environmental stressors increases vulnerability. In the Nigerian context, specific sociocultural stressors play a heavy role. Economic hardships, job insecurity, high cost of living, security challenges, and societal pressures regarding family provision create a continuous background of chronic stress. Furthermore, cultural beliefs that sometimes stigmatize mental illness can delay individuals from seeking early medical help, forcing many to cope in silence until symptoms severely disrupt their lives.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Assessment

Assessing a patient with anxiety requires looking at both emotional and physical complaints, as many individuals initially present to clinics with physical symptoms. Common presentations include:

CardiovascularPalpitations, rapid pulse, chest tightness
RespiratoryShortness of breath, smothering sensation
NeurologicalDizziness, trembling, numbness, headaches
GastrointestinalDry mouth, nausea, abdominal discomfort
PsychologicalRestlessness, irritability, a sense of dread

Nurses must rule out physical causes like hyperthyroidism or cardiac issues before confirming a primary anxiety diagnosis.

Pharmacological and Psychosocial Management

Effective treatment combines medication and talk therapy. Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs), such as sertraline or fluoxetine, are the first-line drug choices because they gradually correct serotonin imbalances. Benzodiazepines provide rapid relief for severe acute anxiety, but they carry a high risk of dependence and are prescribed only for short periods. Alongside medications, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) helps patients identify negative thought patterns, challenge distorted beliefs, and replace them with healthier coping mechanisms.

Home Care Nursing Interventions and Coping Strategies

Nurses play a vital role in supporting patients and families managing anxiety at home. Key interventions include teaching deep breathing exercises, guiding progressive muscle relaxation, and helping establish predictable daily routines. Nurses should educate family members on how to offer a calm, non-judgmental listening ear rather than dismissing the patient's fears as mere worry. Encouraging regular physical activity, reducing excessive intake of caffeine and stimulants, and promoting adequate sleep hygiene are practical lifestyle adjustments that significantly reduce baseline anxiety.

Complications and Crisis Management

Unmanaged anxiety disorders can lead to severe complications, including clinical depression, substance abuse as a form of self-medication, social isolation, and impaired occupational functioning. In severe crises, particularly during unmanaged panic or overwhelming distress, individuals may experience suicidal ideation. Nurses must assess for suicide risk directly and calmly. Crisis management requires creating a safe environment, removing potential hazards, ensuring rapid psychiatric referral, and mobilizing family support networks to keep the patient safe.
