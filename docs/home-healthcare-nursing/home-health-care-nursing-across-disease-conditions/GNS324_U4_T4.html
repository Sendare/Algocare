Cerebral Palsy

Introduction and Definition of Cerebral Palsy

Cerebral palsy refers to a group of permanent, non-progressive neurological disorders that appear in infancy or early childhood. These conditions affect body movement, muscle coordination, and posture due to abnormalities or damage in the developing brain. While the primary brain lesion is static and does not worsen over time, the physical manifestations and musculoskeletal consequences can change as the child grows. In Nigerian pediatric practice, recognizing these motor delays early is vital because timely physical therapy and supportive interventions significantly improve long-term functional independence.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Brain injury leading to cerebral palsy can occur during the prenatal, perinatal, or postnatal periods. Understanding these timelines helps clinicians identify vulnerable infants.

  • Prenatal factors: Maternal infections such as rubella, cytomegalovirus, or toxoplasmosis, severe maternal malnutrition, and placental insufficiency.
  • Perinatal factors: Birth asphyxia, extreme prematurity, low birth weight, intracranial hemorrhage, and neonatal jaundice resulting in kernicterus.
  • Postnatal factors: Bacterial meningitis, viral encephalitis, traumatic head injuries, and severe near-drowning incidents during early childhood.

Pathophysiology and Types of Cerebral Palsy

Cerebral palsy is classified based on the predominant motor abnormality and the anatomical location of the affected limbs.

  • Spastic Cerebral Palsy: The most common form, characterized by increased muscle tone (hypertonicity), stiff muscles, and exaggerated deep tendon reflexes due to damage to the cerebral cortex or pyramidal tract.
  • Dyskinetic (Athetoid) Cerebral Palsy: Involves uncontrolled, slow, writhing movements, particularly in the hands, feet, arms, or legs, stemming from basal ganglia involvement.
  • Ataxic Cerebral Palsy: Marked by poor balance, disturbed spatial sense, and uncoordinated shaky movements, associated with cerebellar pathology.
  • Mixed Cerebral Palsy: A combination of two types, most frequently spastic and dyskinetic features.

Clinical Manifestations and Developmental Milestones

Children with cerebral palsy frequently miss key developmental milestones such as rolling over, sitting independently, crawling, and walking. Clinical signs include asymmetrical movement patterns, persistent primitive reflexes beyond the normal age of disappearance, abnormal posturing, and feeding difficulties due to oral-motor dysfunction. Muscle tone abnormalities can manifest either as severe stiffness or generalized floppiness (hypotonia). In resource-constrained settings, delayed independent sitting past nine months should prompt a thorough neurological evaluation.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnosis

Comprehensive nursing assessment involves monitoring growth parameters, evaluating muscle tone, checking deep tendon reflexes, and observing functional motor abilities during play or routine care. Nurses also screen for associated impairments including epilepsy, visual deficits, hearing loss, and cognitive challenges. Diagnostic confirmation relies heavily on detailed developmental histories, neurological examinations, and neuroimaging studies such as cranial ultrasound in infants or magnetic resonance imaging to pinpoint structural brain anomalies.

Interprofessional Care and Management

Management of cerebral palsy requires a coordinated interprofessional team to address complex, lifelong needs.

PhysiotherapistImproves gross motor function, stretching, and mobility
Occupational TherapistEnhances fine motor skills and activities of daily living
Speech TherapistManages communication deficits and swallowing disorders
Orthopedic SurgeonCorrects contractures and skeletal deformities
Pharmacist / PhysicianManages spasticity with muscle relaxants or botulinum toxin

Family Education and Psychosocial Support

Caring for a child with chronic motor disabilities places significant emotional, physical, and financial demands on the family. Nurses play a pivotal role in teaching parents safe home transfer techniques, proper positioning to prevent contractures, and strategies for administering prescribed antispasmodic medications. Providing psychosocial support involves connecting families with local support groups, social services, and counseling resources. In many Nigerian communities, addressing cultural stigmas and misconceptions surrounding disability is an essential prerequisite for effective family engagement.

Potential Complications and Long-term Prognosis

Secondary complications can severely impact the quality of life for individuals with cerebral palsy if left unmanaged. Common physical complications include joint contractures, spinal deformities such as scoliosis, hip dislocation, chronic pain, and nutritional compromise due to aspiration or dysphagia. The overall long-term prognosis depends largely on the severity of the brain injury and the presence of associated intellectual disabilities or epilepsy. Many individuals with mild to moderate involvement live long, productive lives into adulthood, especially with early and consistent rehabilitative support.
