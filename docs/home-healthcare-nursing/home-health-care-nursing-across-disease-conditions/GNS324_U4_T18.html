Tuberculosis

Introduction to Tuberculosis and Transmission Dynamics

Tuberculosis is a chronic, granulomatous infectious disease primarily caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, an acid-fast bacillus that spreads almost exclusively through airborne droplets. When an individual with pulmonary disease coughs, sneezes, speaks, or sings, microscopic infectious aerosols called droplet nuclei are expelled into the surrounding air and can remain suspended for hours. In densely populated urban settings or poorly ventilated clinical wards across Nigeria, these aerosolized particles present a continuous exposure risk.

Infection begins when a susceptible host inhales these droplet nuclei, which bypass the upper respiratory defenses and settle deep within the pulmonary alveoli. Once inside the alveoli, alveolar macrophages phagocytize the bacteria. While many bacilli are destroyed, some survive and multiply inside the macrophages, initiating the primary immune response. Transmission efficiency depends heavily on the concentration of infectious droplets in the air, the closeness of contact, and the duration of exposure. Extrapulmonary tuberculosis, affecting sites such as the pleura, lymph nodes, spine, or genitourinary tract, generally does not transmit from person to person unless concurrent pulmonary disease is present.

Pathophysiology: Latent versus Active Infection

Following initial inhalation and macrophage ingestion, the host immune system mounts a cell-mediated response that walls off the bacilli. T-lymphocytes and macrophages surround the infected tissue, forming a localized cluster known as a granuloma or tubercle.

  • Latent Tuberculosis Infection (LTBI): The bacilli remain contained within the granuloma in a dormant state. The individual is asymptomatic, has a normal chest radiograph, cannot transmit the bacteria to others, but tests positive on a tuberculin skin test or interferon-gamma release assay.
  • Active Tuberculosis Disease: The immune system fails to contain the bacteria, or cellular immunity becomes compromised due to factors like HIV coinfection or malnutrition. The granuloma undergoes central caseous necrosis, liquefies, and ruptures into the airways, creating cavities and allowing massive bacterial replication and spread.

Active pulmonary disease causes significant tissue destruction and makes the person infectious to close contacts.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Assessment

Recognizing pulmonary tuberculosis requires identifying a constellation of systemic and respiratory complaints. The classic presentation includes a persistent, productive cough lasting longer than two weeks, often accompanied by hemoptysis as lung tissue erodes. Systemic signs reflect the body's chronic inflammatory battle:

  • Night sweats: Drenching episodes that require changing nightclothes.
  • Low-grade fever: Typically spiking in the afternoon or evening.
  • Progressive weight loss: Caused by hypermetabolism and anorexia, historically termed consumption.
  • Fatigue and malaise: Generalized exhaustion limiting daily activities.

In older adults or immunocompromised patients, these classic signs may be muted or attributed erroneously to normal aging or other respiratory infections, delaying proper diagnosis.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Confirming a diagnosis of tuberculosis requires a combination of clinical history, molecular testing, bacteriology, and imaging. The Xpert MTB/RIF assay is a rapid molecular test recommended by the World Health Organization that detects Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA and rifampicin resistance simultaneously within hours from a single sputum sample.

Traditional sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli remains widely used in resource-limited settings due to its low cost, though it has lower sensitivity than molecular methods. Chest radiography reveals classic upper lobe infiltrates, cavitary lesions, or lymphadenopathy, though findings can mimic other pulmonary infections. The tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) identify immune response to the bacteria but cannot distinguish between latent infection and active disease.

Pharmacological Management and Directly Observed Therapy

Standard treatment for drug-susceptible tuberculosis involves a rigorous, multi-drug regimen divided into an intensive phase and a continuation phase to eradicate active bacilli and prevent drug resistance.

Treatment PhaseStandard DurationCore MedicationsPrimary Clinical Goal
Intensive Phase2 MonthsRifampicin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, EthambutolRapidly reduce the bacterial population
Continuation Phase4 MonthsRifampicin, IsoniazidEliminate remaining dormant bacilli

To ensure complete eradication and prevent the emergence of drug-resistant strains, Directly Observed Therapy (DOT) is implemented. Under DOT, a trained healthcare worker, community health extension worker, or treatment supporter watches the patient swallow every single dose of medication. This strategy addresses non-adherence caused by early clinical improvement, when patients often feel better and are tempted to stop taking their drugs.

Infection Control and Environmental Safety

Preventing nosocomial and household transmission requires rigorous environmental and administrative controls. In healthcare facilities, patients with suspected or confirmed infectious tuberculosis must be placed in airborne infection isolation rooms featuring negative pressure ventilation that exhausts air safely outdoors or through high-efficiency particulate air filters.

When caring for infectious individuals in home settings or open-ward clinics, simple architectural measures drastically reduce risk:

  • Maximizing natural ventilation by keeping windows and doors wide open to encourage cross-breeze airflow.
  • Positioning patients outdoors or in well-sunlit areas when weather permits, as ultraviolet sunlight has natural bactericidal properties.
  • Ensuring healthcare workers and family caregivers wear properly fitted N95 respirators rather than basic cloth masks when entering airspace occupied by an infectious patient.
  • Teaching patients to practice proper cough etiquette by covering their nose and mouth with a tissue or their flexed elbow.

Nutritional Support and Patient Education

Tuberculosis places an intense catabolic demand on the body, frequently resulting in severe malnutrition, muscle wasting, and micronutrient deficiencies. Restoring nutritional status is vital for supporting immune function and repairing damaged lung tissue. Dietary counseling should emphasize high-protein, energy-dense foods utilizing locally available ingredients such as beans, groundnuts, eggs, fish, and nutrient-rich grains.

Patient education bridges the gap between clinical instruction and successful recovery at home. Nurses must dispel cultural misconceptions regarding the disease—such as beliefs that it stems from spiritual curses or poisoning—by clearly explaining its bacterial etiology. Education should cover the transmission timeline, reassuring the patient that they typically become non-infectious after two to three weeks of consistent, effective multi-drug therapy. Empowering patients with this knowledge encourages active participation in their healing journey and reduces social stigma within their communities.

Monitoring Medication Adherence and Adverse Effects

First-line anti-tuberculosis drugs are powerful life-saving agents, but they frequently cause adverse effects that can threaten medication adherence if not managed proactively. Nurses must monitor patients closely for drug-specific toxicities:

  • Isoniazid: Can cause peripheral neuropathy, which is mitigated by concurrent administration of pyridoxine (vitamin B6).
  • Rifampicin: Frequently turns body fluids (urine, tears, sweat) a harmless reddish-orange color and is a potent inducer of hepatic enzymes, leading to drug interactions.
  • Pyrazinamide: May cause hyperuricemia, leading to joint pain or gouty arthritis.
  • Ethambutol: Can cause optic neuritis, manifesting as decreased visual acuity or impaired red-green color discrimination.

Furthermore, all first-line drugs carry a risk of hepatotoxicity. Patients must be educated to report warning signs such as yellowing of the sclera, dark urine, right upper quadrant abdominal pain, and persistent nausea immediately.
