Tuberculosis

Introduction to Tuberculosis and Transmission Dynamics

Tuberculosis, commonly known as TB, is a chronic infectious disease caused by the bacterium Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It primarily attacks the lungs, though it can also affect other parts of the body such as the spine, kidneys, and brain. The disease is spread through the air when a person with active pulmonary TB coughs, sneezes, speaks, or sings, releasing microscopic droplets into the environment.

  • Droplet Nuclei: These tiny infectious particles remain suspended in the air for hours, waiting to be inhaled by a susceptible host.
  • Inhalation: Once inhaled, the bacteria travel down the respiratory tract and settle in the alveoli of the lungs.
  • Risk Factors: Overcrowded living conditions, poor ventilation, malnutrition, and a weakened immune system heavily favor the spread of infection.

A common misconception is that TB can be easily contracted by sharing utensils, drinking glasses, or touching surfaces handled by an infected person. In reality, the bacteria require airborne transmission via respiratory droplets, making casual contact much less dangerous than prolonged indoor exposure in poorly ventilated spaces.

Pathophysiology: Latent versus Active Infection

When Mycobacterium tuberculosis enters the lungs, the body's immune system mounts a defense response. Macrophages engulf the bacteria, forming a barrier of immune cells around the infection called a granuloma or tubercle. This walling-off process contains the bacteria and prevents them from multiplying or spreading further.

In latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), the bacteria remain alive inside the granuloma, but they are dormant. The person feels completely well, has no symptoms, and cannot transmit the bacteria to anyone else. A tuberculin skin test or blood test will show a positive immune response, but chest X-rays are typically normal.

In active tuberculosis disease, the immune system fails to keep the bacteria contained. The granulomas break down, allowing the bacteria to multiply rapidly, destroy lung tissue, and spread through the bloodstream or airways. At this stage, the individual becomes sick, exhibits classic clinical symptoms, and is fully contagious to others.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptom Assessment

Active pulmonary tuberculosis presents with a distinct pattern of systemic and respiratory complaints. Nurses must carefully assess patients for these signs during home visits or clinical check-ups.

  • Persistent Cough: Lasting longer than three weeks, often starting dry and progressing to a productive cough with purulent or blood-tinged sputum.
  • Hemoptysis: Coughing up blood, which occurs as expanding bacterial colonies erode surrounding lung blood vessels.
  • Constitutional Symptoms: Unexplained weight loss, chronic fatigue, low-grade afternoon fevers, and heavy drenching night sweats.
  • Chest Pain: Dull, aching pain associated with breathing or coughing due to pleural irritation.

Elderly patients or those with weakened immune systems may present with atypical symptoms, such as sudden confusion or simple failure to thrive, masking the true underlying respiratory infection.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Methods

Detecting tuberculosis requires a combination of screening tests, imaging studies, and laboratory analyses of biological specimens. The two primary initial screening tools are the Mantoux tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs).

The Mantoux test involves intradermal injection of purified protein derivative (PPD) on the inner forearm, with the induration read 48 to 72 hours later. However, a positive skin test only confirms that a person has been exposed to the bacteria; it does not distinguish between latent infection and active disease.

To confirm active pulmonary tuberculosis, nurses assist in collecting sputum specimens for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) smear microscopy and culture confirmation. Chest radiography is also performed to identify characteristic upper-lobe infiltrates, cavitary lesions, or pleural effusions.

Pharmacological Management and Directly Observed Therapy

Treating active tuberculosis requires a prolonged multi-drug regimen to eradicate the bacteria completely and prevent drug-resistant strains from developing. The standard initial phase utilizes a four-drug combination: isoniazid (INH), rifampin (RIF), pyrazinamide (PZA), and ethambutol (EMB), typically given for two months. This is followed by a continuation phase of isoniazid and rifampin for an additional four to seven months.

Because treatment lasts many months, patient adherence is the single biggest challenge in tuberculosis care. To ensure compliance, health systems utilize Directly Observed Therapy (DOT). Under DOT, a healthcare worker or trained community volunteer watches the patient swallow every single dose of medication. This direct supervision significantly reduces treatment failure, relapse, and the emergence of multidrug-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-TB).

Infection Control and Environmental Safety

When managing a patient with infectious pulmonary tuberculosis at home or in a healthcare facility, rigorous environmental safety measures are mandatory to protect family members and the public.

  • Home Isolation: The patient must remain in a private, well-ventilated room with the door closed until sputum smears are negative and clinical symptoms improve.
  • Natural Ventilation: Opening windows regularly allows fresh air to circulate and disperses suspended infectious droplet nuclei.
  • Respiratory Hygiene: Patients must cover their mouth and nose with a tissue when coughing or sneezing, and dispose of used tissues in a sealed bag.
  • Personal Protective Equipment: Family caregivers entering the isolation room should wear appropriate particulate respirators, such as N95 masks, to filter out airborne bacteria.

Once a patient starts effective drug therapy and shows clinical improvement, their infectivity drops rapidly, usually within two to three weeks of consistent treatment.

Nutritional Support and Patient Education

Tuberculosis places a heavy metabolic burden on the body, frequently leading to severe weight loss, muscle wasting, and malnutrition. Restoring nutritional status is a vital component of recovery.

  • High-Calorie, High-Protein Diet: Encouraging frequent, nutrient-dense meals helps repair damaged lung tissue and rebuild depleted energy reserves.
  • Vitamin Supplementation: Pyridoxine (vitamin B6) is commonly prescribed alongside isoniazid to prevent peripheral neuropathy.
  • Comprehensive Education: Nurses must educate patients and their families about the necessity of completing the full treatment course, even after symptoms disappear and they start feeling well.

A common error is for patients to stop taking their medications as soon as their cough subsides and their energy returns. Patient education must emphasize that feeling better means the drugs are working, not that the infection is entirely cured.

Monitoring for Medication Adherence and Adverse Effects

First-line anti-tubercular drugs are powerful agents capable of causing significant adverse effects. Nurses play a crucial role in monitoring patients for early signs of toxicity so interventions can be made before permanent damage occurs.

  • Hepatotoxicity: Isoniazid, rifampin, and pyrazinamide can cause liver injury. Nurses must monitor for jaundice, dark urine, and right upper quadrant abdominal pain.
  • Visual Disturbances: Ethambutol can cause optic neuritis, leading to reduced visual acuity and altered color discrimination.
  • Body Fluid Discoloration: Rifampin harmlessly turns urine, sweat, and tears a bright orange-red color, which can alarm uneducated patients.

Regular baseline and follow-up liver function tests, along with visual acuity checks, are standard clinical practices during the course of antitubercular therapy.
