Fractures

Introduction to Fractures and Home Care Context

A fracture is a complete or partial break in the continuity of bone structure. While acute management traditionally occurs in a hospital setting, a significant portion of recovery happens at home, especially in resource-limited environments across Nigeria where early discharge is common due to bed shortages. Home care shifts the responsibility of monitoring, hygiene maintenance, and environmental safety onto the patient and their family caregivers. Student nurses must recognize that managing a fracture outside the hospital requires assessing the home environment for hazards like loose rugs, uneven concrete steps, and poor lighting that threaten safe mobility during bone healing.

Pathophysiology and Types of Bone Fractures

Bone healing is a dynamic physiological process rather than mere mechanical gluing. When a bone breaks, blood vessels rupture, resulting in a localized hematoma. This inflammatory phase recruits fibroblasts, osteoblasts, and chondrocytes, forming a soft callus that eventually undergoes mineralization into a hard bony callus before final remodeling.

  • Closed fracture: The skin remains intact over the fracture site, minimizing external bacterial contamination.
  • Open fracture: The broken bone pierces the skin, creating a direct pathway for pathogens and a high risk of osteomyelitis.
  • Greenstick fracture: An incomplete break where the bone bends and splinters on one side, most frequently seen in pediatric patients with pliable bones.
  • Comminuted fracture: The bone shatters into three or more pieces, typically resulting from high-impact trauma.

Clinical Manifestations and Initial Assessment

Identifying a fracture relies on a combination of subjective complaints and objective physical findings. Pain usually occurs immediately at the site, exacerbated by any movement. Swelling develops rapidly from extravasation of blood and fluid into surrounding soft tissues.

  • Deformity: Abnormal angulation or rotation of the limb compared to the uninjured side.
  • Loss of function: Inability to bear weight or move the affected extremity normally.
  • Crepitus: A crunchy or grating sound produced by bone fragments rubbing together.
  • Ecchymosis: Discoloration of the skin due to subcutaneous bleeding.

Initial assessment must prioritize neurovascular checks—evaluating pulse, pallor, paresthesia, paralysis, and pain—to rule out acute ischemia or nerve compression.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

Treatment strategies aim to achieve reduction—restoring the bone fragments to their normal anatomical alignment—and immobilization to maintain that alignment until healing occurs. Closed reduction is performed manually under sedation or local anesthesia, followed by casting or splinting. When closed techniques fail or when exact alignment is critical, open reduction internal fixation (ORIF) utilizes surgical incisions to place plates, screws, or intramedullary nails directly onto or inside the bone. Alternatively, external fixation employs metal pins inserted through the skin into the bone, connected to an external stabilizing frame, frequently utilized for severe open fractures with extensive soft tissue damage.

Home Healthcare Nursing Management of Casts and Traction

Casts and traction devices require meticulous nursing oversight to prevent secondary complications. Plaster of Paris casts take up to 48 hours to dry completely and must be handled with the palms of the hands rather than fingertips while wet to prevent indentations that can cause pressure ulcers. Patients in traction must maintain proper body alignment, ensuring that weights hang freely at all times and never touch the floor or bed frame. Nurses must teach families to inspect cast edges for roughness, monitor for foul odors indicating underlying infection, and report persistent burning pain under the cast immediately.

Mobility Support, Assistive Devices, and Safe Transfers

Restoring mobility safely depends on selecting the appropriate assistive device based on the patient's upper body strength, balance, and weight-bearing prescription. Crutches require adequate arm strength and proper sizing, ensuring a two-to-three-finger-width clearance between the axilla and the crutch pad to avoid brachial nerve damage. Walkers provide greater stability for elderly patients or those with generalized weakness. When teaching cane usage, instruct the patient to hold the cane on the unaffected side to provide support opposite the injured limb, moving the cane simultaneously with the affected leg.

Nutrition, Pain Management, and Healing Promotion

Bone repair demands increased metabolic energy and specific nutritional substrates. Diets rich in calcium, vitamin D, and protein are essential for matrix formation and mineralization. In Nigerian home settings, nurses should encourage affordable local food sources such as beans, groundnuts, dairy, leafy green vegetables, and fish. Pain management combines pharmacological agents—ranging from non-opioids to short-term opioids during the acute phase—with non-pharmacological modalities like elevation, cold therapy, and relaxation techniques. Adequate hydration and high-fiber foods are also critical to counteract opioid-induced constipation.

Prevention and Early Identification of Complications

Nurses must remain vigilant for both systemic and local complications following a fracture. Acute compartment syndrome occurs when increased pressure within a fascial compartment compromises circulation and nerve function; the classic manifestation is severe pain unrelieved by opioid analgesics and pain aggravated by passive stretch. Fat embolism syndrome can arise when fat globules from the yellow bone marrow enter the bloodstream, presenting with respiratory distress, petechiae over the chest, and altered mental status. DVT and pulmonary embolism are additional systemic threats mitigated by early mobilization and prescribed anticoagulants.
