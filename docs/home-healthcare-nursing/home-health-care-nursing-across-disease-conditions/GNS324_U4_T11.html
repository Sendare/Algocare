Fractures

Introduction to Fractures and Home Care Context

A fracture is a break or disruption in the continuity of a bone. While many fractures begin with acute hospital admission for reduction and stabilization, a significant portion of recovery happens outside the ward. Home healthcare nurses play a vital role in guiding patients and their families through the prolonged recovery phase. Managing a fracture at home requires a balance between promoting bone healing and preventing secondary complications like immobility, stiffness, and infection. Understanding how bones break and how they heal forms the bedrock of safe, effective nursing care in community and home settings.

Pathophysiology and Types of Bone Fractures

When a bone breaks, the body initiates a sophisticated healing cascade starting with a localized hematoma, followed by fibrocartilaginous callus formation, bony callus formation, and finally bone remodeling. Fractures are classified based on the line of the break and whether the skin remains intact.

  • Closed (Simple) Fracture: The bone breaks, but the overlying skin remains undamaged.
  • Open (Compound) Fracture: The broken bone pierces through the skin, creating a high risk for severe bone infection (osteomyelitis).
  • Greenstick Fracture: The bone bends and cracks rather than breaking completely, most commonly seen in children.
  • Comminuted Fracture: The bone shatters into three or more pieces at the injury site.

Clinical Manifestations and Initial Assessment

Recognizing a fracture requires a careful physical assessment alongside a detailed history of the injury. Patients typically present with localized pain, swelling, bruising, and an obvious inability to use the affected limb. Deformity and shortening of the limb may also be visible.

Nurses must prioritize the neurovascular assessment, checking the five Ps: Pain, Pallalor, Pulselessness, Paresthesia, and Paralysis. This assessment ensures that swelling or displaced bone fragments are not compressing vital blood vessels and nerves, which could lead to permanent tissue damage or loss of the limb.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

Treatment goals for fractures include anatomical alignment of the bone fragments, immobilization to hold them in place, and restoration of normal function.

Reduction can be closed, using manual manipulation and traction, or open, requiring surgery to align the bone directly. Once reduced, the bone must be immobilized. Internal fixation uses metal plates, screws, or rods placed surgically inside the bone. External fixation uses pins inserted through the skin into the bone, attached to an external metal frame. Nurses caring for patients with external fixators must perform meticulous pin-site care to prevent local and systemic infections.

Home Healthcare Nursing Management of Casts and Traction

Casts and traction devices require vigilant nursing oversight, especially in home care settings. Plaster casts take hours to dry and must be handled with the palms of the hands, never fingertips, to avoid creating pressure indentations that can damage underlying skin. Synthetic fiberglass casts dry much faster.

Traction applies a pulling force to align and immobilize a fracture, reducing muscle spasms. Weights used in traction must hang freely at all times; resting them on the floor or a bed frame completely disrupts the prescribed pulling force. Nurses must teach patients and caregivers never to tamper with traction weights or ropes.

Mobility Support, Assistive Devices, and Safe Transfers

A fracture often drastically alters a patient's independence. Home nurses assess the home environment and educate patients on using assistive devices like crutches, walkers, or canes correctly.

When fitting crutches, there should be a two to three-finger width space between the top of the crutch and the patient's axilla, and elbows should flex at approximately 30 degrees. Supporting body weight must happen through the hands, not the armpits, to prevent crutch palsy caused by brachial plexus nerve damage. Safe transfer techniques from bed to chair prevent falls and re-injury.

Nutrition, Pain Management, and Healing Promotion

Bone healing is a high-energy metabolic process that requires proper nutritional support. Home care nurses should encourage diets rich in calcium, vitamin D, protein, and vitamin C to support collagen synthesis and bone mineralization.

Pain management is equally crucial. While prescribed analgesics control acute discomfort, nurses must watch for side effects like opioid-induced constipation, which is common in immobile fracture patients. Non-pharmacological measures such as cold therapy, elevation, and gentle relaxation techniques complement medication and improve overall comfort.

Prevention and Early Identification of Complications

Fracture care requires constant vigilance for life-threatening or limb-threatening complications.

  • Compartment Syndrome: Increased pressure within a confined muscle compartment restricts blood flow, causing severe, unrelenting pain that is not relieved by opioid pain medication.
  • Fat Embolism Syndrome: Fat globules from the bone marrow enter the bloodstream, commonly after long bone fractures, presenting with respiratory distress, confusion, and a petechial rash on the chest.
  • Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT): Sluggish blood flow from immobility can lead to blood clots in the lower extremities, which can travel to the lungs as a pulmonary embolism.
