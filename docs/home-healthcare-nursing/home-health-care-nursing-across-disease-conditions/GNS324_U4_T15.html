Cancer

Introduction to Cancer and Pathophysiology

Cancer is fundamentally a disease of cellular regulation. Normally, cells undergo a tightly controlled cycle of growth, division, and programmed death known as apoptosis. When genetic mutations occur in the proto-oncogenes or tumor suppressor genes, this orderly system collapses. Cells begin to multiply uncontrollably, ignoring signals that would normally halt division. - **Initiation**: A permanent mutation occurs in the cellular DNA from exposure to a carcinogen or an inherited genetic error. - **Promotion**: Repeated exposure to promoting agents stimulates the altered cells to proliferate actively, forming a pre-neoplastic lesion. - **Progression**: The mutated cells acquire aggressive malignant behaviors, including the ability to invade surrounding tissue and establish new blood supplies. Malignant tumors differ from benign ones by their capacity for **metastasis**—the spread of cancer cells to distant organs via the bloodstream or lymphatic system. Understanding this cellular chaos provides the foundation for all oncological nursing interventions.

Common Types and Classifications of Cancer

Cancers are classified primarily by the tissue type from which they originate. This classification guides both treatment protocols and prognostic expectations. - **Carcingins**: Tumors arising from epithelial cells, which line internal and external body surfaces. Examples include breast, lung, colorectal, and cervical cancer. They represent the vast majority of adult malignancies. - **Sarcomas**: Cancers originating in connective or supportive tissues, such as bones, muscles, fat, and blood vessels. - **Leukemias**: Malignancies of the blood-forming tissues, specifically the bone marrow, characterized by the overproduction of abnormal white blood cells. - **Lymphomas and Myelomas**: Cancers affecting the immune system, arising in the lymphatic tissue and plasma cells respectively. In clinical practice, grading and staging further refine these classifications. Grading evaluates how abnormal the cells look under a microscope, indicating aggressiveness, while staging determines the physical extent of the disease within the body.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

Cancer etiology involves a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and infectious factors. While global risk factors like tobacco use, aging, and dietary habits apply in Nigeria, certain infectious agents and environmental exposures hold particular prominence locally. Chronic viral infections are a major contributor to the cancer burden in sub-Saharan Africa. Human Papillomavirus drives the high incidence of cervical cancer, while Hepatitis B and C viruses are primary precursors to hepatocellular carcinoma. Additionally, chronic exposure to aflatoxins from improperly stored grains and peanuts significantly increases liver cancer risk. Socio-cultural factors also influence cancer outcomes in Nigeria. Delayed presentation remains a critical barrier; many patients arrive at healthcare facilities in advanced stages due to reliance on traditional healers, financial constraints, and limited public health awareness regarding early screening.

Clinical Manifestations and Early Warning Signs

Cancer often develops insidiously, producing vague symptoms that patients may initially dismiss. Recognizing early warning signs is vital for timely intervention. The American Cancer Society popularized the acronym CAUTION to guide clinical suspicion: - **C**: Change in bowel or bladder habits - **A**: A sore that does not heal - **U**: Unusual bleeding or discharge - **T**: Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere - **I**: Indigestion or difficulty in swallowing - **O**: Obvious change in a wart or mole - **N**: Nagging cough or hoarseness Systemic manifestations also frequently accompany malignancy. Unexplained weight loss, profound fatigue that does not resolve with rest, persistent low-grade fevers, and night sweats are classic constitutional symptoms. Nurses must assess these systemic clues carefully during admission histories, as they often signal widespread or hematologic disease.

Diagnostic Procedures and Staging

Confirming a cancer diagnosis requires a combination of laboratory investigations, imaging studies, and definitive tissue pathology. Blood tests may reveal elevated tumor markers, anemia, or abnormal leukocyte counts, though these are rarely diagnostic on their own. Imaging modalities such as X-rays, ultrasound, computed tomography scans, and magnetic resonance imaging help locate tumors and evaluate metastasis. However, the definitive gold standard for diagnosing cancer is a **biopsy**—the surgical removal of suspicious tissue for microscopic examination by a pathologist. Once cancer is confirmed, staging systems like the TNM system are applied: - **T**: Describes the size and extent of the primary tumor. - **N**: Indicates the degree of spread to regional lymph nodes. - **M**: Denotes the absence or presence of distant metastasis. Accurate staging dictates the therapeutic strategy, determines prognosis, and allows consistent communication among the multidisciplinary healthcare team.

Medical and Surgical Treatments

Cancer management relies on three primary treatment modalities: surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy, often used in multimodal combinations. Surgery may be curative when the tumor is localized and completely resectable, or it may be palliative to reduce tumor burden and relieve pressure on surrounding organs. Chemotherapy uses cytotoxic medications to destroy rapidly dividing cells throughout the body. Because these drugs affect normal cells with high turnover rates—such as bone marrow, hair follicles, and gastrointestinal mucosa—patients commonly experience side effects like alopecia, myelosuppression, and mucositis. Radiation therapy uses high-energy ionizing radiation to damage the DNA of cancer cells within a targeted local field. Targeted therapy and immunotherapy represent newer advances, working by blocking specific molecules necessary for tumor growth or by harnessing the patient's own immune system to attack malignant cells.

Nursing Care and Symptom Management

Managing the complex physical and psychological needs of cancer patients requires vigilant nursing intervention across the continuum of care. Bone marrow suppression secondary to treatment leads to critical vulnerabilities: neutropenia increases infection risk, thrombocytopenia elevates bleeding tendencies, and anemia causes debilitating fatigue. Nurses must implement protective isolation protocols for neutropenic patients, monitor platelet counts closely, and educate individuals on recognizing signs of bleeding or infection. Gastrointestinal toxicities like nausea, vomiting, and anorexia require proactive antiemetic administration and nutritional support. Pain management is equally paramount, utilizing the World Health Organization analgesic ladder to ensure effective relief through regular dosing schedules rather than relying solely on breakthrough administration.

Palliative Care and End-of-Life Support

Palliative care focuses on improving the quality of life for patients and their families facing life-limiting illnesses. Unlike hospice care, which is reserved for the final months of life, palliative care can and should be integrated concurrently with active disease-directed treatments from the time of diagnosis. Holistic nursing at the end of life involves managing distressing physical symptoms—such as dyspnea, pain, delirium, and terminal secretions—while providing robust psychological, social, and spiritual support. Nurses facilitate crucial conversations regarding advanced directives, ensuring patient autonomy and dignified closure. Supporting family members through the grieving process, both before and after death, remains an essential component of comprehensive oncology nursing practice.
