Cancer

Introduction to Cancer and Pathophysiology

Cancer is not a single disease, but a broad group of diseases involving unregulated cell growth. Normally, human cells grow and divide to form new cells as the body needs them. When cells grow old or become damaged, they die, and new cells take their place.

In cancer, this orderly process breaks down. As cells become more and more abnormal, old or damaged cells survive when they should die, and new cells form when they are not needed. These extra cells can divide without stopping and may form growths called tumors.

  1. Benign Tumors: These are non-cancerous growths that do not spread to other parts of the body or invade nearby tissues.
  2. Malignant Tumors: These are cancerous growths that can invade nearby tissues and break away to travel through the blood or lymph system to other parts of the body, a process known as metastasis.

Not all cancers form solid tumors; blood cancers like leukemias generally do not. The fundamental cellular problem driving all cancers is a genetic mutation that disables normal control over the cell cycle and apoptosis, or programmed cell death.

Classification and Common Types of Cancer

Cancers are classified by the type of tissue in which they begin and are categorized into five major groups.

  • Carcinomas: These begin in the skin or the tissues that line or cover internal organs. They are the most common type and include breast, lung, prostate, and colorectal cancers.
  • Sarcomas: These begin in bone, cartilage, fat, muscle, blood vessels, or other connective or supportive tissue.
  • Leukemias: These are cancers of the blood-forming tissue, such as the bone marrow, and do not form solid tumors.
  • Lymphomas: These cancers begin in the cells of the immune system, specifically within the lymphatic system.
  • Central Nervous System Cancers: These begin in the tissues of the brain and spinal cord.

Healthcare providers use these broad categories to guide treatment decisions, as different tissue origins respond differently to specific chemotherapy drugs and radiation therapies.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Cancer develops due to changes in the DNA within cells. Some people inherit these genetic mutations from their parents, but most mutations occur during a person's lifetime due to errors in cell division or damage to DNA caused by environmental exposures, known as carcinogens.

  • Tobacco use: The leading preventable cause of cancer worldwide, responsible for numerous cancer types beyond the lungs.
  • Infections: Certain viruses and bacteria significantly raise cancer risk. For instance, high-risk human papillomaviruses (HPV) are linked to cervical cancer, and hepatitis B and C viruses are linked to liver cancer.
  • Diet and Lifestyle: Obesity, physical inactivity, and diets high in processed meats contribute to various gastrointestinal and hormonal cancers.
  • Radiation and Chemicals: Ultraviolet radiation from sunlight and occupational exposure to substances like asbestos or benzene alter cellular DNA over time.

Understanding these risk factors helps nurses target health education and community screening programs toward vulnerable populations.

Clinical Manifestations and Warning Signs

Cancer symptoms vary widely depending on the organ affected, the size of the tumor, and whether it has spread. However, certain systemic signs often alert clinicians to investigate further.

Unexplained weight loss of ten pounds or more is frequently one of the first noticeable signs of cancers involving the pancreas, stomach, esophagus, or lung. Persistent fatigue that does not improve with rest is another common complaint, often resulting from the body's increased metabolic demands and anemia.

To help the public recognize potential danger signs early, health workers use simple mnemonic tools. Changes in bowel or bladder habits, a sore that does not heal, unusual bleeding or discharge, thickening or a lump in the breast or elsewhere, indigestion or difficulty swallowing, obvious change in a wart or mole, and a nagging cough or hoarseness are classic warning flags that require prompt medical evaluation.

Diagnostic Procedures and Staging

Diagnosing cancer requires a combination of laboratory tests, imaging studies, and tissue sampling. Blood tests can reveal abnormal blood counts or tumor markers, while imaging techniques like X-rays, CT scans, MRIs, and ultrasounds help locate internal masses. However, a definitive diagnosis almost always requires a biopsy, where a small tissue sample is removed and examined under a microscope by a pathologist.

Once cancer is confirmed, staging determines how far the disease has spread. The widely used TNM system evaluates three criteria:

  • T: The size and extent of the primary tumor.
  • N: The number of nearby lymph nodes involved.
  • M: Whether the cancer has metastasized to distant organs.

Accurate staging guides treatment planning and gives patients a clearer picture of their prognosis.

Medical and Surgical Treatments

Cancer treatment depends on the type, stage, and overall health of the patient. Treatment plans often combine several modalities to achieve the best outcome.

  • Surgery: Used to remove the primary tumor and surrounding tissue. It can be curative for localized cancers or palliative to relieve symptoms caused by bulk pressure.
  • Chemotherapy: Uses powerful medications to kill fast-growing cancer cells throughout the body. Because it also affects healthy rapidly dividing cells like hair follicles and bone marrow lining, patients often experience hair loss, fatigue, and immune suppression.
  • Radiation Therapy: Uses high-energy rays to destroy cancer cells and shrink tumors in a specific, targeted area of the body.
  • Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy: Newer approaches that specifically target the genetic changes unique to cancer cells or harness the patient's own immune system to fight the disease.

Nursing Management and Home Care

Caring for cancer patients outside the hospital requires careful coordination, education, and symptom control. Nurses play a vital role in preparing patients and families for managing side effects at home.

Infection prevention is paramount, especially for patients experiencing chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. Nurses must teach families about rigorous hand hygiene, avoiding crowds, and reporting any temperature spike immediately. Nutritional support is equally critical; small, frequent, high-protein meals can help combat cachexia and severe fatigue.

Pain management is a cornerstone of home nursing care. Nurses educate patients on taking prescribed analgesics on a regular schedule rather than waiting for pain to become severe, ensuring a better quality of life and preventing unnecessary suffering.

Palliative Care and End-of-Life Support

Palliative care focuses on improving the quality of life for patients and their families facing serious, life-limiting illnesses like advanced cancer. Unlike hospice care, which is reserved for the final months of life, palliative care can be initiated at the time of diagnosis alongside curative treatments.

The goal is to relieve physical symptoms such as pain, nausea, and shortness of breath, while also providing psychological, social, and spiritual support. As a patient nears the end of life, nursing care shifts entirely toward comfort measures, ensuring a peaceful and dignified transition.

Nurses support grieving families by explaining physical changes that occur during the dying process, managing distressing symptoms, and offering empathetic listening and bereavement support.
