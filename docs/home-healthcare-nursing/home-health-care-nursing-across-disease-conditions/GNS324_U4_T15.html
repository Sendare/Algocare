Cancer

Introduction to Cancer and Pathophysiology

Cancer is a broad term used to describe a large group of diseases characterized by the uncontrolled growth and spread of abnormal cells in the body. Normally, human cells grow, divide to form new cells as the body needs them, and die when they grow old or get damaged. In cancer, this orderly process breaks down. As cells become more and more abnormal, old or damaged cells survive when they should die, and new cells form when they are not needed. These extra cells can divide without stopping and may form growths called tumors. Tumors can be benign, meaning they do not spread to other parts of the body or invade nearby tissues, or malignant, meaning they can invade nearby tissues and spread to distant parts of the body through the blood and lymphatic systems, a process known as metastasis. Understanding pathophysiology requires recognizing that cancer originates from genetic mutations or epigenetic alterations in proto-oncogenes, which promote cell growth, and tumor suppressor genes, which normally inhibit cell division and repair DNA errors. When proto-oncogenes mutate into hyperactive oncogenes, or tumor suppressor genes are inactivated, cells lose their regulatory controls. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice, grasping these cellular mechanisms is crucial for understanding why treatments like chemotherapy and radiation specifically target rapidly dividing cells, and why patients experience systemic symptoms as cellular pathways are disrupted throughout the body.

Common Types and Classifications of Cancer

Cancers are classified broadly by the type of tissue in which they originate. Understanding these categories helps healthcare professionals anticipate clinical presentations, treatment responses, and specific nursing care needs. Carcinomas are the most common type, originating in the epithelial cells that line the inside and outside surfaces of the body, including organs such as the breast, lungs, prostate, and colon. Sarcomas begin in the connective or supportive tissues of the body, such as bones, muscles, fat, blood vessels, and fibrous tissues. Leukemias are cancers of the blood-forming tissues, such as the bone marrow, characterized by the mass production of abnormal white blood cells that do not function properly. Lymphomas and multiple myelomas originate in the cells of the immune system. Additionally, cancers are graded based on how abnormal the cells look under a microscope (histological grade) and staged based on the extent of the disease within the body. Cancers that remain localized are much easier to treat than those that have metastasized to vital organs. For nursing students in Nigeria, recognizing these classifications assists in interpreting diagnostic reports and collaborating effectively with the multidisciplinary oncology team to provide tailored patient education and support.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

The etiology of cancer involves a complex interplay of genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors. In the Nigerian context, specific risk factors significantly influence the epidemiological landscape of oncology nursing. Infectious agents play a prominent role: Human Papillomavirus (HPV) is strongly linked to cervical cancer, which remains a leading cause of cancer mortality among Nigerian women; Hepatitis B and C viruses are major contributors to hepatocellular carcinoma (liver cancer); and Epstein-Barr virus is associated with Burkitt lymphoma, highly prevalent in parts of sub-Saharan Africa. Environmental exposures, such as prolonged contact with chemical carcinogens, heavy metals, and indoor air pollution from biomass fuels (firewood cooking), further elevate respiratory and systemic cancer risks. Lifestyle factors, including tobacco use, excessive alcohol consumption, and diets high in processed foods combined with physical inactivity, also contribute significantly. Furthermore, late presentation to healthcare facilities is compounded by socioeconomic barriers, cultural beliefs, traditional healing practices, and inadequate diagnostic infrastructure. As nursing professionals, understanding these contextual risk factors enables targeted community health education, early screening campaigns, and culturally sensitive interventions aimed at reducing cancer incidence and improving health-seeking behaviors across Nigerian communities.

Clinical Manifestations and Early Warning Signs

Clinical manifestations of cancer vary widely depending on the organ of origin, size of the tumor, and degree of metastasis. However, nurses must be vigilant in recognizing both local symptoms and constitutional warning signs. Local symptoms include palpable masses, persistent pain due to nerve compression or tissue stretching, obstruction of hollow organs (such as bowel changes or dyspnea), and ulceration or bleeding. Systemic or constitutional manifestations often present as unexplained weight loss, chronic fatigue, persistent low-grade fever, night sweats, and anorexia. The American Cancer Society utilizes the acronym CAUTION to help identify early warning signs: C for Change in bowel or bladder habits, A for A sore that does not heal, U for Unusual bleeding or discharge, T for Thickening or lump in the breast or elsewhere, I for Indigestion or difficulty in swallowing, O for Obvious change in a wart or mole, and N for Nagging cough or hoarseness. In Nigeria, because patients frequently present at advanced stages, nursing assessments must include thorough reviews of these warning signs during community outreaches and primary care visits. Early identification and prompt referral can drastically improve prognosis and reduce morbidity associated with delayed diagnoses.

Diagnostic Procedures and Staging

Accurate diagnosis and staging are essential prerequisites for formulating an effective cancer treatment plan. Diagnostic procedures begin with a comprehensive health history and physical examination, followed by laboratory tests such as complete blood counts, tumor markers (e.g., PSA, CA-125, AFP), and biochemical profiles. Imaging modalities including X-rays, ultrasound, computed tomography (CT) scans, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET) scans help locate tumors and assess involvement of surrounding structures. However, definitive diagnosis almost invariably requires a tissue biopsy, which is examined histopathologically by a pathologist to confirm malignancy and cell type. Once cancer is diagnosed, staging determines the extent of the disease. The most widely used staging system is the TNM system, where 'T' describes the size and extent of the primary tumor, 'N' indicates the extent of regional lymph node involvement, and 'M' signifies the presence or absence of distant metastasis. Staging allows healthcare providers to estimate prognosis, plan targeted therapies, and communicate standardized clinical findings across multidisciplinary care teams. Nurses play a vital role in preparing patients emotionally and physically for diagnostic tests, managing post-biopsy care, and explaining the staging results in understandable terms.

Medical and Surgical Treatments

Medical and surgical treatments for cancer are tailored to the type, stage, and overall health status of the patient, often utilizing a multimodality approach involving surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormone therapy, and immunotherapy. Surgery may be performed for prophylactic, diagnostic, curative, or palliative purposes. Curative surgery involves the complete removal of the localized tumor and surrounding margin of healthy tissue. Chemotherapy utilizes cytotoxic drugs to destroy rapidly dividing cancer cells, though it also affects normal fast-growing cells such as those in the bone marrow, hair follicles, and gastrointestinal tract, leading to side effects like alopecia, myelosuppression, and mucositis. Radiation therapy uses high-energy ionizing radiation to damage the DNA of cancer cells, shrinking tumors or eliminating residual cells after surgery. Targeted therapies and immunotherapies represent modern advancements that harness the body's immune system or focus on specific molecular targets unique to cancer cells, minimizing damage to normal tissues. Nurses administering these treatments must monitor closely for adverse reactions, manage toxicity symptoms proactively, provide meticulous vascular access care (such as central venous catheter maintenance), and ensure safe handling and disposal of hazardous chemotherapeutic agents according to institutional protocols.

Home Healthcare Nursing Management for Cancer Patients

Home healthcare nursing management is essential for cancer patients transitioning from hospital to home settings, particularly in resource-constrained environments like Nigeria. Nurses provide continuous assessment, coordinate home care services, and educate family caregivers on managing symptoms and treatment side effects. Key areas of home care include pain management adhering to the WHO analgesic ladder, nutritional support for patients experiencing anorexia or cachexia, wound care for malignant skin lesions, and prevention of complications such as infection due to neutropenia and deep vein thrombosis due to immobility. Psychosocial support is equally critical, as patients and families frequently navigate anxiety, depression, and financial strain. Nurses facilitate access to community resources, support groups, and spiritual care. In settings where specialized oncology clinics are distant, home care nurses act as a vital lifeline, teaching family members how to recognize red-flag symptoms (such as uncontrolled bleeding, high fever, or severe breathing difficulties) that require immediate hospital readmission. Empowering family caregivers through clear, practical instructions ensures patient safety, dignity, and optimal quality of life throughout the continuum of chronic and advanced cancer care.

Palliative Care and End-of-Life Support

Palliative care and end-of-life support focus on improving the quality of life for patients and their families facing life-threatening illness. Unlike curative treatments, palliative care is applicable early in the disease course, concurrently with cancer therapies, and is not limited to terminal stages. It involves meticulous management of distressing physical symptoms such as severe pain, dyspnea, nausea, and fatigue, while addressing psychological, social, and spiritual needs. End-of-life nursing care requires deep empathy, cultural sensitivity, and open communication with patients and their families as death approaches. Nurses help patients articulate their wishes through advance care planning, manage terminal restlessness, provide comfort measures, and support grieving families through the dying process and bereavement. In the Nigerian cultural and religious context, end-of-life care must respect familial decision-making structures, spiritual beliefs, and traditional customs surrounding death and burial. Nurses act as advocates ensuring comfort, dignity, and pain-free passing, transforming the final stages of life into a period of peace and compassionate closure for both the patient and their loved ones.
