Diabetes Mellitus

Pathophysiology and Classification of Diabetes Mellitus

Diabetes mellitus is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. In Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus, an autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells leads to absolute insulin deficiency, requiring lifelong exogenous insulin replacement. Conversely, Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus involves a combination of peripheral insulin resistance and progressive secretory defect by pancreatic beta cells. Gestational diabetes mellitus is glucose intolerance recognized during pregnancy, posing risks to both mother and fetus if unmanaged.

The disruption in carbohydrate, fat, and protein metabolism stems from a lack of effective insulin. Without insulin, glucose cannot enter target cells like myocytes and adipocytes for energy, leaving the bloodstream overloaded. This cellular starvation triggers glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver, worsening the hyperglycemia. Fat breakdown accelerates, releasing free fatty acids that can lead to ketone body formation when insulin is severely deficient.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment

The classic presentation of uncontrolled hyperglycemia involves three distinct clinical signs, frequently taught as the cardinal symptoms of diabetes:

  • Polyuria: Excessive urination caused by osmotic diuresis when serum glucose exceeds the renal threshold of approximately 10 mmol/L.
  • Polydipsia: Intense, constant thirst triggered by intracellular dehydration as water is pulled out of body tissues into the vascular space.
  • Polyphagia: Excessive hunger resulting from cellular starvation, as the body attempts to acquire energy despite high circulating blood sugar.

Beyond these classic signs, patients frequently report unexplained weight loss, particularly in Type 1 disease, chronic fatigue, blurred vision due to osmotic swelling of the eye lens, and slow-healing dermal wounds. In resource-limited Nigerian clinical settings, patients often present late with advanced complications like neuropathy or foot ulcers because mild early symptoms were ignored or attributed to malaria and general body fatigue.

Blood Glucose Monitoring and Pharmacological Therapy

Self-monitoring of blood glucose provides real-time feedback on metabolic control, empowering patients to adjust therapy and recognize acute glucose excursions. Fasting blood glucose and postprandial checks guide interventions.

Pharmacological management depends heavily on the diabetes classification:

  • Oral Antidiabetics: Agents like metformin decrease hepatic glucose production and improve insulin sensitivity. Sulfonylureas stimulate endogenous insulin secretion.
  • Insulin Therapy: Essential for Type 1 and advanced Type 2 diabetes. Regimens range from basal-bolus schedules to premixed formulations.

Injection technique is critical. Patients must rotate sites—abdomen, thighs, or arms—to prevent lipohypertrophy, a fatty swelling that impairs insulin absorption. In tropical regions like Nigeria, proper insulin storage is a major practical challenge; when refrigeration is unavailable, insulin can be kept in clay pots filled with cool water or insulated containers to maintain potency.

Medical Nutrition Therapy and Cultural Meal Planning

Medical nutrition therapy forms the cornerstone of diabetes management. Nutritional plans must be individualized, culturally appropriate, and sustainable. In West Africa, meal planning involves balancing traditional high-carbohydrate staples like cassava derivatives, yam, and rice with adequate protein, healthy fats, and high-fiber vegetables.

Carbohydrate counting or portion control helps maintain stable postprandial glucose levels. Patients should favor complex carbohydrates with low glycemic indices over refined sugars, which cause rapid glycemic spikes. Spreading carbohydrate intake evenly across meals prevents large glucose fluctuations. Registered nurses must educate patients and their families on modifying traditional cooking methods—such as incorporating beans, melon seeds, and leafy greens—to ensure nutritional adequacy without sacrificing cultural dietary preferences.

Acute and Chronic Complications

Diabetes gives rise to dangerous acute emergencies and debilitating long-term complications if poorly controlled.

Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)Severe insulin deficiency causing lipolysis and ketone accumulationKussmaul breathing, fruity breath, high blood sugar, acidosis
Hyperglycemic Hyperosmolar State (HHS)Severe dehydration and high osmolarity with minimal ketosisExtreme dehydration, profound confusion, very high blood glucose

Chronic complications develop insidiously over years. Microvascular complications damage small blood vessels, leading to retinopathy, nephropathy, and neuropathy. Macrovascular complications affect large arteries, increasing the risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and peripheral vascular disease. Diabetic foot ulcers, resulting from neuropathy and poor circulation, represent a major cause of non-traumatic lower extremity amputations, making daily foot inspection a vital nursing intervention.

Patient Education and Foot Care

Comprehensive patient education transforms passive recipients of care into active managers of their chronic condition. Teaching must encompass symptom recognition for hypoglycemia—such as diaphoresis, tremors, tachycardia, and confusion—and its immediate treatment using fast-acting oral glucose or fruit juice.

Daily foot care education is indispensable in preventing severe ulcerations and subsequent amputations. Nurses must teach patients to inspect their feet daily using a mirror for cuts, blisters, or redness, wash feet in lukewarm water without soaking, dry thoroughly between toes, and wear well-fitting footwear. Walking barefoot must be strictly discouraged, particularly in tropical climates where cuts from stones or thorns can easily go unnoticed due to peripheral neuropathy and lead to severe infection.
