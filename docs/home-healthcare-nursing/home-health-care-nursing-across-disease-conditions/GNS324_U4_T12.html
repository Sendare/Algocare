Amputation

Introduction and Indications for Amputation

Amputation is the surgical or traumatic removal of a limb or part of a limb. While often performed in emergency trauma settings, many elective amputations stem from chronic conditions managed in the community. Nurses encountering these patients must understand the underlying pathology that led to the procedure.

  • Peripheral Vascular Disease (PVD): Poor blood flow, commonly caused by atherosclerosis, leading to tissue ischemia and eventual necrosis.
  • Diabetes Mellitus: Chronic high blood sugar damages nerves and blood vessels, resulting in painless foot ulcers that become infected or gangrenous.
  • Trauma: Severe crush injuries, severe burns, or blast injuries where limb salvage is impossible or unsafe.
  • Osteomyelitis: Severe, unyielding bone infection that destroys bone architecture and resists antibiotic therapy.
  • Malignancy: Primary bone tumors that require removal of the limb to prevent systemic spread.

Surgical Levels and Types of Amputations

The surgical team selects the level of amputation based on achieving adequate blood supply for healing and preserving maximum functional capability for future prosthetic use. Preserving a joint, such as the knee, drastically improves a patient's energy expenditure when walking later.

Upper extremity levels include partial hand, wrist disarticulation, below-elbow (transradial), elbow disarticulation, and above-elbow (transhumeral). Lower extremity levels include toe amputations, transmetatarsal, Syme's (ankle disarticulation), below-knee (transtibial), knee disarticulation, and above-knee (transfemoral).

Surgical flaps can be left open or closed. Open amputations, also called guillotine procedures, are performed when an active infection is present; the wound is left open with skin traction and closed later in a second surgery. Closed amputations involve creating a skin flap that is sutured over the end of the residual bone, forming a smooth stump.

Immediate Post-Operative Nursing Care

The acute recovery phase focuses heavily on preventing hemorrhage, monitoring tissue perfusion, and managing surgical pain. Nurses must keep a tourniquet visibly taped to the head of the bed in case of sudden, massive arterial bleeding from the stump.

Assess the dressing frequently for excessive bright red drainage or rapid expansion. Check distal pulses on any remaining limbs and monitor vital signs for rising heart rate and dropping blood pressure, which signal internal bleeding or shock. Elevate the residual limb on a pillow for the first 24 hours to reduce swelling, but avoid keeping it elevated for longer periods to prevent hip or knee joint contractures. Encourage early deep breathing exercises and frequent position changes to prevent respiratory complications and pressure injuries.

Stump and Residual Limb Care in the Home Setting

Transitioning home requires meticulous hygiene and skin inspection routines. The residual limb must be washed daily with mild soap and warm water, then patted completely dry. Never apply lotion or alcohol to the surgical site or open areas because moisture trapped against the skin promotes fungal and bacterial growth.

Bandaging or using a shrinkage sock helps shape the limb into a tapered cone, which is necessary for proper prosthetic fitting. Wrap the stump in a figure-eight pattern, applying firmer pressure at the distal end and decreasing pressure higher up to encourage fluid return toward the heart. Inspect the skin daily using a mirror to check hard-to-see areas behind the stump for redness, blisters, or skin breakdown caused by friction.

Managing Phantom Limb Pain and Sensation

Patients frequently feel as though the missing limb is still attached. Phantom limb sensation involves feeling tingling, itching, or temperature changes in the missing part and is generally normal. Phantom limb pain, however, involves burning, cramping, or shooting pain that feels like it originates in the missing limb.

Phantom limb pain is real neuropathic pain, not psychological. Nurses should validate the patient's experience rather than dismissing it. Treatments include prescribed medications such as gabapentin or tricyclic antidepressants, mirror therapy to trick the brain into processing visual feedback of two intact limbs, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS).

Mobility, Transfers, and Rehabilitation Exercises

Early mobilization prevents muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, and deep vein thrombosis. Physical therapists work closely with nurses to teach safe transfer techniques from bed to wheelchair, emphasizing the use of the unaffected side for weight-bearing.

Range-of-motion exercises protect joint flexibility, particularly at the hip and knee. Strengthening exercises focus on the upper body for patients using crutches or walkers, as well as the core muscles required for balance. Once the wound heals and swelling stabilizes, the patient begins fitting for a prosthesis, progressing from parallel bars to crutches and independent ambulation.

Psychosocial Support and Body Image

Losing a limb triggers a grief response similar to losing a loved one. Patients frequently cycle through shock, denial, anger, grief, and eventual acceptance. Changes in physical appearance and functional independence impact self-esteem, sexuality, and social interactions.

Nurses provide a non-judgmental space for patients to express emotions. Encouraging the patient to look at and touch the residual limb helps gradual acceptance of their new body reality. Connecting patients with peer support groups who have successfully adapted to life with an amputation provides invaluable practical wisdom and emotional encouragement.

Home Safety Modifications and Preventing Complications

Preparing the home environment prevents falls and secondary injuries. Occupational therapists and home health nurses assess the living space for hazards before discharge.

  • Remove Trip Hazards: Clear loose rugs, electrical cords, and clutter from walkways.
  • Bathroom Safety: Install grab bars near the toilet and inside the shower, and use a shower bench.
  • Lighting: Ensure hallways and stairwells have adequate lighting and easy-to-reach switches.
  • Skin and Fall Prevention: Check the residual limb daily for pressure marks from wheelchairs or crutches, and never attempt unassisted transfers when fatigued.
