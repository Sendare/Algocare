Amputation

Introduction to Amputation and Indications

An amputation is the surgical or traumatic removal of a limb or extremity. In clinical practice, this procedure is considered a last resort when conservative treatments fail to save tissue or preserve life. The primary goal is to remove diseased, infected, or non-viable tissue while creating a functional residual limb, often called a stump, that can eventually bear weight or support a prosthesis.

Common indications requiring surgical amputation include:

  • Peripheral Vascular Disease: Severe ischemia stemming from advanced atherosclerosis or uncontrolled diabetes mellitus.
  • Trauma: Crush injuries, severe degloving, or blast injuries where revascularization is impossible.
  • Malignancy: Aggressive bone or soft tissue tumors that threaten surrounding structures.
  • Infection: Uncontrolled osteomyelitis, gas gangrene, or necrotizing fasciitis unresponsive to high-dose intravenous antibiotics.

Types and Levels of Amputations

Amputations are classified by anatomical site. Surgeons aim for the lowest possible level that guarantees adequate blood supply and primary wound healing, because preserving joints like the knee significantly improves energy expenditure during future ambulation.

Upper extremity levels include partial hand, wrist disarticulation, transradial (below elbow), elbow disarticulation, and transhumeral (above elbow). Lower extremity levels include partial foot (Chopart or Lisfranc), Syme's (ankle disarticulation), transtibial (below knee), knee disarticulation, and transfemoral (above knee). Transtibial and transfemoral are the most frequently encountered levels in general medical-surgical wards.

Pre-operative and Post-operative Nursing Management

Comprehensive nursing care begins before the patient enters the operating theatre. Pre-operative care involves baseline neurovascular assessments, marking the correct surgical site with the surgical team, and providing psychological preparation. Patients often experience profound grief; acknowledging these feelings is vital.

Post-operative care centers on monitoring for hemorrhage and infection. Nurses must perform frequent neurovascular checks on the residual limb, including color, temperature, capillary refill, and distal pulses if a partial limb remains. Elevating the stump on a pillow is common, but prolonged elevation beyond 24 hours must be avoided to prevent hip or knee flexion contractures. Encourage early deep breathing exercises and repositioning to prevent pulmonary complications and pressure injuries.

Stump and Residual Limb Care

Proper care of the residual limb prevents skin breakdown, manages edema, and prepares the tissue for prosthetic fitting. The skin must be inspected daily using a mirror to check for redness, blisters, or skin breakdown, especially over bony prominences.

Hygiene involves washing the limb gently with mild soap and warm water every evening, rinsing thoroughly, and patting dry completely. Avoid applying alcohol, lotions, or oils to the suture line or fragile skin, as moisture trapped under a prosthesis can cause fungal growth and skin maceration. Limb shaping is typically achieved using elastic compression bandages or a stump shrinker sock applied in a figure-eight pattern to reduce swelling.

Pain Management and Phantom Limb Sensations

Patients frequently report two distinct types of discomfort after losing a limb: residual limb pain (surgical incision pain) and phantom limb pain. Phantom limb pain involves tingling, burning, shooting, or cramping sensations originating from the missing body part. It is a real neurological phenomenon caused by mixed signals from the spinal cord and brain, not a psychological delusion.

Management combines pharmacological and non-pharmacological approaches. Adjuvant medications like gabapentin or tricyclic antidepressants are often prescribed for neuropathic phantom pain. Non-pharmacological interventions include transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), gentle massage, mirror therapy, and application of heat or ice to the residual limb.

Mobility, Transfers, and Rehabilitation Exercises

Early mobilization prevents joint stiffness, muscle atrophy, and deep vein thrombosis. Nurses and physiotherapists guide patients through safe transfer techniques, such as moving from the bed to a wheelchair using a sliding board or pivot transfer, always leading with the stronger side.

Range-of-motion exercises must begin early to maintain joint flexibility. Strengthening exercises target the core, upper body, and remaining muscles of the affected limb to prepare for assistive devices like crutches, walkers, or prosthetics. Patients should perform prone lying sessions for 20 to 30 minutes several times a day to stretch hip flexors and prevent flexion contractures.

Psychosocial Support and Body Image

Losing a limb alters body image, independence, and self-worth. Patients frequently cycle through the stages of grief, manifesting as shock, denial, anger, withdrawal, or depression. Nurses play a crucial role by providing an open, non-judgmental environment where patients can express their fears and frustrations.

Encouraging active participation in self-care restores a sense of control. Connecting patients with peer support groups or individuals who have successfully adapted to living with an amputation can significantly improve long-term coping and adjustment outcomes.

Home Safety Modifications and Prevention of Complications

Discharge planning requires evaluating the home environment to ensure safety and independence. Essential modifications include removing loose throw rugs, clearing electrical cords from walkways, installing grab bars in bathrooms, and ensuring adequate lighting along hallways and stairwells.

Patients and caregivers must monitor for complications such as wound dehiscence, local infection (erythema, foul odor, purulent discharge), systemic sepsis, and deep vein thrombosis. Teaching the patient to perform daily skin checks on both the residual limb and the remaining sound limb is critical, especially for individuals with diabetes or vascular disease.
