Amputation

Overview and Indications for Amputation

Amputation is the surgical or traumatic removal of a limb or part of a limb. While often performed in emergency trauma settings or major hospitals, nurses encounter patients recovering from amputation across various care continuums, including outpatient clinics and community health settings.

  • Peripheral Vascular Disease: The leading cause of non-traumatic lower extremity amputation, often driven by uncontrolled diabetes mellitus.
  • Severe Trauma: Crush injuries, high-velocity impacts, or severe burns resulting in unsalvageable tissue destruction.
  • Malignancy: Aggressive bone or soft tissue sarcomas requiring radical removal to prevent systemic metastasis.
  • Intractable Infection: Severe osteomyelitis or gangrene unresponsive to prolonged antibiotic therapy and debridement.

Surgical Levels and Classification

The level of an amputation is determined by the extent of disease or injury, with the overarching goal of preserving maximum functional capacity while ensuring adequate blood supply for wound healing. Below-knee (transtibial) and above-knee (transfemoral) procedures represent the most common lower limb classifications. Preserving the knee joint significantly improves a patient's energy expenditure and mobility potential when using a prosthesis.

Upper extremity amputations are categorized by wrist disarticulation, below-elbow (transradial), and above-elbow (transhumeral) levels. Surgeons aim for functional length preservation, particularly for the hand and fingers, where tactile feedback and grip mechanics are vital for daily independence.

Immediate Post-operative Nursing Care

The acute recovery phase requires vigilant monitoring for life-threatening complications such as hemorrhage and surgical site infection. A large tourniquet should be kept readily available at the bedside in emergency situations if severe bleeding occurs from the residual limb.

Positioning is critical to prevent joint contractures. Nurses must avoid elevating the residual limb on pillows for extended periods after the first 24 hours, as this promotes hip or knee flexion contractures that hinder later prosthetic fitting. Encouraging prone lying several times a day helps stretch the hip flexors.

Residual Limb and Stump Care

Proper care of the residual limb ensures successful skin maturation and prepares the tissue for prosthetic wear. The stump must be inspected daily using a mirror to check all skin surfaces, folds, and the suture line for redness, blisters, or skin breakdown.

  • Hygiene: Wash the residual limb daily with mild soap and warm water, then dry thoroughly by patting rather than vigorous rubbing.
  • Wrapping: Apply elastic bandages or wear a shrinker sock in a figure-eight pattern to reduce edema, shape the limb into a tapered cone, and promote tissue shrinkage.
  • Skin Integrity: Avoid applying lotions, oils, or alcohol to the fresh surgical incision or fragile skin, as these soften tissue and trap moisture.

Managing Residual and Phantom Limb Pain

Patients frequently experience two distinct types of discomfort following limb removal: residual limb pain originating from the surgical site, and phantom limb pain, which is a sensation of pain, itching, or cramping perceived in the missing part of the limb.

Phantom limb pain is a neuropathic phenomenon that is very real to the patient, despite the absence of the anatomical structure. Treatment often combines pharmacological agents such as gabapentin, tricyclic antidepressants, and non-opioid analgesics with non-pharmacological modalities like transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), mirror therapy, and gentle limb massage. Reassuring the patient that these sensations are normal helps reduce anxiety.

Rehabilitation, Mobility, and Transfers

Early mobilization restores physiological function and prevents secondary complications such as deep vein thrombosis and muscle atrophy. Physical therapy begins soon after surgery with range-of-motion exercises and balance training.

Safe patient transfers require careful coordination. When transferring a patient with a lower limb amputation from a bed to a wheelchair, position the chair on the patient's unaffected side so they can lead with their stronger leg and weight-bear safely. Assistive devices like walkers and crutches provide stability during the initial re-education phases of ambulation.

Psychosocial Support and Body Image Adaptation

The loss of a limb represents a profound life disruption that triggers a grieving process similar to losing a loved one. Patients transition through stages of shock, denial, anger, and eventual acceptance.

Nurses play a vital role by providing a non-judgmental environment that encourages open expression of feelings regarding altered body image and loss of independence. Facilitating peer support connections with other successful amputees offers practical encouragement and psychological reassurance that meaningful recovery is achievable.

Home Safety Modifications and Complications

Transitioning back to the home environment requires careful assessment of domestic hazards to promote independence and prevent falls. Simple modifications can drastically reduce injury risks.

  • Floor Hazards: Remove loose throw rugs, electrical cords, and cluttered pathways in high-traffic rooms.
  • Bathroom Safety: Install grab bars near toilets and inside showers, and place non-slip mats on wet surfaces.
  • Lighting: Ensure staircases and hallways are brightly lit with accessible light switches.
  • Infection Monitoring: Educate the family to watch for fever, foul odor, spreading erythema, or purulent drainage from the stump.
