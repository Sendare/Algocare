Mechanism of Erection

Anatomical Structures of the Penis

Erection is fundamentally a hydraulic event driven by specialized erectile tissues within the shaft of the penis. Understanding this mechanism requires recognizing three main cylindrical bodies enclosed by a fibrous sheath called the **tunica albuginea**. - **Corpora cavernosa**: Two parallel columns of erectile tissue forming the bulk of the penile shaft, primarily responsible for the rigidity of an erection. - **Corpus spongiosum**: A single ventral column containing the penile urethra, which expands at the distal end to form the glans penis. - **Tunica albuginea**: A dense layer of white fibrous tissue surrounding the corpora cavernosa, crucial for trapping blood during tumescence. - **Crura**: The divergent proximal ends of the corpora cavernosa attached to the ischial rami, anchoring the penis to the pelvic skeleton.

Vascular Supply and Innervation

Blood delivery to the penile erectile chambers originates from the internal pudendal artery, which branches into the **cavernous arteries** running through the center of each corpus cavernosum. Coiled branches called **helicine arteries** feed directly into the endothelial-lined lacunar spaces or sinusoids. Venous drainage occurs primarily via subtunical plexuses that empty into the deep dorsal veins. Nervous control is a complex interplay of the autonomic and somatic nervous systems. Autonomic input travels through the pelvic nerve (parasympathetic) and hypogastric nerve (sympathetic), converging at the pelvic plexus. Meanwhile, somatic innervation via the pudendal nerve supplies the bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus muscles, mediating rhythmic contractions during ejaculation and rigid-erection phase support.

Flaccid State and Sympathetic Tone

In the absence of sexual stimulation, the penis remains in a state of flaccidity. This condition is actively maintained by continuous **sympathetic tone** originating from the thoracolumbar spinal cord segments (T11-L2). Sympathetic neurotransmitters, primarily **norepinephrine**, bind to alpha-1 adrenergic receptors on the smooth muscle cells of the trabeculae and helicine arteries. This binding triggers intracellular calcium influx, keeping the smooth muscle contracted. Consequently, the helicine arteries remain constricted, restricting blood flow to a mere trickle sufficient only for basal metabolic needs, while venous channels remain wide open.

Neurovascular Initiation and Parasympathetic Activation

Sexual arousal begins with tactile, visual, olfactory, or psychic stimuli integrated within the central nervous system. Signals descend from the brain to spinal erection centers located in the sacral segments (S2-S4). Activation of these parasympathetic pathways overrides sympathetic tone. Nerve terminals release **acetylcholine**, which interacts with muscarinic receptors on endothelial cells. This interaction triggers the biochemical cascade that shifts the penis from flaccidity to tumescence by initiating local vascular relaxation.

Role of Nitric Oxide and Cyclic GMP

The molecular cornerstone of penile erection is **nitric oxide (NO)**, synthesized locally by endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS). Once released, NO diffuses into adjacent trabecular smooth muscle cells. Inside the smooth muscle cell, NO activates the enzyme **guanylyl cyclase**, which converts GTP into cyclic guanosine monophosphate (**cGMP**). This second messenger decreases intracellular calcium levels, leading to smooth muscle relaxation. The signal is ultimately regulated by phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5), an enzyme that breaks down cGMP to terminate the erection pathway.

Trabecular Smooth Muscle Relaxation and Sinusoidal Filling

As cGMP accumulates and intracellular calcium drops, the **trabecular smooth muscle** within the walls of the cavernous spaces relaxes. This relaxation causes the coiled helicine arteries to uncoil and dilate maximally. Blood surges into the sinusoidal spaces under arterial pressure, causing these lacunar networks to expand rapidly. The erectile bodies swell like sponges soaking up water, changing the penis from a soft, dependent organ into an enlarged, lengthening structure during early tumescence.

The Corporal Veno-Occlusive Mechanism

Full rigidity requires more than just arterial inflow; it relies on the **veno-occlusive mechanism** (also known as the corporal entrapment mechanism). As the sinusoids fill, the expanding erectile tissue presses outward against the inelastic **tunica albuginea**. This expansion compresses the subtunical venules between the expanding sinusoids and the rigid tunica albuginea, trapping the blood inside the corpora cavernosa. Outflow is nearly halted, creating high intracavernosal pressure that solidifies the erection. Contraction of the ischiocavernosus muscles further boosts pressure during the rigid-erection phase.

Detumescence and Flaccidity Recovery

Following ejaculation or the cessation of sexual stimulation, the physiological process reverses to restore flaccidity, a phase known as **detumescence**. Sympathetic tone surges once more, releasing norepinephrine. Adrenergic activation causes the trabecular smooth muscle to contract. At the same time, PDE5 rapidly hydrolyzes cGMP, and calcium levels rise inside the cells. Helicine arteries constrict, cutting off high-volume inflow. Simultaneously, the pressure on the subtunical venules is relieved, opening venous channels so trapped blood drains away into the systemic circulation.
