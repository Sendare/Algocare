Mechanism of Erection

Anatomical Structures of the Penis

The human penis consists of three primary cylindrical bodies enclosed in a fibrous sheath called the tunica albufera. Two of these columns run dorsolaterally and are named the corpora cavernosa. They are responsible for the rigid erectile response. The third column, the corpus spongiosum, runs ventrally and contains the urethra. It expands at the distal end to form the glans penis. Inside these erectile bodies is a sponge-like network of vascular spaces called sinusoids, which are lined by endothelial cells and separated by trabeculae containing smooth muscle.

Vascular Supply and Innervation

Arterial blood supply to the penis originates from the internal pudendal arteries, which branch into the cavernous arteries that travel through the center of the corpora cavernosa. These arteries feed directly into the sinusoidal spaces via winding vessels called helical arteries. Venous drainage occurs primarily through a network of subtunical veins that pierce the tunica albuginea. Innervation is supplied by both the autonomic and somatic nervous systems. The pudendal nerve provides somatic sensory and motor innervation to the skin and muscles, while autonomic fibers form the cavernous nerves to regulate vascular tone.

Flaccid State and Sympathetic Tone

In the flaccid state, the penis remains soft due to tonic contraction of the trabecular smooth muscle and the walls of the helical arteries. This contraction is driven by continuous sympathetic tone releasing norepinephrine, which acts on alpha-1 adrenergic receptors. The resulting vasoconstriction limits arterial inflow to a baseline nutritional requirement. Simultaneously, open venous channels allow blood to drain out freely, keeping internal pressure low.

Neurovascular Initiation and Parasympathetic Activation

Erection begins with sexual arousal, which originates in the brain through tactile, visual, or mental stimuli. Signals travel down the spinal cord to activate the sacral parasympathetic outflow (segments S2 to S4 via the pelvic splanchnic nerves). These parasympathetic nerve fibers release acetylcholine at their target endings. Acetylcholine binds to endothelial receptors, triggering a chemical cascade that ultimately leads to the relaxation of smooth muscle in the arteries and trabeculae.

Role of Nitric Oxide and Cyclic GMP

The key chemical messenger in achieving an erection is nitric oxide (NO), synthesized locally by endothelial cells and nerve endings through the action of nitric oxide synthase. Once released, NO diffuses into nearby smooth muscle cells and activates an enzyme called guanylyl cyclase. This enzyme converts GTP into cyclic GMP (cGMP). Cyclic GMP acts as a vital second messenger that lowers intracellular calcium levels, causing the smooth muscle cells to relax. The enzyme phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) breaks down cGMP, ending the signal.

Trabecular Smooth Muscle Relaxation and Sinusoidal Filling

As intracellular calcium drops due to rising cGMP levels, the trabecular smooth muscle cells relax. This relaxation causes the helical arteries to dilate wide open, drastically increasing arterial blood flow into the sinusoidal spaces. The sponge-like lacunae rapidly fill with blood, expanding and swelling the corporal bodies. This active engorgement is the physical phase that changes the penis from a flaccid state to an enlarging, tumescent state.

The Corporal Veno-Occlusive Mechanism

Full rigidity requires more than just filling the sinusoids; it requires trapping the blood inside. As the sinusoidal spaces expand with blood, they press outward against the surrounding inelastic tunica albuginea. This expansion compresses the subtunical venules between the expanding spaces and the fibrous outer coat. This process, known as the veno-occlusive mechanism (or corporal pelvimetery trap), drastically reduces venous outflow while arterial inflow continues, raising internal pressure to rigid levels.

Detumescence and Flaccidity Recovery

Once sexual stimulation ends or ejaculation occurs, the body initiates detumescence, the return to a flaccid state. Sympathetic nerve activity surges once more, releasing norepinephrine and activating alpha-1 adrenergic receptors. This causes the trabecular smooth muscle and helical arteries to contract. At the same time, phosphodiesterase type 5 breaks down remaining cGMP. With arterial inflow restricted and venous channels opened as pressure drops, the trapped blood drains away, and the penis returns to baseline.
