External Male Reproductive Organs

Introduction to the Male Reproductive System

The male reproductive system consists of both internal and external organs working together to produce, nourish, and transport sperm, alongside producing vital sex hormones like testosterone. While internal structures like the testes, prostate gland, and vas deferens handle the manufacturing and fluid composition of semen, the external organs serve two primary functions: delivering sperm into the female reproductive tract and expelling urine from the body. The external structures primarily include the penis and the scrotum, each uniquely adapted through specialized skin, vascular networks, and smooth muscle tissue to protect reproductive cells and facilitate copulation. Understanding these external structures forms the foundation for recognizing normal anatomical variations, performing effective clinical assessments, and identifying common pathologies during nursing care.

Anatomy and Structure of the Scrotum

The scrotum is a cutaneous pouch suspended from the perineal region, positioned behind the penis and below the pubic symphysis. Its primary purpose is to house, protect, and regulate the temperature of the testes, keeping them a few degrees cooler than core body temperature, which is essential for viable spermatogenesis.

  • Skin: Highly pigmented, thin, and possessing numerous sebaceous glands and sparse hair follicles.
  • Dartos Muscle: A layer of smooth muscle beneath the skin that wrinkles the scrotal surface to reduce heat loss when exposed to cold environments.
  • Cremaster Muscle: Skeletal muscle fibers derived from the internal oblique that elevate the testes closer to the pelvic cavity in response to cold or stress.
  • Septum: An internal vertical wall that divides the scrotum into two distinct lateral compartments, each containing one testis, epididymis, and lower spermatic cord.

Anatomy and Structure of the Penis

The penis is the male copulatory organ and the distal conduit for the urinary tract. Anatomically, it is divided into three main sections: the root attached to the pelvic structures, the cylindrical body or shaft, and the expanded distal tip known as the glans penis.

The internal architecture of the shaft consists of three parallel columns of erectile tissue enclosed in a tough fibrous sheath called the tunica albuginea:

  • Corpora cavernosa: Two dorsolateral columns containing vascular sinusoids that fill with blood during an erection, accounting for penile rigidity.
  • Corpus spongiosum: A single ventral column that surrounds the spongy urethra, expanding at the distal end to form the glans and at the proximal end to form the bulb.
  • Prepuce (Foreskin): A fold of loose skin that covers the glans penis, which is frequently removed via circumcision shortly after birth for cultural, hygienic, or medical reasons.

Physiology of Erection and Flaccidity

Penile rigidity and flaccidity are governed by complex vascular, neural, and muscular mechanisms rather than simple skeletal support. In the resting or flaccid state, sympathetic nervous system tone causes constriction of the smooth muscle within the penile arterioles and trabecular walls, restricting blood flow into the erectile spaces.

When sexual arousal occurs, parasympathetic nerve fibers release nitric oxide (NO) within the corpora cavernosa. Nitric oxide activates cyclic GMP, leading to the relaxation of arterial and trabecular smooth muscle. This relaxation allows blood to rush rapidly into the vascular spaces of the corpora cavernosa. As these spaces fill and expand, they compress the subtunica venules against the rigid tunica albuginea, trapping the blood inside. This venous outflow occlusion creates high internal pressure, resulting in a firm erection. Detumescence, or the return to a flaccid state, occurs when sympathetic activity resumes, breaking down cyclic GMP via phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE5) and allowing blood to drain back into systemic circulation.

Vascular and Nerve Supply to the External Genitalia

The external genitalia receive a rich blood supply primarily from branches of the internal iliac artery, specifically the internal pudendal arteries, which supply oxygenated blood to the skin, muscles, and erectile tissues of both the penis and scrotum. Venous drainage flows through a complex network of superficial and deep dorsal veins that ultimately empty into the internal iliac veins.

The nerve supply combines both autonomic and somatic pathways. Autonomic innervation via the pelvic plexus regulates vascular tone for erection (parasympathetic, spinal segments S2–S4) and ejaculation (sympathetic, T11–L2). Somatic sensation from the penile skin and glans travels via the pudendal nerve, providing the sensory feedback loop essential for sexual stimulation and the reflex muscle contractions of ejaculation.

Clinical Assessment and Common Pathologies

Clinical examination of the external male genitalia requires a professional, respectful approach to minimize patient anxiety while thoroughly evaluating for abnormalities. Nurses should inspect and palpate the skin of the shaft and scrotum, checking for lesions, swelling, asymmetry, or tenderness.

Common pathologies encountered in clinical practice include:

  • Hydrocele: An accumulation of serous fluid within the tunica vaginalis of the scrotum, presenting as a painless, scrotal swelling that transilluminates.
  • Varicocele: An abnormal dilation of the pampiniform venous plexus within the spermatic cord, often described as feeling like a 'bag of worms'.
  • Phimosis: A tight foreskin that cannot be retracted backward over the glans penis, which can cause difficulty with hygiene and urination.
  • Priapism: A prolonged, painful erection lasting more than four hours that is unrelated to sexual stimulation, representing a true medical emergency due to the risk of tissue ischemia and permanent erectile dysfunction.
