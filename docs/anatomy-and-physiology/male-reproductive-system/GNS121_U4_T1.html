External Male Reproductive Organs

Introduction to the External Male Reproductive System

The human male reproductive system comprises both internal and external structures designed to produce, nourish, and transport male gametes, known as spermatozoa, alongside the production of key sex hormones like testosterone. While internal structures such as the testes, epididymis, vas deferens, and accessory glands handle gamete maturation and fluid addition, the external structures specialize in copulation and the safe delivery of these reproductive cells. The primary external components are the penis and the scrotum. Together, these organs bridge the gap between endocrine regulation and physical delivery during sexual reproduction.

Anatomy and Structure of the Scrotum

The scrotum is a cutaneous pouch suspended from the perineal region, positioned posterior to the penis and inferior to the pubic symphysis. It is divided internally into two lateral compartments by a vertical septum, with each compartment housing a single testis, epididymis, and the lower portion of the spermatic cord.

  • Skin: Highly pigmented, thin, and possessing numerous sebaceous glands along with sparse hair.
  • Dartos Fascia and Muscle: A layer of smooth muscle beneath the subcutaneous tissue that wrinkles the scrotal skin to reduce surface area when exposed to cold.
  • External Spermatic Fascia: Derived from the aponeurosis of the external oblique muscle during testicular descent.
  • Cremaster Muscle: Derived from the internal oblique muscle, this layer elevates the testes toward the pelvic cavity in response to cold or tactile stimulation (the cremasteric reflex).

Anatomy and Structure of the Penis

The penis serves as the copulatory organ and the distal conduit for the urinary tract. Anatomically, it is divided into three distinct regions: the root, which attaches the organ to the pelvic wall; the body or shaft, the pendant portion containing the primary erectile tissues; and the glans penis, an expanded distal tip.

The structural framework consists of three cylindrical columns of erectile tissue bound together by fibrous sheath known as the tunica albuginea:

  • Corpora Cavernosa: Two parallel columns situated dorsolaterally that fill with the vast majority of blood during an erection.
  • Corpus Spongiosum: A single ventral column that encloses the spongy urethra and expands distally to form the sensitive glans penis.

Physiology of Erection and Flaccidity

Penile rigidity transitions dynamically between flaccidity and erection via vascular and autonomic regulation.

During flaccidity, sympathetic tone predominates, causing contraction of the smooth muscle in the walls of the penile helicine arteries. This limits blood flow, keeping the sinusoidal spaces relatively empty.

During erection, parasympathetic nerve impulses release nitric oxide (NO) within the erectile tissue. Nitric oxide stimulates cyclic GMP production, leading to smooth muscle relaxation in the trabecular walls and helicine arteries. Blood rushes into the corpora cavernosa, compressing the subtunical venules against the rigid tunica albuginea and trapping the blood—a veno-occlusive mechanism. Ejaculation subsequently triggers sympathetic nervous discharge, causing vasoconstriction and a return to flaccidity.

Vascular and Nerve Supply to the External Genitalia

The rich blood supply of the external genitalia originates predominantly from the internal pudendal artery, a branch of the internal iliac artery. This vessel gives rise to the deep artery of the penis, which feeds the corpora cavernosa, and the dorsal artery of the penis, which supplies the skin and glans. Venous drainage occurs through corresponding deep and superficial dorsal veins leading to the internal pudendal plexus.

Innervation is dual and complex:

  • Autonomic supply: Parasympathetic fibers from the pelvic splanchnic nerves mediate erection, whereas sympathetic fibers from the lumbar sympathetic chain drive emission and detumescence.
  • Somatic supply: The pudendal nerve provides sensory innervation to the skin and glans, as well as motor control to the bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus muscles, mediating reflexive contractions during orgasm and micturition.

Clinical Assessment and Common Pathologies

Routine nursing assessment of the external male genitalia requires careful inspection and palpation while maintaining patient dignity and privacy. Clinicians check for symmetry, skin integrity, swelling, or lesions.

Common clinical conditions include:

  • Hydrocele: An abnormal collection of serous fluid within the tunica vaginalis of the scrotum, presenting as a painless, transilluminating scrotal swelling.
  • Phimosis: The inability to retract the foreskin over the glans penis, which can lead to hygiene complications or infections.
  • Priapism: A prolonged, painful erection persisting for hours independent of sexual stimulation, representing a medical emergency that risks permanent tissue fibrosis.
  • Hypospadias: A congenital anomaly where the external urethral orifice opens on the ventral surface of the penis rather than the tip.
