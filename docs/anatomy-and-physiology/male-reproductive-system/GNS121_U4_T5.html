Mechanism of Ejaculation

Anatomy and Physiology of Ejaculation

Ejaculation is the forceful expulsion of semen from the male urethra, occurring as the culmination of sexual stimulation. The process requires precise coordination between the autonomic and somatic nervous systems, acting upon the testes, epididymis, vas deferens, prostate, seminal vesicles, and associated muscular structures. Far from being a single event, the process is divided into two distinct, sequential phases: emission and expulsion. Understanding this dual mechanism is vital for nurses caring for patients with reproductive health issues, spinal cord injuries, or post-surgical complications affecting sexual function.

Neurological Control and Innervation

The nervous system coordinates ejaculation through a complex spinal reflex arc. Sympathetic, parasympathetic, and somatic pathways all play mandatory roles:

  • Sympathetic nervous system: Originates from T10 to L2 segments, mediating the emission phase by contracting smooth muscles in the vas deferens and prostate.
  • Parasympathetic nervous system: Originates from S2 to S4 segments, contributing to glandular secretions and pelvic vasocongestion.
  • Somatic nervous system: Operates via the pudendal nerve, driving the rhythmic contractions of the bulbocavernosus and ischios cavernosus muscles during expulsion.

Phase One: Emission

Emission is the delivery of sperm and glandular secretions into the prostatic urethra, marking the point of inevitable ejaculation. Sympathetic nerve activation causes peristaltic contractions of the vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and prostate gland. At the same time, the internal urethral sphincter contracts to close off the bladder neck. This vital closure prevents retrograde ejaculation, ensuring semen travels forward rather than backward into the bladder.

Phase Two: Expulsion

Expulsion is the forceful propulsion of semen through the penile urethra to the exterior. Once fluid enters the prostatic urethra, sensory receptors trigger somatic motor impulses via the pudendal nerve. The bulbocavernosus, ischiocavernosus, and superficial transverse perineal muscles contract rhythmically at intervals of about 0.8 seconds. These powerful, piston-like contractions generate the high pressure needed to overcome urethral resistance and project the semen outward.

Resolution and the Refractory Period

Following expulsion, the body enters the resolution phase, characterized by a return to the unaroused state. Sympathetic vasoconstriction reduces blood flow to the pelvic organs, leading to detumescence. Concurrently, males experience a refractory period—a time span during which further sexual arousal cannot produce an erection or another ejaculation. The exact physiological mechanisms involve central nervous system neurotransmitters like prolactin and oxytocin, which inhibit sexual drive temporarily.

Clinical Disorders and Pathophysiology

Disruptions along the neurological or muscular pathways of ejaculation lead to distinct clinical disorders. Premature ejaculation involves rapid, uncontrollable release, often managed with behavioral techniques or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Delayed ejaculation or anejaculation can stem from diabetic neuropathy, spinal cord injuries, or retroperitoneal lymph node dissection that damages sympathetic nerve fibers. Additionally, medications like alpha-blockers can relax the bladder neck, causing retrograde ejaculation.
