Mechanism of Ejaculation

Anatomy and Physiology Overview of Ejaculation

Ejaculation is the forceful expulsion of semen from the male urethra, marking the culmination of the male sexual response cycle. This complex physiological event requires the precise coordination of the nervous system, vascular networks, and muscular structures. While erection is primarily a parasympathetic phenomenon, ejaculation is predominantly a sympathetic and somatic event. Understanding this sequence is vital for nurses managing male sexual health, fertility concerns, and post-prostatectomy complications.

Neurological Control and Spinal Reflexes

The neural pathway of ejaculation is an involuntary spinal reflex coordinated supraspinally. Sensory receptors in the glans penis transmit tactile signals via the pudendal nerve to the sacral segments of the spinal cord (S2-S4). From there, ascending signals travel to the brain, while descending pathways from the brainstem and thoracolumbar spinal cord (T10-L2) integrate the sympathetic response. A classic exam pitfall is confusing the spinal level for emission (thoracolumbar) versus expulsion (sacral).

Phase One: Emission

Emission is the first phase of ejaculation, during which sperm and glandular secretions are deposited into the prostatic urethra. Sympathetic stimulation (T10-L2) causes peristaltic contractions of the vas deferens, seminal vesicles, and prostate gland. Simultaneously, the internal urethral sphincter contracts to prevent retrograde ejaculation of semen into the urinary bladder. - **Vas deferens**: Transports sperm via coordinated peristalsis. - **Seminal vesicles and prostate**: Add fluid secretions to form seminal plasma. - **Bladder neck**: Closes tightly to ensure forward flow.

Phase Two: Expulsion

Expulsion, or the second phase, propels semen outward through the external urethral meatus. This phase relies heavily on somatic motor control mediated by the pudendal nerve. The bulbocavernosus and ischiocavernosus muscles undergo rapid, rhythmic contractions occurring at approximately 0.8-second intervals. These contractions create high pressure within the bulbous urethra, overcoming urethral resistance and discharging the ejaculate in spurts.

Resolution and the Refractory Period

Following expulsion, the body enters resolution, a state of physiological calm. Sympathetic tone normalizes, arterial inflow to erectile tissues decreases, and venous outflow opens, leading to detumescence. Concurrently, males experience a refractory period—a refractory phase during which achieving another erection or ejaculation is physiologically impossible. The duration of this period varies widely based on age, fatigue, and hormonal factors.

Clinical Disorders and Pathophysiology of Ejaculation

Ejaculatory dysfunction encompasses several distinct clinical presentations that impact fertility and quality of life. Premature ejaculation involves uncontrollable ejaculation occurring with minimal sexual stimulation. Retrograde ejaculation happens when semen enters the bladder instead of exiting the urethra, often caused by bladder neck incompetence from diabetic neuropathy or prostate surgery. Anejaculation represents the complete absence of an ejaculate, frequently linked to spinal cord injuries or severe sympathetic blockade.
