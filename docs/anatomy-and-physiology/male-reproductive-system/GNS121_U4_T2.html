Internal Male Reproductive Organs

Introduction and Overview of Internal Male Reproductive Organs

The internal male reproductive system comprises a coordinated network of organs responsible for producing, nourishing, transporting, and protecting spermatozoa, alongside synthesizing vital androgens. Unlike the female system, several structures within the male reproductive tract serve dual roles in both the urinary and reproductive systems, particularly the urethra. Development and function rely heavily on the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis. A solid comprehension of these structures provides the clinical baseline required when managing urological disorders, fertility evaluations, and endocrine imbalances commonly encountered in hospital and community practice across Nigeria.

Anatomy and Function of the Testes

Suspended within the scrotum, the paired testes function as the primary male gonads, responsible for both gametogenesis and steroidogenesis. Each testis is subdivided into compartments called lobules containing tightly coiled seminiferous tubules, the literal site where spermatogenesis takes place. Between these tubules lie the interstitial cells of Leydig, which secrete testosterone under the influence of luteinizing hormone. Maintaining the testes at a temperature slightly cooler than core body temperature is essential; failure of testicular descent, known as cryptorchidism, impairs normal sperm production and elevates malignancy risk.

The Epididymis and Sperm Maturation

Once generated in the seminiferous tubules, immature spermatozoa move into the epididymis, a comma-shaped organ resting along the posterior surface of each testis. The epididymis consists of a head, body, and tail, containing a single highly convoluted duct measuring several meters in length.

  • Storage and Transit: Spermatozoa spend roughly 12 to 21 days traversing the epididymis.
  • Maturation: During this transit, sperm acquire motility and the capacity to fertilize an oocyte.
  • Reabsorption: Non-ejaculated or degenerating sperm are phagocytized and reabsorbed by the lining epithelium.

Ductus Deferens and Ejaculatory Ducts

The ductus deferens, commonly called the vas deferens, is a thick muscular tube that continues from the tail of the epididymis. It ascends through the spermatic cord into the inguinal canal, enters the pelvic cavity, and loops over the ureter to the posterior surface of the urinary bladder. Its muscular wall exhibits powerful peristaltic contractions during ejaculation, rapidly propelling sperm forward. Near the base of the bladder, the vas deferens enlarges to form the ampulla before joining the duct of the seminal vesicle to form the short ejaculatory duct, which subsequently empties directly into the prostatic urethra.

Accessory Glands: Seminal Vesicles, Prostate, and Bulbourethral Glands

Spermatozoa account for only a small fraction of total ejaculate volume; the vast majority consists of secretions contributed by three distinct accessory glands:

Seminal Vesicles~60% of semen volumeAlkaline, rich in fructose, prostaglandins, and fibrinogen
Prostate Gland~30% of semen volumeMilky fluid containing citrate, acid phosphatase, and prostate-specific antigen
Bulbourethral Glands<5% of semen volumeClear, mucous fluid that neutralizes residual acidic urine in the urethra

Enlargement of the prostate, known as benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), frequently compromises urinary flow in aging men due to its anatomical position encircling the urethra.

Composition and Physiology of Semen

Semen is a complex, viscous fluid designed to transport, nourish, and protect spermatozoa within the hostile acidic environment of the female reproductive tract. Its normal pH ranges between 7.2 and 7.8, which helps optimize sperm motility and enzymatic activity. Fructose fuels the active flagella of the sperm, while prostaglandins stimulate reverse peristalsis in the female uterus to assist sperm transport. Additionally, seminal plasma contains clotting proteins that temporarily coagulate the semen after ejaculation, keeping it localized, followed shortly by liquefaction via prostatic fibrinolysin to allow free swimming.

Hormonal Regulation of the Male Reproductive System

The functional integrity of the internal male reproductive organs depends on a precise endocrine feedback loop known as the hypothalamic-pituitary-testicular axis. The hypothalamus secretes gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH), which stimulates the anterior pituitary gland to release two key gonadotropins:

  • Luteinizing Hormone (LH): Targets the interstitial cells of Leydig to stimulate testosterone synthesis and secretion.
  • Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH): Acts on the sustentacular cells of Sertoli to promote spermatogenesis and release inhibin.

Negative feedback loops operate continuously: circulating testosterone suppresses both GnRH and LH release, while inhibin selectively inhibits FSH secretion whenever sperm production reaches adequate levels.
