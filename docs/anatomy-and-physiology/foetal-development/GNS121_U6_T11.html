Sutures of the Foetal Skull

Introduction to the Foetal Skull

The foetal skull is anatomically structured to withstand the immense mechanical forces of intrauterine labour and to pass successfully through the maternal pelvis. Unlike the adult skull, the cranial bones of a term foetus are not fused. They remain separated by membranous intervals that allow the cranium to alter its shape dynamically during descent. Understanding this skeletal architecture is fundamental for safe obstetric practice, particularly in resource-constrained Nigerian clinical settings where careful abdominal and vaginal palpation often serves as the primary diagnostic tool for assessing foetal position and pelvic adaptation before instrumentation or augmentation is considered.

Anatomy and Types of Cranial Sutures

Cranial sutures are fibrous joints connecting the flat bones of the foetal vault. These flexible seams accommodate rapid brain expansion and permit overlapping of the bones under pressure.

  • Frontal (Metopic) suture: Runs down the middle of the frontal bone, dividing the two halves from the bregma to the root of the nose.
  • Coronal suture: Extends transversely, separating the frontal bone from the two parietal bones on either side.
  • Sagittal suture: Lies directly in the midline, connecting the two parietal bones from the anterior fontanelle to the posterior fontanelle.
  • Lambdoid suture: Arches across the back of the skull, separating the two parietal bones from the single triangular occipital bone.

Major Landmarks and Fontanelles

Where multiple sutures intersect, the membranous spaces widen into unossified areas known as fontanelles. These soft spots are crucial clinical landmarks used during digital vaginal examinations to determine foetal presentation, position, and attitude.

Physiological Significance during Labour

The presence of sutures and fontanelles transforms a rigid sphere into a compressible, adaptable structure. As uterine contractions exert downward pressure, the unossified gaps allow the cranial bones to flex and approach one another. This reduction in skull diameters prevents excessive pressure on delicate foetal brain tissue and enables the head to negotiate the varying curves and diameters of the maternal bony pelvis. Without this natural compressibility, even an average-sized foetus would struggle to pass through the pelvis, leading to obstructed labour—a major contributor to maternal morbidity and obstetric fistulae in low-resource settings.

Mechanism of Moulding

Moulding is the shaping and overlapping of the foetal cranial bones in response to the pressure exerted by the birth canal. The parietal bones typically slide over the frontal and occipital bones, and one parietal bone may override the other along the sagittal suture. This overlapping significantly decreases the transverse and anteroposterior diameters of the skull.

While a moderate degree of moulding is normal and resolves spontaneously within a few days of birth, excessive or rapid moulding can cause intracranial haemorrhage or tentorial tears. Midwives must monitor moulding closely during vaginal examinations, grading it from zero (bones meeting) to plus three (severe overlapping where bones are locked and irreducible).

Clinical Assessment and Palpation

Assessing the foetal skull through abdominal and vaginal examination provides vital diagnostic clues. During an abdominal palpation (Leopold's manoeuvres), the rigid head can be distinguished from the breech. During vaginal examination, sweeping a gloved finger along the lower uterine segment reveals the suture lines and fontanelles.

Clinicians trace the sagittal suture to find its extremities, differentiating the diamond-shaped anterior fontanelle from the triangular posterior fontanelle. The orientation of these landmarks confirms whether the head is flexed, deflexed, or malpositioned, guiding safe clinical decision-making throughout the active phase of labour.
