Sutures of the Foetal Skull

Introduction to the Foetal Skull

The foetal skull is the largest and least compressible part of the baby during delivery. Understanding its structure is vital for safe midwifery practice because it determines how the baby passes through the birth canal. The skull is divided into two main parts: the base, which is strong and firmly united, and the vault, which consists of several flat bones. Unlike the base, the bones of the vault are not fused together. They are connected by strong membranes of connective tissue called sutures. This flexible arrangement allows the skull to change shape slightly during labour, helping the baby navigate the pelvis without damaging delicate brain tissue.

Anatomy and Types of Cranial Sutures

  • Frontal (metopic) suture: Runs down the middle of the frontal bone, dividing the two halves of the forehead before they fuse.
  • Sagittal suture: Lies directly on top of the head, connecting the two parietal bones from the anterior fontanelle to the posterior fontanelle.
  • Coronal suture: Crosses the skull from ear to ear, separating the frontal bone from the parietal bones.
  • Lambdoid suture: Shaped like an inverted V, separating the two parietal bones from the occipital bone at the back of the head.

Major Landmarks and Fontanelles

Where the sutures intersect, wide spaces of unossified membrane form soft spots known as fontanelles. The two most important fontanelles for clinical assessment are the anterior and posterior fontanelles.

The anterior fontanelle, also called the bregma, is diamond-shaped and sits at the junction of the sagittal, coronal, and frontal sutures. It measures about three by two centimetres and normally stays open until the child is about eighteen months old.

The posterior fontanelle, or lambda, is triangular and smaller, formed where the sagittal and lambdoid sutures meet. It usually closes much sooner, by about six to eight weeks after birth. Midwives use these landmarks during vaginal examinations to determine the exact position and attitude of the foetal head.

Physiological Significance during Labour

The presence of sutures and fontanelles is essential for normal vaginal delivery. As uterine contractions push the foetal head downward, the maternal pelvis exerts considerable pressure. The gaps provided by the sutures allow the cranial bones to move closer together and even slide underneath one another. This adaptation effectively reduces the head dimensions, allowing it to fit through tight pelvic diameters. Without this natural give, many normal deliveries would result in prolonged obstructed labour, putting both mother and baby at severe risk of injury.

Mechanism of Moulding

Moulding is the changing shape of the foetal skull caused by the pressure of the birth canal. During this process, the parietal bones typically slide over the frontal and occipital bones, and one parietal bone may even overlap the other along the sagittal suture. While a moderate degree of moulding is normal and harmless, excessive or rapid moulding can tear intracranial blood vessels and cause severe intracranial haemorrhage. Midwives must monitor the degree of moulding closely during labour using abdominal and vaginal examinations to ensure the pelvis is adequately proportioned for the baby.

Clinical Assessment and Palpation

Midwives and doctors assess the foetal skull through both abdominal palpation and vaginal examination. Abdominal palpation helps determine the lie, presentation, and position of the foetus before labour becomes advanced. During a vaginal examination, the clinician sweeps a sterile gloved finger across the presenting part to feel for the suture lines and fontanelles. By noting whether sutures are open, touching, or overlapping significantly, the clinician gains immediate insight into the progress of labour and can spot signs of cephalopelvic disproportion early.
