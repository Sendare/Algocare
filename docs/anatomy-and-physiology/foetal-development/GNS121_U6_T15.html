Complications of Moulding

Introduction to Fetal Skull Moulding

Fetal skull moulding is the natural overlapping of the cranial bones that occurs as the baby passes through the rigid birth canal during labour. The infant skull is not a single solid bone; instead, it consists of separate flat bones connected by flexible membranes called sutures and wider spaces known as fontanelles. This unique architecture allows the skull to change shape and reduce its overall diameter, easing the descent of the baby. While moderate moulding is a normal, harmless physiological process, excessive or rapid deformation can place undue stress on underlying intracranial structures and blood vessels. Recognizing the difference between adaptive changes and pathological strain is a core competency for midwives and neonatal nurses attending deliveries.

Physiological Versus Pathological Moulding

Not all cranial deformation carries the same clinical significance.

  • Physiological Moulding: Gradual, symmetrical overlapping of bones that reduces skull dimensions without causing tissue damage. The bones easily return to their normal resting positions shortly after birth.
  • Pathological Moulding: Rapid, severe, or asymmetrical distortion caused by disproportion between the fetal head and the maternal pelvis. This excessive pressure can tear bridging veins, rupture dural membranes, and cause acute ischemia to localized brain regions.

A key clinical assessment involves checking whether the cranial bones can be easily separated or if they remain rigidly locked in an overlapping position after delivery.

While caput succedaneum is generally benign and superficial, large cephalhematomas can lead to significant hyperbilirubinemia as the trapped red blood cells break down.

Intracranial Haemorrhage and Vascular Injuries

Extreme moulding places tremendous shear stress on the delicate venous sinuses and bridging veins inside the cranium. When cranial bones overlap excessively, the tentorium cerebelli or the falx cerebri can stretch and tear. This mechanical trauma frequently results in subdural or subarachnoid haemorrhage.

Infants suffering from intracranial bleeding may display subtle early signs such as lethargy, poor feeding, abnormal high-pitched crying, or subtle focal seizures. Nurses must monitor neurological vital signs closely, as rapid intracranial bleeding can cause unheralded deterioration in the immediate neonatal period.

Assessment and Clinical Evaluation

Systematic physical examination of the neonatal head is essential following any complicated or instrument-assisted delivery. Palpate the fontanelles to ensure they are soft and flat; a bulging fontanelle suggests increased intracranial pressure. Inspect the suture lines to determine if the bones are approximated, touching, or severely overlapped and locked. Document the exact location, size, and texture of any swelling, noting whether it crosses suture lines. Routine head circumference measurements help track sudden expansions that might indicate internal fluid accumulation or bleeding.

Nursing Management and Documentation

Comprehensive nursing care for an infant with significant moulding focuses on stabilization, observation, and family support. Handle the infant gently to minimize unnecessary movement that could worsen intracranial stress. Maintain a neutral thermal environment and monitor respiratory status continuously. Document all findings objectively, detailing the degree of moulding, precise descriptions of any scalp lesions, and the baseline neurological status. Clear documentation protects the healthcare team and provides an accurate baseline for detecting clinical deterioration.
