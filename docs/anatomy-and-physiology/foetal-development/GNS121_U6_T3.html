Embryonic Development

Gametogenesis and Fertilization

Embryonic development begins long before sperm meets egg, starting with gametogenesis—the production of mature male and female sex cells. Spermatogenesis yields four functional spermatozoa, while oogenesis produces one large oocyte and polar bodies due to uneven cytoplasmic division. When a sperm successfully penetrates the secondary oocyte in the fallopian tube, fertilization occurs. This fusion restores the diploid chromosome number, combines genetic material from both parents, and immediately triggers the cortical reaction to block polyspermy, ensuring the zygote has the exact correct number of chromosomes.

Early Cleavage and Transport

Following fertilization, the zygote undergoes a rapid series of mitotic cell divisions known as cleavage. These divisions increase the number of cells without increasing the overall size of the embryo.

  • Zygote: The single-celled fertilized egg.
  • Morula: A solid ball of 16 or more cells resembling a mulberry, reached around day three or four.
  • Blastocyst: A hollow structure containing an inner cell mass that will become the embryo, and an outer layer called the trophoblast.

While these divisions happen, cilia and smooth muscle contractions in the fallopian tube transport the developing structure toward the uterine cavity.

Implantation and Placental Formation

Around day six or seven, the blastocyst reaches the uterus and begins implantation. The outer trophoblast cells burrow into the endometrium, secreting human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) to signal the corpus luteum to keep producing progesterone.

The placenta eventually develops from both maternal endometrial tissue and fetal chorionic villi. It acts as a vital exchange organ for oxygen, nutrients, and waste products, while also serving as an endocrine organ that maintains pregnancy.

Gastrulation and Primary Germ Layers

During the third week, the embryonic disc transforms from a simple two-layered structure into a trilaminar embryo through a process called gastrulation. This critical event establishes the three primary germ layers from which all adult body tissues arise:

  1. Ectoderm: Forms the nervous system, epidermis of the skin, and tooth enamel.
  2. Mesoderm: Forms muscles, bones, the cardiovascular system, and the kidneys.
  3. Endoderm: Forms the epithelial lining of the digestive and respiratory tracts, as well as associated glands.

Neurulation and Organogenesis

Following gastrulation, neurulation begins as the notochord signals the overlying ectoderm to thicken and form the neural plate. This plate folds inward to create the neural groove, which eventually fuses to form the neural tube. The neural tube is the precursor to the brain and spinal cord. Failure of this tube to close properly leads to neural tube defects such as spina bifida. Organogenesis then proceeds rapidly across all systems, shaping the basic layout of the internal organs.

Embryonic Folding and Body Shape

While organ systems begin to form, the flat trilaminar disc undergoes dramatic embryonic folding in both the head-tail and lateral directions. This folding converts the flat sheet into a cylindrical tube, tucking the endoderm inside to form the primitive gut tube. Folding also incorporates part of the yolk sac to form the lining of the digestive tract and positions the developing heart and umbilical cord to their proper anatomical locations on the ventral surface of the body.

Teratology and Developmental Anomalies

The embryonic period is a time of immense vulnerability. Teratogens are environmental agents—such as certain medications, alcohol, infections, and radiation—that can cause physical birth defects. The timing of exposure is everything; during the first two weeks, exposure usually causes spontaneous abortion, whereas organogenesis (weeks three through eight) is the most sensitive window for structural malformations. Understanding teratology helps nurses educate expectant mothers on avoiding harmful exposures during early pregnancy.
