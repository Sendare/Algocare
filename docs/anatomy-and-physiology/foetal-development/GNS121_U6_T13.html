Diameters of the Foetal Skull

Anatomical Regions and Landmarks of the Foetal Skull

Understanding the neonatal head requires dividing it into distinct anatomical regions. The cranium houses the developing brain and consists of bones that must overlap during childbirth.

  • Vault (Calvarium): The upper dome-shaped portion made up of the frontal, parietal, occipital, and temporal bones. These bones are soft and compressible because they are not yet fully fused.
  • Face: The region from the orbital ridges down to the chin. It is rigid and does not undergo significant compression during labour.
  • Base: Formed by tightly fused, thick bones at the bottom of the skull that protect vital brainstem centers.

Key bony landmarks help midwives and obstetricians determine the exact position of the baby's head during a vaginal examination. The occiput is the area overlying the occipital bone, representing the back of the head. The vertex is the area bounded by the two parietal eminences and the anterior and posterior fontanelles. The brow (synciput) extends from the root of the nose to the anterior fontanelle, while the mentum is the chin.

Sutures and Fontanelles

Spaces between the cranial bones are crucial for successful passage through the maternal pelvis. Sutures are the fibrous joints connecting the cranial bones, allowing them to slide over one another in a process called moulding.

Where these sutures intersect, wider membrane-covered spaces called fontanelles form. The anterior fontanelle (bregma) is diamond-shaped, measures about 3 by 2 centimeters, and remains open until roughly 18 months after birth. It is formed by the junction of the frontal, sagittal, and coronal sutures. The posterior fontanelle (lambda) is smaller and triangular, formed where the lambdoidal and sagittal sutures meet. It usually closes much earlier, around six to eight weeks postpartum.

Locating these fontanelles and sutures via digital vaginal examination tells the birth attendant the exact presentation and position of the foetus during the first and second stages of labour.

Transverse Diameters

Side-to-side measurements of the foetal skull complement anteroposterior values in defining how the head fits through the maternal pelvic inlet, cavity, and outlet.

  • Biparietal Diameter (BPD): Measuring approximately 9.5 cm, this is the distance between the two parietal eminences. It is the most important transverse diameter and serves as the standard reference for foetal head size on ultrasound scans.
  • Bitemporal Diameter: Measuring about 8.0 to 8.5 cm, this spans the widest distance between the two temporal bones. Because it is narrower than the biparietal diameter, it enters the pelvis readily during early descent.

Clinical monitoring relies heavily on the biparietal diameter. If this transverse measurement is disproportionately larger than the maternal pelvic capacity, cephalopelvic disproportion occurs, requiring careful obstetric intervention.

Oblique Diameters and Attitudes

The degree of neck flexion or extension determines which oblique diameters engage in the maternal pelvis. When the head is intermediate between full flexion and full extension, other pathways present.

The submento-bregmatic diameter measures 9.5 cm and runs from below the chin to the center of the bregma. This diameter engages when the foetal head faces a face presentation with complete extension of the neck. Conversely, the submento-vertical diameter measures about 11.5 cm, extending from below the chin to the highest point of the vertex.

Foetal attitude describes the relationship of foetal body parts to one another. Normally, the foetus assumes a general posture of flexion: the back is curved, the head is flexed so the chin touches the chest, the arms are crossed over the chest, and the thighs are drawn up to the abdomen. Any deviation from this compact attitude forces wider, less favourable skull diameters to present, prolonging labour.

Moulding and Pelvic Adaptation

As the foetal head moves through the birth canal, it undergoes moulding, an adaptive process where the cranial bones shift and overlap at the sutures. This temporary overlapping reduces the skull's overall dimensions to better match the tight curves of the pelvis.

Moulding is a normal and protective mechanism, but excessive or rapid compression can cause intracranial trauma or tearing of bridging veins. Midwives assess moulding during vaginal examinations by feeling the degree of suture overlap.

  • Grade 0: Bones lie side by side with no overlap.
  • Grade 1: Bones touch each other without overlapping.
  • Grade 2: Bones overlap but can be easily separated by finger pressure.
  • Grade 3: Bones overlap firmly and cannot be separated easily, indicating severe cephalopelvic disproportion.
