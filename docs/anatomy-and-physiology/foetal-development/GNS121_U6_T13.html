Diameters of the Foetal Skull

Anatomical Regions and Landmarks of the Foetal Skull

The foetal skull is anatomically divided into the face, the base, and the vault. Understanding these regions is vital for accurate abdominal and vaginal palpation during labour monitoring in Nigerian obstetric wards. The vault, or cranium, holds the greatest clinical significance because it is the largest part of the foetal head that must negotiate the maternal bony pelvis. - **Vault**: Encloses the brain and consists of compressible membranous bones that allow for shape changes. - **Face**: Comprises the fixed, non-compressible bones of the orbital, nasal, and jaw structures. - **Base**: Formed by thick, tightly fused bones that protect the vital brainstem centres. Key bony landmarks serve as reference points during digital vaginal examinations to determine the exact position and attitude of the foetal head.

Sutures and Fontanelles: Significance in Labour

Spaces between the cranial bones are filled with tough membranes. The lines of intersection are called **sutures**, while the wider membranous spaces where multiple bones meet are known as **fontanelles**. - **Frontal suture**: Lies between the two halves of the frontal bone before they fuse. - **Sagittal suture**: Runs directly along the midline, separating the two parietal bones. - **Coronal suture**: Extends transversely, separating the frontal bone from the parietal bones. - **Lambdoidal suture**: Separates the occipital bone from the two parietal bones. The two most clinically relevant fontanelles are the anterior fontanelle (bregma) and the posterior fontanelle (lambda). The anterior fontanelle is diamond-shaped, large, and remains open until about 18 months postpartum. Conversely, the posterior fontanelle is small and triangular, closing much earlier. Midwives rely on feeling these shapes through the cervix to chart progress and ascertain foetal malpositions like occipitoposterior states.

Anteroposterior Diameters of the Foetal Skull

The anteroposterior diameters determine how well the head fits the pelvis depending on the degree of flexion. When the head is well-flexed, a smaller diameter presents to the birth canal, easing labour. | Diameter | Extent / Landmarks | Length | Clinical Implication | | --- | --- | --- | --- | | Suboccipito-bregmatic | From below occiput to center of bregma | 9.5 cm | Presents in well-flexed vertex presentation; most favourable. | | Occipito-frontalis | From bridge of nose to posterior fontanelle | 11.5 cm | Presents in deflexed or moderate attitude. | | Mentio-verticalis | From chin to highest point of vertex | 13.5 cm | Presents in brow presentation; usually causes obstructed labour. | Examiners frequently test these measurements because an uncorrected brow presentation with a 13.5 cm diameter often necessitates a caesarean section.

Transverse Diameters of the Foetal Skull

Transverse measurements cross the skull from side to side, providing key data on the lateral breadth of the head. These diameters correspond closely to the transverse dimensions of the maternal pelvic brim and cavity. - **Biparietal diameter (BPD)**: Measures 9.5 cm between the two parietal prominences, representing the greatest transverse breadth of the foetal skull. - **Bitemporal diameter**: Measures 8.2 cm between the furthest points of the coronal suture at the temples. The biparietal diameter serves as a primary reference point in obstetric ultrasound for estimating gestational age and assessing foetal growth patterns in antenatal clinics.

Submentobregmatic and Other Oblique Diameters

When the head adopts a face presentation, oblique measurements become the presenting diameters. The relationship between the chin and the neck dictates how the head negotiates the pelvic curve. - **Submento-bregmatic**: Extends from the junction of the chin and neck to the centre of the bregma, measuring 9.5 cm. It presents during a completely extended face presentation, allowing safe vaginal delivery if rotation occurs. - **Submento-verticalis**: Extends from below the chin to the highest point of the vertex, measuring 11.5 cm, presenting in an incomplete face presentation. Recognising these oblique dimensions prevents misdiagnosis during prolonged second-stage labour in busy rural maternity units.

Foetal Skull Attitudes and Their Clinical Implications

Attitude refers to the relationship of the foetal head and limbs to its trunk. Normally, the foetus assumes a posture of general flexion, where the head is bowed forward onto the chest, the spine is bowed outward, and the limbs are folded across the torso. Deviations from this normal attitude alter the presenting diameter. Deflexion changes the presenting part from the vertex to the brow or face, increasing the circumference that must pass through the maternal pelvis. Midwives evaluate attitude through Leopold's manoeuvres and vaginal examination to anticipate mechanical dystocia early.

Moulding and Adaptation During Passage Through the Birth Canal

Moulding is the overlapping of cranial bones at the sutures during labour. This natural adaptation reduces the skull's overall volume without injuring the underlying brain tissue, provided the forces are not excessive or rapid. - **Degree 0**: No overlapping of bones; sutures are easily palpable. - **Degree 1**: Bones meet edge to edge without overlapping. - **Degree 2**: Bones overlap slightly but can be easily separated by digital pressure. - **Degree 3**: Bones overlap markedly and cannot be easily separated, indicating severe disproportion or obstructed labour. Monitoring moulding is a core component of the partograph. Excessive or asymmetrical moulding warns the attending nurse of fetopelvic disproportion, calling for urgent escalation of care.
