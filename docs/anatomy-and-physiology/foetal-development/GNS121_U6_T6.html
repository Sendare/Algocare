Abnormalities of the Placenta

Normal Placental Anatomy and Function

The placenta is a temporary organ that connects the developing fetus to the uterine wall, allowing for nutrient uptake, waste elimination, and gas exchange via the mother's blood supply. At term, a normal placenta is a discoid organ weighing about 500 grams, measuring roughly 22 centimeters in diameter and 2 to 3 centimeters in thickness. The maternal side, known as the maternal surface or decidua basalis, is divided into 15 to 20 lobules called cotyledons. The fetal side, covered by the amnion, features the insertion of the umbilical cord and branching chorionic vessels. Understanding this normal structure is essential for recognizing when a placenta deviates from standard shapes or insertion sites during delivery or ultrasound scans.

Shape and Morphological Variations

Placental shape anomalies occur when the organ develops unusual physical configurations. While a standard placenta is discoid, variations can affect clinical outcomes, especially during the third stage of labor.

  • Placenta succenturiata: One or more small accessory lobes develop in the membranes at a distance from the main placenta. Blood vessels connect these lobes to the main mass.
  • Placenta bilobata: The placenta is divided into two roughly equal-sized lobes, with the umbilical cord inserting between them.
  • Placenta circumvallata: The fetal surface has a thick, white, opaque ring around its edges, caused by a double fold of chorion and amnion.
  • Battledore placenta: The umbilical cord inserts directly into the margin or edge of the placenta rather than the center.

Abnormalities of Cord Insertion and Velamentous Vessels

The site where the umbilical cord enters the placenta can create serious risks for the fetus, particularly when blood vessels are left unprotected by Wharton's jelly.

In a velamentous cord insertion, the umbilical vessels travel across the membranes before reaching the placental mass, leaving them unsupported by the protective jelly matrix. When these aberrant vessels cross the internal cervical os below the presenting fetal part, the condition is called vasa previa. During labor or when membranes rupture, these exposed vessels can tear, leading to rapid and catastrophic fetal hemorrhage. Midwives and nurses must carefully review ultrasound scans for low-lying cord insertions to prevent unexpected fetal distress.

Placenta Accreta Spectrum and Invasive Placentation

The placenta accreta spectrum (PAS) describes conditions where placental villi attach abnormally deep into the uterine wall due to a deficient decidua basalis layer. This is strongly linked to previous uterine surgeries, especially cesarean sections.

  • Placenta accreta: Villi attach directly to the myometrium without invading deeply.
  • Placenta increta: Villi invade directly into the uterine muscle wall.
  • Placenta percreta: Villi penetrate completely through the myometrium and can attach to nearby organs like the bladder.

The greatest danger with PAS is profuse, life-threatening hemorrhage during attempted manual removal of the placenta after birth, often requiring an emergency hysterectomy.

Placental Infarctions and Pathological Lesions

Pathological changes within the placenta can impair oxygen and nutrient transfer to the fetus, leading to growth restriction or fetal demise. Placental infarctions occur when areas of the placental tissue die due to interrupted blood flow, often associated with maternal vascular disorders like preeclampsia or chronic hypertension. While small calcifications and minor infarcts are normal findings in a term placenta, widespread ischemic damage severely limits fetal reserve. Other lesions include chorioamnionitis, an infection of the membranes caused by ascending bacteria, and retroplacental hematoma, a blood clot behind the placenta that signals an abruption.

Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Evaluation

Detecting placental abnormalities early allows healthcare teams to plan safe deliveries and prevent catastrophic bleeding. Painless vaginal bleeding during the second or third trimester is the hallmark symptom of placenta previa. Conversely, invasive placentation like placenta accreta is frequently asymptomatic during pregnancy and is diagnosed via routine antenatal ultrasound and color Doppler imaging, which show loss of the normal clear zone behind the placenta and turbulent blood flow. Nurses must monitor pregnant women for sentinel bleeds and report any abnormal ultrasound findings immediately to the attending physician.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Abnormalities of the placenta pose significant threats to both mother and baby. Maternal complications are dominated by severe postpartum hemorrhage, which can quickly lead to hypovolemic shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and the need for emergency hysterectomy or massive blood transfusions. Fetal complications stem from chronic hypoxia, nutrient deprivation, and prematurity caused by early delivery prompted by severe bleeding. When vasa previa or placental abruption occurs, the fetus faces acute asphyxia and high mortality rates if delivery is not expedited.

Nursing Management and Collaborative Care

Care for a patient with a known placental abnormality requires vigilant monitoring and multidisciplinary teamwork. For patients with bleeding or high-risk insertions, nurses must establish reliable intravenous access, ensure cross-matched blood is available, and avoid digital vaginal examinations when placenta previa is suspected. Fetal monitoring helps assess well-being, while maternal vital signs track hemodynamic stability. Postpartum care focuses on watching for excessive lochia, administering uterotonic medications as ordered, and offering emotional support to parents facing complicated deliveries.
