Abnormalities of the Placenta

Normal Anatomy and Variations of the Placenta

The human placenta serves as the vital physiological bridge between mother and fetus, handling gas exchange, nutrient transfer, and endocrine synthesis. A normal term placenta is discoid, weighs approximately 500 grams, and measures roughly 20 centimeters in diameter. It typically consists of 15 to 20 cotyledons on the maternal surface, covered by the basal plate, while the fetal surface is smooth, shiny, and covered by the chorion and amnion, with the umbilical cord inserting near the center. Variations in shape and lobe formation frequently occur without clinical consequence, yet require careful inspection during the third stage of labor: - **Succenturiate placenta**: Presence of one or more small accessory lobes developing in the membranes at a distance from the main placental mass. - **Bipartite or bilobate placenta**: Division of the placenta into two roughly equal-sized lobes. - **Circumvallate placenta**: A condition where the chorionic plate is smaller than the basal plate, creating a thick, raised, opaque white ring at the margin. Failing to notice an extra lobe or a torn vessel can lead to retained placental fragments, postpartum hemorrhage, and severe uterine infection. Nigerian midwives and obstetric nurses must meticulously inspect every delivered placenta, checking cotyledons and membrane edges routinely before discarding biological waste.

Abnormalities of Cord Insertion

The umbilical cord normally inserts near the central area of the fetal surface of the placenta. Variations in insertion location can pose significant mechanical and vascular risks during gestation and labor. - **Battledore placenta**: The umbilical cord inserts directly into the margin or edge of the placental disc rather than the center. While usually benign, it can occasionally weaken structural support. - **Velamentous insertion**: The umbilical vessels diverge in the fetal membranes before reaching the placental mass, leaving them unsupported by Wharton's jelly and highly vulnerable to rupture. - **Vasa previa**: A specific and dangerous subset of velamentous insertion where unprotected fetal vessels cross the internal cervical os ahead of the presenting fetal part. When membranes rupture in cases of vasa previa, the exposed fetal vessels routinely tear, causing rapid exsanguination and fetal death within minutes. Obstetric nurses monitoring clients with known velamentous insertions must watch for painless vaginal bleeding coinciding with spontaneous or artificial rupture of membranes, alerting the medical team instantly for emergency abdominal delivery.

Placenta Previa and Implantation Anomalies

Placenta previa occurs when the blastocyst implants in the lower uterine segment, resulting in the placental tissue overlying or encroaching upon the internal cervical os as the lower uterine segment stretches and thins in the third trimester. Classification relies on the anatomical relationship between the placenta and the cervical os: - **Complete previa**: The internal os is entirely covered by placental tissue. - **Partial previa**: The placental edge partially covers the os. - **Marginal previa**: The placental margin extends to the exact margin of the os without covering it. - **Low-lying placenta**: The placenta is implanted in the lower uterine segment, with its edge lying close to but not reaching the internal os. The classic clinical hallmark is painless, bright red vaginal bleeding occurring after the 20th week of gestation. The initial bleeding episode is rarely fatal, but warning bleeding can precede catastrophic hemorrhage. Crucially, digital vaginal examination is strictly contraindicated in unconfirmed third-trimester bleeding until placenta previa is ruled out by ultrasound, because digital manipulation can dislodge placental tissue and trigger torrential, uncontrollable hemorrhage.

Spectrum of Placenta Accreta and Invasive Placentation

Morbidly adherent placenta, or the placenta accreta spectrum, involves abnormal trophoblastic invasion into the myometrium due to a deficient decidua basalis, frequently associated with prior uterine scars from cesarean sections or curettage. The spectrum is graded by depth of invasion: - **Placenta accreta**: Chorionic villi attach directly to the myometrium without intermediate decidua, failing to separate spontaneously at birth. - **Placenta increta**: Chorionic villi invade deeper into the myometrium itself. - **Placenta percreta**: Chorionic villi penetrate through the entire myometrium and serosa, occasionally invading adjacent pelvic organs like the urinary bladder. This condition carries extreme risk of massive postpartum hemorrhage, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and maternal mortality. Antenatal diagnosis via ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging allows planned multidisciplinary management, often requiring scheduled cesarean hysterectomy with the placenta left in situ to avoid fatal surgical blood loss.

Pathological Infarctions and Degenerative Changes

Placental tissue undergoes various degenerative and pathological changes throughout gestation, reflecting aging processes or systemic maternal vascular disease. While small focal areas of calcification and fibrin deposition are normal physiological signs of a maturing placenta at term, extensive pathological lesions compromise placental reserve and fetal well-being. Placental infarctions result from interruption of maternal blood flow within the intervillous space, commonly linked to maternal vascular disorders such as pre-eclampsia, chronic hypertension, and thrombophilias. Large or multiple infarcts reduce the functional surface area available for gas and nutrient exchange, leading to intrauterine growth restriction and fetal hypoxia. Other pathological findings include chorioamnionitis indicating ascending bacterial infection, and retroplacental hematomas associated with abruptio placentae. Nurses caring for pregnant women with hypertensive disorders must recognize that placental compromise directly threatens fetal oxygenation, necessitating rigorous fetal surveillance.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Imaging

Accurate and timely identification of placental abnormalities relies heavily on modern diagnostic imaging modalities, primarily ultrasonography. Transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound scans allow clinicians to visualize placental location, thickness, and structure with high precision. Transvaginal sonography is exceptionally safe and accurate for diagnosing low-lying placentas and placenta previa, because the probe remains distant from the internal os while providing high-resolution views of the lower uterine segment. Color Doppler imaging enhances the detection of invasive placentation by revealing hypervascular lacunae, turbulent blood flow, and loss of the normal hypoechoic retroplacental zone. Magnetic resonance imaging is reserved for complex cases where ultrasound findings are inconclusive, particularly when evaluating posterior placenta accreta or deep myometrial invasion. Nurses play an essential role in preparing clients for these diagnostic procedures by explaining the scanning process, alleviating anxiety, and ensuring adequate bladder filling when required for specific transabdominal views.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Abnormalities of the placenta create severe physiological hazards for both the mother and the developing fetus. Maternal complications center primarily around massive obstetric hemorrhage, which can rapidly precipitate hypovolemic shock, dilutional coagulopathy, multi-organ failure, and death if prompt surgical and blood-banking interventions are unavailable. Surgical complications are equally pronounced; conditions like placenta percreta often necessitate emergency or planned cesarean hysterectomy, carrying risks of bladder and ureteral lacerations. Fetal complications stem from chronic uteroplacental insufficiency or acute catastrophic vessel rupture: - **Fetal hypoxia and asphyxia**: Resulting from reduced functional surface area or placental abruption. - **Intrauterine growth restriction**: Caused by chronic nutritional deprivation. - **Fetal exsanguination**: Occurring rapidly during vasa previa rupture. - **Premature birth**: Induced by medical necessity following severe hemorrhage episodes. In resource-constrained settings across sub-Saharan Africa, delayed recognition of these anomalies contributes significantly to preventable maternal mortality statistics.

Nursing Management and Collaborative Care

Comprehensive nursing care for clients with placental abnormalities requires constant vigilance, rapid assessment skills, and seamless interprofessional collaboration. Upon identifying vaginal bleeding or risk factors during antenatal booking, the nurse must prioritize maternal stabilization: establishing large-bore intravenous access, drawing blood for baseline complete blood count, cross-matching units of whole blood or packed cells, and maintaining strict bed rest. Continuous electronic fetal monitoring is mandatory to assess fetal tolerance of any sub-clinical bleeding events. If hemorrhage occurs, the nurse coordinates emergency protocols, administers prescribed uterotonics or blood products, and prepares the client for urgent surgical intervention. Psychological support is equally critical; expectant mothers experiencing prolonged antepartum hospitalizations face intense anxiety regarding fetal survival. Nurses must provide clear, reassuring explanations of care plans, involve multidisciplinary teams including obstetricians, neonatologists, and anesthetists, and ensure emergency surgical kits remain immediately accessible at the bedside.
