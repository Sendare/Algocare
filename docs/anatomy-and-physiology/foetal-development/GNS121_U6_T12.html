Fontanelles

Introduction to Fontanelles and Cranial Development

Fontanelles are the soft spots on a newborn's skull where several cranial bones meet. These spaces are made of tough, fibrous connective tissue. They allow the rigid plates of the skull to overlap during birth, helping the baby pass through the narrow birth canal safely without brain injury. After birth, fontanelles provide the necessary room for rapid brain growth during infancy. Without these flexible gaps, the growing brain would face severe pressure constraints.

Anatomical Locations and Structure of Major Fontanelles

A healthy newborn has several fontanelles, but two major ones stand out for clinical assessment:

  • Anterior Fontanelle: Located at the junction of the frontal and parietal bones, shaped like a diamond. It is the largest fontanelle.
  • Posterior Fontanelle: Located at the junction of the parietal and occipital bones, shaped like a triangle. It is much smaller than the anterior fontanelle.

Clinical Assessment Techniques for Nursing Students

Assessing fontanelles requires patience and a gentle touch. Always examine the infant while the baby is quiet and resting in an upright or semi-upright position. Crying, vomiting, or lying flat can temporarily cause a false bulge. Use the pads of your clean fingers to gently palpate the skull. Feel for the size, contour, and tension of the membrane. Document whether the area feels flat, sunken, or raised.

Normal Timeline of Fontanelle Closure

Fontanelles do not close all at once. Each specific gap has its own expected timeline for bone ossification:

  • Posterior Fontanelle: Usually closes first, around 2 months of age.
  • Anterior Fontanelle: Remains open longer, typically closing between 9 and 18 months of age.

Identifying Abnormalities: Sunken and Bulging Fontanelles

A sunken fontanelle often points toward dehydration, which is a frequent concern in pediatric wards due to severe diarrhea or vomiting. Conversely, a tense, bulging fontanelle suggests increased intracranial pressure. This pressure buildup can stem from hydrocephalus, meningitis, or intracranial hemorrhage. Nurses must report these abnormal findings immediately to the attending physician.

Pathological Conditions Associated with Fontanelles

Abnormal timing of fontanelle closure reveals underlying health issues. Delayed closure or an unusually large anterior fontanelle can be a sign of rickets, hypothyroidism, or increased intracranial pressure. On the other hand, premature closure, known as craniosynostosis, restricts brain expansion and causes misshapen skulls unless surgically corrected.
