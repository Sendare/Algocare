Fontanelles

Introduction to Fontanelles and Cranial Development

During birth, an infant's head must squeeze through the narrow birth canal. To make this possible without damaging the brain, the skull is not formed as one solid bone. Instead, it consists of several flat bones connected by flexible, fibrous membranes known as sutures. Where these sutures intersect, wider membrane-covered spaces form, which we call fontanelles, commonly referred to as the soft spots. These membranous gaps allow the cranial bones to overlap during labor—a process called molding—greatly reducing the risk of birth trauma. As the child grows, these spaces provide the necessary flexibility for rapid brain growth and development during the first two years of life.

Anatomical Locations and Structure of Major Fontanelles

While an infant has several small fontanelles at birth, two main ones matter most in routine clinical assessment:

  • Anterior fontanelle: Located at the junction of the frontal and parietal bones, this space is diamond-shaped. It is the largest fontanelle, measuring roughly two to three centimeters across.
  • Posterior fontanelle: Located at the junction of the parietal and occipital bones, this space is smaller and triangular, usually measuring less than one centimeter.

Familiarity with these shapes and landmarks allows nurses to quickly orient themselves during a head-to-toe assessment and accurately document their findings.

Physiological Functions of Fontanelles

Fontanelles are much more than temporary gaps in the skull; they serve vital physiological roles. They act as natural shock absorbers, cushioning the delicate brain tissue from minor bumps and falls as the infant begins to explore the environment. Furthermore, they accommodate the rapid expansion of the brain volume, which triples during the first year of life. Without this expansile capacity, intracranial pressure would rise dangerously as the brain tissue grows, leading to neurological damage.

Normal Timeline of Fontanelle Closure

Tracking fontanelle closure is a standard part of pediatric physical examination. Each fontanelle has a typical window for ossification, the process where soft membrane turns into hard bone:

  • Posterior fontanelle: Usually closes early, around two to three months of age.
  • Anterior fontanelle: Closes much later, typically between nine and eighteen months of age.

Premature closure or delayed closure can both signal underlying developmental or neurological issues, making it vital for nurses to know these timelines by heart.

Clinical Assessment Techniques for Nursing Students

Assessing fontanelles requires patience, gentle touch, and quiet observation. The nurse should perform this assessment while the infant is calm and in an upright or sitting position, because crying, lying flat, or vomiting can temporarily cause the soft spot to bulge. Gently place the pads of your index and middle fingers over the fontanelle area to check its contour and tension. Feel whether it is flat, firm, sunken, or bulging. You should also note the exact size by measuring across its widest points if any deviation from normal is suspected.

Identifying Abnormalities: Sunken and Bulging Fontanelles

Deviations from a normal, flat fontanelle give crucial clues about an infant's fluid and neurological status. A sunken fontanelle—where the soft spot dips below the level of the skull bones—is a classic sign of dehydration, often seen after severe vomiting or diarrhea. Conversely, a bulging fontanelle—which feels tight, raised, and dome-like—indicates increased intracranial pressure. This pressure can stem from conditions like meningitis, hydrocephalus, or head trauma, and requires immediate medical reporting.

Pathological Conditions Associated with Fontanelles

Abnormal fontanelle timing or size can point to deeper medical conditions. Delayed closure—where the anterior fontanelle remains open past eighteen months—can be linked to conditions like rickets, hypothyroidism, or increased intracranial pressure. On the other hand, premature closure may lead to craniosynostosis, a condition where the skull bones fuse too early, restricting brain growth and causing abnormal head shape. Regular head circumference measurements help catch these issues early.
