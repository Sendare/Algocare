Foetal Skull Injuries

Anatomy and Physiology of the Foetal Skull

The foetal skull is structurally adapted to withstand the mechanical forces of labour and delivery while protecting the rapidly growing brain. Anatomically, it is divided into the face, the base, and the vault. The vault consists of several flat bones—the two frontal bones, two parietal bones, and the occipital bone—which are not yet fused. Instead, these bones are connected by wide, fibrous spaces of connective tissue called sutures. Where multiple sutures intersect, larger membrane-covered spaces form the fontanelles, most notably the diamond-shaped anterior fontanelle and the triangular posterior fontanelle. This membranous architecture allows for moulding, a physiological process where the cranial bones overlap slightly to reduce the skull's diameters as it navigates the maternal pelvic canal. Without this flexibility, cephalopelvic disproportion would occur with much higher frequency. Understanding these bony landmarks is essential for clinical palpation during vaginal examinations in labour to determine foetal position and attitude.

Introduction and Classification of Foetal Skull Injuries

Foetal skull injuries encompass a range of traumatic structural damages sustained during the birth process. These injuries can be broadly categorised based on the specific anatomical tissue layers involved, ranging from superficial scalp trauma to deep intracranial haemorrhage. - **Caput succedaneum**: Oedema of the scalp soft tissues superficial to the periosteum, crossing suture lines. - **Cephalhaematoma**: Subperiosteal collection of blood that is strictly bounded by individual cranial bone suture lines. - **Subgaleal haemorrhage**: Bleeding into the potential space between the galea aponeurosis and the periosteum, capable of holding massive blood volumes. - **Linear or depressed skull fractures**: Structural breaks in the cranial vault bones resulting from direct mechanical force. - **Intracranial injuries**: Trauma-induced bleeding within the cranial vault, including subdural, subarachnoid, or intraventricular haemorrhages.

Predisposing Risk Factors and Mechanics

The development of foetal skull injuries is usually linked to mechanical forces exceeding the elastic limits of neonatal tissues. Prolonged or obstructed labour places abnormal, sustained pressure on the presenting part. Instrument-assisted deliveries, particularly mid-cavity or rotational forceps and improperly applied vacuum extractors, substantially elevate the risk of both surface trauma and underlying fractures. Cephalopelvic disproportion, macrosomia, and abnormal foetal presentations such as brow or face presentations also alter the normal vectors of force during descent. In busy clinical settings across Nigeria, delayed referral of obstructed labour cases significantly increases the frequency of severe birth trauma. Recognizing these high-risk scenarios allows intrapartum care providers to alter their management plan, potentially opting for a timely caesarean section before traumatic instrument application becomes necessary.

Clinical Assessment and Neonatal Diagnosis

Identifying skull injuries requires a systematic, gentle physical examination of the newborn head immediately following delivery. The nurse inspects and gently palpates the scalp, noting the size, texture, and exact boundaries of any swelling, mass, or depression. Swelling that fluctuates or crosses sutures must be differentiated from firm, demarcated bony steps indicative of a depressed skull fracture. Neurological assessment is equally vital. The clinician evaluates tone, primitive reflexes such as Moro and grasp, spontaneous movements, and the level of alertness. Lethargy, irritability, or focal neurological deficits can signal underlying intracranial haemorrhage. Diagnostic imaging, including transfontanelle cranial ultrasonography or skull radiography, is frequently employed to confirm the diagnosis and rule out underlying parenchymal brain damage.

Nursing Management and Interventions

Care for an infant with a skull injury centres on close monitoring, minimizing unnecessary handling, and supporting physiological stability. The infant should be placed in a quiet environment where handling is minimized to reduce stress and prevent exacerbation of intracranial pressure. Vital signs, including temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation, must be monitored and recorded at frequent intervals. The nurse should closely observe the head circumference, charting baseline measurements to detect any rapid increases that might indicate ongoing, hidden bleeding such as a subgaleal haemorrhage. Pain assessment using validated neonatal pain scales is critical, and prescribed analgesia should be administered promptly. Parents require compassionate communication, clear explanations of transient conditions like caput succedaneum, and reassurance regarding the often favorable healing trajectory of simple birth injuries.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

While many common foetal skull injuries are benign and resolve spontaneously without intervention, severe trauma can lead to significant morbidity. Hyperbilirubinemia is a frequent complication following the resorption of large blood collections, such as cephalhaematomas or extensive bruising, as red blood cell breakdown overwhelms the neonatal liver's conjugating capacity. In cases involving subgaleal haemorrhage or intracranial bleeding, acute hypovolemic shock, severe anaemia, and neurological impairment can develop rapidly. Depressed skull fractures occasionally require surgical elevation if they exert pressure on the underlying brain cortex, though many correct themselves spontaneously over time. Long-term prognosis largely depends on whether the underlying brain parenchyma was injured during the traumatic event, with superficial bone and soft-tissue injuries typically carrying an excellent recovery outlook.
