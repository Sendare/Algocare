Foetal Skull Injuries

Anatomy and Physiology of the Foetal Skull

The foetal skull is uniquely designed to withstand the tremendous pressures of labor and pass safely through the birth canal. It is divided into the face, the base, and the vault. The vault consists of several flat bones—two frontal, two parietal, and two temporal bones, along with the occipital bone—which are not yet fused together.

Instead of rigid seams, these bones are separated by wide spaces of fibrous connective tissue known as sutures, and at the junctions of these sutures lie the fontanelles, most notably the diamond-shaped anterior fontanelle and the triangular posterior fontanelle. This membranous structure allows the flat bones to overlap one another during descent, a mechanical process called moulage or molding. Molding significantly reduces the skull dimensions to fit maternal pelvic diameters. However, excessive or rapid molding can stretch the underlying dural membranes and rupture bridging veins, predisposing the neonate to intracranial injury even when no external fracture is visible.

Introduction and Causes of Foetal Skull Injuries

Foetal skull injuries refer to damage sustained by the infant's cranium, scalp, or intracranial structures during the antepartum or intrapartum periods, with the vast majority occurring during difficult or instrument-assisted deliveries. While the newborn head is remarkably resilient, extreme mechanical forces can overwhelm its natural protective adaptations.

Common causes and predisposing risk factors include:

  • Instrumental delivery: Difficult or poorly applied forceps and vacuum extractors exert intense localized pressure.
  • Cephalopelvic disproportion: A mismatch between the foetal head size and the maternal pelvic capacity causes prolonged impaction.
  • Macrosomia: Excessively large babies face prolonged second-stage labor and mechanical obstruction.
  • Breech presentation: Difficult delivery of the aftercoming head requires manual manipulation and traction.
  • Rapid labor: Precipitous delivery does not allow adequate time for gradual physiological molding.

Caput succedaneum is a benign, pitting edema of the scalp that crosses suture lines and disappears within a few days without treatment. Conversely, a cephalhematoma is bleeding beneath the cranial periosteum; it is bounded by suture lines and may take weeks to absorb, sometimes releasing enough bilirubin to cause severe jaundice. Subgaleal hemorrhage is the most lethal extracranial lesion, as blood accumulates in the loose connective tissue beneath the galea aponeurosis, holding a massive volume of blood that can lead to rapid hypovolemic shock.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

A systematic physical examination is vital for identifying foetal skull injuries immediately after birth. Nurses and midwives must inspect and gently palpate the newborn's head, noting any localized swellings, depressions, or discoloration.

When assessing scalp swellings, note whether the swelling crosses suture lines, feels soft or firm, or increases in size over time. Measure the head circumference baseline and monitor it closely; a rising head circumference is a red flag for ongoing bleeding such as a subgaleal or intracranial hemorrhage. Neurological assessment is equally critical. Look for signs of intracranial pressure or irritation, including lethargy, hypotonia, high-pitched crying, poor feeding, and seizures.

Diagnostic imaging confirms physical findings. Skull radiography easily identifies linear or depressed fractures. Cranial ultrasound is widely used in the neonatal intensive care unit to detect intracranial bleeding, while computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging provides definitive detail for complex skull and brain injuries.

Nursing Management and Interventions

Care for an infant with a foetal skull injury centers on stabilization, monitoring, and supportive therapy. Once an injury is identified, minimize handling to prevent further trauma or increased intracranial pressure. Keep the baby in a calm, quiet environment with minimal sensory stimulation.

Maintain a patent airway and provide supplemental oxygen if respiratory distress occurs. Closely monitor vital signs, paying special attention to heart rate, oxygen saturation, and neurological status every few hours. If the infant has a large cephalhematoma or bruising, observe closely for hyperbilirubinemia as the trapped red blood cells break down. Administer ordered vitamin K if not already given, and report any signs of neurological decline or shock immediately. Reassuring parents is another key nursing duty; explain that minor injuries like caput or linear fractures usually heal completely without long-term consequences, helping to ease their anxiety.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

The prognosis for foetal skull injuries depends heavily on the specific type and severity of the lesion. Simple linear fractures and mild scalp swellings generally carry an excellent prognosis, healing spontaneously over several weeks with no lasting neurological deficits.

However, more severe injuries can lead to significant complications. Depressed skull fractures may require surgical elevation if they exert pressure on the underlying brain tissue. Intracranial hemorrhages—such as subdural, epidural, or subarachnoid bleeds—can result in post-hemorrhagic hydrocephalus, developmental delays, cerebral palsy, or chronic seizure disorders. Furthermore, severe blood loss from a subgaleal hemorrhage can cause profound anemia and hypovolemic shock, requiring rapid blood transfusion and intensive care support. Early detection and prompt multidisciplinary intervention remain the best defenses against permanent damage.
