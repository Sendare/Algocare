Placenta at Term: Structure and Functions

Introduction to the Term Placenta

The placenta at term is a temporary, highly specialized organ that plays a crucial role in maintaining fetal life and development throughout gestation. By the time pregnancy reaches term, usually around 40 weeks, the placenta has undergone substantial structural and functional maturation to support the metabolic demands of a fully grown fetus. It acts as the physiological bridge between the mother and the developing fetus, mediating vital exchanges of gases, nutrients, and waste products without allowing direct intermingling of their separate blood supplies. In addition to its transport duties, the term placenta functions as an essential endocrine gland, producing hormones that regulate both maternal physiological adaptations and fetal growth. Understanding the anatomy and physiology of the term placenta is paramount for nursing students preparing for clinical practice and professional licensing examinations in Nigeria, as complications involving this organ—such as placental abruption, placenta previa, or placental insufficiency—directly impact both maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality rates. As student nurses, recognizing normal placental characteristics allows for prompt identification of pathological deviations during the third stage of labor and immediate postpartum assessments.

Gross Anatomy and Structural Characteristics at Term

At term, the human placenta exhibits a distinct macroscopic structure typically described as a discoid organ. It measures approximately 15 to 20 centimeters in diameter, is about 2 to 3 centimeters thick near its center, and weighs roughly 500 to 600 grams, which corresponds to about one-sixth of the birth weight of the term neonate. Grossly, the term placenta presents two distinct surfaces: the maternal surface and the fetal surface. The maternal surface, or basal plate, is dark red and divided into 15 to 20 convex areas known as cotyledons, which are separated by grooves formed by decidual septa. The fetal surface, or chorionic plate, is smooth, shiny, and covered by the amnion. The umbilical cord is typically inserted near the center of this surface, branching out into chorionic vessels that radiate across the plate. Inspecting the placenta immediately following delivery is a core nursing responsibility; the nurse must verify that all cotyledons and membranes are intact to prevent retained products of conception, which can lead to severe postpartum hemorrhage and life-threatening uterine infection.

Microscopic Architecture and the Placental Membrane

The microscopic architecture of the term placenta is centered on the chorionic villi, which provide an immense surface area for maternofetal exchange. Terminal villi contain capillaries surrounded by a thin, specialized epithelial covering consisting of two layers: an inner layer of cytotrophoblast cells and an outer layer of syncytiotrophoblast. By term, the cytotrophoblast cells become discontinuous and largely sparse, leaving the multinucleated syncytiotrophoblast as the primary continuous epithelial barrier in direct contact with maternal blood in the intervillous space. The placental membrane, also known as the blood-placental barrier, separates maternal and fetal blood streams. This membrane consists of the syncytiotrophoblast, a basal lamina of the trophoblast, a small connective tissue layer (stroma), and the endothelium of the fetal capillaries. Over the course of pregnancy, this membrane thins considerably to enhance diffusion efficiency, though certain large molecules, bacteria, and viruses can still cross under specific pathological conditions. For nursing students, understanding this microscopic barrier is essential when studying how medications, alcohol, and infectious agents cross from the mother to the developing fetus.

Uteroplacental and Fetoplacental Circulation

Circulation within the uteroplacental and fetoplacental units is vital for sustaining fetal oxygenation and metabolism. Uteroplacental circulation involves maternal blood flowing into the intervillous spaces through spiral endometrial arteries, bathing the chorionic villi where nutrient and gas exchange takes place, and then draining back out through endometrial veins. This high-flow, low-resistance maternal blood circuit depends on adequate maternal blood pressure and uterine perfusion. Conversely, fetoplacental circulation involves deoxygenated fetal blood traveling from the fetus through the two umbilical arteries to the chorionic villus capillaries, where it picks up oxygen and nutrients, and returning to the fetus via the single umbilical vein. Interruption of either of these circulatory pathways—such as through uterine hyperstimulation during labor, maternal hypotension, or umbilical cord compression—can rapidly result in fetal distress and hypoxia. Nurses monitor fetal heart rate patterns closely during labor to detect signs of compromised uteroplacental or fetoplacental blood flow, allowing timely interventions such as maternal repositioning or oxygen administration.

Respiratory and Excretory Functions

The placenta at term completely fulfills the respiratory and excretory requirements of the fetus, as the fetal lungs and kidneys are not yet functional for these tasks. In terms of respiration, oxygen diffuses across the placental membrane from maternal blood in the intervillous space into fetal capillary blood, driven by the partial pressure gradient of oxygen. Simultaneously, carbon dioxide diffuses in the opposite direction—from the fetus to the mother—to be eliminated through maternal respiration. Fetal hemoglobin exhibits a higher affinity for oxygen than adult maternal hemoglobin, facilitating efficient oxygen uptake in the placental bed (the Bohr and double Haldane effects). Regarding excretion, metabolic waste products such as urea, uric acid, and creatinine pass freely from the fetal circulation across the placental membrane into the maternal blood, where they are ultimately excreted by the maternal kidneys. Nurses caring for pregnant women with renal or respiratory compromise must recognize that maternal illness can directly impair these vital clearance and delivery functions, placing the fetus at high risk for intrauterine growth restriction or acidosis.

Nutrient Transfer and Metabolism

Nutrient transfer and metabolism are vital functions of the term placenta, ensuring the fetus receives adequate glucose, amino acids, lipids, vitamins, and minerals for terminal growth and fat deposition. Glucose is the primary energy source for the fetus and is transferred rapidly across the placenta via facilitated diffusion mediated by glucose transporter proteins (GLUTs). Amino acids are actively transported against a concentration gradient, ensuring the fetus maintains higher concentrations than the mother for essential protein synthesis. Lipids undergo complex processing, where maternal triglycerides are broken down and re-esterified by the placenta or transported directly depending on chain length. In addition to transport, the placenta is metabolically active, consuming significant amounts of oxygen and glucose to sustain its own cellular operations. Midwifery and nursing students must understand that maternal nutritional deficits or conditions like gestational diabetes can alter these transfer mechanisms, leading to either macrosomia (oversized baby) or intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR).

Endocrine Functions of the Placenta

Beyond its transport and exchange capabilities, the term placenta functions as a major endocrine organ that synthesizes and secretes numerous hormones, enzymes, and neuropeptides. It produces large quantities of protein hormones, notably human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and human placental lactogen (hPL), also known as chorionic somatomammotropin. hPL modifies maternal metabolism, decreasing maternal insulin sensitivity to ensure an adequate supply of glucose is readily available for transport to the fetus. The placenta also synthesizes steroid hormones, including progesterone and estrogens. Progesterone is essential for maintaining uterine quiescence throughout pregnancy, while estrogens promote uterine growth and enhance myometrial sensitivity to oxytocin as term approaches. Because the placenta relies on precursor molecules from both maternal and fetal adrenal glands to produce certain estrogens, it is often referred to as part of a 'maternal-placental-fetal unit.' Nurses should be aware of these endocrine roles when educating mothers about metabolic changes during pregnancy, such as the development of gestational diabetes.

Immunological Protection and Barrier Functions

The placenta provides essential immunological protection and acts as a selective barrier to protect the immunologically foreign fetus from the maternal immune system. Because the fetus expresses paternal antigens, it could theoretically be rejected as a graft; however, the syncytiotrophoblast lacks classic HLA class I and II molecules, which helps prevent maternal T-cell recognition and immune attack. Furthermore, the placenta facilitates passive immunity by transferring maternal immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies across the placental membrane via receptor-mediated pinocytosis, predominantly during the third trimester. This confers vital humoral immunity to the newborn, protecting the neonate against various bacterial and viral pathogens during early life. While the placenta acts as a formidable barrier, it is not impenetrable. Many drugs, alcohol, carbon monoxide, and infectious pathogens—such as rubella, cytomegalovirus, HIV, and the malaria parasite—can breach this barrier and cause severe fetal harm. Student nurses must emphasize these immunological and barrier vulnerabilities during antenatal health education campaigns.
