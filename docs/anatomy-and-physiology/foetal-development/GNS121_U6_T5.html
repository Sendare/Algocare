Placenta at Term: Structure and Functions

Introduction and Gross Anatomy of the Term Placenta

At term, the human placenta is a discoid organ, typically measuring about 15 to 20 centimeters in diameter and roughly 2 to 3 centimeters in thickness. It usually weighs around 500 grams, which is roughly one-sixth the weight of the newborn. Grossly, you can examine the placenta by looking at its two distinct surfaces: the fetal surface and the maternal surface.

The fetal surface faces the baby and is covered by a smooth, glistening amnion that gives it a shiny appearance. The umbilical cord usually inserts near the center of this surface, branching out large chorionic vessels like the roots of a tree. The maternal surface faces the uterine wall and is dark red, divided into 15 to 20 irregular convex areas called cotyledons. These cotyledons are separated by shallow grooves formed by decidual septa. When inspecting a delivered placenta, nurses must carefully count these cotyledons and check the edges to ensure no fragments remain inside the uterus, as retained tissue causes severe postpartum hemorrhage and infection.

Microscopic Architecture and the Placental Membrane

Deep within the cotyledons lie the functional units of the placenta called chorionic villi. As pregnancy progresses toward term, these villi branch extensively to maximize surface area for exchange between mother and baby.

The placental membrane, sometimes called the placental barrier, separates maternal blood from fetal blood. It consists of multiple tissue layers early in pregnancy, but by term, it thins out significantly to ease transport. These remaining layers include:

  1. Syncytiotrophoblast: A continuous, multinucleated outer layer that directly contacts maternal blood in the intervillous space.
  2. Cytotrophoblast: An inner layer of individual cells that becomes very sparse and discontinuous by term.
  3. Villous connective tissue: A thin layer of connective tissue, also known as the core stroma.
  4. Fetal capillary endothelium: The lining of the fetal blood vessels inside the villus.

Despite the name 'barrier,' almost all substances in maternal blood cross this membrane to some degree, including many drugs, viruses, and antibodies.

Uteroplacental and Fetoplacental Circulation

Blood circulation through the placenta involves two completely separate vascular systems that run side by side without mixing under normal conditions: the maternal circulation and the fetal circulation.

Maternal blood enters the placenta through spiral endometrial arteries, spraying high-pressure blood directly into the intervillous space. Here, maternal blood bathes the branching chorionic villi, exchanging oxygen and nutrients. The blood then drains out slowly through endometrial veins back to the maternal systemic circulation. This entire intervillous space holds about 150 milliliters of blood, which refreshes three to four times every minute.

Fetal blood travels from the fetus through the two umbilical arteries into the chorionic villi capillaries, where it picks up oxygen and dumps waste products. Freshly oxygenated blood then returns to the fetus through a single umbilical vein. Any drop in maternal blood pressure severely compromises this blood flow, quickly leading to fetal distress.

Respiratory and Excretory Functions

The term placenta acts as the lungs and kidneys for the fetus, since the baby's own organs are not yet performing these tasks inside the womb.

For respiration, oxygen diffuses continuously from maternal blood in the intervillous space across the placental membrane into the fetal capillaries. Carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction, passing easily from fetal blood into the mother's circulation to be exhaled through her lungs. Fetal hemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen than adult hemoglobin, which helps pull oxygen across the membrane.

For excretion, metabolic waste products like urea, uric acid, and creatinine pass freely from the fetal blood through the placental membrane into the maternal blood. The mother's kidneys then filter these wastes out of her body. If the placenta fails, these toxic waste products quickly build up in the fetal bloodstream.

Nutrient Transfer and Metabolism

The fetus requires a constant supply of building blocks for growth, which the placenta extracts from the maternal circulation using several different transport mechanisms.

  • Simple Diffusion: Used by water, gases, and fat-soluble vitamins.
  • Facilitated Diffusion: Used by glucose, the primary energy source for the fetus, moving down a concentration gradient via carrier proteins.
  • Active Transport: Used by essential amino acids, calcium, iron, and water-soluble vitamins, moving against a concentration gradient requiring energy.

In addition to transport, the placenta synthesizes vital substances. It stores large amounts of glycogen, iron, and protein early in pregnancy and converts various molecules to support fetal development before the fetal liver and endocrine organs mature fully.

Endocrine Functions of the Placenta

At term, the placenta functions as a massive temporary endocrine gland, producing essential hormones that maintain pregnancy, support fetal growth, and prepare the mother's body for lactation.

  • Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG): Produced mainly in early pregnancy to signal the corpus luteum to keep producing progesterone, though its levels drop significantly by term.
  • Human Placental Lactogen (hPL): Also known as chorionic somatomammotropin, it alters maternal metabolism to ensure the fetus gets enough glucose by making maternal cells somewhat resistant to insulin.
  • Progesterone: Maintains the uterine lining, keeps the myometrium relaxed, and prevents premature uterine contractions.
  • Estrogens: Stimulate uterine growth and increase blood flow to the uteroplacental unit.

Because of hPL action, pregnant women experience a normal state of peripheral insulin resistance during the latter half of pregnancy.

Immunological Protection and Barrier Functions

The fetus represents a semi-allograft because it carries foreign genetic material from the father, yet the maternal immune system rarely rejects it. The placenta achieves this unique immunological tolerance through local immunosuppressive factors and the physical properties of the syncytiotrophoblast, which lacks classic HLA class I and II antigens.

Furthermore, the placenta provides passive immunity to the fetus by actively transferring maternal antibodies, specifically Immunoglobulin G (IgG), across the membrane via receptor-mediated pinocytosis. This transfer peaks in the third trimester, giving the newborn vital protection against common bacterial and viral infections during the first few months of life.

However, the barrier is not foolproof. Many harmful agents, including certain bacteria, viruses like rubella and HIV, and most recreational drugs or medications, can cross the placental membrane and harm the fetus.
