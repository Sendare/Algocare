Placenta at Term: Structure and Functions

Introduction and Gross Anatomy at Term

At term, the human placenta is a discoid, highly vascular organ that acts as the physiological bridge between mother and fetus. It typically measures about 15 to 20 centimeters in diameter, is approximately 2 to 3 centimeters thick, and weighs roughly 500 grams, which represents about one-sixth of the newborn's weight.

Inspection of the organ reveals two distinct surfaces:

  • Fetal surface: Covered by the shiny amnion, through which the umbilical cord inserts, usually near the center, and branching chorionic vessels radiate outward.
  • Maternal surface: Dark red and divided into 15 to 20 convex areas known as cotyledons, which are separated by decidual septa and covered by a thin layer of maternal decidua basalis.

Failure of the entire placenta to deliver promptly after birth can lead to postpartum hemorrhage due to uterine atony. Midwives and nurses must meticulously inspect the maternal surface immediately following delivery to ensure no cotyledon or fragment of tissue remains retained inside the uterine cavity.

Microscopic Architecture and the Placental Membrane

The functional unit of the placenta is the chorionic villus. As pregnancy progresses to term, the microscopic structure of these villi adapts to maximize exchange efficiency by thinning the tissue barrier separating maternal and fetal bloodstreams.

The placental membrane (sometimes called the placental barrier) consists of the layers separating fetal capillary blood from maternal blood in the intervillous space. At term, these layers include:

  1. Syncytiotrophoblast: A continuous, multinucleated outer layer lacking distinct cell boundaries, responsible for hormone production and active transport.
  2. Cytotrophoblast cells: Discontinuous individual cells beneath the syncytiotrophoblast; these largely disappear in terminal villi to reduce barrier thickness.
  3. Villus connective tissue: A thin layer of mesenchymal stroma and Hofbauer cells (placental macrophages).
  4. Fetal capillary endothelium: The lining of the fetal blood vessels within the core of the villus.

This extreme thinning shortens the diffusion distance, facilitating rapid transfer of respiratory gases and nutrients. Despite the name 'barrier,' many substances, including certain drugs and infectious agents, readily cross this membrane.

Uteroplacental and Fetoplacental Circulation

Efficient exchange requires continuous, high-volume blood flow on both sides of the placental membrane without direct mixing of maternal and fetal blood.

  • Maternal Circulation: Uterine arteries deliver oxygenated maternal blood into the intervillous space under lower pressure. The blood bathes the branching chorionic villi, allowing exchange, and then drains back via uterine veins.
  • Fetal Circulation: Deoxygenated fetal blood travels from the fetus through the two umbilical arteries to the chorionic villi capillaries. After picking up oxygen and nutrients, oxygenated blood returns to the fetus via a single umbilical vein.

Normal placental function relies heavily on adequate maternal blood pressure and uterine perfusion. Maternal hypotension or excessive uterine hyperstimulation can rapidly compromise fetal oxygenation, manifesting clinically as non-assuring fetal heart rate patterns.

Respiratory and Excretory Functions

The placenta serves as the lungs and kidneys for the developing fetus throughout intrauterine life.

Oxygen diffuses continuously from maternal blood in the intervillous space across the placental membrane into fetal capillaries, driven by a concentration gradient. Fetal hemoglobin has a higher affinity for oxygen than adult hemoglobin, facilitating efficient oxygen extraction. Carbon dioxide moves in the opposite direction, passing easily from fetal blood into the maternal circulation to be eliminated by maternal breathing.

Metabolic waste products such as urea, uric acid, and creatinine similarly cross the placental membrane from the fetal blood into the maternal blood for renal excretion by the mother. If placental perfusion fails, waste products accumulate in the fetal circulation, leading to fetal acidosis and distress.

Nutrient Transfer and Metabolism

The growing fetus requires a constant supply of energy substrates, building blocks, and micronutrients, all of which are managed by the placenta.

  • Glucose: The primary energy source for the fetus, transported across the placenta via facilitated diffusion (carrier-mediated) independent of maternal insulin.
  • Amino Acids: Transported actively against a concentration gradient, ensuring fetal protein synthesis even if maternal levels fluctuate.
  • Lipids and Electrolytes: Complex lipids are broken down and resynthesized, while electrolytes like sodium, potassium, and chloride move via active transport and diffusion.
  • Vitamins: Water-soluble vitamins cross rapidly by active transport, while fat-soluble vitamins cross more slowly.

Beyond transport, the placenta itself is a highly metabolically active organ, consuming substantial amounts of the glucose and oxygen supplied by the maternal circulation to sustain its own cellular operations.

Endocrine Functions of the Placenta

At term, the placenta functions as a major temporary endocrine gland, synthesizing and secreting numerous hormones essential for maintaining pregnancy and preparing the maternal body for lactation.

Immunological Protection and Barrier Functions

The fetus is a semi-allograft containing foreign paternal antigens, yet the maternal immune system typically does not reject it. The placenta achieves this immune tolerance through various mechanisms, including the expression of non-classical human leukocyte antigens (such as HLA-G) by trophoblast cells, which inhibit maternal natural killer cell activity.

Furthermore, the placenta provides passive humoral immunity by actively transferring maternal immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies to the fetus, particularly during the third trimester. This confers temporary neonatal immunity against common pathogens after birth.

Despite these protective mechanisms, the placental barrier is not impenetrable. Many viruses (such as rubella, cytomegalovirus, and HIV), certain bacteria, and most maternal medications or recreational drugs can cross the membrane, potentially causing fetal harm or congenital anomalies.
