Foetal Skull

Introduction and Importance of the Foetal Skull in Obstetrics

The foetal skull is structurally the most challenging part of the baby to pass through the maternal pelvis during birth. Unlike an adult skull, the bones here are not fused together solid. They remain pliable and separated by membranous spaces. This design allows the head to compress and change shape, a process critical for successful vaginal delivery. Midwives and doctors must understand skull anatomy thoroughly to assess foetal position, monitor progress, and anticipate complications during labour.

Bones of the Foetal Skull

The vault of the foetal skull consists of several distinct flat bones that protect the developing brain.

  • Frontal bones: Two separate bones at the front that eventually fuse together later in childhood to form the forehead.
  • Parietal bones: Two large bones forming the sides and top of the skull, which play a major role in overlapping during labour.
  • Occipital bone: A single bone located at the back of the head, forming the base of the skull.
  • Temporal bones: Two bones situated on the lower sides of the skull near the ears.

Sutures of the Foetal Skull

Sutures are flexible joints made of tough fibrous connective tissue that hold the cranial bones together. They give the skull flexibility and prevent the bones from cracking under pressure.

  • Frontal suture: Runs right down the middle of the two frontal bones.
  • Sagittal suture: Lies between the two parietal bones, connecting the front and back fontanelles.
  • Coronal suture: Runs across the head, separating the frontal bones from the parietal bones.
  • Lambdoidal suture: Separates the occipital bone from the two parietal bones at the back.

Fontanelles and Their Clinical Significance

Fontanelles are membrane-covered spaces where multiple sutures intersect. They provide extra room for brain growth and allow the skull bones to telescope over one another during birth.

  • Anterior fontanelle (Bregma): Diamond-shaped, located at the junction of the sagittal, coronal, and frontal sutures. It typically closes between 12 and 18 months of age.
  • Posterior fontanelle (Lambda): Triangular-shaped, smaller, located where the sagittal and lambdoidal sutures meet. It usually closes much earlier, around 6 to 8 weeks after birth.

Clinical assessment of these spaces helps nurses check for hydration status. A sunken fontanelle indicates dehydration, while a bulging fontanelle can signal increased intracranial pressure.

Regions and Landmarks of the Foetal Skull

To accurately describe the position of the baby's head, midwives divide the skull into specific anatomical regions.

RegionLocation Description
VertexThe area bounded by the two frontal eminences, two parietal eminences, and the posterior fontanelle.
OcciputThe area behind the posterior fontanelle, overlying the occipital bone.
FaceThe area from the root of the nose to the chin.
BrowThe area between the root of the nose and the anterior fontanelle.

Diameters of the Foetal Skull

The size and shape of the foetal head are measured along specific imaginary lines called diameters. The way the baby's head enters the pelvis depends entirely on which diameter leads the way.

  • Suboccipitobregmatic (9.5 cm): The smallest anteroposterior diameter, running from the base of the occiput to the center of the anterior fontanelle. It occurs when the head is fully flexed.
  • Occipitofrontal (11.5 cm): Runs from the bridge of the nose to the occipital protuberance when the head is in a neutral or deflexed position.
  • Mentovertical (13.5 cm): The longest diameter, stretching from the chin to the highest point of the vertex, seen in a brow presentation.

Moulding of the Foetal Skull During Labour

Moulding is the process by which the cranial bones overlap at the sutures as the head navigates the birth canal. Because the sutures and fontanelles are soft and pliable, the skull can alter its shape without injuring the brain tissue inside. This natural adaptation reduces the dimensions of the head, helping it squeeze past tight pelvic bones. After birth, the skull gradually returns to its normal rounded shape within a couple of days.
