Foetal Skull

Introduction and Importance of the Foetal Skull in Obstetrics

The foetal skull is the most critical and least compressible part of the infant body during childbirth. Because it must successfully navigate the rigid maternal bony pelvis, its structural design is uniquely adapted for birth. The skull is divided into the vault, the face, and the base. The vault, or cranium, consists of flat bones that are not yet fused together, allowing them to shift and overlap during descent through the birth canal. Understanding these anatomical adaptations helps midwives and obstetric nurses anticipate dystocia, assess progress during the first and second stages of labour, and recognise potential birth injuries.

Bones of the Foetal Skull

The vault of the foetal skull is formed by several distinct flat bones separated by membranous spaces.

  • Frontal bones: Two bones at the front that eventually fuse together after birth to form the forehead.
  • Parietal bones: Two large, curved bones forming the sides and roof of the cranium, representing the widest part of the vault.
  • Occipital bone: Situated at the back of the head, forming the base of the skull and the area surrounding the foramen magnum.
  • Temporal bones: Two bones located on the lower sides of the skull, housing delicate inner ear structures.

Sutures of the Foetal Skull

Sutures are membranous joints that unite the bones of the foetal skull. These flexible seams permit the overlapping of adjacent bone margins during labour, a process known as moulding.

  • Frontal suture: Lies between the two halves of the frontal bone.
  • Sagittal suture: Runs longitudinally along the midline, separating the two parietal bones.
  • Coronal suture: Extends transversely, separating the frontal bones from the parietal bones.
  • Lambdoid suture: Separates the occipital bone from the two parietal bones, resembling an inverted letter V.

Fontanelles and Their Clinical Significance

Fontanelles are wide spaces where two or more sutures intersect, covered by tough membranes. They provide additional flexibility for the growing brain and skull bones during birth.

  • Anterior fontanelle (Bregma): Diamond-shaped, formed by the junction of the sagittal, coronal, and frontal sutures. It remains open until about 18 months of age and serves as a vital landmark for assessing hydration and intracranial pressure.
  • Posterior fontanelle (Lambda): Triangular-shaped, formed by the meeting of the sagittal and lambdoid sutures. It closes much earlier, typically around 6 to 8 weeks after birth.

Regions and Landmarks of the Foetal Skull

The topography of the foetal skull is categorized into specific anatomical areas that assist clinicians in determining foetal position and presentation.

  • Vertex: The area bounded by the anterior fontanelle, the two parietal eminences, and the posterior fontanelle. This is the most common presenting part in normal deliveries.
  • Occiput: The area situated behind the posterior fontanelle, overlying the occipital bone.
  • Binciput: The region extending from the anterior fontanelle to the root of the nose (glabella).
  • Mentum: The chin, which serves as the reference point in face presentations.

Diameters of the Foetal Skull

The foetal skull presents various anteroposterior and transverse diameters depending on the degree of flexion or extension of the head. When the head is well-flexed (chin on chest), it presents its smallest anteroposterior diameter, allowing an efficient passage through the pelvis. Unfavourable deflexed attitudes present larger diameters, frequently leading to prolonged or obstructed labour.

Moulding of the Foetal Skull During Labour

Moulding refers to the alteration of the shape and dimensions of the foetal skull as it passes through the birth canal under the influence of uterine contractions and maternal pelvic resistance. Because the cranial bones are separated by yielding sutures and fontanelles, they can slide over one another without causing structural brain damage. Excessive or rapid moulding, however, can result in intracranial haemorrhage, severe caput succedaneum, or tearing of the tentorium cerebelli. Midwives must monitor moulding patterns carefully during the active phase of labour using a partograph.
