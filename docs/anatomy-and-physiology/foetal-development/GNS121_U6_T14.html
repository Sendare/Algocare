Moulding During Labour

Fetal Skull Anatomy and Sutures

The fetal skull is structurally adapted to navigate the narrow maternal pelvis during childbirth. It consists of several flat bones that are not yet fused together. Instead, these bones are connected by wide, fibrous membranes known as **sutures**, and at the junctions of these sutures lie the **fontanelles**. - **Frontal suture**: Separates the two halves of the frontal bone in the immature skull. - **Sagittal suture**: Runs along the midline of the vertex, connecting the two parietal bones. - **Coronal suture**: Separates the frontal bone from the parietal bones on each side. - **Lambdoid suture**: Separates the occipital bone from the two parietal bones posteriorly. These membranous spaces allow the rigid bony plates to slide over one another without damaging the underlying soft brain tissue. Recognizing these anatomical landmarks via digital vaginal examination allows midwives and obstetricians to determine fetal position and assess the progress of descent.

Definition and Physiological Mechanism of Moulding

**Moulding** refers to the overlapping of the fetal skull bones at the sutures and fontanelles in response to the compressive forces of the maternal pelvis during labour. As strong uterine contractions push the fetus downward, the bones are forced to shift, reducing the overall diameters of the fetal head. This adaptation is entirely normal and physiological. The parietal bones commonly slide beneath the occipital bone and over the frontal bones. This overlapping temporarily alters the shape of the head, often giving it a slightly elongated appearance after birth, which resolves spontaneously within the first few days of life. Without this inherent flexibility, many term fetuses would experience mechanical obstruction in the birth canal.

Degrees and Classification of Moulding

Clinically, moulding is graded to quantify the degree of cranial bone overlap observed during vaginal examinations. These grades are standardly recorded on a partograph to monitor the progress and safety of labour. - **Grade 0**: Bones are touching each other or separated by a normal suture line width. - **Grade 1**: Bones are meeting edge-to-edge with no significant overlap. - **Grade 2**: Bones are overlapping, but they can be easily separated or disengaged by digital pressure (reducible). - **Grade 3**: Bones are severely overlapping and fixed, meaning they cannot be separated by digital pressure (non-reducible). Grades 0 and 1 are common and reassuring findings. Grade 2 indicates significant pelvic resistance. Grade 3 serves as an urgent warning sign of cephalopelvic disproportion or obstructed labour, requiring immediate medical intervention.

Clinical Significance and Cephalopelvic Disproportion

Moulding acts as a natural indicator of how the fetal head is coping with the mechanical resistance of the maternal pelvis. When the pelvis is roomy and the fetus is average-sized, minimal moulding occurs. However, excessive or rapid moulding suggests a mismatch between the size of the fetal head and the capacity of the maternal pelvis, a condition known as **cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD)**. In Nigerian obstetric practice, where delayed presentation to health facilities can lead to prolonged obstructed labour, tracking moulding on the partograph is lifesaving. Severe moulding combined with caput succedaneum formation can obscure anatomical landmarks and indicate that spontaneous vaginal delivery is unlikely, necessitating an emergency caesarean section or instrumental delivery if criteria are safely met.

Assessment, Partograph Monitoring, and Nursing Documentation

Midwives and nurses evaluate moulding during routine vaginal examinations performed throughout active labour. The findings must be meticulously recorded on the **partograph** in the designated moulding section, directly aligned with the cervical dilatation and fetal head descent curve. Accurate documentation prevents missed warning signs. The nurse must distinguish between true cranial bone moulding and **caput succedaneum**—a diffuse, edematous swelling of the scalp soft tissues that can also cross suture lines. Unlike caput, true moulding involves the rigid displacement of the skull bones themselves. If moulding reaches Grade 3, or if it increases while cervical dilatation remains static past the alert line, the nurse must immediately notify the senior medical officer on duty and prepare the patient for emergency intervention.
