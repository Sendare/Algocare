Moulding During Labour

Fetal Skull Anatomy and Sutures

The fetal skull is uniquely constructed to survive the tight squeeze through the birth canal. Unlike an adult skull, the newborn's head is made up of several individual bones that are not yet fused together. These bony plates are separated by wide spaces of connective tissue called sutures, and where these sutures intersect, larger soft spots known as fontanelles are formed.

The most important sutures to remember in labour ward practice include:

  • Frontal suture: Runs down the middle of the forehead between the two halves of the frontal bone.
  • Sagittal suture: Lies right along the top of the head, separating the two parietal bones from front to back.
  • Coronal suture: Crosses horizontally between the frontal bone and the parietal bones.
  • Lambdoidal suture: Separates the occipital bone at the back from the two parietal bones.

These flexible joints and soft spaces are not design flaws; they are brilliant evolutionary adaptations. They allow the rigid cranial bones to shift, slide, and overlap one another as the baby navigates the varying diameters of the pelvis.

Definition and Physiological Mechanisms of Moulding

Moulding refers to the shaping of the fetal head caused by the pressure of the maternal pelvis during labour. As strong uterine contractions push the baby downward, the overlapping of adjacent skull bones reduces the overall circumference of the head.

This overlapping happens in a very predictable pattern:

  • The parietal bones are usually the most active players. Typically, one parietal bone slides beneath the other, or both slide slightly under the occipital and frontal bones.
  • The flexible membranes at the sutures stretch and compress, absorbing the mechanical forces.

Moulding is entirely normal and physiologic. It acts as a built-in safety valve, protecting the delicate brain tissue from sudden, crushing pressure while helping the head adapt to the narrowest pelvic planes. Once the baby is born and the pressure is relieved, the elastic skull bones gradually spring back to their normal rounded shape within a couple of days.

Degrees and Clinical Classification of Moulding

During vaginal examinations, midwives and doctors assess moulding by feeling how closely the skull bones relate to one another at the sagittal suture. Clinical grading helps healthcare providers determine if the descent is progressing safely or if the baby is struggling against a tight pelvis.

The standard grading scale includes:

  • Grade 0: The bones are touching each other, but there is no overlapping. Suture lines are easily felt.
  • Grade 1: The bones are overlapping one another, but they can easily be separated or pushed back into place with a finger.
  • Grade 2: The bones are overlapping firmly and cannot be easily separated, but the bones are still separate entities.
  • Grade 3: Severe overlapping where the bones are locked tightly together, often with one bone riding high over the other.

Grades 0 and 1 are common and reassuring findings in normal labour. However, Grade 3 moulding is a serious red flag. It tells the clinician that the head is facing intense, prolonged resistance, often pointing to cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD).

Assessment on the Partograph

The partograph is a graphic tool used to monitor the progress of labour and the well-being of both mother and baby. Tracking moulding on this chart is vital because it gives an early warning sign of mechanical obstruction.

When performing a vaginal examination, the birth attendant checks the relationship of the parietal bones at the sagittal suture and records the findings using specific symbols on the partograph grid:

  • A separate line or box is dedicated to moulding.
  • Recording uses the plus system or descriptive numbers (0, +, ++, +++).

A rising moulding score, especially when combined with slow cervical dilation or poor descent of the presenting part, alerts the nurse to intervene. It separates normal, slow progress from dangerous obstruction where the baby cannot fit through the pelvis.

Distinguishing Moulding from Caput Succedaneum

Beginner midwives often confuse moulding with caput succedaneum, but they are two completely different physical findings. Both happen due to pressure on the head during labour, yet their underlying structures and clinical meanings vary greatly.

FeatureMouldingCaput Succedaneum
DefinitionOverlapping of skull bonesSwelling of the scalp soft tissues
Suture linesCan be felt disappearing under bonesCrosses over suture lines
CauseBony compression in the pelvisPressure from the cervix on dependent scalp
DisappearanceWithin a few daysWithin 24 to 48 hours after birth

Caput is simply fluid accumulation under the skin of the scalp caused by restricted venous return from the cervix. Moulding, on the other hand, involves the actual skeletal plates of the cranium. Palpating carefully during a vaginal examination helps the nurse tell the difference between the two.

Complications and Pathological Moulding

While mild moulding is a normal part of vaginal delivery, excessive or rapid moulding can become dangerous. When the maternal pelvis is too small or the baby is too large, the intense mechanical forces do not just shape the head—they can damage it.

Severe, fixed moulding (Grade 3) is a hallmark of obstructed labour. If this goes unnoticed, the excessive pressure can lead to:

  • Intracranial hemorrhage: Tearing of the delicate membranes and blood vessels inside the brain.
  • Fetal distress: Reduced oxygen supply due to cord compression and prolonged head compression.
  • Maternal exhaustion and uterine rupture: The uterus works harder and harder against an impassable block, putting the mother at high risk of tearing.

Prompt recognition of pathological moulding allows the nurse to call for emergency obstetric help and prepare the patient for an immediate Caesarean section.

Nursing Responsibilities and Documentation

Nurses play a frontline role in monitoring fetal well-being and progress during childbirth. Accurate assessment and clear charting of moulding protect both mother and baby from preventable birth injuries.

Key nursing responsibilities include:

  • Performing aseptic vaginal examinations at regular intervals to assess cervical change, station, and skull moulding.
  • Accurately plotting findings on the partograph using standard symbols.
  • Reporting abnormal trends, such as increasing moulding grades or poor descent, to the attending physician or senior midwife without delay.
  • Providing continuous reassurance and explaining procedures to the mother and her birth partner to reduce anxiety.

Clear documentation must include the degree of moulding, the presence of any caput, and the state of the amniotic fluid. Diligent record-keeping ensures the entire healthcare team stays informed and acts quickly if complications arise.
