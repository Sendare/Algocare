Foetal Circulation

Introduction to Foetal Circulation and the Placenta

In utero, the developing foetus cannot use its lungs for gas exchange, nor its digestive tract for nutrition, nor its kidneys for waste filtration. The placenta assumes all these vital functions. Oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood travels from the maternal blood pools within the intervillous spaces of the placenta, diffusing across the placental membrane into the chorionic villi. From there, this blood enters a single **umbilical vein** to be delivered directly to the foetus. Conversely, deoxygenated and waste-laden blood leaves the foetus through two **umbilical arteries** to return to the placenta for maternal clearance. This unique setup means the placenta serves as the foetus's lungs, liver, and kidneys combined, establishing a parallel circulatory system entirely distinct from adult physiology.

Key Anatomical Structures: Umbilical Cord and Ductus Venosus

Once the oxygenated blood enters the foetal body via the umbilical vein, it faces a major physiological challenge: the foetal liver requires only a fraction of this blood supply for its developing metabolic needs. To bypass the hepatic capillary bed, the majority of this well-oxygenated blood is shunted directly into the inferior vena cava through a specialized vascular channel called the **ductus venosus**. The umbilical cord itself houses these vital vessels—specifically one large umbilical vein and two smaller umbilical arteries—surrounded by a gelatinous substance known as **Wharton's jelly**, which prevents compression and kinking of the cord during movement. Understanding this bypass mechanism explains why the blood entering the right side of the foetal heart already possesses a moderate level of oxygen saturation.

Intracardiac Shunts: Foramen Ovale and Ductus Arteriosus

Because the foetal lungs are collapsed and filled with fluid, pulmonary vascular resistance is extremely high, preventing blood from flowing usefully through the pulmonary circuit. Foetal heart anatomy relies on two specific shunts to redirect blood away from the lungs: - **Foramen ovale**: An opening in the atrial septum that allows oxygenated blood entering the right atrium to pass directly into the left atrium, bypassing the non-functional lungs and routing blood to the brain and coronary circulation. - **Ductus arteriosus**: A muscular blood vessel connecting the pulmonary trunk to the descending aorta, diverting any blood that does manage to enter the right ventricle away from the lungs and into systemic circulation.

Pathways of Oxygenated and Deoxygenated Blood

Blood routing in the foetus is a masterclass in efficiency, ensuring the most vital organs receive the highest possible oxygen concentrations. 1. **Oxygenated Route**: Blood leaves the placenta via the umbilical vein, flows through the ductus venosus into the inferior vena cava, mixes slightly with systemic venous return, enters the right atrium, and is preferentially guided across the foramen ovale to the left atrium, left ventricle, and out the ascending aorta to supply the brain and heart. 2. **Deoxygenated Route**: Blood returning from the upper body enters the superior vena cava, flows into the right atrium, passes into the right ventricle, and is pumped into the pulmonary trunk. Most of this blood is diverted through the ductus arteriosus into the descending aorta, supplying the lower body before returning to the placenta via the paired umbilical arteries.

Physiological Differences Between Foetal and Adult Circulation

Foetal circulation operates under systemic and pulmonary parameters that are completely inverted compared to postnatal life. In the adult, pulmonary vascular resistance is low and systemic vascular resistance is high, meaning blood flows from the right side of the heart through the lungs and out the left side to the body. In the foetus, pulmonary vascular resistance is exceptionally high because the alveoli are filled with fluid, while placental resistance is remarkably low. Consequently, the right ventricle performs more work than the left ventricle during foetal life, resulting in a physiological right ventricular dominance that is clearly visible on routine neonatal ECGs.

Circulatory Changes at Birth and Neonatal Transition

The first breath of independent life triggers a dramatic transformation in the cardiovascular system. As the newborn's lungs expand, pulmonary vascular resistance plummets, causing a surge of blood flow through the pulmonary circuit and raising pressure in the left atrium. Simultaneously, clamping the umbilical cord removes the low-resistance placental bed, causing systemic vascular resistance to rise sharply. - **Foramen ovale closure**: Functional closure happens almost immediately as left atrial pressure exceeds right atrial pressure, pressing the primary septum against the secondary septum. Permanent anatomical closure follows over the first few months of life. - **Ductus arteriosus closure**: Increased arterial oxygen tension and the sudden withdrawal of maternal prostaglandins cause smooth muscle contraction, functionally closing the ductus arteriosus within hours of birth. - **Ductus venosus closure**: Disruption of blood flow from the clamped umbilical cord causes the vessel to collapse and fibrose into the ligamentum venosum.
