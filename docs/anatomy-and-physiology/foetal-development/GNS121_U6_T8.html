Foetal Circulation

Introduction to Foetal Circulation and the Placenta

Unlike adults whose lungs handle oxygen exchange, the foetus relies entirely on the placenta to sustain life in utero. The mother's blood and the baby's blood do not mix directly; instead, substances cross via microscopic membranes where waste products like carbon dioxide pass from the foetus to the mother, and oxygen-rich nutrients travel back. Because the foetal lungs are collapsed and filled with fluid, they do not perform gas exchange. Therefore, the cardiovascular system is uniquely wired to bypass the pulmonary system entirely, redirecting vital supplies directly to the brain, heart, and developing organs.

Key Anatomical Structures: Umbilical Cord and Ductus Venosus

Blood travels between the foetus and the placenta through the umbilical cord, which contains one large umbilical vein and two smaller umbilical arteries.

  • Umbilical vein: Carries oxygen- and nutrient-rich blood from the placenta straight to the baby.
  • Umbilical arteries: Carry deoxygenated, waste-laden blood from the foetus back to the placenta.
  • Ductus venosus: A specialized shunt that allows a large portion of the oxygenated blood from the umbilical vein to bypass the immature foetal liver and empty directly into the inferior vena cava.

Intracardiac Shunts: Foramen Ovale and Ductus Arteriosus

The foetal heart relies on two major structural shortcuts to redirect blood away from the lungs: the foramen ovale and the ductus arteriosus.

  • Foramen ovale: A flap-like opening between the right and left atria. It allows oxygenated blood entering the right atrium to flow straight into the left atrium, bypassing the non-functioning lungs.
  • Ductus arteriosus: A muscular vessel connecting the pulmonary artery to the descending aorta. Any blood that does manage to enter the right ventricle and pulmonary artery is safely diverted into the systemic circulation here.

Pathways of Oxygenated and Deoxygenated Blood

Blood in the foetus does not follow a simple single loop. Highly oxygenated blood enters via the umbilical vein, passes through the ductus venosus, and mixes slightly in the inferior vena cava before entering the right atrium. Because of the angle of the foramen ovale, this oxygen-rich stream is preferentially pushed into the left atrium, down to the left ventricle, and pumped straight to the brain and heart muscle. Conversely, deoxygenated blood returning from the upper body enters the right atrium, moves down into the right ventricle, and gets pushed out through the pulmonary artery, where the ductus arteriosus diverts it down to the descending aorta and out to the lower body and placenta.

Physiological Differences Between Foetal and Adult Circulation

Foetal blood contains a special type of protein called foetal hemoglobin (HbF). Unlike adult hemoglobin, HbF has a much stronger chemical attraction for oxygen, allowing the foetus to pull oxygen molecules away from the mother's blood across the placental membrane. Additionally, the foetal pulmonary blood vessels are tightly constricted because the lungs are not expanded, creating high resistance. In contrast, the systemic vascular resistance in the placenta is very low, making it easy for the foetus to pump blood out toward the mother.

Circulatory Changes at Birth and Neonatal Transition

When a baby takes its first breath, the entire circulatory system undergoes a dramatic, rapid makeover. The lungs expand, filling with air, which causes pulmonary blood vessels to relax and drop their resistance dramatically. Blood rushes into the lungs for oxygen, increasing pressure in the left side of the heart. This pressure change physically slams the foramen ovale shut. Meanwhile, higher oxygen levels and the loss of placental hormones cause the ductus arteriosus, ductus venosus, and umbilical vessels to constrict and eventually turn into fibrous ligaments.
