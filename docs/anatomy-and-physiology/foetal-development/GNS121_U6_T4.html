Placenta Formation

Introduction and Early Embryonic Development

Placenta formation begins at fertilization, when a sperm fuses with an oocyte in the fallopian tube to create a single-celled zygote. This zygote immediately undergoes rapid mitotic divisions called cleavage as it travels down toward the uterine cavity. By day three, the collection of cells forms a solid ball known as a morula. Fluid soon enters this ball, creating a hollow cavity and transforming the structure into a blastocyst by days four to five. The blastocyst consists of two distinct cell populations: the inner cell mass, which eventually forms the embryo proper, and the outer layer called the trophoblast, which mediates implantation and directly builds the fetal portion of the placenta.

Implantation and Decidualization

Around day six or seven after fertilization, the blastocyst reaches the uterine cavity and attaches to the endometrium, usually along the upper posterior wall. The trophoblast cells secrete enzymes that break down the endometrial epithelium, allowing the blastocyst to burrow completely beneath the surface. Once embedded, the surrounding maternal endometrial cells undergo a dramatic structural and biochemical transformation called decidualization. These modified cells, now called decidual cells, accumulate glycogen and lipids to nourish the early conceptus before the placenta is fully functional. The portion of the decidua directly beneath the embryo is the decidua basalis, which becomes the maternal component of the placenta.

Trophoblast Differentiation and Chorionic Villi

As implantation proceeds, the trophoblast rapidly differentiates into two distinct layers:

  • Cytotrophoblast: The inner layer of mononucleated, actively dividing cells that provide a continuous supply of cells to the outer layer.
  • Syncytiotrophoblast: The outer multinucleated protoplasmic mass formed by the fusion of cytotrophoblast cells. It lacks distinct cell membranes and actively erodes maternal blood vessels.

Projecting outward from these layers are finger-like projections called chorionic villi. These villi evolve through primary, secondary, and tertiary stages as fetal blood vessels grow into them, drastically increasing the surface area available for the exchange of gases, nutrients, and waste products between the mother and the developing fetus.

Establishment of Maternal-Placental Circulation

By the end of the first trimester, the maternal-placental circulation is fully established. The invading syncytiotrophoblast erodes the maternal spiral arteries in the decidua basalis, allowing maternal blood to empty directly into large spaces called intervillous spaces. Fetal blood flows from the fetus through the umbilical arteries into the capillaries of the chorionic villi, where exchange occurs, and returns via the umbilical vein. Crucially, normal human placentation ensures that maternal blood and fetal blood do not mix directly; they are separated by a thin barrier of placental tissues known as the placental membrane. This separation protects the fetal circulation from many maternal pathogens and immune cells while allowing efficient diffusion.

Structure of the Mature Placenta

At term, the human placenta is a disk-shaped organ measuring roughly 20 centimeters in diameter and weighing about 500 grams. It has two distinct surfaces:

  • Fetal surface: Smooth and shiny, covered by the amnion, with the umbilical cord inserting near the center and major fetal blood vessels radiating outward.
  • Maternal surface: Rough and dark red, divided into 15 to 20 bulging areas called cotyledons, which are formed by the compartmentalization of maternal decidual septa.

The functional unit of the mature placenta is the cotyledon, each containing its own system of branching villi and maternal blood pools.

Physiological Functions of the Developed Placenta

The placenta acts as the lungs, kidneys, gastrointestinal tract, and endocrine gland for the developing fetus. Respiratory gases like oxygen and carbon dioxide cross the placental membrane freely via simple diffusion. Nutrient transfer includes glucose, amino acids, vitamins, and electrolytes, driven by active transport or facilitated diffusion. As an endocrine organ, the syncytiotrophoblast synthesizes vital hormones such as human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), human placental lactogen (hPL), estrogen, and progesterone, which maintain the pregnancy and prepare the maternal body for lactation. Additionally, the placenta provides passive immunity by transferring maternal IgG antibodies to the fetus.

Common Anomalies in Placental Development

Placental development can sometimes deviate from normal patterns, leading to clinical complications during pregnancy and delivery. Common anomalies include:

  • Placenta previa: Implantation occurs in the lower uterine segment, partially or completely covering the internal cervical os, leading to painless vaginal bleeding.
  • Placenta accreta spectrum: Abnormal deep attachment of the placenta into or through the myometrium due to a deficient decidua basalis, risking severe postpartum hemorrhage.
  • Succenturiate placenta: One or more small accessory lobes develop apart from the main placental disc, which can cause retained tissue after delivery.
  • Circumvallate placenta: The fetal membranes double back around the edge of the placenta, forming a thick, dense ring associated with bleeding and premature rupture of membranes.
