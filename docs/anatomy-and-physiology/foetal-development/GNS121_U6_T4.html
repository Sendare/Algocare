Placenta Formation

Introduction and Early Embryonic Development

Placenta formation begins soon after fertilization, setting up a temporary vital organ that bridges maternal and fetal physiologies. Following the fusion of a sperm and oocyte within the fallopian tube, the resulting zygote undergoes rapid mitotic cleavages known as cleavage divisions while traveling toward the uterine cavity. This journey takes approximately three to four days, during which the cells divide without increasing the overall mass of the conceptus. By day four post-fertilization, the structure is a solid ball of 16 cells called a morula. Fluid soon enters the intercellular spaces of the morula, creating a central cavity known as the blastocoele. At this stage, the conceptus is redesignated as a blastocyst, featuring two distinct cell populations: an inner cell mass that will eventually form the embryo proper, and an outer single-layered ring of cells termed the trophoblast, which drives all subsequent placental development.

Blastocyst Implantation and Decidualization

Around the sixth to seventh day after fertilization, the blastocyst positions itself against the endometrium, typically along the upper posterior wall of the uterine cavity. The zona pellucida surrounding the blastocyst must first degenerate, a process termed hatching, to allow direct contact between the trophoblast cells and the uterine epithelial lining. Upon contact, the trophoblast differentiates into two distinct layers: an inner cellular layer called the cytotrophoblast and an outer multinucleated layer called the syncytiotrophoblast. The syncytiotrophoblast aggressively erodes the endometrial epithelium, embedding the entire conceptus into the functional layer of the endometrium by day twelve. Concurrently, the endometrial stromal cells undergo decidualization, transforming into large, glycogen-rich decidual cells under the influence of progesterone. This decidual reaction provides a rich nutritive matrix for the early embryo and limits the invasive depth of the trophoblast.

Development of Chorionic Villi

As implantation completes, the trophoblastic shell elaborates complex projections that drastically increase the surface area available for exchange. - **Primary villi**: Solid columns of cytotrophoblast cells covered by a layer of syncytiotrophoblast that appear around the second week. - **Secondary villi**: Formed when extraembryonic mesoderm cores penetrate the center of the primary villi, giving them a distinct central axis. - **Tertiary villi**: Established by the end of the third week as fetal blood vessels differentiate within the mesodermal core, completing the functional unit for fetal-maternal transport. These branching villi eventually anchor the placenta to the maternal decidua and establish the structural framework where all future nutrient, gas, and waste exchange will occur.

Establishment of Maternal-Placental Circulation

The establishment of uteroplacental circulation relies on the remodeling of maternal spiral arteries by invasive trophoblast cells. Cytotrophoblasts migrate into the lumina of maternal spiral arterioles in the decidua, destroying their muscular and elastic coats. This transformation converts narrow, high-resistance vessels into wide, low-resistance sinusoids capable of delivering a high volume of maternal blood at a reduced pressure. Maternal blood empties directly into the intervillous spaces, bathing the chorionic villi where gas and nutrient exchange takes place without the maternal and fetal blood actually mixing. Deoxygenated maternal blood then drains back through endometrial venous channels. In clinical conditions like pre-eclampsia, this arterial remodeling is frequently shallow or incomplete, leading to high-resistance placental ischemia and systemic maternal hypertension.

Anatomy of the Mature Placenta

By the fourth month of pregnancy, the placenta is fully formed as a discoid organ, typically weighing about 500 grams and measuring roughly 15 to 20 centimeters in diameter. The fetal surface is smooth, shiny, and covered by the amnion, with the umbilical cord inserting near or at its center. Radiating outward from the cord attachment are large chorionic vessels branching across the surface. Conversely, the maternal surface is rough, dark red, and divided into 15 to 20 distinct convex areas known as cotyledons, which are separated by decidual septa. Cotyledons represent functional subdivisions of the placenta, each containing its own main stem villus system. A thorough postnatal inspection of these cotyledons by the midwife is mandatory to ensure that no fragments remain inside the uterus, which would otherwise precipitate postpartum hemorrhage or severe endometritis.

Physiological Functions of the Developed Placenta

The placenta acts as a multifaceted organ, serving metabolic, endocrine, and protective roles to support fetal growth. Nutrients, electrolytes, and oxygen cross from mother to fetus via simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, or active transport. As an endocrine gland, the syncytiotrophoblast synthesizes vital hormones including human chorionic gonadotropin to maintain the corpus luteum, human placental lactogen to alter maternal metabolism, along with large amounts of progesterone and estrogen. Immunologically, the placenta transfers maternal immunoglobulin G antibodies across to the fetus, granting passive immunity against many common pathogens. Although it acts as a selective barrier, it is not impenetrable; many viruses, drugs, and alcohol readily cross, posing significant teratogenic risks during gestation.

Common Pathological Anomalies in Placental Development

Abnormalities in implantation depth, shape, or insertion can lead to severe obstetric complications requiring careful monitoring. | Anomaly | Clinical Feature | Major Risk | |---|---|---| | **Placenta Previa** | Implantation over or near the internal cervical os | Painless third-trimester hemorrhage | | **Placenta Accreta** | Abnormally deep invasion into or through the myometrium | Massive postpartum hemorrhage and hysterectomy | | **Battledore Placenta** | Umbilical cord inserts at the margin of the placental disc | Usually benign unless cord traction causes separation | Placenta previa prevents normal vaginal delivery due to the cervical blockage, whereas morbidly adherent placentas like accreta fail to separate spontaneously, necessitating surgical intervention.
