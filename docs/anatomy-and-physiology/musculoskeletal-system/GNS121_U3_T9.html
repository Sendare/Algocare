Skeletal Muscle

Introduction and Functions of Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscle is one of three major muscle types in the human body, alongside cardiac and smooth muscle. It is uniquely attached to bones, making up the largest mass of muscle tissue in a healthy adult. Because we can consciously control its movement, it is classified as voluntary muscle. Under a microscope, it shows distinct alternating light and dark bands, earning it the secondary name of striated muscle.

The primary job of skeletal muscle is movement—everything from walking to blinking. However, it does much more than move bones. When these muscles contract, they generate significant body heat, which is vital for maintaining a normal core body temperature. Skeletal muscles also provide crucial postural support, keeping the spine straight and holding joints in place against the pull of gravity. Furthermore, they act as a physical shield, protecting internal abdominal organs from external trauma.

Gross Anatomy and Organization of Skeletal Muscle

A whole skeletal muscle is a complex organ built of muscle cells, connective tissue wrappings, blood vessels, and nerve fibers. To protect and organize these delicate living fibers, connective tissue wraps around them at different structural levels.

  • Epimysium: A tough layer of dense irregular connective tissue that surrounds the entire skeletal muscle, separating it from surrounding tissues.
  • Perimysium: A layer of fibrous connective tissue that groups muscle fibers into bundles called fascicles.
  • Endomysium: A wispy layer of fine areolar connective tissue that surrounds each individual muscle fiber, insulating it from its neighbors.

At the ends of the muscle, these three connective tissue layers merge to form a thick tendon or a flat sheet-like aponeurosis. This tendon anchors the muscle firmly to the periosteum of a bone, allowing the force of contraction to pull on the skeleton.

Microscopic Anatomy: Muscle Fibers and Myofibrils

An individual skeletal muscle cell is commonly called a muscle fiber. Because they are long and cylindrical, they are referred to as fibers rather than typical round cells. Unlike most cells, muscle fibers are multinucleated, meaning they contain many nuclei pushed off to the sides just beneath the cell membrane.

The specialized plasma membrane of a muscle fiber is the sarcolemma, and its cytoplasm is the sarcoplasm. Running deep into the sarcoplasm are tiny tunnel-like projections called T-tubules (transverse tubules), which help carry electrical signals quickly into the cell. Stored inside the sarcoplasm is an elaborate smooth endoplasmic reticulum known as the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), which specializes in storing and releasing calcium ions.

Packed tightly inside each muscle fiber are hundreds to thousands of rod-like organelles called myofibrils. These contain the actual contractile machinery of the cell, laid out in repeating dark A-bands and light I-bands.

The Sarcomere and Sliding Filament Theory

The sarcomere is the smallest functional unit of a skeletal muscle fiber, spanning from one Z-line to the next Z-line. Within each sarcomere lie two types of protein filaments: thick filaments made of myosin and thin filaments made mostly of actin.

According to the sliding filament theory, muscle shortening does not happen because the protein filaments themselves shrink. Instead, the thick and thin filaments slide past one another, causing the sarcomere to shorten:

  1. Resting state: Tropomyosin blocks the binding sites on actin so myosin cannot attach.
  2. Calcium influx: Calcium binds to troponin, causing it to move tropomyosin out of the way.
  3. Cross-bridge formation: Myosin heads attach to the exposed binding sites on actin.
  4. Power stroke: The myosin head pivots, pulling the thin filament toward the center of the sarcomere and shortening the muscle.

As thousands of sarcomeres shorten at the same time, the entire muscle contracts.

Neuromuscular Junction and Muscle Contraction

Skeletal muscle fibers cannot contract on their own; they must be stimulated by a motor neuron. The site where a motor neuron's axon terminal meets a muscle fiber's sarcolemma is the neuromuscular junction (NMJ).

When an electrical nerve impulse reaches the end of the motor neuron, it triggers tiny vesicles to release a chemical messenger called acetylcholine (ACh) into the synaptic cleft. ACh crosses this tiny gap and binds to specific receptor proteins on the motor end plate of the sarcolemma. This binding opens ion channels, allowing sodium ions to rush into the muscle cell. This sudden shift in electrical charge creates an action potential that travels across the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules, ultimately telling the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release calcium and start the contraction cycle.

Energy Metabolism and Muscle Fatigue

Muscle contraction is an energy-hungry process that requires a constant supply of ATP (adenosine triphosphate). Because muscle cells store only a tiny amount of ready-to-use ATP, they must constantly regenerate it using three main pathways:

  • Creatine phosphate breakdown: A quick, short-term energy source that donates a phosphate group to ADP to make ATP in seconds.
  • Anaerobic glycolysis: Breaks down glucose without oxygen to produce a small amount of ATP and lactic acid, suitable for short bursts of intense activity.
  • Aerobic respiration: Uses oxygen and glucose or fatty acids in the mitochondria to yield a large amount of ATP for long-endurance activity.

When muscles work harder and longer than their oxygen and energy supply can support, muscle fatigue sets in. The muscle loses its ability to contract effectively, often due to a buildup of metabolic byproducts like lactic acid and a depletion of cellular energy stores.

Types of Skeletal Muscle Fibers

Not all skeletal muscle fibers are the same. Depending on how they make energy and how fast they contract, they are grouped into distinct fiber types. Most muscles contain a mixture of these types, though the proportion varies based on genetics and training.

Fast Oxidative (Type IIa)FastAerobic and anaerobicWalking, sprintingIntermediate

Endurance athletes like marathon runners usually have a higher percentage of slow oxidative fibers, while sprinters and weightlifters tend to have more fast glycolytic fibers.
