Skeletal Muscle

Introduction and Functions of Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscle is a specialized contractile tissue that makes up roughly 40 percent of total human body weight. Attached directly or via tendons to the skeleton, it pulls on bones to create movement. Beyond locomotion, these muscles maintain upright posture against gravity, stabilize unstable joints, and generate internal heat to protect against hypothermia. Movement in the human body requires coordinated contraction and relaxation of opposing muscle groups. - **Locomotion**: Moving the body through space by manipulating joint angles. - **Postural Support**: Continuous, low-level tonic contraction holding the body upright. - **Heat Generation**: Shivering and normal metabolic activity producing thermal energy. - **Protection**: Guarding internal abdominal and thoracic structures against mechanical trauma.

Gross Anatomy and Hierarchical Organization

A whole skeletal muscle is a discrete organ structured in nested layers of connective tissue that organize thousands of individual cells and route blood vessels and nerves. Each layer plays a structural and protective role during contraction: 1. **Epimysium**: The dense irregular connective tissue sheath wrapping the entire gross muscle belly. 2. **Perimysium**: Fibrous connective tissue partitioning the muscle fibers into bundles known as fascicles. 3. **Endomysium**: Delicate reticular and areolar connective tissue surrounding each individual muscle fiber. These connective tissue layers converge at the extremities of the muscle to form tendons, anchoring the contractile machinery firmly to the periosteum of bones.

Microscopic Anatomy: Muscle Fibers and Myofibrils

Skeletal muscle cells are elongated, multinucleated cylinders commonly called muscle fibers. Their specialized plasma membrane is the sarcolemma, and their cytoplasm is the sarcoplasm, which is packed with oxygen-storing myoglobin and glycogen granules. Deep invaginations of the sarcolemma, termed transverse tubules (T-tubules), plunge into the cell to transmit electrical impulses rapidly. Inside the sarcoplasm lie hundreds to thousands of cylindrical protein bundles called myofibrils. These myofibrils feature repeating dark A-bands and light I-bands, giving the whole fiber a striated appearance under a light microscope. The sarcoplasmic reticulum, a specialized smooth endoplasmic reticulum, forms a complex web around each myofibril to sequester and release calcium ions on demand.

The Sarcomere and Sliding Filament Theory

The sarcomere is the smallest functional contractile unit of a skeletal muscle fiber, spanning from one Z-line to the next. It consists of alternating thick filaments made of myosin and thin filaments composed primarily of actin, alongside regulatory proteins troponin and tropomyosin. According to the sliding filament theory, muscle shortening occurs when thick and thin filaments slide past one another without changing their individual lengths. During contraction, myosin heads latch onto actin binding sites, forming cross-bridges that swivel and pull the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere. Consequently, the H-zone and I-band narrow, while the A-band remains constant in length. This microscopic mechanical action repeats continuously as long as calcium and ATP remain abundantly available.

Neuromuscular Junction and Muscle Contraction

Skeletal muscle fibers are voluntary, requiring stimulation from a somatic motor neuron to contract. The synapse where the neuron communicates with the muscle fiber is the neuromuscular junction. When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open, triggering the exocytosis of acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft. Acetylcholine binds to specialized receptors on the motor end plate, causing a local depolarization that initiates a muscle action potential. This electrical wave travels down the sarcolemma and T-tubules, signaling the sarcoplasmic reticulum to flood the cytosol with calcium ions. Calcium binds to troponin, shifting tropomyosin away from actin binding sites and permitting cross-bridge cycling to begin.

Energy Metabolism and Muscle Fatigue

Skeletal muscle requires continuous supplies of adenosine triphosphate to power both contraction and relaxation. Because intracellular ATP stores are extremely limited, cells rely on three main pathways to regenerate ATP: creatine phosphate metabolism, anaerobic glycolysis, and aerobic respiration. Creatine phosphate provides rapid, short-lived energy during the first few seconds of intense exertion. When high-intensity activity continues, anaerobic glycolysis breaks down glucose without oxygen, producing lactic acid as a byproduct. Long-duration endurance activities depend on aerobic respiration in mitochondria, which yields substantial ATP but requires a steady oxygen supply. Muscle fatigue occurs when metabolites accumulate, glycogen depletes, or electrolyte imbalances disrupt excitation-contraction coupling.

Types of Skeletal Muscle Fibers

Skeletal muscles contain a mixture of fiber types specialized for different functional demands. These fibers are classified primarily by their speed of contraction and primary pathway for ATP synthesis: - **Slow Oxidative (Type I)**: Fatigue-resistant fibers specialized for endurance, relying on aerobic metabolism, rich blood supply, and high myoglobin content. - **Fast Oxidative-Glycolytic (Type IIa)**: Intermediate fibers with fast contraction speeds and moderate resistance to fatigue, combining aerobic and anaerobic capabilities. - **Fast Glycolytic (Type IIx)**: Powerful fibers specialized for short, explosive movements, relying on anaerobic glycolysis and fatiguing quickly due to low myoglobin.
