Skeletal System: Composition, Structure and Functions

Introduction and Overview of the Skeletal System

The skeletal system is the structural framework of the human body, providing the rigid scaffolding that supports soft tissues and allows upright posture. At birth, a human infant has around 270 to 300 bones, but many fuse together during growth, leaving an adult human with 206 distinct bones. Beyond simple mechanical support, this dynamic organ system acts as a protective shield for vital internal organs, anchors skeletal muscles to produce movement, manufactures blood cells, and stores essential minerals like calcium and phosphorus. Understanding bone as a living, highly vascularized tissue is fundamental for nursing care, as skeletal health directly influences mobility, metabolic balance, and overall systemic well-being.

Chemical Composition and Cellular Components of Bone

Bone tissue is a composite material combining organic and inorganic substances to achieve a delicate balance of strength and flexibility. The organic component, known as osteoid, is made mostly of collagen fibers and gives bone its tensile strength, allowing it to resist twisting and stretching. The inorganic component consists of mineral salts, primarily hydroxyapatite crystals made of calcium and phosphate, which give bone its hard, weight-bearing rigidity.

Four main cell types manage bone maintenance and remodeling:

  • Osteogenic cells: Stem cells that develop into bone-forming cells.
  • Osteoblasts: Active cells that synthesize and secrete organic bone matrix.
  • Osteocytes: Mature bone cells trapped in the matrix that monitor and maintain daily bone tissue.
  • Osteoclasts: Large, multinucleated cells that break down bone tissue to release minerals into the blood.

Macroscopic and Microscopic Structure of Bone

When examining bone at different scales, two distinct structural architectures emerge: compact bone and spongy bone. Compact bone is dense, smooth, and solid, forming the outer shell of all bones and providing strong protection and support. Microscopically, compact bone is organized into cylindrical units called osteons or Haversian systems. Each osteon features concentric rings of matrix called lamellae surrounding a central Haversian canal that houses blood vessels and nerves.

In contrast, spongy bone—also called cancellous or trabecular bone—appears porous, consisting of a honeycomb-like network of tiny branching plates called trabeculae. These spaces are often filled with red or yellow bone marrow. Spongy bone is found inside the ends of long bones and inside the interior of flat bones, helping to reduce the overall weight of the skeleton while absorbing shock from various directions.

Classification of Bones by Shape

Bones are classified into five major categories based on their anatomical shape, which directly dictates their specific mechanical function in the body:

  • Long bones: Longer than they are wide, featuring a shaft and two ends; they act as levers for movement (e.g., femur, humerus).
  • Short bones: Roughly cube-shaped, providing stability and support with limited motion (e.g., carpals in the wrist, tarsals in the ankle).
  • Flat bones: Thin, flattened, and often curved; they provide extensive protection for internal organs and broad surfaces for muscle attachment (e.g., sternum, skull bones, ribs).
  • Irregular bones: Complex shapes that do not fit any other category, tailored for specific protective and supporting roles (e.g., vertebrae, facial bones).
  • Sesamoid bones: Small, sesame seed-shaped bones embedded within tendons to reduce friction and mechanical stress (e.g., patella).

Axial versus Appendicular Skeleton

The human skeleton is divided into two primary divisions: the axial skeleton and the appendicular skeleton.

The axial skeleton forms the central vertical axis of the body, consisting of 80 bones. It includes the skull, the auditory ossicles of the middle ear, the hyoid bone, the vertebral column, and the thoracic cage (ribs and sternum). Its main responsibilities are protecting the brain, spinal cord, heart, and lungs, as well as maintaining an upright posture.

The appendicular skeleton consists of 126 bones that make up the upper and lower limbs, plus the pectoral (shoulder) and pelvic (hip) girdles that attach the limbs to the axial skeleton. This division is specialized for locomotion, manipulating objects in the environment, and interacting with the physical world.

Physiological Functions of the Skeletal System

Beyond simple structural support, bones perform several vital physiological roles essential for human survival.

Protection and movement work hand in hand: the cranium shields the brain, the rib cage protects the thoracic organs, and bones act as rigid levers that skeletal muscles pull against to create locomotion.

At a metabolic level, bones serve as a massive mineral reservoir, storing approximately 99% of the body's calcium and 85% of its phosphorus, releasing them into the bloodstream when systemic levels drop. Furthermore, the interior of certain bones contains red bone marrow, the site of hematopoiesis where red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets are continuously produced. Finally, yellow bone marrow stores triglycerides within the medullary cavity as an energy reserve.

Bone Development, Growth, and Remodeling

Bones develop during embryonic life through ossification, replacing a primary template with true bone tissue. Long bones primarily grow in length at the epiphyseal plate (growth plate) through endochondral ossification, where cartilage is continuously replaced by bone until puberty ends and the plate closes. Growth in width, called appositional growth, continues throughout life as osteoblasts lay down new bone tissue on the external surface while osteoclasts resorb bone on the inner medullary surface.

Once adulthood is reached, bones do not remain static; they undergo continuous bone remodeling. This ongoing cycle of resorption by osteoclasts and deposition by osteoblasts repairs micro-fractures and adapts bone architecture to mechanical stress, a principle known as Wolff's Law.

Clinical Relevance and Common Skeletal Disorders in Nursing Practice

Nurses frequently care for patients affected by skeletal disorders that impair mobility and threaten safety. Osteoporosis is a common metabolic bone disease characterized by a severe loss of bone mass and structural deterioration, making bones fragile and highly susceptible to fractures, particularly in postmenopausal women. Osteomyelitis is a serious bone infection caused by bacteria entering through wounds, surgery, or the bloodstream, requiring prompt antibiotic therapy and meticulous wound care. Fractures—breaks in the bone continuity—demand careful assessment for neurovascular compromise, pain management, and immobilization. Understanding these conditions helps nurses implement effective fall prevention strategies and patient education on calcium, vitamin D, and weight-bearing exercise.
