Skeletal System: Composition, Structure and Functions

Introduction and Overview of the Skeletal System

The skeletal system forms the rigid internal framework of the human body, providing the structural foundation necessary for upright posture, movement, and the protection of delicate internal organs. For nursing students in Nigeria studying anatomy and physiology, a thorough understanding of the skeletal system is fundamental to providing holistic patient care, assessing mobility deficits, and managing musculoskeletal disorders. The adult human skeleton is composed of 206 distinct bones, alongside associated cartilages, ligaments, and joints that connect bones to one another. Beyond mere structural support, the skeletal system functions as a dynamic, living organ system that undergoes continuous metabolic activity throughout a person's lifespan. It acts as a vital physiological reservoir for critical minerals, particularly calcium and phosphorus, which are essential for neuromuscular function, blood coagulation, and cellular signaling. Furthermore, the inner cavities of specific bones house hematopoietic tissue, where blood cell production takes place to maintain systemic oxygenation and immunity. Pathologies affecting this system can lead to severe complications, including chronic pain, impaired mobility, pathological fractures, and systemic metabolic imbalances. By mastering the fundamental architecture and functions of the skeletal system, nurses can better anticipate complications related to immobility, design effective rehabilitation plans, and educate patients on maintaining lifelong bone health through proper nutrition, vitamin D supplementation, and weight-bearing exercises.

Chemical Composition and Cellular Components of Bone

Bone is a specialized connective tissue characterized by a unique composite chemical composition that combines organic and inorganic materials to achieve both tensile strength and compressive rigidity. Approximately 35% of bone matrix consists of organic components, primarily collagen fibers and various glycoproteins, which provide flexibility and resistance to pulling or stretching forces. The remaining 65% of the bone matrix is inorganic, consisting predominantly of hydroxyapatite crystals, which are specialized complexes of calcium phosphate. These mineral salts deposit along the collagen fibers, hardening the tissue and enabling the skeleton to withstand heavy weight-bearing loads without shattering. To maintain, repair, and remodel this complex matrix, bone tissue relies on four primary types of specialized cells: osteogenic cells, osteoblasts, osteocytes, and osteoclasts. Osteogenic cells are stem cells located in the periosteum and endosteum that divide to differentiate into osteoblasts. Osteoblasts are the bone-forming cells responsible for synthesizing and secreting the organic components of the matrix, a process known as osteogenesis. Once these active building cells become trapped within the newly formed mineralized matrix, they mature into osteocytes, which are the principal mature bone cells that monitor and maintain the surrounding mineral matrix. Conversely, osteoclasts are large, multinucleated cells derived from white blood cell lineages that specialize in bone resorption, secreting acids and enzymes to dissolve old or damaged bone matrix. The balanced, coordinated action between osteoblasts and osteoclasts ensures continuous skeletal maintenance throughout life.

Macroscopic and Microscopic Structure of Bone

At the macroscopic level, every bone in the human body exhibits two primary structural regions: compact bone and spongy bone. Compact bone, also known as cortical bone, forms the dense, hard outer shell of bones, providing maximum protection and support while resisting the stresses produced by weight and movement. Spongy bone, or cancellous/trabecular bone, is found inside the interior of bones, particularly at the expanded ends (epiphyses) and lining the medullary cavities; it consists of a honeycomb-like network of trabeculae that reduces skeletal weight while effectively distributing mechanical forces. Microscopically, the organization of compact bone is centered around the osteon, or Haversian system, which is the structural unit of mature compact bone. Each osteon is structured as a series of concentric rings of calcified matrix called lamellae, surrounding a central canal (Haversian canal) that houses blood vessels and nerve fibers. Nutrient and waste exchange between the central blood supply and the isolated osteocytes occurs through tiny interconnecting channels called canaliculi, which radiate outward through the lamellae to connect lacunae (the small spaces housing individual osteocytes). Volkmann's canals, or perforating canals, run perpendicular to the Haversian canals, supplying blood from the periosteum and endosteum into the deeper interior networks. In contrast, spongy bone lacks osteons; its trabeculae are arranged along lines of stress to provide structural resilience, with the open spaces between trabeculae frequently filled with red or yellow bone marrow.

Classification of Bones by Shape

Bones are classified into five major categories based on their overall anatomical shape, which directly correlates with their specific biomechanical functions within the body. Long bones are longer than they are wide, featuring a shaft (diaphysis) and two ends (epiphyses); they function primarily as levers to facilitate movement driven by skeletal muscles, with prime examples including the femur, humerus, and tibia. Short bones are roughly cube-shaped, providing stability and limited motion across complex joints, such as the carpals in the wrist and tarsals in the ankle. Flat bones are thin, flattened, and usually curved, providing extensive surface areas for muscle attachment and robust protection for underlying vital organs; examples include the cranial bones, sternum, ribs, and scapulae. Irregular bones possess complex, multifaceted shapes that do not fit neatly into the other categories, offering specialized structural support and protection, such as the vertebrae and certain facial bones. Finally, sesamoid bones are small, sesame seed-shaped bones embedded within tendons to reduce friction and mechanical stress across joints, with the patella ( kneecap) being the largest and most prominent example in the human body. Understanding these shape classifications helps nurses accurately interpret radiological reports, identify anatomical landmarks during physical examinations, and comprehend the specific mechanical stresses associated with different skeletal injuries.

Axial versus Appendicular Skeleton

The human skeleton is functionally and anatomically divided into two distinct divisions: the axial skeleton and the appendicular skeleton. The axial skeleton forms the central, vertical axis of the body, consisting of 80 bones that protect the vital organs of the head, neck, and trunk. Its primary anatomical components include the skull, the auditory ossicles of the middle ear, the hyoid bone, the vertebral column (spine), and the thoracic cage (ribs and sternum). Nurses frequently assess the axial skeleton when evaluating patients for spinal deformities, respiratory excursion issues related to chest wall injuries, and neurological trauma following head impacts. Conversely, the appendicular skeleton comprises 126 bones that make up the upper and lower limbs (appendages) as well as the pectoral and pelvic girdles that attach the limbs to the axial skeleton. The pectoral girdle (clavicles and scapulae) attaches the upper limbs to the trunk, allowing for exceptional mobility and dexterity, while the pelvic girdle (hip bones) securely attaches the lower limbs to the axial skeleton, providing the stability and weight-bearing strength necessary for locomotion. By dividing the skeleton into these two major segments, healthcare professionals can systematically document fractures, surgical interventions, and orthopedic abnormalities during clinical assessments and nursing documentation.

Physiological Functions of the Skeletal System

The skeletal system performs several critical physiological functions that are essential for maintaining overall body homeostasis and survival. First, it provides mechanical support by acting as a rigid structural framework that bears body weight and maintains posture, and it offers attachment points for skeletal muscles to enable bodily movement through lever systems. Second, the skeleton provides vital protection for delicate internal organs; for example, the cranium encases the brain, the vertebral column protects the spinal cord, and the thoracic cage shields the heart and lungs from traumatic injury. Third, bones serve as a crucial metabolic reservoir for mineral storage, particularly regulating serum calcium and phosphate homeostasis, which are indispensable for nerve conduction, muscle contraction, and blood clotting. Fourth, hematopoiesis, the production of blood cells, occurs within the red bone marrow found in specific spongy bone cavities, generating erythrocytes, leukocytes, and thrombocytes. Finally, bones participate in energy storage through yellow bone marrow, which consists largely of adipose tissue housed within the medullary cavities of long bones, serving as an important chemical energy reserve for the body.

Bone Development, Growth, and Remodeling

Bone development, growth, and lifelong remodeling are dynamic processes that ensure structural integrity, repair microfractures, and regulate systemic mineral levels. Bone formation, or osteogenesis, begins in the embryo through two primary mechanisms: intramembranous ossification, which forms the flat bones of the skull and clavicles from fibrous connective tissue membranes, and endochondral ossification, which forms most other bones of the skeleton by replacing a hyaline cartilage model with bone tissue. During childhood and adolescence, long bones grow in length at the epiphyseal plate (growth plate) through the proliferation of cartilage cells followed by their ossification, and they grow in width through appositional growth managed by the periosteum and endosteum. Once skeletal maturity is reached, the epiphyseal plates close and become epiphyseal lines, halting further linear growth. However, bone remodeling continues throughout life as a coupled process where old bone tissue is continuously resorbed by osteoclasts and replaced with new matrix laid down by osteoblasts. This lifelong remodeling is regulated by mechanical stress (Wolf's law, which states that bone grows or remodels in response to the forces placed upon it) and hormonal controls, notably parathyroid hormone (PTH) and calcitonin, which maintain strict calcium homeostasis in the bloodstream. Nurses must understand these developmental and remodeling principles to recognize how immobility, malnutrition, and endocrine disorders can impair bone density and strength.

Clinical Relevance and Common Skeletal Disorders in Nursing Practice

Skeletal disorders present significant clinical challenges in nursing practice, requiring comprehensive assessments, meticulous patient care, and targeted interventions. Osteoporosis is a prevalent metabolic bone disease characterized by a severe reduction in bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration, leading to fragile bones and a high risk of fragility fractures, particularly in postmenopausal women. Nurses play a crucial role in preventing osteoporosis by educating patients on adequate calcium and vitamin D intake, encouraging weight-bearing exercises, and implementing fall-prevention protocols in clinical and home environments. Osteomyelitis is a severe, painful bone infection usually caused by bacterial pathogens such as Staphylococcus aureus, which can enter the bone tissue via open fractures, surgical procedures, or bacteremia; treating osteomyelitis requires strict adherence to long-term intravenous antibiotic therapy and vigilant wound care. Fractures—disruptions in bone continuity—require prompt nursing assessment of neurovascular status (the '5 Ps': pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, and paralysis) to prevent permanent nerve or vascular damage, alongside proper pin and traction care for immobilized patients. Other notable skeletal pathologies include rickets and osteomalacia, caused by vitamin D deficiencies leading to impaired bone mineralization, and Paget's disease, characterized by excessive, disorganized bone remodeling resulting in weak, deformed bones. By recognizing the clinical manifestations of these disorders, nurses can collaborate effectively with multidisciplinary healthcare teams to optimize patient outcomes and promote musculoskeletal wellness.
