Skeletal System: Composition, Structure and Functions

Introduction and Overview of the Skeletal System

The skeletal system serves as the structural framework of the human body, providing internal support, protecting vital organs, and anchoring skeletal muscles to facilitate movement. In adults, this dynamic system consists of 206 distinct bones, alongside cartilages, ligaments, and joints that connect them. While bones appear inert and rock-hard post-mortem, they are living, highly vascularized organs undergoing constant metabolic activity. Understanding bone architecture is foundational for clinical nursing, as alterations in skeletal integrity directly impact mobility, mineral homeostasis, and overall physiological stability.

Chemical Composition and Cellular Components of Bone

Bone tissue is a composite material made of roughly one-third organic components and two-thirds inorganic minerals. The organic matrix, known as **osteoid**, consists mainly of type I collagen fibers and ground substance, granting bones tensile strength and flexibility to resist snapping. The inorganic component comprises **hydroxyapatite** crystals (primarily calcium phosphate and calcium carbonate), which deposit around the collagen fibers to provide extreme hardness and weight-bearing capacity. Embedded within this matrix are four primary types of specialized bone cells: - **Osteogenic cells**: Stem cells that divide and differentiate into bone-forming cells. - **Osteoblasts**: Synthetic cells responsible for bone deposition, building the organic matrix and directing its mineralization. - **Osteocytes**: Mature bone cells trapped in lacunae that monitor and maintain the surrounding bone matrix. - **Osteoclasts**: Large, multinucleated cells derived from white blood cell lineages that break down bone tissue during resorption.

Macroscopic and Microscopic Structure of Bone

Grossly, bones display two distinct structural architectures: **compact bone** and **spongy bone**. Compact bone forms the dense, smooth outer layer of all bones, providing robust protection and support. Spongy bone, also called cancellous or trabecular bone, occupies the interior and consists of a honeycomb-like network of bony struts called **trabeculae**, which reduce skeletal weight and house red bone marrow. Under the microscope, compact bone is organized into cylindrical units called **osteons** or Haversian systems. Each osteon features concentric rings of calcified matrix called **lamellae** surrounding a central **Haversian canal** that contains blood vessels and nerves. Tiny channels called **canaliculi** radiate through the matrix, connecting osteocytes to nutrient supplies and allowing cellular communication across the dense bone layers.

Classification of Bones by Shape

Bones are categorized into distinct morphological groups that reflect their primary mechanical roles: - **Long bones**: Longer than they are wide, featuring a shaft and two ends (e.g., femur, humerus); act as levers for movement. - **Short bones**: Cube-shaped structures providing stability and limited motion (e.g., carpals, tarsals). - **Flat bones**: Thin, parallel plates of compact bone enclosing a layer of spongy bone, offering extensive protection and muscle attachment surfaces (e.g., sternum, cranial bones). - **Irregular bones**: Complex shapes that fit no other category (e.g., vertebrae, facial bones). - **Sesamoid bones**: Small, sesame seed-shaped bones embedded within tendons to reduce friction and mechanical stress (e.g., patella).

Axial versus Appendicular Skeleton

The human skeleton is divided into two major anatomical divisions. The **axial skeleton** forms the central vertical axis of the body, comprising the skull, vertebral column, thoracic cage (ribs and sternum), and hyoid bone. Its primary function is protection of the brain, spinal cord, and thoracic organs. The **appendicular skeleton** consists of the bones of the upper and lower limbs, alongside the pectoral and pelvic girdles that attach the limbs to the axial skeleton. This division is specialized for locomotion, environmental manipulation, and weight-bearing support during daily activities.

Physiological Functions of the Skeletal System

Beyond providing structural architecture, bones perform several vital physiological roles essential for survival: - **Support and Protection**: Creates a rigid framework holding up the body and encasing delicate structures like the brain and lungs. - **Movement**: Works in tandem with skeletal muscles attached via tendons, utilizing joints as pivots to generate locomotion. - **Mineral Storage**: Acts as a vast reservoir for calcium and phosphorus, releasing or storing minerals to maintain strict serum electrolyte balances. - **Blood Cell Production**: Houses red bone marrow within spongy bone cavities, where **hematopoiesis** produces erythrocytes, leukocytes, and platelets. - **Triglyceride Storage**: Stores adipose tissue in yellow bone marrow within long bone shafts for long-term energy reserves.

Bone Development, Growth, and Remodeling

Bone formation begins in the embryo through two distinct mechanisms: **intramembranous ossification**, which forms flat cranial bones directly from fibrous membranes, and **endochondral ossification**, which replaces a hyaline cartilage model with bone tissue for most other skeletal structures. Longitudinal bone growth occurs at the **epiphyseal plate** (growth plate) through chondrocyte proliferation until adulthood, when the plate closes and fuses into an epiphyseal line. Even after growth ceases, bones undergo lifelong **remodeling**—a continuous, coupled cycle of bone resorption by osteoclasts and deposition by osteoblasts. This process repairs micro-fractures, reshapes bone in response to physical stress (Wolff's law), and adjusts systemic mineral levels.

Clinical Relevance and Common Skeletal Disorders in Nursing Practice

Nurses frequently encounter pathologies affecting skeletal integrity. **Osteoporosis** is a prevalent metabolic bone disease characterized by porous, fragile bones due to an imbalance where resorption outpaces bone deposition, significantly increasing fracture risk in elderly populations. **Osteomyelitis**, an infection of the bone and bone marrow, often results from bacterial invasion via open fractures or surgical procedures and requires rigorous antibiotic therapy and meticulous wound care. Nutritional deficiencies also impair skeletal health; a lack of vitamin D or calcium in children causes **rickets**, leading to soft, bowed bones, while the adult equivalent is **osteomalacia**. Clinical assessment of skeletal disorders requires careful monitoring of mobility, pain levels, dietary intake, and fracture history to prevent debilitating complications.
