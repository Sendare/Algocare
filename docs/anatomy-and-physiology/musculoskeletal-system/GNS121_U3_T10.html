Cardiac Muscle

Introduction and Structural Characteristics

Cardiac muscle, or myocardium, forms the thick middle layer of the heart wall, responsible for the rhythmic pumping action that circulates blood throughout the body. Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac tissue operates involuntarily, driven by an intrinsic pacemaker system. The fibers are striated, branching extensively to form a complex, interconnected cellular network. Each cardiocyte typically contains a single, centrally located nucleus, although binucleated cells occasionally appear. Surrounding these cells is a rich capillary network that ensures a constant supply of oxygen and nutrients, which is critical because the heart muscle cannot afford prolonged periods of ischemia.

Histology and Ultra-Structure of Cardiocytes

At the microscopic level, cardiocytes are packed with cellular machinery dedicated to sustained mechanical work.

  • Myofibrils: The contractile elements composed of repeating sarcomeres, giving the tissue its characteristic striated appearance under a light microscope.
  • Sarcoplasmic Reticulum (SR): Stores and releases calcium ions, though it is less elaborate than the SR found in skeletal muscle.
  • T-tubules: Large invaginations of the sarcolemma that penetrate deep into the cell near the Z-lines, ensuring rapid delivery of the electrical impulse to the cell interior.
  • Mitochondria: Abundant and large, occupying roughly 40% of the cytoplasmic volume to satisfy the relentless energy demands of aerobic respiration.

Intercalated Discs and the Functional Syncytium

Cardiocytes are bound end-to-end by specialized junctional complexes known as intercalated discs. These structures contain two primary mechanical and electrical components:

  • Desmosomes (macula adherens): Strong protein filaments that anchor adjacent cells together, preventing them from pulling apart during the high-pressure stress of forceful contractions.
  • Gap junctions: Low-resistance tunnels that allow ions to pass freely from one cell to the cytoplasm of the next.

Because gap junctions permit rapid electrical coupling, an action potential generated in one cell spreads instantaneously across the entire network. This anatomical arrangement allows the atria and the ventricles to contract as single unified units, termed functional syncytia.

Electrophysiology and Action Potential

The cardiac action potential is notably prolonged compared to nerve or skeletal muscle, lasting upwards of 200 to 300 milliseconds. This duration prevents tetanic contractions, ensuring the heart has adequate time to relax and fill with blood between beats.

  1. Phase 0 (Rapid Depolarization): Voltage-gated sodium channels open rapidly, causing a sharp influx of sodium ions and shifting the membrane potential from negative to positive.
  2. Phase 1 (Initial Repolarization): Sodium channels rapidly close, and potassium efflux begins, causing a brief downward notch.
  3. Phase 2 (Plateau Phase): Slow voltage-gated calcium channels open, balancing the outflow of potassium. This extended influx of calcium maintains membrane depolarization.
  4. Phase 3 (Rapid Repolarization): Calcium channels close while delayed rectifier potassium channels remain open, restoring the negative resting membrane potential.
  5. Phase 4 (Resting Membrane Potential): The cell stabilizes at its baseline resting voltage, maintained by ionic pumps.

Excitation-Contraction Coupling

The mechanism linking electrical excitation to mechanical contraction in cardiac muscle relies heavily on an extracellular trigger. During the plateau phase of the action potential, calcium enters the cardiocyte through L-type calcium channels in the T-tubules. This small entry of extracellular calcium stimulates a much larger release of calcium stored within the sarcoplasmic reticulum through ryanodine receptors, a process known as calcium-induced calcium release (CICR).

Flooding the cytosol with calcium ions allows them to bind to troponin C, shifting tropomyosin and exposing binding sites on actin filaments for myosin cross-bridge cycling. Relaxation occurs when calcium is actively pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum by SERCA pumps and extruded from the cell via the sodium-calcium exchanger.

Myocardial Metabolism and Energy Supply

Under normal resting conditions, the heart relies almost exclusively on aerobic metabolism to generate adenosine triphosphate (ATP). While skeletal muscle easily shifts to anaerobic glycolysis during heavy exertion, the myocardium has very limited capacity for anaerobic energy production.

Under normal physiological states, roughly 60 to 70 percent of myocardial energy is derived from the oxidation of fatty acids, with the remainder coming from glucose, lactate, and small amounts of ketone bodies or amino acids. Because of this absolute dependence on continuous oxygen delivery, any disruption in coronary blood flow quickly leads to cellular hypoxia, depletion of ATP stores, cessation of active calcium pumping, and rapid heart failure.

Regulation of Cardiac Contractility

Contractility refers to the intrinsic strength of cardiac muscle contraction independent of fiber length. It is primarily modulated by the autonomic nervous system and circulating hormones.

  • Sympathetic Stimulation: Norepinephrine and epinephrine bind to beta-1 adrenergic receptors on cardiocytes, activating cyclic AMP pathways. This increases calcium influx during the action potential, enhancing both the speed and force of contraction.
  • Parasympathetic Stimulation: Acetylcholine acts via vagal nerves primarily to slow the heart rate and slightly depress atrial contractility.
  • Frank-Starling Law: An intrinsic mechanical property where increased venous return stretches the myocardial fibers prior to contraction, resulting in a stronger subsequent stroke volume.
