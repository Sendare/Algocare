Cardiac Muscle

Characteristics and Structure of Cardiac Muscle

Cardiac muscle, or myocardium, forms the thick middle layer of the heart wall, pumping blood continuously throughout the body without conscious control. Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle cells, known as cardiocytes or myocytes, are typically short, branched, and feature a single centrally located nucleus. These cells connect end-to-end through specialized junctions called intercalated discs, allowing the entire heart chamber to contract in a coordinated wave. Histologically, cardiac muscle appears striated under a microscope due to the organized arrangement of actin and myosin filaments.

  • Branching fibers: Creates a complex, mesh-like network that distributes tension evenly across the heart wall.
  • Central nuclei: Most cardiocytes contain one or two nuclei situated right in the middle of the cell.
  • Striations: Alternating light and dark bands visible under magnification, reflecting the underlying sarcomere structure.

Intercalated Discs and the Functional Syncytium

The mechanical and electrical integration of the heart relies entirely on intercalated discs. These dark-staining transverse lines cross the cardiac muscle fibers at the boundaries between adjacent cells. Structurally, intercalated discs contain two critical elements: desmosomes (macula adherens) and gap junctions. Desmosomes act as strong mechanical spot-welds that stop the powerful contracting heart cells from pulling apart under high pressure. Gap junctions are protein channels that allow ions to pass freely from one cell to the next with virtually zero resistance.

Because gap junctions let electrical currents travel directly between cells, the atria contract together as a single unit, followed shortly by the ventricles. Physiologists call this interconnected network a functional syncytium. This arrangement ensures that the heart behaves as a coordinated pump rather than a disorganized bundle of twitching fibers.

Electrophysiology and the Cardiac Action Potential

The resting membrane potential of a ventricular cardiocyte sits around -90 millivolts. When an electrical signal arrives from a neighboring cell, voltage-gated sodium channels fly open, causing a rapid influx of positive sodium ions and a sudden spike in voltage called depolarization.

Unlike skeletal muscle, the cardiac action potential features a long plateau phase. This plateau is maintained by the slow influx of calcium ions through L-type calcium channels while potassium channels remain mostly closed. This prolonged phase keeps the heart muscle contracted long enough to successfully eject blood into circulation. Eventually, potassium channels open fully, letting potassium rush out to restore the resting voltage, a process called repolarization.

Excitation-Contraction Coupling

The process that links the electrical action potential to the mechanical contraction of the heart is known as excitation-contraction coupling. As the action potential sweeps across the sarcolemma and travels down the wide T-tubules, voltage-gated calcium channels open in the cell membrane. A small amount of calcium enters the cytoplasm from the extracellular space.

This tiny entry triggers a much larger release of stored calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum via ryanodine receptors, a phenomenon called calcium-induced calcium release. Floodgates of calcium ions then bind to troponin, pulling tropomyosin away from actin-myosin binding sites. Myosin heads attach to actin, swivel, and generate the muscular tension needed for a heartbeat. Relaxation occurs when active transport pumps actively pump calcium back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

Myocardial Metabolism and Energy Supply

The heart is an aerobic organ that demands a continuous, uninterrupted supply of oxygen and fuel to maintain its relentless pumping cycle. Under normal resting conditions, cardiac muscle derives most of its energy from fatty acid oxidation, though it readily utilizes glucose, lactate, and ketone bodies depending on nutrient availability. Because the heart lacks significant energy reserves, any interruption in blood flow quickly depletes cellular adenosine triphosphate (ATP).

Abundant mitochondria pack the interior of every cardiocyte, occupying nearly forty percent of the cell volume. These organelles supply the massive amounts of ATP required to run the contractile machinery and power the calcium pumps responsible for relaxation. When coronary blood flow drops, myocardial cells switch to anaerobic metabolism, rapidly accumulating lactic acid and losing contractile function.

Regulation of Contractility and Heart Function

The strength of a cardiac contraction can be adjusted to meet the changing demands of the body. Factors that increase contractility are called positive inotropic agents, while those that decrease it are negative inotropes. Sympathetic nervous system stimulation acts as a powerful positive inotrope by releasing norepinephrine, which activates beta-1 receptors, boosting intracellular calcium levels and speeding up relaxation.

Another fundamental regulatory principle is the Frank-Starling law of the heart. This law states that within physiological limits, the greater the stretch of the ventricular muscle during diastole (preload), the more forceful the subsequent contraction will be. This intrinsic mechanism ensures that the heart automatically pumps out whatever volume of blood flows into it from the veins.
