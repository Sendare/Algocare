Types of Joints

Introduction to Joints and Their Functions

A joint, or articulation, is any point where two bones, bone and cartilage, or bone and teeth meet. Without joints, our skeletal system would be a rigid, unbendable cage. Joints serve two main purposes: they hold our bones together securely, and they give our skeleton the flexibility it needs to allow movement.

When studying joints, student nurses often fall into the trap of thinking all joints move. In truth, some joints are designed to be completely immovable to protect vital internal organs. The design of a joint always matches its specific job in the human body. A joint in your skull needs to offer maximum protection to the brain, so it is fixed tightly in place. On the other hand, your shoulder joint needs a massive range of motion to help you reach, lift, and throw, so it is built loosely with wide movement capabilities.

Structural and Functional Classification of Joints

To make sense of the hundreds of joints in the body, anatomists classify them using two different systems: structural and functional.

  • Structural classification: Based on what material binds the bones together and whether a cavity is present. The three structural groups are fibrous, cartilaginous, and synovial.
  • Functional classification: Based on how much movement the joint allows. The three functional groups are synarthroses (immovable), amphiarthroses (slightly movable), and diarthroses (freely movable).

There is a neat overlap between these two systems. All fibrous joints are synarthroses, all cartilaginous joints are amphiarthroses, and all synovial joints are diarthroses.

Fibrous Joints and Their Subtypes

In fibrous joints, the adjacent bones are joined by hard collagen fibers of connective tissue. Because there is no joint cavity and the fibers are short, these joints generally do not move at all, making them classic synarthroses.

  1. Sutures: These are found only between the bones of the skull. The irregular edges of the bones interlock tightly and are bound by very short connective tissue fibers.
  2. Syndesmoses: In this type, the bones are connected exclusively by ligaments, which are cords or bands of fibrous tissue. Depending on how long the fibers are, some syndesmoses allow a slight give, such as the joint between the tibia and fibula.
  3. Gomphoses: This is a peg-in-socket fibrous joint. The only examples in the human body are the articulations of teeth with their bony alveolar sockets in the mandible and maxilla.

Cartilaginous Joints

Cartilaginous joints connect articulating bones using cartilage. These joints lack a joint cavity and fall into the amphiarthrosis or synarthrosis functional categories.

  • Synchondroses: A bar or plate of hyaline cartilage unites the bones. Almost all synchondroses are immovable synarthroses. A prime example is the epiphyseal plate in growing long bones, as well as the joint between the first rib and the manubrium of the sternum.
  • Symphyses: The articulating bone surfaces are covered with articular cartilage, which is fused to an intervening pad of compressible fibrocartilage. Because fibrocartilage is resilient, symphysis joints are designed for strength with flexibility, acting as shock absorbers. Examples include the pubic symphysis of the pelvis and the intervertebral discs between adjacent vertebrae.

Synovial Joints: Structure and Characteristics

Synovial joints are distinguished by the presence of a fluid-filled joint cavity. This unique structural arrangement allows these joints to be freely movable diarthroses, making them responsible for nearly all limb movements we perform daily.

Every typical synovial joint shares five key structural features:

  • Articular cartilage: Glassy hyaline cartilage covers the ends of the opposing bones to cushion them and absorb compression.
  • Joint cavity: A unique, potential space filled with a small amount of slippery synovial fluid.
  • Articular capsule: A two-layered capsule encloses the cavity; a tough outer fibrous layer continuous with periosteum, and an inner synovial membrane that makes fluid.
  • Synovial fluid: A viscous, egg-white-like fluid that reduces friction between cartilages and nourishes their cells.
  • Reinforcing ligaments: Band-like ligaments strengthen and reinforce the joint.

Many synovial joints also feature fatty pads for cushioning and fibrocartilage discs called menisci that improve bone fit.

Types of Body Movements at Synovial Joints

Every movement at a synovial joint can be classified by how the bones move relative to each other.

  • Gliding: One flat bone surface slips or glides over another, such as between wrist bones.
  • Angular movements: These increase or decrease the angle between two bones. They include flexion (bending to decrease the angle), extension (straightening to increase the angle), abduction (moving a limb away from the body midline), adduction (moving a limb toward the midline), and circumduction (moving a limb in a cone-like circle).
  • Rotation: The turning of a bone around its own long axis, commonly seen in the neck or hip.
  • Special movements: Movements that only occur at specific joints, such as supination (turning palms up) and pronation (turning palms down), or dorsiflexion (lifting the foot upwards) and plantar flexion (pointing the toes).

Clinical Relevance and Common Joint Pathologies

Because joints bear our body weight and undergo continuous friction, they are prone to injury and degenerative conditions. Nurses frequently care for patients managing these common joint issues:

  • Sprains and strains: In a sprain, the ligaments reinforcing a joint are stretched or torn. Because ligaments have a poor blood supply, healing is notoriously slow. A strain involves a stretched or torn muscle or tendon.
  • Arthritis: This broad term covers over 100 different types of joint-damaging inflammation or degeneration. Osteoarthritis is the most common chronic wear-and-tear arthritis, where the articular cartilage gradually breaks down. Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic, painful autoimmune disease where the body's immune system attacks its own joint tissues, leading to deformity.
  • Bursitis and tendonitis: Inflammation of the bursa sacs or tendon sheaths, often caused by friction or repetitive athletic or work movements.

Assessing pain, swelling, and range of motion helps nurses monitor these conditions and evaluate the effectiveness of prescribed treatments.
