Smooth Muscle

Introduction and Location in the Body

Unlike the striated skeletal and cardiac muscles that pull on bones or pump blood, smooth muscle lines the walls of hollow organs throughout the body. You find it wrapped around blood vessels, the digestive tract, the urinary bladder, the uterus, and the airways of the lungs. Because it operates without conscious thought, it is classified as involuntary muscle. Its primary job is to move fluids, food, or waste through internal passageways by squeezing or relaxing its walls.

Microscopic Anatomy and Ultrastructure

Smooth muscle cells are small, spindle-shaped fibers with a single central nucleus. They lack the alternating dark and light bands, or striations, seen in skeletal muscle because their contractile proteins are not arranged in orderly sarcomeres. Instead, actin and myosin filaments crisscross the cell, anchored at structures called dense bodies scattered through the cytoplasm and attached to the cell membrane. When the cell contracts, it twists and bunches up like a closing purse string.

Excitation-Contraction Coupling and Calcium Regulation

Calcium is the master switch for smooth muscle contraction, just as it is in skeletal muscle, but the pathway is different. Smooth muscle lacks troponin. Instead, incoming calcium ions bind to a regulatory protein called calmodulin.

  1. Calcium Entry: Calcium enters the cell from the extracellular fluid and a sparse sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  2. Calmodulin Binding: Calcium binds to calmodulin in the cytoplasm.
  3. Enzyme Activation: The calcium-calmodulin complex activates an enzyme called myosin light chain kinase (MLCK).
  4. Cross-Bridge Formation: MLCK adds a phosphate group to myosin heads, allowing them to bind to actin and create tension.

Single-Unit vs. Multi-Unit Smooth Muscle

  • Single-Unit (Visceral) Smooth Muscle: This is the most common type. Cells are linked electrically by gap junctions, allowing a whole sheet of muscle to contract in unison as a single syncytium. You find this in the digestive tract, uterus, and small blood vessels.
  • Multi-Unit Smooth Muscle: These cells operate independently with very few gap junctions. Each fiber receives its own nerve ending or hormonal signal. This type allows for fine, graded control and is found in the large airways of the lungs, the iris of the eye, and the walls of large arteries.

Nervous and Hormonal Control

Unlike skeletal muscle, which relies entirely on somatic motor nerves releasing acetylcholine, smooth muscle responds to multiple stimuli. Autonomic nerve fibers branch out and release neurotransmitters like acetylcholine or norepinephrine onto diffuse swellings called varicosities. Furthermore, hormones circulating in the bloodstream, local chemical changes like low oxygen or high acidity, and even mechanical stretch can stimulate or inhibit smooth muscle activity.

Physiological Properties and Plasticity

Smooth muscle has remarkable physiological quirks. One is the latch state, where myosin and actin remain locked together for a long time with very little energy consumption, allowing organs like blood vessels or the bladder to maintain tone without getting tired. Another is stress-relaxation response, meaning the muscle can stretch significantly to accommodate increased volume—such as when the stomach fills with food—without a dramatic rise in internal pressure.

Clinical Correlates and Pathophysiology

Disruptions in smooth muscle function underlie several common clinical conditions. In asthma, inappropriate bronchospasm causes excessive contraction of airway smooth muscle, narrowing the breathing tubes. In hypertension, chronic over-constriction of vascular smooth muscle raises systemic blood pressure. On the other hand, conditions like gastroparesis involve poor motility due to weak smooth muscle activity in the digestive tract.
