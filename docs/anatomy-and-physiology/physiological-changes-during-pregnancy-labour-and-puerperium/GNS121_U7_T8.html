Musculoskeletal Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Hormonal Drivers of Musculoskeletal Laxity

Pregnancy triggers profound biochemical shifts that fundamentally alter connective tissue stability. The primary biochemical driver is **relaxin**, a peptide hormone synthesized by the corpus luteum, placenta, and decidua. Relaxin remodels collagen matrices, specifically targeting pelvic ligaments and the extracellular matrix to encourage joint laxity. This targeted softening prepares the birth canal for fetal passage. Concurrently, elevated **progesterone** levels contribute to smooth muscle relaxation and generalized joint hypermobility throughout the axial and appendicular skeleton. While these adaptations are necessary for parturition, they simultaneously compromise structural integrity, predisposing pregnant women to sprains, strains, and chronic joint instability.

Biomechanical Shifts: Center of Gravity and Posture

As the conceptus grows, the maternal center of gravity shifts upward and forward. To compensate for this anterior load, the woman automatically adopts a compensatory posture. Shoulders pull backward, the upper torso hyperextends, and the pelvis tilts anteriorly. This kinematic chain reaction alters baseline biomechanics, placing abnormal tensile stress on paravertebral muscles, hip flexors, and knee joints. Standing for prolonged periods in busy clinical settings or local markets exacerbates this postural strain, frequently resulting in fatigue and localized aching across weight-bearing joints.

Spinal Realignment and Back Pain Etiology

The compensatory posture of pregnancy inevitably forces the lumbar spine into exaggerated concavity, a condition known as **gestational lordosis**. This unnatural curvature concentrates axial loads onto the posterior elements of the spine, facet joints, and intervertebral discs. Mechanical back pain is therefore exceedingly common, aggravated by the sheer weight of the gravid uterus stretching the abdominal wall and diminishing its stabilizing support. When abdominal musculature stretches beyond its optimal length-tension relationship, spinal erectors must work continuously without adequate rest, leading to myofascial fatigue and localized spasm.

Pelvic Girdle Pain and Symphysis Pubis Dysfunction

The pelvic ring is composed of the sacrum and two innominate bones connected anteriorly by the symphysis pubis and posteriorly by the sacroiliac joints. Under hormonal influence, these joints experience micro-motion and widening. When this laxity becomes excessive or asymmetrical, women experience **symphysis pubis dysfunction (SPD)** or broader **pelvic girdle pain (PGP)**. - **Clinical presentation**: Sharp, stabbing pain over the pubic symphysis, radiating to the groin, medial thighs, or lower back. - **Aggravating factors**: Weight-bearing on one leg (such as climbing stairs or putting on trousers), rolling over in bed, and stride-widening movements.

Extremity Adaptations: Gait, Edema, and Nerve Compression

Beyond the axial skeleton, the limbs undergo significant structural adjustments. The expanding uterus and widened pelvis alter foot mechanics, frequently flattening the longitudinal arch and causing a slight increase in shoe size. Peripheral dependent edema develops due to elevated venous pressure in the lower extremities from gravid uterine compression on the inferior vena cava. Furthermore, fluid retention within confined anatomical spaces can compress peripheral nerves. **Carpal tunnel syndrome** frequently occurs due to median nerve edema at the wrist, causing numbness and tingling in the lateral digits, while lower extremity sciatic nerve irritation can stem from direct pressure by the fetal head or deep pelvic muscle spasm.

Intrapartum Musculoskeletal Dynamics

During labour and delivery, the musculoskeletal system undergoes extreme mechanical stress. Relaxin-induced laxity peaks, allowing the pelvic joints to undergo passive widening and nutation to accommodate the descending fetal head. The maternal sacrum moves posteriorly, and the ischial spines slightly outward, maximizing pelvic outlet diameters. Lithotomy positioning during second-stage labour can strain unphysiological angles if not supported carefully, especially given that joint protection mechanisms are compromised by anesthesia or exhaustion. Midwives and nurses must ensure careful limb alignment during transfer and positioning to prevent postpartum joint injury.

Postpartum Puerperium and Structural Recovery

Following delivery, the physiological stressors of pregnancy begin to reverse, a phase known as puerperal involution. Hormone levels plummet rapidly: estrogen, progesterone, and placental relaxin drop within days of placental expulsion, though circulating relaxin may persist in joints for several months, keeping new mothers at risk for strain during early infant care activities. Ligaments gradually regain their pre-pregnant tensile strength, and lumbar lordosis flattens back toward baseline as the abdominal wall regains tone. However, diastasis recti abdominis—the separation of the rectus abdominis muscles—may persist, requiring targeted postnatal rehabilitation to restore core integrity.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Management

Comprehensive nursing care involves systematic screening for musculoskeletal discomforts across antenatal and postnatal visits. Assessment includes evaluating gait, palpating the symphysis pubis for tenderness, checking for diastasis recti, and screening for carpal tunnel paresthesia. Management focuses on conservative, non-pharmacological interventions: - Recommending low-heeled, supportive footwear. - Teaching proper body mechanics for lifting and rising from bed (log-rolling techniques). - Advising warm compresses and pelvic support belts for symphysis pubis dysfunction. - Encouraging safe, supervised core-strengthening exercises once cleared by medical staff.
