Musculoskeletal Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Hormonal Drivers of Musculoskeletal Laxity

Pregnancy triggers profound systemic changes, largely orchestrated by shifting hormone levels that prepare the maternal body for childbirth. Relaxin and progesterone are the primary biochemical drivers behind joint and ligamentous laxity. Relaxin, produced by the corpus luteum and placenta, actively remodels collagen fibers, softening connective tissue throughout the body. While this is essential for widening the pelvic outlet, it reduces joint stability universally. Progesterone acts synergistically, further relaxing smooth muscle and ligamentous structures. Consequently, pregnant women experience generalized joint hypermobility, leaving them susceptible to strain even with routine daily activities.

Postural Shifts and Center of Gravity

As the uterus expands anteriorly to accommodate the growing fetus, the maternal center of gravity shifts forward. To maintain upright balance, the body must naturally compensate. The woman's shoulders and trunk drift backward over the lower limbs, altering the normal standing posture. This compensatory adjustment increases biomechanical stress on the supporting muscles of the back and hips, making fatigue a common daily complaint for expectant mothers.

Spinal Alignment and Lordosis

The forward displacement of weight forces the lumbar spine to curve inward more deeply, a condition known as compensatory lumbar lordosis. This exaggerated spinal curve shifts weight back toward the heels but places intense, continuous pressure on the lumbar vertebrae, facet joints, and erector spinae muscles. Back pain is a nearly ubiquitous symptom resulting from this muscular fatigue and ligamentous strain. Common aggravating factors include prolonged standing, heavy lifting, and poor footwear choices that fail to support changing arches.

Pelvic Girdle Relaxation and Symphysis Pubis Dysfunction

The pelvic ring undergoes significant structural adaptation. Under the influence of relaxin, the sacroiliac joints and the symphysis pubis widen and become highly mobile. While this allows passage for the baby during birth, excessive movement can cause symphysis pubis dysfunction (SPD).

  • Symptoms: Sharp, burning pain over the pubic bone, radiating to the thighs and hips.
  • Aggravating Movements: Weight-bearing on one leg, climbing stairs, or rolling over in bed.
  • Clinical Note: Healthcare providers must differentiate normal pregnancy discomfort from true SPD, which severely limits mobility.

Lower Extremity Adaptations and Gait Changes

Lower limbs bear the brunt of increased total body weight and altered fluid dynamics. Dependent edema frequently develops in the feet and ankles late in the day due to elevated venous pressure from the enlarged uterus compressing the inferior vena cava. Night cramps in the calves are also common, often linked to altered calcium-phosphorus metabolism or muscle fatigue. Combined with pelvic widening, these changes produce a characteristic wide-based, waddling gait known as the waddling gait of pregnancy.

Musculoskeletal Mechanics During Labour and Delivery

During labour, the musculoskeletal system undergoes extreme functional demands. The pelvic ligaments allow the pelvic diameters to increase slightly as the fetus passes through the birth canal. Maternal positioning during labour—such as lithotomy or prolonged kneeling—can place unusual stress on the lower back and sacroiliac joints. Healthcare staff must ensure proper body alignment and padding during operative or prolonged deliveries to prevent postpartum joint strain and nerve compression injuries.

Postpartum Puerperium and Musculoskeletal Recovery

Following childbirth, the musculoskeletal system begins a gradual return to its pre-pregnant state, known as puerperal recovery. Relaxin lingers in the bloodstream for several weeks, meaning joint laxity persists temporarily even after delivery. Diastasis recti, the separation of the rectus abdominis muscles, frequently occurs during pregnancy and requires careful postpartum evaluation and targeted core rehabilitation. Ligaments slowly tighten, and posture normalizes as the center of gravity shifts back, though some women may experience persistent back or pelvic pain if recovery is sluggish.

Nursing Assessment and Management of Discomforts

Nurses play a vital role in identifying, assessing, and managing pregnancy-related musculoskeletal complaints. Assessment involves evaluating posture, gait, spinal curvature, and pinpointing areas of tenderness around the pelvis and spine. Management strategies focus on non-pharmacological comfort measures:

  • Supportive Devices: Maternity belts to relieve pelvic and back strain.
  • Postural Education: Teaching proper lifting techniques and avoiding high heels.
  • Exercise: Recommending safe, low-impact activities like swimming or prenatal yoga to strengthen core and back muscles.
  • Rest: Encouraging side-lying sleeping positions with a pillow between the knees.
