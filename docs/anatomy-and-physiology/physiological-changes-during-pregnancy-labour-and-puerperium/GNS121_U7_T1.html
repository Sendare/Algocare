Haematological Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Introduction and Plasma Volume Expansion

During pregnancy, a woman's body undergoes massive blood changes to support the growing baby and prepare for childbirth. The most striking change is the massive increase in total blood volume, which can rise by up to 40 to 50 percent above non-pregnant levels. This increase starts early, often in the first trimester, and peaks around the late second or early third trimester.

Plasma, the fluid part of the blood, increases much more than the actual red blood cells. Because the fluid portion expands faster than the solid cellular portion, the blood becomes more diluted. This condition is called physiological anaemia of pregnancy or pseudoanaemia. It is not a true disease or a lack of nutrients, but a normal balancing act. The extra fluid protects the mother from dangerous drops in blood pressure when she stands up or loses blood during delivery, and it ensures proper blood flow through the placenta.

Red Blood Cell Production and Iron Demands

Even though plasma expands faster, the body still makes more red blood cells to carry extra oxygen to the fetus. This increased production is driven by the hormone erythropoietin, which is released in higher amounts by the kidneys during pregnancy.

To build these new red blood cells, the body needs a large amount of iron. The total iron requirement during a normal pregnancy is about 1000 mg. Most of this iron goes to the fetus and placenta, while some is used for the mother's expanding red blood cell mass. Because many women in resource-limited settings enter pregnancy with low iron stores, routine iron and folic acid supplementation is standard antenatal practice in places like Nigeria to prevent true iron-deficiency anaemia.

White Blood Cells and Immune System Shifts

Pregnancy acts as a unique immunological state where the mother's body must tolerate a semi-allogeneic fetus (containing foreign genetic material from the father) while still defending against outside infections.

  • Leukocyte count: Total white blood cell (WBC) counts normally rise throughout pregnancy. The count can range from 6,000 to 16,000 /uL, and can even climb higher during active labour.
  • Neutrophils: The rise in total WBC count is mostly driven by an increase in neutrophils, which are the body's primary defense against bacterial infections.
  • Cellular immunity: Cell-mediated immunity is slightly suppressed during pregnancy, which explains why pregnant women are more susceptible to certain viral and intracellular infections, such as malaria.

Platelets and Coagulation Changes

The body prepares heavily for the bleeding risks of childbirth by altering the blood clotting system. This puts pregnant women in a hypercoagulable state (a state where blood clots more easily than usual).

  • Clotting factors: Most clotting factors, especially fibrinogen (Factor I) and factors VII, VIII, IX, and X, increase significantly as pregnancy progresses.
  • Anticoagulants: Natural clot-busting proteins like Protein S decrease, reducing the body's ability to dissolve unwanted clots.
  • Platelets: Platelet counts often drop slightly due to increased dilution and faster destruction, a condition called gestational thrombocytopenia, but counts usually remain within safe limits.

While this clotting shift protects the mother from haemorrhage during delivery, it vastly increases her risk of developing dangerous blood clots, such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT).

Haematological Adjustments During Labour and Delivery

Childbirth places sudden and intense stress on the circulatory system. During active labour, maternal cardiac output and blood pressure spike with every uterine contraction, pushing more blood back into the central circulation.

White blood cell counts surge even higher during labour and immediately after delivery, driven by physical stress, pain, and tissue trauma. At the same time, the body prepares for immediate blood loss. Vaginal delivery typically involves a blood loss of about 300 to 500 mL, while a caesarean section involves roughly 1000 mL. Thanks to the expanded blood volume built up over the prior nine months, a healthy woman tolerates this blood loss well without going into shock.

Postpartum Changes and Puerperium Recovery

The puerperium, or the postpartum period, is the time after delivery when the mother's body returns to its non-pregnant state. Immediately after birth, a large volume of blood that was previously inside the uteroplacental unit rushes back into the mother's general circulation, causing a temporary spike in cardiac output.

Within the first few days postpartum, the excess plasma volume is eliminated through heavy urination (diuresis) and sweating (diaphoresis). This causes haemoglobin and haematocrit levels to stabilize or temporarily rise as the fluid diluting the blood is lost. However, if the mother experienced a heavy postpartum haemorrhage, true anaemia will persist. The hypercoagulable state lingers for several weeks after delivery, meaning the risk of blood clots remains high well into the postnatal period.

Clinical Implications and Nursing Care in Nigeria

Understanding these blood changes is vital for Nigerian nurses providing antenatal, intrapartum, and postnatal care. High rates of malaria, nutritional deficiencies, and sometimes late booking for antenatal care mean that pregnant women are uniquely vulnerable to severe complications from blood disorders.

Nurses must educate mothers on the importance of adhering to routine iron and folic acid supplements, as well as intermittent preventive treatment for malaria during pregnancy. Nurses must also monitor postpartum patients closely for signs of postpartum haemorrhage and encourage early ambulation to prevent deep vein thrombosis. Recognizing the difference between normal physiological anaemia and true pathological deficiency ensures timely, life-saving interventions.
