Haematological Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Introduction and Plasma Volume Expansion

Pregnancy induces profound systemic alterations to meet the metabolic demands of the developing fetoplacental unit and to safeguard the mother against expected blood loss during childbirth. The most striking cardiovascular and haematological shift is the massive expansion of plasma volume, which begins as early as the sixth week of gestation and peaks around the thirty-fourth week. Total plasma volume increases by roughly forty to fifty percent above pre-pregnancy levels.

This expansion is primarily driven by activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, leading to renal sodium and water retention. Because plasma volume increases disproportionately compared to red blood cell mass expansion, the resulting state is often termed physiological anaemia of pregnancy or pseudoanaemia. This is a normal dilutional phenomenon rather than a true pathological deficiency, serving to reduce blood viscosity and optimize uteroplacental perfusion.

Erythrocyte Production and Iron Requirements

To support the expanding vascular network and supply the fetus, erythropoiesis is upregulated throughout gestation via elevated levels of erythropoietin. This heightened demand places a heavy burden on maternal iron stores.

  • Total iron requirement: Approximately one thousand milligrams are needed across an entire gestation.
  • Distribution: Three hundred milligrams are actively transferred to the fetus and placenta, five hundred milligrams are utilized for maternal red blood cell expansion, and two hundred milligrams are lost through normal baseline shedding.
  • Absorption: Intestinal absorption of dietary iron increases significantly, particularly in the later trimesters, facilitated by downregulated hepcidin levels.

When dietary intake fails to meet these demands, iron deficiency anaemia develops, increasing the risk of preterm birth and low birth weight.

Leukocyte Dynamics and Immune Shifts

Leukocyte counts undergo a progressive rise throughout gestation, primarily driven by an absolute increase in circulating neutrophils. Total white blood cell counts can range from six thousand up to fifteen thousand per microlitre during a normal pregnancy, and may surge even higher during the stress of active labour.

This leukocytosis is attributed to a combination of physiological stress, elevated cortisol levels which demarginate neutrophils from vascular walls, and a state of altered immune tolerance. Because normal baseline white blood cell counts are shifted upward, standard non-pregnant reference ranges do not apply. A leukocyte count that might suggest an infection in a non-pregnant individual could simply reflect a normal, healthy pregnancy state, requiring nurses to evaluate concurrent clinical signs rather than relying on laboratory values alone.

Coagulation and Haemostatic Alterations

Pregnancy is a naturally hypercoagulable state, an evolutionary adaptation designed to protect the mother from catastrophic haemorrhage during placental separation and delivery. This shift affects all branches of the haemostatic system.

Because clotting factors rise while natural anticoagulants like protein S decline and clot breakdown is inhibited, the risk of thromboembolic events is markedly elevated throughout pregnancy and the puerperium.

Platelet Count Variations

Platelet counts often experience a gradual, mild decline as pregnancy progresses, a condition known as gestational thrombocytopenia. While the total count drops, platelet functionality generally remains intact.

The reduction is primarily attributed to hemodilution from plasma volume expansion and accelerated platelet consumption within the uteroplacental circulation. In most cases, counts remain safely above seventy thousand to one hundred thousand per microlitre. However, differentiating benign gestational thrombocytopenia from pathological conditions like pre-eclampsia or immune thrombocytopenic purpura is essential for safe obstetric management.

Labour, Delivery, and Postpartum Shifts

The immediate peripartum period brings intense haemodynamic and haematological adjustments. During uterine contractions, blood is squeezed out of the intervillous space and back into the maternal systemic circulation, causing temporary spikes in cardiac output and blood pressure.

Blood loss during delivery is a normal expectation. Average blood loss is estimated at five hundred millilitres for a vaginal birth and one thousand millilitres for a caesarean section. To compensate for this anticipated loss and the removal of the low-resistance placental bed, maternal blood volume rapidly reconfigures.

During the early puerperium, a massive diuresis occurs as extracellular fluid is mobilized and excreted, resolving the pregnancy-induced hypervolemia within the first two weeks after childbirth.

Nursing Care and Clinical Context in Nigeria

In Nigerian clinical practice, managing maternal haematological changes requires heightened vigilance due to the high background prevalence of nutritional anaemias, malaria, and hemoglobinopathies such as sickle cell trait or disease.

Nurses play a vital frontline role in antenatal care by ensuring routine administration of haematinics—specifically iron and folic acid supplementation—and providing education on malaria prophylaxis through intermittent preventive treatment. Assessment goes beyond reviewing lab reports; nurses must monitor for pallor, severe fatigue, and signs of postpartum haemorrhage or deep vein thrombosis. Recognizing that a Nigerian mother may present with baseline nutritional deficits underscores why prophylactic supplementation and prompt screening are non-negotiable standards of care.
