Respiratory Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Introduction and Hormonal Drivers of Maternal Respiration

Pregnancy induces profound systemic transformations to meet the heightened metabolic demands of both the mother and the developing fetus. At the center of these adaptations is progesterone, a hormone that surges steadily throughout gestation. Progesterone directly stimulates the respiratory center in the medulla oblongata, lowering the threshold and increasing sensitivity to carbon dioxide. Consequently, minute ventilation rises significantly by up to fifty percent, driven primarily by an increase in tidal volume rather than respiratory rate. A common clinical pitfall in obstetric nursing is mistaking this normal, pregnancy-induced hyperventilation for pathological respiratory distress or hypoxia. Pregnant women frequently report a subjective, benign sensation of breathlessness, termed physiological dyspnea, which begins early in the first trimester without any underlying cardiopulmonary pathology.

Anatomical Modifications of the Upper and Lower Airways

The expanding uterus alters maternal biomechanics, pushing the diaphragm upward by as much as four centimeters. Concurrently, the subcostal angle widens, and the transverse diameter of the thoracic cage increases by approximately two centimeters to compensate for the loss of vertical height. Upper airway tissues undergo marked hyperemia, capillary engorgement, and edema due to elevated estrogen levels and systemic vasodilation. These mucosal changes render the nasal passages and larynx highly vascular and friable. Routine clinical procedures such as nasotracheal suctioning or endotracheal intubation carry a drastically increased risk of bleeding and airway trauma during pregnancy and labour.

Alterations in Pulmonary Volumes and Capacities

- **Tidal Volume (TV)**: Increases significantly as deeper breaths are taken to accommodate heightened oxygen consumption. - **Residual Volume (RV)**: Decreases markedly due to the upward displacement of the diaphragm and mechanical compression of the lungs. - **Functional Residual Capacity (FRC)**: Drops by twenty to thirty percent, representing the combined reduction of residual volume and expiratory reserve volume. - **Total Lung Capacity (TLC)**: Remains largely unchanged or decreases only slightly, as the upward shift in diaphragm height is offset by the outward expansion of the lower rib cage.

Arterial Blood Gas and Compensated Respiratory Alkalosis

The chronic hyperventilation of pregnancy blows off excessive amounts of carbon dioxide, leading to a state of compensated respiratory alkalosis. | Blood Gas Parameter | Normal Non-Pregnant Value | Normal Pregnant Value | |---|---|---| | PaCO2 | 35 - 45 mmHg | 27 - 32 mmHg | | pH | 7.38 - 7.42 | 7.40 - 7.45 (Slightly alkalotic) | | Serum Bicarbonate (HCO3-) | 22 - 26 mEq/L | 18 - 22 mEq/L | To buffer this alkaline shift, the kidneys excrete excess bicarbonate into the urine, lowering serum bicarbonate levels. If a pregnant patient experiences a sudden crisis, such as severe sepsis or diabetic ketoacidosis, this depleted bicarbonate buffer reserve means she has a very limited margin of safety before slipping into uncompensated metabolic acidosis.

Respiratory Adjustments During Labour and Delivery

Labour places an immense physical demand on the maternal respiratory system. Uterine contractions cause repeated, transient increases in cardiac output and oxygen consumption. Anxiety, pain, and physical exertion during the first and second stages of labour frequently trigger bouts of acute hyperventilation, which can paradoxically lead to maternal hypocapnia, uterine vasoconstriction, and fetal distress. During the second stage, maternal pushing involves prolonged Valsalva maneuvers, temporarily impeding venous return to the heart and reducing uteroplacental perfusion.

Postpartum Respiratory Recovery During the Puerperium

Following the expulsion of the placenta, the sudden removal of progesterone initiates the reversal of gestational respiratory changes. The diaphragm rapidly descends as intra-abdominal pressure is relieved by uterine involution. Functional residual capacity begins to climb back toward pre-pregnant baseline levels within days of delivery. However, complete restoration of chest wall mechanics and blood gas parameters can take several weeks into the puerperium.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Implications

Midwives and nurse practitioners working in Nigerian clinical settings must carefully differentiate normal physiological adaptations from true pathology during antenatal and intrapartum care. When evaluating a pregnant woman complaining of breathlessness, nurses should assess for red flags such as tachypnea, chest pain, hemoptysis, or asymmetric lower limb swelling that might indicate a pulmonary embolism. In environments with high burdens of anemia, the compounded stress of pregnancy-induced respiratory changes can precipitate frank heart failure. Routine care must include educating women on normal breathing exercises, advising against tight abdominal binding in the postpartum period, and ensuring vigilant monitoring of airway patency during anesthesia.
