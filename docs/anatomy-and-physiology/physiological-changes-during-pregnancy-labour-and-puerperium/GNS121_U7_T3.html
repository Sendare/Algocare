Respiratory Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Introduction to Maternal Respiratory Adaptations

Pregnancy induces a profound series of anatomical, physiological, and biochemical changes across all body systems to support the developing foetus and prepare the maternal body for birth. Among these, the respiratory system undergoes remarkable transformations to meet the heightened metabolic demands of both the mother and the uteroplacental unit. Oxygen consumption increases significantly throughout gestation, rising by about twenty to thirty percent due to the metabolic activity of the foetus, placenta, and enlarged maternal organs such as the uterus and heart. To cope with this increased oxygen demand and ensure adequate waste elimination, the maternal respiratory system modifies its function remarkably without compromising safety. Understanding these adaptations is crucial for nursing students in Nigeria, as baseline respiratory values shift entirely during a normal pregnancy. Conditions that might present with specific respiratory parameters in non-pregnant individuals can be misinterpreted if the physiological shifts of gestation are not factored in. Nurses must recognize that these changes are entirely normal and adaptive, serving to protect the foetus from hypoxia and maintain a necessary concentration gradient for carbon dioxide exchange across the placenta. This foundational knowledge forms the basis of comprehensive antenatal, intrapartum, and postpartum maternal care, ensuring that clinical interventions respect these natural physiological boundaries.

Anatomical Changes of the Respiratory Tract During Pregnancy

The anatomical structure of the respiratory tract undergoes noticeable changes as the pregnancy progresses, driven largely by the mechanical growth of the uterus. As the uterus expands into the abdominal cavity, it exerts upward pressure on the diaphragm, elevating the diaphragmatic dome by as much as four centimetres by the third trimester. This upward displacement restricts downward movement, but to compensate for the loss of vertical thoracic space, the transverse diameter of the chest wall increases by about two centimetres, and the subcostal angle widens significantly. Additionally, the increasing levels of circulating hormones, particularly oestrogen, cause hyperaemia, oedema, and capillary engorgement of the mucous membranes lining the entire upper respiratory tract. This mucosal swelling can lead to symptoms such as nasal stuffiness, epistaxis, and a sensation of upper airway obstruction, which pregnant women frequently report as rhinitis of pregnancy. Nursing students must be aware that these anatomical and mucosal changes make pregnant patients more vulnerable to upper respiratory tract infections and complications during endotracheal intubation. The larynx and vocal cords may also exhibit mild oedema, requiring healthcare providers to use smaller endotracheal tubes if general anesthesia becomes necessary during obstetric emergencies. Recognizing these structural shifts aids in delivering safe, individualized nursing care across all stages of maternity practice in Nigeria.

Physiological and Hormonal Drivers of Respiratory Alterations

The physiological and functional alterations of the respiratory system are heavily regulated by hormonal changes, particularly the surge in progesterone and oestrogen. Progesterone acts as a powerful respiratory stimulant, directly affecting the respiratory center in the medulla oblongata to increase sensitivity to carbon dioxide. This central stimulation leads to a persistent increase in maternal minute ventilation, which is achieved primarily through an increase in tidal volume rather than respiratory rate. Because the maternal respiratory center responds to lower thresholds of carbon dioxide, the mother chronically blows off excess carbon dioxide, resulting in a state of mild, chronic respiratory alkalosis. Oestrogen complements these changes by promoting mucosal vascularity and tissue laxity within the thoracic cage, allowing the ribs to flare outward and accommodate the expanding uterus without causing severe respiratory distress. Understanding these hormonal drivers explains why pregnant women often experience a subjective sensation of breathlessness, known as physiological dyspnoea, even in the absence of any underlying pathology or cardiac compromise. Nurses must be equipped to distinguish this normal, progesterone-driven breathlessness from pathological dyspnoea caused by anaemia, asthma, or heart failure, providing reassurance and proper monitoring to expectant mothers in Nigerian healthcare facilities.

Changes in Pulmonary Volumes and Capacities

Pulmonary volumes and capacities undergo distinct alterations throughout gestation as a direct consequence of both anatomical compression and hormonal stimulation. Tidal volume, the amount of air inhaled and exhaled with each normal breath, increases significantly by about thirty to forty percent. Because of this deeper breathing pattern alongside a relatively constant respiratory rate, minute ventilation rises substantially. In contrast, residual volume—the volume of air remaining in the lungs after a maximal expiration—decreases by roughly twenty percent due to the upward displacement of the diaphragm by the enlarged uterus. Consequently, functional residual capacity, which is the sum of residual volume and expiratory reserve volume, is reduced by approximately twenty to thirty percent. This reduction in functional residual capacity has profound clinical implications because it diminishes the oxygen reservoir within the maternal lungs. During periods of apnoea or airway manipulation, pregnant women desaturate much faster than non-pregnant individuals, leading to rapid onset hypoxaemia. Inspiratory capacity increases to compensate, while total lung capacity remains relatively unchanged or only slightly decreased. Nurses caring for obstetric patients in Nigeria must keep these volume shifts in mind, especially during surgical procedures or emergency resuscitations, ensuring that pre-oxygenation is prioritized to safeguard maternal and foetal oxygenation.

Arterial Blood Gas and Acid-Base Balance Alterations

The arterial blood gas profile changes notably during pregnancy as a result of progesterone-induced hyperventilation. Because the mother breathes off more carbon dioxide, the arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide decreases from a non-pregnant level of about forty millimetres of mercury down to around thirty millimetres of mercury, creating a state of chronic respiratory alkalosis. To compensate for this alkaline shift and maintain systemic pH within a safe physiological range, the kidneys increase the renal excretion of bicarbonate, lowering serum bicarbonate levels from twenty-four to roughly twenty milliequivalents per litre. Despite this compensatory renal excretion, the arterial blood pH remains slightly shifted toward the alkaline side, typically resting around seven decimal four zero to seven decimal four five. Meanwhile, the arterial partial pressure of oxygen increases, rising to approximately one hundred to one hundred and six millimetres of mercury, which enhances the oxygen pressure gradient across the placenta to facilitate optimal oxygen transfer to the foetus. Understanding these specific arterial blood gas values is vital for nursing and medical personnel in Nigeria when managing critically ill obstetric patients, ensuring that normal pregnancy values are not misdiagnosed as uncompensated metabolic acidosis or severe respiratory alkalosis.

Respiratory Adjustments During Labour and Delivery

Labour and delivery impose immense physical and metabolic demands on the maternal respiratory system, requiring rapid adjustments. During the first stage of labour, uterine contractions cause maternal anxiety, pain, and increased muscular effort, which can elevate both respiratory rate and tidal volume. During the second stage of labour, voluntary and involuntary pushing manoeuvres—often accompanied by the Valsalva manoeuvre—cause dramatic fluctuations in intrathoracic pressure. When the mother holds her breath and pushes during contractions, venous return to the heart transiently drops, followed by a surge when the breath is released. This mechanical strain can lead to hyperventilation if pain is poorly controlled, potentially causing acute respiratory alkalosis, hypocapnia, and subsequent uterine vasoconstriction that might temporarily reduce foetal oxygenation. Conversely, extreme exhaustion can lead to hypoventilation and hypoxia. Effective nursing support, encouraging controlled breathing techniques, and offering reassurance during contractions are critical intrapartum interventions. Midwives and nurses in Nigerian labour wards must monitor respiratory patterns closely, guiding mothers to take steady breaths between pushes to prevent maternal exhaustion and maintain optimal gas exchange for both mother and foetus throughout the birthing process.

Postpartum Respiratory Recovery During the Puerperium

The puerperium, or postpartum period, marks the rapid reversal of gestational adaptations as the maternal body returns to its pre-pregnant state. Immediately following the delivery of the placenta, the sudden removal of the uteroplacental unit and the rapid reduction in uterine size relieve the upward mechanical pressure on the diaphragm. The diaphragm returns to its normal anatomical position within weeks, allowing the chest wall and lungs to regain their pre-pregnant dimensions. Progesterone and oestrogen levels drop precipitously within the first few days postpartum, causing the central respiratory drive to normalize and ventilation rates to return to baseline levels. Tidal volume, minute ventilation, and respiratory rates gradually decrease to normal non-pregnant parameters, usually taking between one to six weeks to fully normalize. However, residual pulmonary changes and mild airway hyperreactivity may persist briefly. Nurses must monitor postpartum women in Nigerian maternity units for signs of respiratory complications such as postpartum hemorrhage-induced shock, pulmonary thromboembolism—which is a major risk due to postpartum hypercoagulability—and atelectasis following caesarean sections. Encouraging early ambulation, deep breathing exercises, and incentive spirometry helps prevent postpartum pulmonary complications and ensures a smooth recovery.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Implications for Nigerian Practice

Nursing assessment of the respiratory system in Nigerian practice requires a thorough understanding of normal gestational variations to accurately identify clinical abnormalities. Routine assessments should include evaluating respiratory rate, depth, rhythm, breath sounds, and oxygen saturation using pulse oximetry, keeping in mind that a normal oxygen saturation in pregnancy is typically high. Nurses must proactively distinguish between harmless physiological dyspnoea and pathological conditions such as asthma exacerbations, pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, or thromboembolism. In resource-constrained or busy clinical settings across Nigeria, health education is a vital nursing responsibility; mothers should be taught warning signs like chest pain, severe shortness of breath, persistent cough, or cyanosis that necessitate immediate medical evaluation. Furthermore, nurses managing women undergoing caesarean sections must prioritize postoperative respiratory care, including pain management to permit deep breathing, early mobilization, and chest physiotherapy to prevent postoperative atelectasis. Documenting findings accurately and communicating subtle changes in respiratory status to the multidisciplinary healthcare team ensures prompt intervention, reduces maternal morbidity and mortality, and promotes excellent healthcare outcomes in maternal and child health nursing across Nigeria.
