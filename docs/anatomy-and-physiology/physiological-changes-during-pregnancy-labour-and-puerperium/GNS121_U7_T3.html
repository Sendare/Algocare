Respiratory Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Introduction and Hormonal Drivers of Maternal Respiration

Pregnancy triggers remarkable shifts across every body system to support the growing foetus, and the respiratory system is no exception. These changes begin very early in gestation, often before a woman even knows she is pregnant. The primary catalyst for these profound alterations is the hormone progesterone, alongside rising levels of oestrogen. Progesterone acts directly on the respiratory control centre in the brainstem, dramatically increasing the mother's sensitivity to carbon dioxide. As a result, the pregnant woman breathes deeper and slightly faster, pulling in more air to meet the higher oxygen demands of both her own body and the developing placenta.

Anatomical Alterations of the Respiratory Tract

As the uterus expands week by week, it pushes upward against the abdominal organs, which in turn displaces the diaphragm upward by about four centimeters. This physical crowding changes the shape of the thoracic cavity. To compensate for the loss of vertical space, the lower rib cage flares outward, and the subcostal angle widens significantly. At the same time, high circulating oestrogen levels cause increased vascularity, generalized oedema, and hyperaemia of the entire upper respiratory mucosa. This means the mucous membranes in the nose and throat become swollen and soft, making pregnant women prone to nasal stuffiness, nosebleeds, and a feeling of upper airway fullness that can mimic a persistent cold.

Shifts in Pulmonary Volumes and Capacities

Even though the diaphragm is pushed upward, total lung capacity remains relatively unchanged because the rib cage widens. However, the specific volumes of air moving in and out shift noticeably:

  • Tidal Volume: Increases substantially, meaning the volume of air inhaled and exhaled with each normal breath goes up.
  • Residual Volume: Decreases because the upward pressure of the uterus compresses the lower lung zones.
  • Functional Residual Capacity: Decreases markedly as a combined result of the lower residual volume and reduced expiratory reserve volume.
  • Vital Capacity: Remains largely unchanged throughout a normal, uncomplicated pregnancy.

Arterial Blood Gas and Acid-Base Adjustments

Because the pregnant woman breathes deeper and faster, she blows off more carbon dioxide than usual. This creates a state of chronic compensated respiratory alkalosis.

To prevent the blood from becoming too alkaline, the kidneys excrete excess bicarbonate in the urine. A common clinical pitfall is misinterpreting these normal pregnancy lab values as a sign of disease or metabolic acidosis if the nurse is unfamiliar with normal maternal physiology.

Respiratory Adjustments During Labour and Delivery

Labour places an intense physical and metabolic demand on the mother's respiratory system. With every uterine contraction, oxygen consumption spikes significantly. Pain, anxiety, and physical exertion can easily trigger hyperventilation, especially during the second stage of pushing. If a labouring woman hyperventilates too severely, she blows off too much carbon dioxide, which can constrict the blood vessels in the placenta and temporarily reduce oxygen flow to the foetus. Conversely, maternal exhaustion can lead to inadequate breathing and subsequent low oxygen levels in the blood. Calm, controlled breathing techniques and position changes help maintain a stable oxygen balance throughout delivery.

Postpartum Respiratory Recovery During the Puerperium

Immediately after the baby and placenta are delivered, the upward pressure on the diaphragm is suddenly relieved. The diaphragm drops back down, the abdominal organs settle, and the rib cage begins its slow return to its pre-pregnancy shape. However, full anatomical and functional restoration of the respiratory tract takes time. Tidal volume and respiratory rate usually return to normal baseline levels within one to three weeks following birth. Residual volume and functional residual capacity take slightly longer, often requiring several weeks for complete normalization as chest wall compliance fully recovers.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Implications for Nigerian Practice

In busy Nigerian clinical settings, nurses must accurately distinguish normal physiological breathlessness from true respiratory pathology. Mild shortness of breath—often called physiological dyspnoea of pregnancy—affects up to seventy percent of healthy pregnant women due to elevated tidal volumes, and it requires reassurance rather than medical treatment. However, any sudden onset of breathlessness, chest pain, a productive cough with blood-tinged sputum, or low oxygen saturation must be investigated immediately for conditions like pulmonary embolism or pneumonia. Nurses should educate women on recognizing danger signs early, promote gentle ambulation postpartum to prevent blood clots, and ensure proper positioning during antenatal clinics.
