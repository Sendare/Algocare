Sympathetic Nervous System

Introduction and Anatomical Organization

The sympathetic nervous system (SNS) is a primary division of the autonomic nervous system, responsible for preparing the body for stressful or emergency situations, commonly summarized as the fight-or-flight response. Anatomically, it originates in the thoracolumbar region of the spinal cord, specifically from segments T1 to L2. This structural arrangement features distinct pathways: preganglionic neurons emerge short and synapse in ganglia close to the spinal column—primarily within the sympathetic chain ganglia or prevertebral ganglia. From these ganglia, long postganglionic fibers travel to target effector organs throughout the body. This structural design ensures widespread, simultaneous activation across multiple organ systems when a threat is perceived, distinguishing it from the localized, discrete responses of the parasympathetic system.

Neurotransmitters and Adrenergic Receptors

Chemical signaling in the sympathetic nervous system relies primarily on two key neurotransmitters: acetylcholine and norepinephrine. Preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine to stimulate postganglionic neurons via nicotinic receptors. In contrast, most postganglionic fibers release norepinephrine onto target organs, classifying them as adrenergic.

The physiological effect on an organ depends entirely on the specific receptor subtype present on its cells:

  • Alpha-1 receptors: Typically mediate smooth muscle contraction, leading to vasoconstriction of blood vessels and mydriasis (pupil dilation).
  • Alpha-2 receptors: Function largely through negative feedback loops to inhibit further norepinephrine release.
  • Beta-1 receptors: Primarily located in the heart, increasing heart rate (positive chronotropy) and contractility (positive inotropy).
  • Beta-2 receptors: Predominantly found in the smooth muscle of bronchioles and blood vessels supplying skeletal muscle, promoting relaxation and vasodilation.

The Fight-or-Flight Physiological Mechanism

When an individual encounters physical danger or severe psychological stress, the hypothalamus rapidly activates the sympathetic nervous system alongside the adrenal-medullary system. Sympathetic nerve impulses stimulate the adrenal medulla to secrete epinephrine and norepinephrine directly into the bloodstream, amplifying and prolonging the neural response. This neuroendocrine cascade shifts metabolic priorities immediately away from non-essential functions like digestion and renal filtration, redirecting oxygen and glucose toward skeletal muscles, the heart, and the brain. Blood pressure spikes, airways dilate to maximize oxygen intake, and liver glycogenolysis releases stored energy into circulation, priming the organism for intense physical exertion.

Target Organ Responses

Sympathetic activation produces profound, targeted changes across nearly every organ system in the human body to optimize physical performance during a crisis.

Clinical Pharmacology and Nursing Implications

Understanding sympathetic physiology is essential for safe nursing practice, particularly when administering medications that mimic or block adrenergic activity. Sympathomimetics (adrenergic agonists) like epinephrine or salbutamol are used clinically to treat conditions such as anaphylaxis and acute asthma attacks by stimulating fight-or-flight pathways. Conversely, sympatholytics (adrenergic antagonists) are widely prescribed for cardiovascular disorders. For example, beta-blockers reduce cardiac workload and lower blood pressure by blocking beta-1 receptors, while alpha-blockers relax vascular smooth muscle to treat hypertension or benign prostatic hyperplasia.

When caring for patients on these drugs, nurses must monitor for predictable extension effects: beta-blockers can precipitate bronchospasm in patients with asthma and mask early signs of hypoglycemia, while adrenergic stimulants can cause tachycardia, palpitations, and severe anxiety.
