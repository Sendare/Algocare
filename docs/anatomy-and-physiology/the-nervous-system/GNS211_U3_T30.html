Sympathetic Nervous System

Introduction to the Sympathetic Nervous System

The sympathetic nervous system (SNS) is a division of the autonomic nervous system that prepares the body for stressful or emergency situations. Often called the fight-or-flight system, it operates mostly outside of conscious control to mobilize energy and protect the body from perceived danger. When activated, it shifts bodily resources away from resting processes like digestion and toward survival actions like running or fighting.

Anatomical Organization and Pathways

The sympathetic nervous system originates in the thoracolumbar region of the spinal cord, specifically from segments T1 to L2. Structurally, it relies on a two-neuron pathway to reach target organs.

  • Preganglionic neurons: These are short fibers that emerge from the spinal cord and release acetylcholine to stimulate postganglionic neurons.
  • Sympathetic chain ganglia: A paired bundle of nerve clusters running parallel to the spinal column where most preganglionic fibers synapse.
  • Postganglionic neurons: Long fibers extending from the ganglia directly to target organs, releasing norepinephrine to affect organ function.

Neurotransmitters and Receptors

Communication within the sympathetic nervous system involves specific chemical messengers called neurotransmitters and the cellular structures that detect them, known as adrenergic receptors.

  • Acetylcholine: Released by all preganglionic nerve fibers to stimulate postganglionic neurons.
  • Norepinephrine: The primary neurotransmitter released by postganglionic fibers onto target organs.
  • Alpha receptors: Subtypes like alpha-1 generally cause blood vessel constriction and pupil dilation.
  • Beta receptors: Subtypes like beta-1 increase heart rate, while beta-2 relax bronchial tubes in the lungs.

The Fight-or-Flight Response Mechanism

When the brain perceives danger, the hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system and triggers the adrenal medulla to release epinephrine and norepinephrine directly into the bloodstream. This hormonal surge acts alongside direct nerve signals to create a whole-body reaction. Heart rate accelerates, breathing deepens, liver cells break down glycogen into glucose for quick energy, and blood flow is diverted away from the skin and digestive tract toward skeletal muscles.

Effects on Major Body Organs

Sympathetic activation produces distinct, predictable changes across various organ systems to optimize physical performance during stress.

Organ / SystemSympathetic Effect
HeartIncreases heart rate and contraction strength
LungsDilates bronchioles to increase airflow
EyesDilates pupils to let in more light
SkinCauses sweating and constricts blood vessels
Digestive TractReduces motility and blood flow
BladderRelaxes the detrusor muscle and contracts sphincters to hold urine

Clinical Implications and Pharmacological Agents

Understanding sympathetic function helps nurses anticipate how certain drugs will alter vital signs. Medications can either mimic sympathetic effects, called sympathomimetics or adrenergics, or block them, called sympatholytics or beta-blockers. For example, beta-blockers lower blood pressure and heart rate by blocking norepinephrine at receptor sites, which is useful in treating hypertension and heart failure. Conversely, adrenergic agonists like epinephrine are used during severe allergic reactions to open airways and maintain blood pressure.
