Development of the Nervous System

Introduction and Neurulation: The Neural Tube

The complex architecture of the human nervous system originates remarkably early in embryonic life through a process called neurulation. During the third week of gestation, the ectoderm—the outermost germ layer—thickens along the dorsal midline to form the neural plate. As this plate elongates, its lateral edges rise up as neural folds, creating a central depression known as the neural groove. These folds eventually converge and fuse at the midline, transforming the flat plate into a closed cylindrical neural tube.

Fusion begins in the future cervical region and zips simultaneously toward the cranial and caudal ends, leaving temporary openings called the anterior (cranial) neuropore and posterior (caudal) neuropore. Failure of these neuropores to close properly leads to severe structural malformations. The anterior neuropore typically closes around the 25th day, while the posterior closes by the 27th day. Concurrently, a population of cells pinching off from the edges of the neural folds forms the neural crest, a remarkably versatile group often termed the fourth germ layer due to its wide-ranging contributions to peripheral structures.

Neural Crest Cells and Their Derivatives

As the neural folds fuse, a distinct population of transient cells detaches and migrates away from the dorsal margins of the neural tube. These are the neural crest cells. Their remarkable migratory capacity and pluripotency allow them to populate diverse regions of the developing embryo, differentiating into an astonishing array of cell types.

  • Sensory Ganglia: Form the dorsal root ganglia and sensory ganglia of cranial nerves V, VII, IX, and X.
  • Autonomic Ganglia: Give rise to sympathetic chain ganglia, parasympathetic ganglia, and enteric plexuses.
  • Schwann Cells: Provide the myelin sheaths for peripheral nerves throughout the body.
  • Melanocytes: Migrate to the epidermis to produce skin and hair pigmentation.
  • Craniofacial Structures: Form the bones, cartilage, and odontoblasts of the face and skull.
  • Adrenal Medulla: Differentiate into catecholamine-secreting chromaffin cells.

Brain Vesicle Development and Regionalization

Shortly after the neural tube closes, its cranial end undergoes rapid volumetric expansion and bends to form distinct swellings known as primary brain vesicles. By the fourth week of development, three distinct chambers are visible:

  1. Prosencephalon: The forebrain, which subsequently divides into the telencephalon and diencephalon.
  2. Mesencephalon: The midbrain, which remains undivided throughout embryonic development.
  3. Rhombencephalon: The hindbrain, which subdivides into the metencephalon and myelencephalon.

By the fifth week, these three primary vesicles further differentiate into five secondary brain vesicles. The telencephalon expands massively to form the cerebral hemispheres and lateral ventricles, while the diencephalon gives rise to the thalamus, hypothalamus, and optic vesicles. The mesencephalon retains the cerebral aqueduct and develops the tectum and tegmentum. The metencephalon forms the pons and cerebellum, and the myelencephalon develops into the medulla oblongata. The hollow core of these vesicles persists as the ventricular system of the brain, lined by ependymal cells and filled with cerebrospinal fluid.

Spinal Cord Morphogenesis and Histological Layering

While the cranial end balloons into the brain, the caudal portion of the neural tube undergoes a much simpler elongation process to form the spinal cord. The walls of this tubular region consist initially of a pseudostratified neuroepithelium. Through active cell division, this layer gives rise to three distinct concentric zones:

  • Ventricular Zone (Ependymal Layer): The innermost layer lining the central canal, containing neural progenitor cells that generate neurons and macroglial cells.
  • Intermediate Zone (Mantle Layer): The middle layer formed by migrating neuroblasts, which ultimately becomes the gray matter containing neuronal cell bodies.
  • Marginal Zone: The outermost layer composed of nerve fiber tracts running up and down the cord, which myelinate to form the white matter.

Furthermore, the neural tube walls are partitioned longitudinally into a dorsal alar plate, which processes sensory information, and a ventral basal plate, which houses motor neurons. A sulcus limitans physically separates these two functional domains.

Neurogenesis, Migration, and Axonal Pathfinding

Generating a functional nervous system requires millions of neurons to be born, travel vast distances across the embryo, and connect with precise target cells. Neurogenesis occurs primarily within the ventricular zones, where progenitor cells divide symmetrically and asymmetrically. Once born, immature post-mitotic neurons begin their journey.

Most cortical neurons utilize radial glial cells as physical scaffolds, climbing outward from the inner ventricular surface toward the pial surface in an inside-out fashion—meaning older neurons occupy deeper cortical layers while younger ones migrate past them to form superficial layers.

Upon reaching their destination, neurons extend axons tipped with dynamic structures called growth cones. These specialized endings sample the extracellular microenvironment for chemical and physical cues. Chemoattractants like netrins draw the growth cone forward, while chemorepellants like semaphorins steer it away from incorrect pathways, ensuring precise target innervation.

Synaptogenesis and Myelination

Establishing functional neural circuits requires the formation of synaptic contacts and the insulation of axonal pathways. Synaptogenesis begins when an arriving growth cone encounters an appropriate postsynaptic target cell. Specialized adhesion molecules anchor the membranes together, while presynaptic vesicles cluster and postsynaptic neurotransmitter receptors aggregate, forming a functional synapse.

Initially, many more synapses are formed than are retained in the adult brain. A massive wave of programmed cell death (apoptosis) and synaptic pruning subsequently refines these connections based on neural activity and trophic factor availability.

Simultaneously, myelination accelerates action potential propagation. In the peripheral nervous system, Schwann cells wrap their cell membranes repeatedly around a single axon. In the central nervous system, oligodendrocytes extend multiple processes to myelinate segments of several adjacent axons. Myelination begins during the mid-fetal period but continues postnatally well into adolescence, particularly within prefrontal cortical association areas.

Congenital Malformations of the Nervous System

Errors during early embryogenesis lead to significant congenital neurological disorders. Neural tube defects (NTDs) arise from failed closure of the cranial or caudal neuropores during the fourth week of gestation.

  • Anencephaly: Results from failure of the anterior neuropore to close, causing the forebrain and cranial vault to remain underdeveloped, which is incompatible with long-term survival.
  • Spina Bifida: Results from failure of the caudal neuropore or posterior vertebral arches to close properly.
  • *Spina bifida occulta* is a mild, often asymptomatic bony defect covered by skin, sometimes marked by a tuft of hair.
  • *Meningocele* involves protrusion of the meninges through the defect.
  • *Myelomeningocele* is the most severe form, where both meninges and neural tissue protrude, leading to permanent lower limb paralysis and neurogenic bowel and bladder dysfunction.

Adequate maternal periconceptional intake of folic acid (vitamin B9) significantly reduces the incidence of these structural anomalies by supporting rapid DNA synthesis during neural tube closure.
