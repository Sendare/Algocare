Development of the Nervous System

Introduction to Nervous System Development

The human nervous system starts as a very simple structure in the early embryo long before birth. During the third week of pregnancy, a specialized layer of cells called the ectoderm thickens to form the neural plate. This plate is the very foundation from which the entire brain and spinal cord will eventually grow. Understanding this embryological journey helps nurses and midwives recognize why certain birth defects happen very early in pregnancy, often before a mother even knows she is pregnant. The process relies heavily on precise cellular signals that instruct cells where to go and what to become.

Neurulation and Neural Tube Formation

As development continues, the flat neural plate folds inward to create a channel known as the neural groove. The edges of this groove rise up, curl toward each other, and fuse together to form the neural tube. This tube closes like a zipper, starting in the middle and moving outward toward the head and tail ends.

  • Neural Crest Cells: Cells that pinch off during the closing process to form the peripheral nervous system.
  • Anterior Neuropore: The head end of the tube that must close properly to form a normal brain.
  • Posterior Neuropore: The tail end of the tube that closes to form the lower spinal structures.

Differentiation of the Neural Tube and Brain Vesicles

Once the neural tube closes completely, the top portion expands and bends to form primary brain swellings called vesicles. These primary vesicles quickly subdivide into secondary vesicles that give rise to specific regions of the adult brain.

Development of the Spinal Cord and Peripheral Nervous System

While the brain forms from the upper neural tube, the lower cylindrical portion develops into the spinal cord. Inside the developing spinal cord, cells divide and organize into distinct functional zones. The inner region becomes gray matter, housing cell bodies, while the outer region becomes white matter containing nerve tracts.

Meanwhile, the neural crest cells migrate away from the fusing neural tube to build the peripheral nervous system. These versatile cells turn into sensory ganglia, autonomic ganglia, and Schwann cells, which are responsible for wrapping peripheral nerve fibers.

Neurogenesis, Migration, and Axonal Guidance

Producing enough neurons for the brain requires massive cell division, a process called neurogenesis. Once created, immature neurons must travel long distances from their birthplace to their final destinations in the developing brain, known as neuronal migration. They often slide along special support fibers provided by glial cells.

After reaching the correct neighborhood, neurons extend long projections called axons. These growing tips, called growth cones, use chemical signposts in the environment to navigate toward their proper targets, ensuring correct wiring between distant parts of the nervous system.

Synaptogenesis and Myelination

Wiring the nervous system together requires more than just getting axons to their targets; neurons must also form functional connections, or synapses. Synaptogenesis happens rapidly during late fetal life and early childhood, allowing electrical and chemical communication between cells.

Shortly after, myelination begins. Specialized glial cells wrap around axons with a fatty substance called myelin. This insulation greatly increases the speed at which nerve impulses travel. Myelination starts in the spinal cord and sensory pathways before moving to higher brain centers, continuing well into adolescence.

Congenital Malfunctions and Developmental Disorders

Because nervous system development is complex, disruptions can lead to serious birth defects. Neural tube defects occur when the neural tube fails to close completely. An open spinal defect is known as spina bifida, while failure to close the head end results in anencephaly, a condition where major parts of the brain fail to develop.

Adequate maternal intake of folic acid before and during early pregnancy has proven remarkably effective in preventing many of these structural birth defects, highlighting the importance of early prenatal education.
