Structure of the Autonomic Nervous System

Introduction to the Autonomic Nervous System

The Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) is a branch of the peripheral nervous system that regulates involuntary bodily functions. Without your conscious thought, it keeps your heart beating, digests your food, adjusts your blood pressure, and controls your breathing rate. It operates mainly through two opposing pathways to keep the body in a stable, balanced state known as homeostasis.

Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Divisions

The ANS is split into two main parts that usually have opposite effects on the target organs.

  • Sympathetic nervous system: This system drives the fight-or-flight response. It prepares the body for action, exercise, or danger by speeding up the heart, dilating airways, and releasing stored energy.
  • Parasympathetic nervous system: This system drives the rest-and-digest response. It conserves energy, slows down the heart rate, and promotes routine house-keeping duties like digestion and waste elimination.

Two-Neuron Chain Organization

Unlike somatic motor pathways that use a single neuron from the spinal cord to skeletal muscle, autonomic pathways use a two-neuron chain to reach target organs.

  1. Preganglionic neuron: Its cell body starts in the brain or spinal cord. Its axon travels out to meet the second neuron.
  2. Postganglionic neuron: Its cell body sits in an autonomic ganglion outside the central nervous system. Its axon then extends all the way to the target effector organ, such as a smooth muscle, gland, or cardiac muscle.

Sympathetic Pathways and Ganglia

The sympathetic nervous system is also called the thoracolumbar division because its preganglionic neurons originate in the thoracic and lumbar segments of the spinal cord (specifically T1 to L2). Because these spinal segments are relatively short, sympathetic pathways feature unique structural arrangements:

  • Sympathetic trunk ganglia: A paired chain of ganglia running alongside the vertebral column, allowing preganglionic fibers to synapse close to the spinal cord.
  • Collateral ganglia: Unpaired ganglia located further out in the abdomen, such as the celiac and mesenteric ganglia, supplying abdominal organs.
  • Short preganglionic and long postganglionic fibers: Because the ganglia sit close to the spinal cord, the first fiber is short and the second fiber must travel a longer distance to reach the target.

Parasympathetic Pathways and Ganglia

The parasympathetic system is known as the craniosacral division because its preganglionic neurons emerge from the brainstem and the sacral region of the spinal cord (S2 to S4).

Parasympathetic fibers travel through specific cranial nerves—such as the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X), which supplies the heart, lungs, and most digestive organs. Because of this layout, parasympathetic pathways have very long preganglionic fibers that travel all the way to the target organ. They synapse in terminal ganglia located very close to, or directly inside, the walls of the target organs, resulting in very short postganglionic fibers.

Neurotransmitters and Receptors

Communication across the autonomic nervous system relies on specific chemical messengers and cell receptors. Understanding these chemicals helps nurses comprehend how medications like beta-blockers or atropine work in the body.

FeatureSympathetic DivisionParasympathetic Division
Preganglionic NeurotransmitterAcetylcholine (ACh)Acetylcholine (ACh)
Postganglionic NeurotransmitterNorepinephrine (mostly)Acetylcholine (ACh)
Target Organ ReceptorsAdrenergic (Alpha and Beta)Cholinergic (Muscarinic)

All preganglionic fibers release acetylcholine. However, most sympathetic postganglionic fibers release norepinephrine onto adrenergic receptors, while parasympathetic postganglionic fibers release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors.
