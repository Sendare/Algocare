The Spinal Cord

Gross Anatomy and External Structure

The spinal cord serves as the main information highway between the brain and the rest of the body. Running down from the brainstem inside the protective spinal canal, it typically ends around the first or second lumbar vertebra in adults at a tapered point called the conus medullaris. Below this point, a loose bundle of nerve roots continues down, known as the cauda equina because it resembles a horse's tail. Along its length, the cord features two noticeable enlargements: the cervical enlargement, which supplies nerves to the arms, and the lumbosacral enlargement, which supplies nerves to the legs.

Spinal Meninges and Protection

Delicate nervous tissue requires heavy physical defense. The spinal cord is wrapped in three protective membrane layers called the meninges. The outermost layer is the tough dura mater, followed by the web-like arachnoid mater. Between the arachnoid and the deepest layer, the vascular pia mater, lies the subarachnoid space filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), which cushions the cord against physical shock. Clinicians tap into this fluid space during a lumbar puncture to diagnose infections or bleeding.

Internal Grey and White Matter

A cross-section of the spinal cord reveals two distinct regions: grey matter and white matter. The grey matter sits in the center, shaped roughly like a butterfly or the letter H, and contains cell bodies, interneurons, and synapses. It is divided into horns: the dorsal horns process sensory information, the ventral horns control motor movement, and the lateral horns handle autonomic functions. Surrounding the grey matter is the white matter, made up of myelinated axons grouped into columns, or funiculi, that travel up and down the central nervous system.

Sensory and Motor Pathways

Information travels through the spinal cord in distinct nerve tracts. Ascending tracts carry sensory signals up toward the brain, passing through structures like the spinothalamic tract for pain and temperature, and the dorsal columns for touch and vibration. Conversely, descending tracts carry motor commands down from the brain. The corticospinal tract is the main pathway for conscious, precise movements of the limbs, traveling down from the motor cortex to synapse with lower motor neurons.

Spinal Nerves and Segmental Innervation

Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves emerge from the spinal cord, connecting it to the rest of the body. Each spinal nerve is formed by the union of a dorsal root (carrying sensory fibers with a dorsal root ganglion) and a ventral root (carrying motor fibers). These nerves exit through spaces between the vertebrae and are named according to their spinal region: cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral, and coccygeal. Each nerve supplies a specific area of skin called a dermatom, which helps clinicians pinpoint the exact level of a spinal injury.

Spinal Reflexes and Reflex Arcs

Not all nerve signals have to travel all the way to the brain to produce a reaction. A reflex is a rapid, predictable, and involuntary motor response to a stimulus. The pathway it follows is called a reflex arc, which typically includes five basic parts: a sensory receptor, a sensory neuron, an integration center in the spinal cord, a motor neuron, and an effector muscle or gland. The knee-jerk patellar reflex is a classic example of a monosynaptic stretch reflex, where the sensory neuron synapses directly onto a motor neuron without an interneuron.

Clinical Implications and Common Injuries

Damage to the spinal cord can cause profound disability, depending on the injury's location and severity. Injuries higher up in the cervical region can impair breathing and cause tetraplegia (paralysis of all four limbs), while thoracic or lumbar injuries typically result in paraplegia (paralysis of the lower body). A common clinical emergency is spinal shock, a temporary loss of all reflex activity and autonomic tone immediately following trauma. Nurses must monitor patients closely for respiratory distress, blood pressure instability, and bladder retention.
