The Spinal Cord

Gross Anatomy and Protective Structures

The spinal cord is a delicate, cylindrical structure extending from the brainstem down to the lumbar region. It resides securely within the vertebral canal, protected by the bony vertebrae of the spinal column. In adults, the cord typically terminates around the lower border of the first lumbar vertebra, a tapering region known as the **conus medullaris**. Below this point, a delicate strand of pia mater called the **filum terminale** anchors the cord to the coccyx. Because the vertebral column grows longer than the spinal cord during embryonic development, lower spinal nerves must travel inferiorly within the canal before exiting through their respective intervertebral foramina. This bundle of nerve roots resembles a horse's tail and is appropriately called the **cauda equina**. This anatomical layout is clinically vital in Nigerian practice, as lumbar punctures are safely performed below the second lumbar vertebra—usually between L3-L4 or L4-L5—to sample cerebrospinal fluid without risking direct trauma to the spinal cord itself.

Spinal Meninges and Spaces

Surrounding and safeguarding the spinal cord are three continuous connective tissue membranes known as the **spinal meninges**. From superficial to deep, these layers are: - **Dura mater**: The toughest and outermost layer, forming a durable, protective tube. - **Arachnoid mater**: The delicate, web-like middle layer that lines the inner surface of the dura. - **Pia mater**: The innermost, highly vascularized layer that clings tightly to the actual contours of the spinal cord and roots. Between these membranes lie clinically significant fluid-filled spaces. The **epidural space** sits between the bony vertebra and the dura mater, serving as the site for epidural anesthesia during labor. Deep to the arachnoid mater is the **subarachnoid space**, which is filled with circulating **cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)** that cushions the cord against mechanical shock.

Internal Organization: Grey and White Matter

A cross-section of the spinal cord reveals an inner core of grey matter shaped roughly like a butterfly or the letter 'H', surrounded by an outer layer of white matter. The **grey matter** consists largely of neuronal cell bodies, neuroglia, and unmyelinated axons. It is subdivided into horns: the **posterior (dorsal) horns** contain sensory processing neurons, the **anterior (ventral) horns** house motor neuron cell bodies that innervate skeletal muscles, and the **lateral horns**—present in thoracic and upper lumbar segments—contain autonomic motor neurons for the sympathetic nervous system. The **white matter** surrounds the grey core and is composed of myelinated nerve fibers organized into distinct columns or **funiculi** (posterior, lateral, and anterior). These columns act as major superhighways, containing ascending and descending tracts that relay signals between the peripheral body and the brain.

Ascending Sensory Pathways

Sensory information originating from receptors in skin, muscles, and joints travels upward to the brain via sensory pathways located in the white matter columns. These pathways typically involve a chain of three neurons: 1. **First-order neuron**: Detects the stimulus in the periphery and transmits the impulse from the receptor into the spinal cord via the dorsal root. 2. **Second-order neuron**: Resides in the spinal cord or brainstem, receives the signal, and its axon typically crosses over (decussates) to the opposite side before ascending to the thalamus. 3. **Third-order neuron**: Relays the impulse from the thalamus to the primary somatosensory cortex in the cerebrum for conscious perception. Major ascending tracts include the **spinothalamic tract**, which carries pain and temperature signals, and the **posterior column-medial lemniscal pathway**, which transmits fine touch, vibration, and conscious proprioception. Because many of these tracts cross the midline, damage to one side of the spinal cord results in sensory deficits on the opposite side of the body.

Descending Motor Pathways

To produce voluntary movement and maintain posture, the brain issues commands that travel downward through motor pathways in the spinal white matter. These pathways are generally divided into pyramidal (corticospinal) and extrapyramidal systems. The **corticospinal tracts** originate in the cerebral cortex, descend through the brainstem, and form distinct pyramids in the medulla. The majority of these fibers cross over in the lower medulla, forming the **lateral corticospinal tract**, which controls skilled movements of the limbs. A smaller portion remains uncrossed as the **anterior corticospinal tract**, controlling axial musculature. Other descending pathways, such as the vestibulospinal and reticulospinal tracts, originate in brainstem nuclei and regulate involuntary motor activities like muscle tone, balance, and reflexive postural adjustments. Disruption of these descending pathways interrupts brain control over skeletal muscles, leading to signs like paralysis or spasticity.

Spinal Nerves and Segmental Innervation

Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves emerge directly from the spinal cord, linking the central nervous system to receptors, muscles, and glands throughout the body. They are categorized by region: 8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral, and 1 coccygeal. Each spinal nerve is formed by the union of two roots just outside the spinal cord: - **Dorsal (posterior) root**: Contains sensory axons bringing information into the spinal cord, featuring an enlargement called the **dorsal root ganglion** where sensory cell bodies reside. - **Ventral (anterior) root**: Contains motor axons carrying impulses away from the spinal cord to effector organs. After emerging through the intervertebral foramen, each spinal nerve splits into branches called rami. A **dermatome** is a specific strip of skin supplied by a single spinal nerve, which is clinically helpful when assessing the level of a spinal cord lesion by mapping where a patient loses skin sensation.

Spinal Reflexes and Reflex Arcs

A spinal reflex is a rapid, predictable, and involuntary motor response to an environmental stimulus that bypasses conscious brain processing to ensure quick protection or postural stability. The neural wiring underlying this action is called a **reflex arc**, typically comprising five essential components: 1. **Receptor**: Detects the physical or chemical change (e.g., muscle spindle). 2. **Sensory neuron**: Conducts the nerve impulse along the dorsal root into the spinal cord. 3. **Integration center**: A region within the grey matter where the sensory neuron synapses directly (monosynaptic) or via interneurons (polysynaptic) with a motor neuron. 4. **Motor neuron**: Transmits the efferent impulse out through the ventral root to the effector. 5. **Effector**: The muscle or gland that executes the final response. The classic knee-jerk or patellar reflex is a familiar example of a monosynaptic stretch reflex, clinically tested with a tendon hammer to verify the integrity of specific spinal segments.

Clinical Implications and Common Spinal Injuries

Spinal cord injuries (SCIs) disrupt communication between the brain and the body, leading to profound physiological changes depending on the level and completeness of the lesion. Common etiologies include road traffic accidents, falls, and sports trauma—frequently encountered in emergency departments across Nigeria. When injury occurs in the cervical region, it can result in **quadriplegia** (impairment of all four limbs), whereas thoracic or lumbar injuries typically result in **paraplegia** (paralysis of the lower extremities). Immediately following acute trauma, the patient may experience **spinal shock**, a temporary loss of all reflex activity, autonomic tone, and sensation below the lesion level. Another critical emergency is **autonomic dysreflexia**, a potentially life-threatening hypertensive crisis triggered by noxious stimuli (such as a distended bladder or tight clothing) in individuals with spinal cord injuries at or above the T6 level. Recognizing these complications allows nurses to provide swift, life-saving interventions.
