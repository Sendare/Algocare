Nervous Tissue

Introduction to Nervous Tissue and Functions of the Nervous System

Nervous tissue forms the master control and communication network of the human body. By integrating rapid electrical signals and chemical messengers, it coordinates virtually every activity we perform, from conscious thought to involuntary heart rate regulation.

  • Sensory Input: Gathers information by detecting changes both inside and outside the body using specialized receptors.
  • Integration: Processes and interprets incoming sensory data in the brain and spinal cord to decide the appropriate response.
  • Motor Output: Activates effector organs, such as muscles and glands, to produce a physical or chemical action.

Nursing assessments frequently rely on checking these functions, such as testing pupil response or checking limb movement, to see if the nervous system is working properly.

Cellular Composition: Neurons and Neuroglial Cells

Nervous tissue is built from two principal cell types: neurons and neuroglia.

Neurons are the structural units of the nervous system. These excitable cells specialize in transmitting electrical signals across long distances. They possess high metabolic rates, requiring a continuous, uninterrupted supply of oxygen and glucose.

Neuroglia, often simply called glial cells, vastly outnumber neurons. Far from being mere packing material, neuroglia support, insulate, and protect neurons. Unlike neurons, most glial cells retain the ability to divide throughout life, which explains why most brain tumors originate from glial cells rather than neurons.

Anatomy and Classification of Neurons

A typical neuron consists of a cell body, branching dendrites, and a single axon.

  • Cell Body (Soma): Contains the nucleus and major organelles, acting as the metabolic center of the cell.
  • Dendrites: Short, tapering extensions that act as the main receiving regions, pulling signals toward the cell body.
  • Axon: A single long fiber that generates and conducts nerve impulses away from the cell body toward other cells.
  • Axon Terminals: The branched endings of an axon that release neurotransmitters into a synapse.

Structurally, neurons are classified by the number of processes extending from their cell body. Multipolar neurons have many processes and make up most brain and spinal cord cells. Bipolar neurons have two processes and are found in special sensory organs like the retina. Unipolar neurons have a single short process that splits into central and peripheral branches, common in sensory ganglia.

Types and Functions of Neuroglia in the CNS and PNS

Neuroglia provide a specialized environment for nervous tissue, split between the Central Nervous System and Peripheral Nervous System.

In the Central Nervous System:

  • Astrocytes: Star-shaped cells that anchor neurons to capillaries, control the chemical environment, and form the blood-brain barrier.
  • Microglia: Small defensive cells that monitor health and phagocytize microorganisms or neuronal debris.
  • Ependymocytes (Ependymal cells): Line fluid-filled cavities and use cilia to circulate cerebrospinal fluid.
  • Oligodendrocytes: Wrap processes around CNS nerve fibers, forming insulating myelin sheaths.

In the Peripheral Nervous System:

  • Schwann cells: Surround peripheral nerve fibers and form myelin sheaths.
  • Satellite cells: Surround neuron cell bodies in PNS ganglia, regulating their chemical environment much like astrocytes do centrally.

Myelination and Nerve Fiber Structure

Myelin is a whitish, protein-lipid wrap that covers many long nerve fibers. This fatty layer acts as an electrical insulator, significantly increasing the speed at which nerve impulses travel.

In the PNS, a single Schwann cell wraps around a segment of an axon multiple times. The inner layers form the myelin, while the outermost cytoplasm and nucleus squeeze to the outside as the neurolemma. In contrast, oligodendrocytes myelinate multiple axons in the CNS and lack a distinct outer neurolemma.

Myelin sheaths are not continuous. Gaps left between adjacent Schwann cells or oligodendrocyte segments are called nodes of Ranvier. Action potentials jump from node to node in a rapid process called saltatory conduction, which makes signaling much faster than continuous impulse travel along unmyelinated fibers.

Electrophysiology: Resting Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

Neurons communicate using changes in their membrane potential. In a resting neuron, the inside of the cell membrane is negatively charged compared to the outside. This resting membrane potential sits around -70 millivolts, maintained primarily by the sodium-potassium pump, which moves three sodium ions out for every two potassium ions brought in.

When a stimulus triggers a neuron, voltage-gated ion channels open:

  1. Depolarization: Sodium channels open, allowing positive sodium ions to rush inside, making the inner cell membrane positive.
  2. Repolarization: Potassium channels open and sodium channels close, letting potassium rush out to restore the negative internal charge.
  3. Hyperpolarization: Potassium channels remain open briefly, dipping the membrane potential even lower than resting level before stabilizing.

This rapid electrical wave is an action potential.

Synaptic Transmission and Neurotransmitters

The junction where a neuron communicates with another cell is called a synapse. Most communication relies on chemical synapses, where an electrical impulse is converted into a chemical message.

When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open. Calcium influx causes synaptic vesicles to fuse with the membrane, releasing neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. These chemical messengers diffuse across the tiny gap and bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic cell membrane, triggering a new electrical change.

Common neurotransmitters include acetylcholine, which activates skeletal muscle, and norepinephrine, which influences mood and autonomic functions. Once the message passes, enzymes quickly break down or transport the remaining neurotransmitter away to prevent continuous stimulation.

Regeneration and Repair of Nervous Tissue

Damage to nervous tissue presents significant recovery challenges depending on the location of the injury within the nervous system.

In the Central Nervous System, repair is extremely limited. Oligodendrocytes do not form guiding regeneration tubes, and astrocytes produce scar tissue containing growth-inhibiting molecules that block axon regrowth. As a result, spinal cord injuries and brain strokes often result in permanent deficits.

In the Peripheral Nervous System, however, axons can regenerate if the cell body remains intact and damage is mild:

  1. Wallerian degeneration: The severed axon distal to the injury site breaks down along with its myelin sheath, and phagocytes clean up the debris.
  2. Regeneration tube formation: Schwann cells multiply and form a cord that guides the sprouting axon back toward its original target.
  3. Reinnervation: The axon grows back into position, and function gradually returns as myelin is restored.
