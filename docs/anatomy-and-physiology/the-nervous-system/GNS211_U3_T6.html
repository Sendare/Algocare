Nervous Tissue

Introduction to Nervous Tissue and Functions of the Nervous System

Nervous tissue forms the intricate communication network of the human body, responsible for coordinating virtually all bodily activities. It consists primarily of two cell types: excitable neurons and supportive neuroglia. Functionally, the nervous system operates through three overlapping phases: sensory input, integration, and motor output. Sensory receptors monitor internal and external changes, generating signals that travel via afferent pathways to the central nervous system. Integration involves processing these signals, analyzing them, and making decisions about appropriate responses. Finally, motor output activates effector organs—muscles and glands—through efferent pathways to execute the response. Clinically, understanding these pathways helps nurses localize neurological deficits. For instance, an inability to withdraw a limb from a painful stimulus points to a disruption either in the sensory afferent pathway, central integration, or motor efferent pathway.

Anatomy and Structure of Neurons

Neurons share a common structural plan designed for rapid communication. The **soma**, or cell body, houses the nucleus, prominent nucleoli, and abundant **Nissl bodies** (rough endoplasmic reticulum), reflecting high metabolic activity and protein synthesis. Branching off the soma are **dendrites**, tapering extensions that act as the primary receiving regions, conveying graded potentials toward the cell body. The **axon hillock** marks the cone-shaped region where the soma transitions into the axon. This critical zone acts as the trigger zone, where summation of graded potentials initiates an **action potential** if the threshold is reached. Axons vary greatly in length, terminating in numerous **axon terminals** (telodendria) that swell into **synaptic knobs**. These knobs contain **synaptic vesicles** filled with chemical messengers required to bridge the gap to the next target cell.

Functional and Structural Classification of Neurons

Neurons are classified structurally by the number of processes extending from the soma, and functionally by the direction in which nerve impulses travel relative to the central nervous system. - **Multipolar neurons**: Possess one axon and multiple dendrites; the most common structural type, including motor neurons and interneurons. - **Bipolar neurons**: Have one main dendrite and one axon extending from opposite sides of the soma; restricted to special sense organs like the retina and olfactory mucosa. - **Unipolar (pseudounipolar) neurons**: Feature a single short process that emerges from the soma and divides into proximal and distal branches; typical of sensory root ganglia. - **Sensory (afferent) neurons**: Transmit impulses from sensory receptors toward the central nervous system. - **Motor (efferent) neurons**: Carry instructions away from the central nervous system to effector organs. - **Interneurons (association neurons)**: Lie entirely within the central nervous system, shuttling signals through integration pathways.

Neuroglial Cells of the Central and Peripheral Nervous Systems

Neuroglia, or glial cells, outnumber neurons significantly and provide vital physical and metabolic support. Their specific roles vary by location. | Glial Cell Type | Location | Primary Function | | --- | --- | --- | | Astrocytes | CNS | Support neurons, anchor them to blood vessels, and form the blood-brain barrier | | Microglia | CNS | Act as phagocytes to clear away neuronal debris and pathogens | | Ependymal cells | CNS | Line fluid-filled ventricles and circulate cerebrospinal fluid with cilia | | Oligodendrocytes | CNS | Form protective myelin sheaths around multiple central axons | | Schwann cells | PNS | Form myelin sheaths around a single segment of a peripheral axon | | Satellite cells | PNS | Surround and cushion cell bodies in peripheral ganglia | In clinical practice, dysfunction of astrocytes contributes to scar formation after central nervous system injury, which can impede nerve regeneration.

Myelination and Nerve Fiber Conduction

Myelin is a whitish, protein-lipid substance that wraps around axons to insulate them and dramatically increase the speed of impulse propagation. In the central nervous system, oligodendrocytes send out cytoplasmic extensions that coil around multiple axons. Conversely, a single Schwann cell wraps around just a small segment of a single peripheral axon, winding repeatedly to form concentric layers of plasma membrane. Regular gaps exist between adjacent myelin segments along the axon, known as **nodes of Ranvier**. Voltage-gated sodium channels concentrate heavily at these nodes. Instead of continuous propagation along the entire membrane, the action potential leaps from one node to the next in a process called **saltatory conduction**. This jumping mechanism is markedly faster and consumes less metabolic energy than unmyelinated fiber conduction.

Membrane Potentials: Resting, Graded, and Action Potentials

Neuronal communication relies on changes in the electrical charge across the plasma membrane. At rest, a neuron maintains a **resting membrane potential** of approximately -70 millivolts, kept stable by sodium-potassium pumps and potassium leakage channels. The interior is relatively negative compared to the exterior. When a stimulus triggers sensory receptors or neurotransmitters bind to dendrites, localized changes called **graded potentials** occur. If these localized voltage changes summate at the axon hillock and reach a critical threshold (typically around -55 mV), an **action potential** fires. An action potential involves rapid **depolarization** as voltage-gated sodium channels flood the cell with positive ions, followed by **repolarization** as potassium channels open to let potassium rush out. A brief period of hyperpolarization follows before the resting state is restored.

Synaptic Transmission and Neurotransmitters

Neurons communicate with other cells across specialized junctions called **synapses**. At an electrical synapse, ions flow directly between cells through gap junctions for rapid synchronization. Chemical synapses, which are far more common, rely on chemical messengers. When an action potential reaches the axon terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open, triggering exocytosis of **synaptic vesicles**. Neurotransmitters diffuse across the narrow **synaptic cleft** and bind to specific receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, producing a graded potential. Common neurotransmitters include **acetylcholine**, **norepinephrine**, **dopamine**, and **serotonin**. To prevent continuous stimulation, enzymes degrade leftover neurotransmitters in the cleft, or transporter proteins clear them away via reuptake.

Injury and Repair of Nervous Tissue

The regenerative capacity of nervous tissue depends heavily on whether the injury occurs in the central or peripheral nervous system. Neurons in the central nervous system generally cannot regenerate effectively. Astrocytes proliferate at injury sites to form glial scars, and oligodendrocytes express growth-inhibiting proteins, preventing axonal regrowth. In contrast, peripheral nerve fibers possess a remarkable ability to repair themselves if the cell body remains intact and enough neurolemma survives. Through **Wallerian degeneration**, the axon distal to the injury site degenerates, and macrophages clear the debris. Schwann cells form a regeneration tube that guides the sprouting axon back toward its original effector organ, slowly restoring function over weeks or months.
