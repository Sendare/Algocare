Nervous Tissue

Introduction to Nervous Tissue and Functions of the Nervous System

Nervous tissue is a specialized tissue that forms the intricate communication network of the human body. As nursing students preparing for clinical practice and exams in Nigeria, understanding nervous tissue is fundamental because it controls every physiological system, from simple reflex actions to complex cognitive processes. The primary functions of the nervous system are traditionally categorized into three overlapping phases: sensory input, integration, and motor output. Sensory receptors located throughout the body monitor internal and external changes, known as stimuli, and transmit this vital information as electrical signals to the central nervous system, which consists of the brain and spinal cord. Once these signals arrive, integration occurs, where the nervous system processes and interprets the sensory input to make decisions regarding an appropriate response. Following integration, motor output is initiated, sending signals via efferent pathways to effector organs—namely muscles and glands—to elicit a coordinated response, such as muscle contraction or glandular secretion. Furthermore, the nervous system regulates homeostasis by constantly monitoring vital parameters like blood pressure, body temperature, and blood gas concentrations, adjusting bodily functions dynamically to maintain a stable internal environment.

Cellular Composition: Neurons and Neuroglial Cells

Nervous tissue is composed of two principal types of cells: neurons and neuroglial cells, also known as glial cells. Neurons are the structural and functional units of the nervous system, highly specialized to generate and transmit electrical impulses known as action potentials across long distances within the body. Despite their critical signaling roles, neurons are remarkably delicate and require extensive physiological support to survive and function optimally. This is where neuroglial cells come into play. Outnumbering neurons significantly in the central nervous system, neuroglial cells do not transmit nerve impulses themselves; instead, they provide a vital support system, physically packing, nourishing, insulating, and protecting neurons. Understanding the cellular composition of nervous tissue helps nursing students appreciate how pathology can affect neural function, as diseases like multiple sclerosis or brain tumors often originate from or affect neuroglia rather than neurons directly. Together, neurons and neuroglia form a tightly integrated network that maintains the delicate extracellular environment necessary for rapid, efficient neural communication.

Anatomy and Classification of Neurons

Neurons share a common structural plan consisting of a cell body, dendrites, and an axon, though their shapes and sizes vary widely based on location and function. The cell body, or soma, contains the nucleus and major organelles, serving as the metabolic center of the cell. Extending from the cell body are branching processes called dendrites, which act as the primary receiving regions, accepting incoming signals from other neurons and transmitting them toward the cell body. The axon is a single, often long projection that arises from a specialized region called the axon hillock and conducts nerve impulses away from the cell body toward other neurons, muscles, or glands. Axons may branch into axon terminals at their distal ends, where neurotransmitters are stored and released. Structurally, neurons are classified into unipolar, bipolar, and multipolar types based on the number of processes extending from the cell body. Multipolar neurons have one axon and multiple dendrites and are the most common type in the human CNS. Bipolar neurons possess one axon and one dendrite and are found in specialized sensory organs like the retina. Unipolar neurons have a single short process that divides into central and peripheral branches, typical of most sensory neurons. Functionally, neurons are classified as sensory, motor, or interneurons depending on the direction in which they transmit impulses relative to the central nervous system.

Types and Functions of Neuroglia in the CNS and PNS

Neuroglia, or glial cells, are essential partners to neurons, and their types and functions differ between the Central Nervous System (CNS) and the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS). In the CNS, there are four primary types of neuroglia: astrocytes, microglia, ependymal cells, and oligodendrocytes. Astrocytes are star-shaped cells that form the blood-brain barrier, regulate the chemical environment, and provide structural support. Microglia are small, phagocytic cells that act as the immune defense of the CNS, clearing away dead cells and pathogens. Ependymal cells line the fluid-filled ventricles of the brain and central canal of the spinal cord, producing and circulating cerebrospinal fluid using their cilia. Oligodendrocytes wrap around CNS nerve fibers, forming the myelin sheath that speeds up electrical conduction. In contrast, the PNS contains two distinct types of neuroglia: Schwann cells and satellite cells. Schwann cells perform a function analogous to oligodendrocytes by wrapping around peripheral nerve fibers to form myelin sheaths, though a single Schwann cell myelinates only a single segment of one axon. Satellite cells surround the neuron cell bodies in peripheral ganglia, regulating the microenvironment and providing physical support similar to astrocytes in the CNS.

Myelination and Nerve Fiber Structure

Myelination is a vital process that enhances the speed and efficiency of nerve impulse propagation along axons. The myelin sheath is a whitish, protein-lipid rich covering that wraps tightly around nerve fibers. In the CNS, myelination is accomplished by oligodendrocytes, while in the PNS, it is performed by Schwann cells. Because these wrappings leave small gaps between adjacent glial cells along the axon—known as nodes of Ranvier—action potentials do not travel continuously down the entire length of the fiber. Instead, the electrical signal jumps rapidly from one node of Ranvier to the next in a process called saltatory conduction. This saltatory propagation is significantly faster and consumes less metabolic energy than continuous conduction found in unmyelinated fibers. Nerve fibers can therefore be classified as either myelinated or unmyelinated. Clinical conditions such as multiple sclerosis involve the autoimmune destruction of myelin in the CNS, leading to slowed or blocked nerve conduction, which manifests clinically as muscle weakness, visual disturbances, and coordination difficulties that student nurses must recognize in affected patients.

Electrophysiology: Resting Membrane Potentials and Action Potentials

Electrophysiology is the study of the electrical properties of nervous tissue, centering around the resting membrane potential and the action potential. In a resting, non-signaling neuron, the inside of the cell membrane is negatively charged compared to the outside, maintaining a typical resting membrane potential of about -70 millivolts (mV). This polarization is established and maintained primarily by the sodium-potassium pump, which actively transports three sodium ions out of the cell for every two potassium ions pumped in, alongside leak channels that allow potassium to diffuse down its concentration gradient. When a neuron is adequately stimulated, voltage-gated ion channels open, leading to an action potential. An action potential is a rapid, temporary reversal of the membrane potential, consisting of depolarization (influx of sodium ions making the inside positive), repolarization (outflow of potassium ions restoring negativity), and a brief hyperpolarization phase before returning to the resting state. This electrical impulse is propagated along the axon without degradation, serving as the fundamental language of the nervous system.

Synaptic Transmission and Neurotransmitters

Synaptic transmission is the process by which signaling occurs between neurons or between a neuron and an effector cell across a junction known as a synapse. Most synapses in the human body are chemical synapses, which involve a presynaptic neuron, a synaptic cleft (the microscopic gap between cells), and a postsynaptic neuron. When an action potential reaches the axon terminal of the presynaptic neuron, it triggers the influx of calcium ions, causing synaptic vesicles to fuse with the membrane and release chemical messengers called neurotransmitters into the synaptic cleft. These neurotransmitters diffuse across the cleft and bind to specific receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, generating either an excitatory or inhibitory graded potential in the postsynaptic cell. Common neurotransmitters include acetylcholine, norepinephrine, dopamine, serotonin, and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA). Once the neurotransmitter has exerted its effect, it must be rapidly removed from the synaptic cleft through enzymatic degradation, reuptake by the presynaptic cell, or diffusion, ensuring that the signal is brief and controlled.

Regeneration and Repair of Nervous Tissue

The capacity for nervous tissue to regenerate and repair depends heavily on whether the damage occurs in the central nervous system (CNS) or the peripheral nervous system (PNS). In the PNS, damaged nerve fibers have a limited ability to regenerate, provided that the cell body remains intact and enough of the neurilemma (the outermost layer of the Schwann cell) is preserved. When a peripheral axon is severed, the distal portion undergoes Wallerian degeneration, where the axon and its myelin sheath break down and are cleared away by macrophages. Meanwhile, surviving Schwann cells form a regeneration tube that guides the sprouting proximal axon back toward its original effector organ, allowing functional recovery over time. In stark contrast, nervous tissue in the CNS has virtually no functional regeneration. This is primarily due to inhibitory proteins produced by oligodendrocytes (such as myelin-associated inhibitors) and the rapid formation of glial scars by astrocytes, which physically and chemically block axonal sprouting. Consequently, spinal cord injuries or strokes in the CNS often result in permanent neurological deficits, making prevention and supportive nursing care critical components of patient management.
