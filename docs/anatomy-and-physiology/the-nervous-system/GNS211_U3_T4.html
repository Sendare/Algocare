Peripheral Nervous System

Introduction and Structural Organization

The peripheral nervous system serves as the communication network linking the central nervous system to the rest of the body. Structurally, it consists of all neural tissue outside the brain and spinal cord, including cranial nerves, spinal nerves, ganglia, and sensory receptors. Individual peripheral nerve fibers are surrounded by a delicate layer of connective tissue called the endoneurium. Bundles of these fibers, known as fascicles, are wrapped in the perineurium, while the entire nerve is enclosed by a tough outer sheath called the epineurium. This layered architecture provides structural strength and metabolic support to delicate axons traveling through dynamic body regions.

Cranial Nerves Overview

There are twelve pairs of cranial nerves that emerge directly from the brain, primarily serving the head, neck, and facial structures, with the exception of the vagus nerve which extends into the thoracic and abdominal cavities. These nerves can be strictly sensory, strictly motor, or mixed.

  • Olfactory Nerve (CN I): Mediates the sense of smell.
  • Optic Nerve (CN II): Transmits visual information from the retina.
  • Oculomotor Nerve (CN III): Controls most extraocular eye movements and pupillary constriction.
  • Trigeminal Nerve (CN V): Provides general sensation to the face and motor innervation for mastication.
  • Facial Nerve (CN VII): Controls muscles of facial expression and conveys taste from the anterior tongue.
  • Vagus Nerve (CN X): Regulates autonomic functions of visceral organs in the chest and abdomen.

Spinal Nerves and Nerve Plexuses

Thirty-one pairs of spinal nerves emerge from the spinal cord, categorized into cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral, and coccygeal regions. Each spinal nerve is formed by the union of a dorsal sensory root and a ventral motor root. Rather than traveling directly to their destinations, the ventral rami of most spinal nerves merge to form complex networks called nerve plexuses.

Sensory Receptors and Transduction

Sensory receptors detect internal and external environmental changes, translating physical stimuli into electrical signals through a process called transduction. They are classified based on the stimulus type they detect:

  • Mechanoreceptors: Respond to mechanical pressure, touch, vibration, and stretch.
  • Thermoreceptors: Detect changes in temperature.
  • Photoreceptors: Respond to light energy in the retina.
  • Chemoreceptors: Detect chemical substances in solution, such as blood gases or taste molecules.
  • Nociceptors: Specialized free nerve endings that respond to tissue-damaging stimuli, signaling pain.

Once a receptor's threshold is reached, an action potential travels along sensory afferent fibers toward the central nervous system for interpretation.

Somatic and Autonomic Divisions

The motor output of the peripheral nervous system is divided into somatic and autonomic branches. The somatic nervous system innervates skeletal muscles, allowing conscious control of movement. It relies on a single motor neuron pathway extending all the way from the central nervous system to the effector muscle, utilizing the neurotransmitter acetylcholine to stimulate contraction.

Conversely, the autonomic nervous system regulates visceral functions, smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands without conscious effort. It operates via a two-neuron chain consisting of a preganglionic neuron and a postganglionic neuron. The autonomic system splits into the sympathetic division, which mobilizes the body during stress ('fight or flight'), and the parasympathetic division, which promotes housekeeping functions during rest ('rest and digest').

Reflex Arcs

A reflex is a rapid, involuntary, and predictable motor response to a specific stimulus, designed to protect the body from harm or maintain posture. The pathway traveled by nerve impulses during a reflex is called a reflex arc. It consists of five essential components: a sensory receptor, a sensory afferent neuron, an integration center within the central nervous system, a motor efferent neuron, and an effector organ.

Reflexes can be autonomic, controlling visceral organs, or somatic, controlling skeletal muscle. The classic stretch reflex, such as the knee-jerk reaction, is a monosynaptic somatic reflex where the sensory neuron synapses directly onto the motor neuron without interneurons, ensuring minimal delay in response time.

Clinical Pathology and Neuropathies

Damage to peripheral nerves disrupts sensory, motor, and autonomic function, a condition broadly termed peripheral neuropathy. Common causes include metabolic disorders like diabetes mellitus, nutritional deficiencies, trauma, and certain infections. Symptoms often begin distally in a 'stocking-glove' distribution, manifesting as numbness, tingling, burning pain, or muscle weakness.

In clinical assessment, evaluating deep tendon reflexes and sensory modalities helps localize the lesion. A common diagnostic pitfall is attributing diabetic foot ulcers solely to vascular insufficiency without recognizing underlying sensory neuropathy, which robs patients of protective pain feedback and leads to unnoticed trauma.
