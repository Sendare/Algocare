Autonomic Nervous System

Introduction to the Autonomic Nervous System

The Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) is a critical branch of the peripheral nervous system responsible for regulating involuntary physiological processes. It maintains internal balance, known as homeostasis, without conscious effort from the individual. While somatic pathways control skeletal muscles and conscious movements, the autonomic pathways coordinate visceral functions such as heart rate, digestion, respiratory rate, pupillary response, and urination. Operating primarily below the level of conscious awareness, the ANS continuously adjusts organ activity to match internal needs and external environmental stressors.

Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Divisions

The autonomic nervous system splits into two primary divisions that generally exert opposing actions on target organs:

  • Sympathetic Division: Often termed the fight-or-flight system, it mobilizes the body during stress, exercise, or emergency situations. Anatomically, its preganglionic neurons originate in the thoracolumbar region of the spinal cord (segments T1 to L2).
  • Parasympathetic Division: Known as the rest-and-digest system, it conserves energy and promotes housekeeping functions like digestion and nutrient storage. Its preganglionic neurons emerge from the craniosacral region (brainstem and sacral segments S2 to S4).

Most visceral organs receive dual innervation from both divisions, allowing for precise, graded control of organ function.

Neurotransmitters and Receptors

Communication across autonomic pathways relies on specific chemical messengers and membrane receptors. All preganglionic fibers—both sympathetic and parasympathetic—release acetylcholine (ACh), which binds to nicotinic receptors on postganglionic neurons.

At the target organ level, the neurotransmitters differ:

  • Parasympathetic postganglionic fibers release acetylcholine, acting on muscarinic receptors.
  • Most sympathetic postganglionic fibers release norepinephrine, acting on adrenergic receptors (alpha and beta types).

A notable exception is sympathetic fibers supplying sweat glands, which release acetylcholine onto muscarinic receptors.

Physiological Effects on Target Organs

The interplay between sympathetic and parasympathetic inputs maintains dynamic control over organ systems.

HeartIncreases heart rate and contractilityDecreases heart rate
PupilsDilation (mydriasis)Constriction (miosis)
BronchiDilation of airwaysConstriction of airways
DigestionInhibits motility and secretionsStimulates motility and secretions
Blood VesselsConstriction in skin and visceraLittle to no effect in most vessels

In clinical practice, observing these responses helps nurses assess autonomic stability, shock states, or drug toxicities.

Enteric Nervous System and Central Regulation

The Enteric Nervous System (ENS) functions as an independent meshwork of neurons embedded within the walls of the gastrointestinal tract. Often called the second brain, it contains millions of neurons that regulate gut motility, secretion, and blood flow locally without central nervous system input. However, the ANS modulates ENS activity; parasympathetic input enhances digestive activity, whereas sympathetic input shuts it down.

Centrally, autonomic output is integrated and commanded by higher brain centers. The hypothalamus serves as the master controller, coordinating visceral responses with endocrine functions, temperature regulation, and emotional states. The brainstem nuclei manage crucial autonomic reflexes like coughing, swallowing, and cardiovascular regulation.

Clinical Implications and Autonomic Disorders

Dysfunction within the autonomic nervous system leads to diverse clinical presentations. Autonomic neuropathy, frequently seen in long-standing diabetes mellitus, can cause resting tachycardia, orthostatic hypotension, and gastroparesis.

Another significant condition is autonomic dysreflexia, an acute medical emergency occurring in individuals with spinal cord injuries above T6. A noxious stimulus below the injury level—such as a distended bladder—triggers an uncoordinated, massive sympathetic reflex surge causing severe hypertension, throbbing headache, and flushing above the lesion site. Nurses managing spinal cord injury patients must identify and remove these triggers immediately.
