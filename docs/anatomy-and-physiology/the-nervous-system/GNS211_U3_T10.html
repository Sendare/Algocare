Motor Areas of the Brain

Introduction to Functional Areas of the Cerebral Cortex

The cerebral cortex is the outer layer of neural tissue of the human brain, playing a critical role in memory, attention, perceptual awareness, thought, language, and consciousness. To manage these complex tasks, the cortex is divided into specialized functional zones. Sensory areas receive and process information from the environment, association areas deal with complex thoughts, memory, and reasoning, and motor areas control all voluntary muscle movements throughout the body. Understanding how these regions work together helps nurses recognize specific neurological deficits when a patient suffers a stroke or head injury.

Primary Motor Cortex (Brodmann Area 4)

Located in the precentral gyrus of the frontal lobe, the primary motor cortex is the main region responsible for generating the neural impulses that control execution of movement. It corresponds to Brodmann Area 4. Neurons here, particularly the large Betz cells, send signals down the spinal cord to target skeletal muscles. Electrical stimulation of this area causes specific, discrete muscle contractions on the opposite side of the body due to contralateral decussation.

Premotor Cortex and Motor Homunculus Organization

Just anterior to the primary motor cortex lies the premotor cortex, which helps plan complex movements and coordinates posture. The body is mapped onto the motor cortex in a distorted spatial representation known as the motor homunculus. Parts of the body requiring extremely fine, precise motor control—such as the fingers, lips, and tongue—occupy a disproportionately large area of the cortex, while gross movers like the trunk and thighs take up much less space.

Supplementary Motor Area (SMA) and Movement Planning

The supplementary motor area sits medial and anterior to the primary motor cortex. It plays a vital role in the planning and coordination of complex, internally generated movement sequences, such as playing a musical instrument or typing a familiar password. It works closely with the basal ganglia to ensure smooth transitions between individual actions within a broader movement pattern before the primary motor cortex executes them.

Broca's Area and Motor Speech Control

  • Location: Found in the inferior frontal gyrus, typically in the left hemisphere.
  • Function: Coordinates the physical muscles of the tongue, lips, larynx, and jaw required for producing clear, articulate speech.
  • Clinical connection: Damage to this region leads to expressive aphasia, where a patient knows what they want to say but cannot physically form the words properly.

Descending Motor Pathways: Pyramidal and Extrapyramidal Systems

Motor commands travel from the brain to skeletal muscles through major descending tracts.

  1. Pyramidal tracts: These include the corticospinal tracts, which run directly from the cortex down the spinal cord. They are responsible for fine, skilled voluntary movements, especially in the hands and fingers.
  2. Extrapyramidal tracts: These originate in subcortical brain nuclei like the basal ganglia and brainstem. They modulate automatic posture, muscle tone, and gross supportive movements.

Clinical Implications of Motor Area Lesions

Damage to the motor areas of the brain—whether from stroke, tumor, or trauma—produces distinct clinical presentations depending on the exact site affected. Injury to the primary motor cortex results in contralateral hemiplegia (paralysis) or hemiparesis (weakness). Spasticity and hyperreflexia often develop over time as upper motor neurons lose their inhibitory control over lower motor units. Nurses must perform thorough neurological assessments to monitor these motor changes and prevent secondary complications like contractures and pressure injuries.
