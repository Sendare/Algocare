Organization of the Nervous System

Introduction to the Nervous System and Its General Functions

The nervous system is the master control and communication center of the body. Every thought, action, and emotion reflects its activity. Working together with the endocrine system, it maintains homeostasis by monitoring internal and external changes.

Its general functions are categorized into three main steps:

  1. Sensory Input: Millions of sensory receptors monitor changes happening both inside and outside the body, known as stimuli.
  2. Integration: The brain and spinal cord process and interpret this sensory information to decide what action should be taken.
  3. Motor Output: A response is sent to effector organs—muscles or glands—to cause a physical action or hormone secretion.

Overview of Structural Organization: Central versus Peripheral Nervous Systems

Structurally, the nervous system is divided into two primary parts: the Central Nervous System (CNS) and the Peripheral Nervous System (PNS).

The CNS consists of the brain and the spinal cord. It acts as the integrating and control center of the nervous system. It interprets incoming sensory information and dictates outgoing motor responses based on past experience, reflexes, and current conditions.

The PNS is the part of the nervous system outside the CNS. It mainly consists of nerves that extend from the brain and spinal cord. Spinal nerves carry impulses to and from the spinal cord, while cranial nerves carry impulses to and from the brain. These communication lines link all parts of the body to the CNS.

Anatomy and Functional Divisions of the Central Nervous System

The central nervous system is enclosed and protected by bone and membranes. The brain sits inside the cranial vault, and the spinal cord runs through the vertebral canal. Both are cushioned by cerebrospinal fluid and covered by protective membranes called meninges.

The brain contains regions specialized for different tasks, including the cerebrum for higher thought and voluntary movement, the cerebellum for balance and coordination, and the brainstem for vital autonomic functions like breathing and heart rate. The spinal cord serves as a major highway for signal transmission between the brain and the body, as well as a center for quick reflex arcs that bypass conscious thought.

Anatomy and Functional Divisions of the Peripheral Nervous System

The peripheral nervous system acts as the delivery network for the nervous system. It is functionally divided into two main directions of traffic:

  • Sensory (Afferent) Division: Consists of nerve fibers that convey impulses to the central nervous system from sensory receptors located throughout the body.
  • Motor (Efferent) Division: Transmits impulses from the CNS out to effector organs, which are muscles and glands, bringing about a motor response.

Somatic versus Autonomic Nervous Systems

The motor division of the peripheral nervous system further splits based on whether we consciously control the target organs.

The somatic nervous system allows us to consciously control our skeletal muscles. It is often referred to as the voluntary nervous system.

The autonomic nervous system (ANS) regulates events that are automatic or involuntary, such as smooth muscle activity, cardiac muscle activity, and glandular secretion. This is our involuntary nervous system, keeping our heart beating and digestion moving without conscious thought.

Sympathetic and Parasympathetic Divisions of the Autonomic Nervous System

The autonomic nervous system has two parts that usually work in opposition to each other to maintain balance:

  • Sympathetic Division: Mobilizes the body during extreme situations such as exercise, excitement, or emergency. This is the "fight-or-flight" response, which increases heart rate, dilates airways, and slows digestion.
  • Parasympathetic Division: Allows us to "rest and digest." It conserves energy and promotes normal housekeeping functions during calm states, lowering heart rate and promoting digestive processes.

Microscopic Organization: Neurons and Neuroglial Cells

At the microscopic level, nervous tissue is made up of two principal types of cells: neurons and neuroglia.

Neurons, or nerve cells, are the structural units of the nervous system. They are specialized to transmit messages in the form of nerve impulses. A typical neuron has a cell body and slender processes that receive and send signals.

Neuroglial cells (or glia) are smaller cells that surround, support, insulate, and protect delicate neurons. Unlike neurons, glial cells do not transmit nerve impulses, and they retain the ability to divide throughout life, which is why most brain tumors originate from glial cells.

Clinical Implications for Nursing Practice

Understanding the organization of the nervous system helps nurses assess and monitor patients with neurological injuries or disorders. Damage to the central nervous system often results in widespread deficits because the CNS controls integration and higher function. Conversely, peripheral nerve injuries tend to affect specific localized motor or sensory pathways.

When conducting a neurological assessment, nurses evaluate cranial nerve function, reflexes, sensory perception, and motor strength. Recognizing whether a problem stems from the somatic system, autonomic system, or specific brain regions guides accurate documentation and timely reporting to the healthcare team.
