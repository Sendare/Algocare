Parasympathetic Nervous System

Introduction to the Parasympathetic Nervous System

The parasympathetic nervous system is a vital division of the autonomic nervous system that operates largely outside of conscious control. Often called the rest-and-digest system, it conserves energy as it slows the heart rate, increases intestinal and gland activity, and relaxes sphincter muscles in the gastrointestinal tract. While the sympathetic system prepares the body for sudden stress or danger, the parasympathetic system maintains baseline housekeeping functions during calm states, ensuring that internal organs work efficiently without burning excess energy.

Anatomical Organization and Cranio-Sacral Outflow

Parasympathetic nerves originate in the central nervous system in a specific pattern known as cranio-sacral outflow. This means the nerve fibers leave the body via the brainstem and the sacral region of the spinal cord.

  • Cranial outflow: Fibers travel through specific cranial nerves, including the oculomotor (CN III), facial (CN VII), glossopharyngeal (CN IX), and vagus (CN X) nerves. The vagus nerve alone accounts for about seventy-five percent of all parasympathetic outflow, supplying visceral organs in the chest and abdomen.
  • Sacral outflow: Fibers originating from the second through fourth sacral segments (S2-S4) of the spinal cord supply the lower bowels, urinary bladder, and reproductive organs.

Parasympathetic Ganglia and Neurotransmitter Pathways

Unlike the sympathetic system, which usually has short preganglionic fibers and long postganglionic fibers, the parasympathetic pathway features long preganglionic fibers and short postganglionic fibers. The preganglionic neuron travels a long distance from the brainstem or sacral spinal cord all the way to the target organ wall.

Within the wall of the target organ, the preganglionic fiber synapses with the postganglionic neuron inside a terminal ganglion, often called an intramural ganglion. The primary chemical messenger used at both the preganglionic and postganglionic synapses in this system is acetylcholine (ACh).

Cholinergic Receptors: Nicotinic and Muscarinic

For acetylcholine to exert its effects, it must bind to specific cholinergic receptors on the cell membrane. There are two primary types:

  • Nicotinic receptors: Located at autonomic ganglion synapses, on the adrenal medulla, and at the neuromuscular junction. They are ligand-gated ion channels that always produce a fast excitatory response when stimulated.
  • Muscarinic receptors: Located on all target organs controlled by parasympathetic postganglionic fibers, such as the heart, smooth muscle, and glands. They operate via G-protein coupled mechanisms and can either excite or inhibit the target tissue depending on the specific subtype.

Physiological Effects on Target Organs

Activation of the parasympathetic system produces widespread, predictable changes geared toward resource conservation and maintenance.

Eyes (Pupils)Constriction (miosis) for close vision
Salivary GlandsStimulation of watery, abundant saliva
HeartDecreased heart rate and force of contraction
LungsBronchoconstriction and increased mucus secretion
Gastrointestinal TractIncreased motility, digestion, and enzyme release
BladderContraction of the detrusor muscle and relaxation of sphincters to promote urination

Role in Homeostasis and Clinical Implications

The parasympathetic nervous system works in constant balance with the sympathetic system to maintain homeostasis. While sympathetic bursts handle acute challenges, the parasympathetic system restores normal baseline parameters once the threat has passed. In clinical practice, understanding these pathways helps nurses anticipate drug side effects. Medications that mimic parasympathetic activity are called cholinergic drugs or parasympathomimetics, which can cause increased secretions, low blood pressure, and slowed heart rate. Conversely, anticholinergic drugs block these effects, often resulting in dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, and urinary retention.
