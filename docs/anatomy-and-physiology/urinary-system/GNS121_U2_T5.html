Nephrons

Introduction to the Nephron and Renal Structure

The nephron is the fundamental structural and functional unit of the human kidney. Each adult human kidney contains roughly one million of these microscopic processing units, working tirelessly to filter blood, balance electrolytes, and eliminate metabolic waste products. Structurally, the kidney is divided into an outer renal cortex and an inner renal medulla. Most nephrons sit high up in the cortex, while others dip deep into the medulla. Blood enters the kidney through the large renal artery, which branches progressively smaller until it forms the specialized capillary beds where filtration begins.

Anatomy of the Renal Corpuscle: Glomerulus and Bowman's Capsule

The renal corpuscle is the initial filtering component of the nephron, consisting of two main parts: the glomerulus and Bowman's capsule.

  • Glomerulus: A tuft of high-pressure capillaries fed by an afferent arteriole and drained by an efferent arteriole. This high pressure forces water and small solutes out of the blood.
  • Bowman's capsule: A double-walled epithelial cup that surrounds the glomerulus, catching the filtered fluid and directing it into the renal tubule.

A common clinical misconception is that blood cells routinely pass through this filter; however, the healthy glomerular membrane has tight fenestrations and slit diaphragms that block blood cells and large proteins from escaping into the urine.

The Renal Tubule System: Proximal Convoluted Tubule to Collecting Duct

Once fluid enters Bowman's capsule, it travels through a continuous, winding tube divided into distinct functional segments:

  1. Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Heavily coiled segment lined with microvilli to maximize the reabsorption of water, ions, and organic nutrients.
  2. Loop of Henle: A hairpin loop dipping into the medulla that establishes a concentration gradient.
  3. Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Fine-tunes ion secretion and reabsorption based on immediate body needs.
  4. Collecting Duct: Receives urine from multiple nephrons and carries it downward toward the renal pelvis, adjusting final water volume under hormonal influence.

Cortical and Juxtamedullary Nephrons

Nephrons are categorized into two structural types based on their location and loop length. Cortical nephrons make up about 85% of all nephrons. They feature short loops of Henle that barely dip into the outer medulla and are primarily responsible for standard day-to-day filtration and reabsorption.

In contrast, juxtamedullary nephrons have long loops of Henle that plunge deep into the medullary pyramids. Their long loops are surrounded by specialized capillaries called vasa recta, enabling the body to concentrate urine effectively and conserve vital water when hydration is low.

Mechanisms of Urine Formation: Glomerular Filtration

Urine formation begins with glomerular filtration, a passive process driven by the hydrostatic pressure inside the glomerular capillaries. Blood pressure pushes water and dissolved solutes across the filtration membrane into Bowman's capsule. The resulting fluid is known as the glomerular filtrate. The rate at which this filtrate is produced is called the glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Stable GFR is vital; if blood pressure drops too low, filtration stops, and toxic waste products quickly build up in the bloodstream.

Tubular Reabsorption and Secretion Processes

Once filtrate enters the renal tubule, the body must reclaim essential substances while discarding waste.

  • Tubular reabsorption moves useful items—such as glucose, amino acids, water, and sodium—from the tubule lumen back into the surrounding peritubular capillaries. Most of this heavy lifting occurs in the proximal convoluted tubule.
  • Tubular secretion works in the reverse direction, pumping unwanted substances like excess hydrogen ions, potassium, and certain drugs directly from the blood into the tubule for removal.

Together, these two opposing processes ensure that the final urine excreted contains metabolic waste while preserving fluid and electrolyte balance.

Role of the Loop of Henle in Countercurrent Multiplier Systems

The loop of Henle acts as a countercurrent multiplier, establishing a salty concentration gradient in the interstitial fluid of the renal medulla. The descending limb is freely permeable to water but impermeable to sodium, causing water to leave the tubule as it enters the salty medulla. Conversely, the ascending limb is impermeable to water but actively pumps sodium and chloride ions out into the tissue fluid. This opposing flow creates an osmotic ladder that allows the kidney to produce either concentrated or dilute urine depending on body hydration.

Hormonal Regulation of Nephron Function and Fluid Balance

Nephrons do not operate in isolation; they respond dynamically to circulating hormones to maintain blood pressure and fluid volume.

  • Aldosterone: Acts on the distal tubule and collecting duct to stimulate sodium reabsorption, which draws water along with it and raises blood pressure.
  • Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH): Makes the walls of the collecting duct permeable to water, allowing water to be pulled back into the body when dehydrated, resulting in concentrated urine.

When a patient is severely dehydrated, ADH release increases sharply, allowing the kidneys to conserve every possible drop of water.
