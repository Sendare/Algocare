Nephrons

Introduction to the Nephron and Renal Structure

The nephron serves as the structural and functional unit of the human kidney. Each adult human kidney contains roughly one million of these microscopic filtering units, embedded across distinct internal regions. Grossly, a kidney splits into an outer renal cortex and an inner renal medulla. Cortical tissue arches over the medullary pyramids, creating distinct zones where different segments of the nephron reside. Blood enters each kidney via the renal artery, branching progressively into interlobular arteries, afferent arterioles, and finally into the specialized capillary beds that initiate blood purification. Beyond filtering metabolic wastes, these tiny tubules regulate blood volume, maintain systemic electrolyte concentrations, stabilize blood pH, and secrete hormones such as erythropoietin and renin.

Anatomy of the Renal Corpuscle: Glomerulus and Bowman's Capsule

Blood filtration begins inside the renal corpuscle, a spherical structure comprising a tuft of capillaries known as the glomerulus, enclosed by Bowman's capsule. The glomerulus receives blood from an afferent arteriole and drains it into a narrower efferent arteriole, creating high hydrostatic pressure that forces fluid out of the blood plasma. - **Glomerular capillary endothelium**: Features large pores called fenestrations that block blood cells and platelets while allowing fluid and solutes to pass. - **Basement membrane**: A gel-like extracellular matrix that repels negatively charged plasma proteins like albumin. - **Podocytes**: Specialized visceral epithelial cells wrapping around the capillaries with interdigitating foot processes, forming filtration slits for ultrafiltration.

The Renal Tubule System

Once fluid passes through the glomerular filtration barrier, it enters the renal tubule system, a continuous winding tube leading to the renal pelvis. 1. **Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT)**: Located in the cortex, lined with simple cuboidal epithelium featuring dense microvilli forming a brush border for maximal reabsorption. 2. **Loop of Henle**: Dips down into the medulla with a descending limb permeable to water and an ascending limb permeable to ions. 3. **Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT)**: Shorter than the PCT, lacking a brush border, and specialized for fine-tuning ion secretion and reabsorption. 4. **Collecting Duct**: Receives fluid from multiple nephrons, descending through the medulla to empty urine into the minor calyces.

Cortical and Juxtamedullary Nephrons

Nephrons are categorized into two structural populations based on the location of their renal corpuscles and the depth to which their loops of Henle extend into the medulla. | Feature | Cortical Nephrons | Juxtamedullary Nephrons | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Abundance** | Constitute roughly 85% of all nephrons | Constitute about 15% of all nephrons | | **Corpuscle Location** | Outer region of the renal cortex | Near the border of the cortex and medulla | | **Loop of Henle Depth** | Short, barely dipping into the outer medulla | Long, plunging deep into the medullary pyramids | | **Primary Function** | Standard blood filtration and general reabsorption | Establishing osmotic gradients to concentrate urine | In clinical practice across Nigerian teaching hospitals, understanding these structural variations helps nurses comprehend pathologies involving concentrating defects and tubular necrosis.

Mechanisms of Urine Formation: Glomerular Filtration

Urine production begins with glomerular filtration, a passive process driven by hydrostatic pressure within the glomerular capillaries. Blood pressure forces water and dissolved small solutes across the filtration membrane into Bowman's capsule, forming an ultrafiltrate. Net filtration pressure depends on three interacting forces: - **Glomerular blood hydrostatic pressure**: Promotes filtration by pushing fluid out of the capillary. - **Capsular hydrostatic pressure**: Opposes filtration by pushing fluid back into the capillary from the capsule lumen. - **Blood colloid osmotic pressure**: Opposes filtration due to plasma proteins pulling water back into the blood. If systemic blood pressure drops drastically, such as during severe hypovolemic shock from dehydration or trauma, glomerular filtration can cease entirely, leading to acute kidney injury.

Tubular Reabsorption and Secretion Processes

As the filtrate flows through the renal tubules, the body reclaims essential substances while actively eliminating waste products. Tubular reabsorption moves water, glucose, amino acids, and vital ions out of the tubular lumen and back into the surrounding peritubular capillaries. The proximal convoluted tubule reabsorbs nearly all filtered glucose and amino acids through sodium-coupled cotransport mechanisms. Conversely, tubular secretion transfers additional waste products, excess hydrogen ions, potassium, and certain drugs from the peritubular blood directly into the tubule lumen. This dual process ensures that the final urine composition accurately reflects the body's current homeostatic needs, retaining what is valuable and expelling metabolic byproducts like urea, creatinine, and metabolic acids.

Role of the Loop of Henle in Countercurrent Multiplier Systems

The loop of Henle acts as a countercurrent multiplier, a physiological mechanism that establishes a high salt concentration in the deep renal medulla. Fluid flows in opposite directions through the descending and ascending limbs, running parallel to the vasa recta blood vessels. The descending limb is highly permeable to water but impermeable to solutes, causing water to leave the tubule via osmosis as the filtrate enters the hypertonic medulla. The ascending limb is impermeable to water but actively transports sodium, potassium, and chloride ions out into the interstitial fluid. This active pumping builds up high osmolality in the medullary tissue, allowing the kidney to later produce concentrated urine when water conservation is necessary.

Hormonal Regulation of Nephron Function and Fluid Balance

Nephron function is continuously adjusted by circulating hormones to maintain blood pressure, electrolyte balance, and hydration status. - **Aldosterone**: Released from the adrenal cortex in response to renin and angiotensin II, it promotes sodium and water reabsorption while enhancing potassium excretion in the distal tubules and collecting ducts. - **Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH)**: Secreted by the posterior pituitary gland when blood osmolality rises or blood volume drops, it inserts aquaporin water channels into the collecting duct cells, allowing water to be reabsorbed into the body. - **Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP)**: Released by stretched heart muscle cells when blood volume is too high, inhibiting sodium reabsorption to promote diuresis.
