Urinary Bladder

Anatomical Location and Basic Structure of the Urinary Bladder

The urinary bladder is a hollow, muscular organ that acts as a temporary reservoir for urine before it leaves the body. In the human body, it sits within the pelvic cavity, specifically posterior to the pubic bone. When empty, a normal adult bladder is roughly the size and shape of a pear, resting entirely within the pelvis. As it fills with urine from the ureters, it rises superiorly into the lower abdominal cavity, changing shape from ovoid to spherical.

Positioning differs slightly between biological sexes due to pelvic architecture. In females, the bladder lies directly anterior to the vagina and inferior to the uterus. In males, it rests directly anterior to the rectum and superior to the prostate gland. Understanding these structural neighbors helps nurses anticipate why pelvic inflammation, tumors, or pregnancy frequently lead to urinary frequency and pressure symptoms.

Gross Anatomy: The Trigone, Sphincters, and Neck

Inside the bladder interior, a smooth triangular region called the trigonum vesicae, or simply the trigone, marks a distinct landmark. The trigone is defined by three openings: two superior entry points where the ureters empty urine into the bladder, and one inferior opening at the internal urethral orifice leading out of the bladder.

Unlike the rest of the bladder lining, the mucosa of the trigone is smooth and firmly bound to the muscular wall, meaning it does not wrinkle when the bladder empties. This area is a frequent site of clinical infections and inflammation, known clinically as trigonitis. At the bladder neck, smooth muscle thickens to form the internal urethral sphincter, which works involuntarily to prevent urine from leaking out prematurely. Just below this, the external urethral sphincter provides voluntary control using skeletal muscle within the urogenital diaphragm.

Histology and Layers of the Bladder Wall

The wall of the urinary bladder consists of four distinct tissue layers adapted for stretching and holding liquid:

  1. Mucosa: The innermost layer lined with transitional epithelium (urothelium). This specialized tissue can stretch and change shape as the bladder fills and empties without tearing.
  2. Submucosa: A supportive layer of connective tissue containing blood vessels and nerves that cushions the inner lining.
  3. Detrusor muscle: A thick, three-layered smooth muscle coat that contracts powerfully to squeeze urine out during urination.
  4. Adventitia: The outer fibrous connective tissue layer covering most of the bladder, though the superior surface also receives a slippery coating of serosa (peritoneum).

When the bladder is empty, the mucosa appears wrinkled into folds called rugae, which flatten out as the organ fills.

Blood Supply and Nerve Control

The bladder receives rich arterial blood primarily from branches of the internal iliac arteries, including the superior and inferior vesical arteries. Venous drainage mirrors this layout, feeding into the vesical venous plexus before emptying into the internal iliac veins.

Neural control balances involuntary autonomic regulation with voluntary somatic control. Sympathetic fibers from the hypogastric nerves promote urine storage by relaxing the detrusor muscle and contracting the internal sphincter. Parasympathetic fibers from the pelvic splanchnic nerves stimulate micturition by contracting the detrusor muscle and relaxing the internal sphincter. Meanwhile, the pudendal nerve carries somatic motor signals to give conscious, voluntary control over the external urethral sphincter in the pelvic floor.

Physiology of Storage and Micturition

Bladder function alternates between two phases: the storage phase and the micturition phase.

During the storage phase, low-pressure filling occurs. As urine accumulates, stretch receptors in the bladder wall send signals up the spinal cord to the pontine micturition center in the brainstem. Sympathetic tone remains high, keeping the detrusor relaxed and the internal sphincter closed, while somatic signals keep the external sphincter tightly shut.

When volume reaches approximately 200 to 400 milliliters, conscious awareness of bladder fullness arises. If conditions are appropriate for voiding, the micturition reflex is initiated. The cerebral cortex relaxes voluntary control over the external sphincter, parasympathetic outflow fires to contract the detrusor muscle, the internal sphincter opens, and urine is expelled through the urethra.

Clinical Relevance and Common Bladder Disorders

The urinary bladder is susceptible to various clinical conditions that alter its storage and emptying capabilities. Urinary tract infections (UTIs), such as cystitis, cause painful urination, frequency, and lower abdominal discomfort, often triggered by bacteria ascending the short female urethra. Urinary incontinence involves the involuntary leakage of urine, manifesting as stress incontinence from weakened pelvic floor muscles or urge incontinence from overactive bladder spasms.

Conversely, urinary retention is the inability to empty the bladder completely, often caused by prostate enlargement in older men or nerve damage following surgery. Nurses frequently manage retention by performing catheterization to relieve distension and prevent permanent renal damage from backpressure.
