Urinary Bladder

Anatomical Location and Gross Structure

The urinary bladder is a hollow, muscular, distensible pelvic organ that acts as a temporary reservoir for urine before elimination. In the human body, it sits within the pelvic cavity, specifically posterior to the pubic symphysis. Its exact position shifts depending on how full it is; when empty, the bladder lies entirely within the pelvis, but as it fills with urine from the ureters, it rises superiorly into the abdominal cavity.

Grossly, the bladder is divided into distinct anatomical regions:

  • Apex: The superior tip pointing toward the anterior abdominal wall, connected to the umbilicus by the median umbilical ligament (a remnant of the embryonic urachus).
  • Fundus (Base): The broad posterior surface facing backward and slightly downward.
  • Body: The major central area situated between the apex and the fundus.
  • Neck: The inferior constricted portion tapering downward to join the urethra, containing the internal urethral sphincter.

Inside the bladder floor lies the trigone, a smooth, triangular area defined by the two ureteric orifices entering postero-superiorly and the internal urethral opening inferiorly. Because the mucosa in this region is firmly bound to the muscular coat, the trigone remains smooth even when the rest of the bladder mucosa is folded into rugae during an empty state.

Histology and Layers of the Bladder Wall

The wall of the urinary bladder consists of four distinct tissue layers specialized for stretching and protection against toxic waste products in urine.

  1. Mucosa: The innermost layer lined by transitional epithelium (urothelium). This specialized tissue changes shape as the bladder fills, flattening out to accommodate increased volume without tearing. Beneath the epithelium lies a lamina propria composed of connective tissue.
  2. Submucosa: A supporting layer containing blood vessels, lymphatics, and nerves that connect the mucosa to the muscularis layer.
  3. Muscularis (Detrusor muscle): A thick layer consisting of three interwoven layers of smooth muscle fibers running in longitudinal, circular, and oblique directions. Coordinated contraction of this detrusor muscle squeezes urine out of the bladder during elimination.
  4. Adventitia/Serosa: The outermost covering. The superior surface is covered by peritoneum (serosa), while the rest of the outer surface consists of fibrous connective tissue (adventitia) attaching the bladder to surrounding pelvic structures.

Vascular Supply and Venous Drainage

Blood supply to the urinary bladder is delivered primarily by branches of the internal iliac arteries. The superior vesical arteries supply the upper parts of the bladder, while the inferior vesical arteries (in males) or vaginal/uterine arteries (in females) supply the lower fundus and base.

Venous blood drains into a dense network of veins known as the vesical venous plexus. This plexus surrounds the neck and base of the bladder before draining into the internal iliac veins. In clinical practice, this venous network is notable because it communicates extensively with pelvic veins surrounding the prostate in males or the vaginal venous plexus in females, providing a pathway for pelvic infections or metastatic cells.

Nerve Supply and Neural Control

Neural regulation of the urinary bladder involves complex coordination between the autonomic nervous system and somatic motor pathways.

  • Parasympathetic innervation: Originates from the pelvic splanchnic nerves (S2-S4). These fibers stimulate contraction of the detrusor muscle and relaxation of the internal urethral sphincter, promoting micturition.
  • Sympathetic innervation: Originates from the hypogastric plexus (T11-L2). These fibers inhibit detrusor contraction and contract the internal urethral sphincter, promoting urine storage.
  • Somatic innervation: Travels via the pudendal nerve (S2-S4) to control the external urethral sphincter, which consists of skeletal muscle under voluntary conscious control.

Sensory stretch receptors embedded in the bladder wall detect filling and transmit afferent impulses via pelvic nerves to the sacral spinal cord, initiating the micturition reflex loop.

Physiology of Urine Storage and Micturition

The physiological management of urine involves two alternating phases: filling/storage and voiding (micturition).

During the storage phase, the bladder accommodates increasing volumes of urine at low intravesical pressure due to the viscoelastic properties of its wall. Sympathetic activity dominates, keeping the detrusor relaxed and the internal sphincter closed. As filling reaches approximately 200 to 400 milliliters, stretch receptors fire with increasing frequency.

When voluntary urination occurs, the micturition reflex takes over:

  1. Sensory signals travel to the pontine micturition center in the brainstem.
  2. Parasympathetic outflow fires, causing the detrusor muscle to contract rhythmically.
  3. Sympathetic tone is inhibited, and somatic motor impulses to the external urethral sphincter cease, allowing the sphincter to relax.
  4. Urine flows smoothly through the urethra to the exterior.

Clinical Conditions and Assessment

Pathology affecting the urinary bladder is common in clinical practice, ranging from infections to structural changes.

  • Cystitis: Inflammation of the bladder, typically caused by bacterial infection ascending from the urethra. Common symptoms include dysuria (painful urination), frequency, and suprapubic discomfort.
  • Urinary Incontinence: The involuntary loss of urine, which can manifest as stress incontinence (leakage with coughing or laughing due to weak pelvic floors) or urge incontinence (sudden intense need to void due to overactive detrusor contractions).
  • Urinary Retention: The inability to empty the bladder completely, often caused by BPH in older men or neurologic injury.

Nurses assess bladder status through palpation, percussion of the lower abdomen, and bedside bladder scanners to measure post-void residual volume non-invasively.
