Urinary Bladder

Anatomical Location and Gross Structure

The urinary bladder sits within the pelvic cavity, posterior to the pubic bones and anterior to the rectum in males or the uterus and vagina in females. When empty, it resembles a three-sided pyramid, resting firmly on the pelvic floor. As urine accumulates, the organ ascends into the lower abdominal cavity, changing shape from pelvic to abdominal. The superior surface is covered by peritoneum, while the inferiorly positioned neck connects directly to the urethra. Clinically, this extraperitoneal positioning of the lower anterior wall allows safe surgical access via a suprapubic approach without entering the peritoneal cavity.

Internal Features: The Trigone and Mucosa

The interior surface of the bladder features a smooth, triangular region called the trigone. Located at the base of the bladder, the three corners of the trigone are marked by three openings: two posterolaterally for the ureteric orifices and one inferiorly for the internal urethral orifice. Unlike the rest of the bladder mucosa, which is heavily folded into rugae to allow expansion, the mucosa overlying the trigone is firmly attached to the muscular coat and remains smooth. This anatomical constancy makes the trigone a key landmark during cystoscopy and a frequent site for pathological changes such as inflammation or malignancy.

Histology and Wall Layers

The wall of the urinary bladder is composed of four distinct tissue layers:

  1. Mucosa: Lined by transitional epithelium (urothelium), which uniquely stretches and thins as the organ fills while maintaining an impermeable barrier against toxic waste products in urine.
  2. Submucosa: A supportive layer of connective tissue carrying blood vessels and nerves to the mucosal lining.
  3. Muscularis layer: Known as the detrusor muscle, consisting of three intertwined layers of smooth muscle fibers that contract synchronously to expel urine.
  4. Adventitia/Serosa: The outermost covering, consisting of fibrous connective tissue, with only the superior dome receiving a peritoneal serosal layer.

Blood Supply and Innervation

Blood reaches the bladder primarily through branches of the internal iliac arteries, notably the superior and inferior vesical arteries, with venous return following a corresponding network into the internal iliac veins. Neural control involves both autonomic and somatic pathways. Sympathetic fibers from the hypogastric plexus promote urine storage by relaxing the detrusor muscle and contracting the internal urethral sphincter. Parasympathetic fibers from the pelvic splanchnic nerves stimulate micturition by contracting the detrusor muscle. Additionally, somatic motor fibers via the pudendal nerve exert voluntary control over the skeletal muscle of the external urethral sphincter.

Physiology of Urine Storage and Micturition

The bladder alternates between two main phases: storage and elimination. During the storage phase, low sympathetic tone allows the detrusor muscle to relax while high sympathetic activity keeps the internal sphincter closed. As volume increases, stretch receptors in the wall send signals to the sacral spinal cord. When the micturition threshold is reached, the micturition reflex is activated. Parasympathetic output surges to contract the detrusor muscle, while sympathetic and somatic inhibition relaxes both the internal and external urethral sphincters, allowing coordinated expulsion of urine.

Clinical Conditions and Assessment

Disorders of the urinary bladder frequently present with alterations in storage or emptying. Urinary tract infections (UTIs) cause inflammation of the mucosa, leading to frequency, urgency, and dysuria. Urinary retention, often caused by benign prostatic hyperplasia in older males, occurs when the bladder cannot empty completely, risking overflow incontinence and renal damage. Clinical assessment relies heavily on palpation and percussion of the suprapubic region to detect bladder distension, alongside diagnostic tools like bladder scanners to measure post-void residual volume safely and non-invasively.
