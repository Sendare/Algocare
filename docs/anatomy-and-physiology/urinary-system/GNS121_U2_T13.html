Acid-Base Balance

Introduction to Acid-Base Balance and pH

Acid-base balance refers to the precise regulation of free hydrogen ions in the body fluids. The term **pH** literally means the potential of hydrogen, serving as a negative logarithm of the hydrogen ion concentration. A higher concentration of hydrogen ions yields a lower pH, indicating acidity, whereas a lower concentration results in a higher pH, indicating alkalinity. This logarithmic scale runs from 0 to 14, with 7.0 considered neutral. Cellular enzymes, structural proteins, and metabolic pathways rely entirely on this ionic stability to function without denaturation.

Normal Physiological pH Ranges and Homeostasis

Arterial blood plasma maintains a strict physiological pH range between **7.35 and 7.45**. Values falling below 7.35 designate **acidemia**, while those rising above 7.45 designate **alkalemia**. Survival is severely threatened if blood pH drops below 6.8 or climbs above 7.8, thresholds where vital organ failure becomes imminent. Homeostasis depends on three integrated lines of defense: chemical buffer systems acting within seconds, the respiratory tract responding within minutes, and the kidneys providing definitive correction over hours to days.

Role of Buffer Systems in Body Fluids

Buffers are the body's first line of defense, acting as chemical sponges that absorb or release hydrogen ions. - **Bicarbonate buffer system**: Operates primarily in the extracellular fluid, balancing carbonic acid and bicarbonate ions. - **Phosphate buffer system**: Works intensely inside cells and within renal tubules where phosphate concentrations are higher. - **Protein buffer system**: The most abundant intracellular and plasma buffer, utilizing amino acid side chains to accept or donate hydrogen ions.

Respiratory Regulation of Acid-Base Balance

The lungs control volatile acids by regulating carbon dioxide elimination through ventilation. When metabolic acids accumulate, peripheral and central chemoreceptors detect the drop in pH and stimulate the respiratory center in the medulla oblongata. This triggers **hyperventilation**, blowing off carbon dioxide and shifting the carbonic acid equation to reduce acid load. Conversely, respiratory depression retains carbon dioxide to counter alkalosis. This system responds within minutes, though it cannot fully restore balance on its own if the primary disturbance is metabolic.

Renal Regulation and Bicarbonate Management

The kidneys provide the ultimate regulation of acid-base balance by excreting non-volatile, fixed acids and managing bicarbonate reabsorption and generation. Renal tubules can actively secrete hydrogen ions into the urine while reabsorbing virtually all filtered bicarbonate. When systemic acidosis persists, the renal cells manufacture new bicarbonate and dump excess acid into the urine, often buffered by ammonia or phosphate. While slower than chemical buffers or the lungs, renal compensation can sustain adjustments for days until complete recovery occurs.

Overview of Acid-Base Imbalances: Acidosis and Alkalosis

Imbalances occur when compensation fails or overwhelming pathology strikes. **Respiratory acidosis** stems from hypoventilation, trapping carbon dioxide, while **respiratory alkalosis** results from hyperventilation blowing off too much carbon dioxide. On the metabolic side, **metabolic acidosis** involves a primary deficit of bicarbonate or accumulation of organic acids like ketones or lactate, whereas **metabolic alkalosis** arises from excessive bicarbonate retention or severe gastrointestinal acid loss through prolonged vomiting.

Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) Interpretation for Nurses

Analyzing an Arterial Blood Gas sample requires a systematic approach to uncover underlying disturbances. First, look at the pH to identify acidosis or alkalosis. Next, check the partial pressure of carbon dioxide to evaluate the respiratory component, and examine the serum bicarbonate level for the metabolic component. Using the **ROME** mnemonic—Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal—helps determine if abnormal values align with the pH change. Finally, assess whether the body has initiated full or partial compensation.
