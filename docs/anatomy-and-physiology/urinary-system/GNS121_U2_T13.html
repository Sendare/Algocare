Acid-Base Balance

Introduction to Acid-Base Balance and pH

Acid-base balance refers to the precise regulation of hydrogen ions ($H^+$) in body fluids. The term pH literally means the potential of hydrogen. In the human body, chemical reactions, energy production, and cellular survival depend entirely on maintaining this delicate ionic equilibrium.

  • Acids: Substances that donate hydrogen ions into a solution. In the body, volatile acids like carbonic acid and non-volatile metabolic acids are produced continuously.
  • Bases (Alkalis): Substances that accept hydrogen ions, with bicarbonate ($HCO_3^-$) being the primary chemical base in human blood.

The scale runs from 0 to 14, where a lower number indicates a high concentration of free hydrogen ions and high acidity, while a higher number indicates fewer hydrogen ions and greater alkalinity. A neutral solution sits right at 7.0.

Normal Physiological pH Ranges and Homeostasis

Normal arterial blood pH is tightly regulated within a narrow alkaline window of 7.35 to 7.45. If the blood pH falls below 7.35, the patient is in acidemia. If it rises above 7.45, the patient is in alkalemia.

Survival outside a pH range of 6.80 to 7.80 is rare because extreme changes alter the electrical charges of proteins, causing them to denature and lose their vital functions. Enzymes stop working, and cellular membranes become unstable. Homeostasis depends on three primary defense lines working together:

  1. Chemical buffer systems, which react within seconds.
  2. Respiratory regulation, which adjusts within minutes to hours.
  3. Renal regulation, which takes days to achieve full compensation.

Role of Buffer Systems in Body Fluids

Chemical buffers are the body's fastest line of defense against acid-base disturbances. They act within fractions of a second by binding to excess hydrogen ions or releasing them when levels drop too low. However, buffers do not eliminate acids or bases from the body; they merely tie them up temporarily.

The carbonic acid-bicarbonate system is especially vital because it links directly with the respiratory and urinary tracts.

Respiratory Regulation of Acid-Base Balance

The respiratory system controls blood pH by regulating the elimination or retention of carbon dioxide ($CO_2$), which acts as an acid in the body because it combines with water to form carbonic acid.

Chemoreceptors in the brainstem and carotid arteries monitor $CO_2$ levels and blood pH. When arterial $CO_2$ rises or pH drops, the respiratory center stimulates an increase in the rate and depth of breathing (hyperventilation). This blows off more $CO_2$, reducing carbonic acid and raising the pH back toward normal. Conversely, when blood is too alkaline, breathing slows (hypoventilation) to trap $CO_2$ in the bloodstream.

This system responds within minutes, making it much faster than the kidneys, though it cannot fully correct metabolic imbalances on its own.

Renal Regulation and Bicarbonate Management

The kidneys provide the most powerful, definitive regulation of acid-base balance. While the lungs handle volatile carbon dioxide, the kidneys excrete non-volatile metabolic acids—such as sulfuric and phosphoric acids—that cannot leave via the lungs.

  • Bicarbonate reabsorption: The renal tubules actively reabsorb filtered bicarbonate so it is not lost in the urine.
  • Hydrogen ion excretion: The tubules secrete excess hydrogen ions directly into the urine for elimination.
  • Buffer generation: The kidneys can synthesize *new* bicarbonate ions and add them to the blood when the body is too acidic.

Renal compensation is powerful, but it takes anywhere from 48 to 72 hours to achieve full corrective capacity.

Overview of Acid-Base Imbalances: Acidosis and Alkalosis

When homeostatic mechanisms fail or become overwhelmed, acid-base imbalances occur. These are categorized by origin into respiratory or metabolic disturbances.

  1. Respiratory Acidosis: Caused by hypoventilation, leading to $CO_2$ retention and high carbonic acid. Common causes include chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), opioid overdose, and chest trauma.
  2. Respiratory Alkalosis: Caused by hyperventilation, blowing off too much $CO_2$. Common triggers include panic attacks, fever, and early sepsis.
  3. Metabolic Acidosis: Caused by a gain of fixed acids or a loss of bicarbonate. Seen in diabetic ketoacidosis, severe diarrhea, and renal failure.
  4. Metabolic Alkalosis: Caused by a loss of strong acids or a gain of bicarbonate. Often results from prolonged vomiting, nasogastric suctioning, or diuretic overuse.

Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) Interpretation for Nurses

Analyzing an Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) sample gives nurses a clear picture of a patient's oxygenation, ventilation, and acid-base status. A systematic approach prevents misreading.

  • Step 1: Check the pH. Determine if the value indicates acidemia ($< 7.35$) or alkalemia ($> 7.45$).
  • Step 2: Look at the $PaCO_2$ (Normal: 35–45 mmHg). This represents the respiratory component. If pH is low and $PaCO_2$ is high, it is respiratory acidosis.
  • Step 3: Look at the $HCO_3^-$ (Normal: 22–26 mEq/L). This represents the metabolic component. If pH is low and $HCO_3^-$ is low, it is metabolic acidosis.
  • Step 4: Assess for compensation. If the parameter not primarily responsible for the imbalance is moving in the same direction, the body is attempting compensation.

Remember the acronym ROME: Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal. In respiratory issues, pH and $PaCO_2$ move in opposite directions. In metabolic issues, pH and $HCO_3^-$ move in the same direction.
