Urethra

Introduction and Definition of the Urethra

The urethra is a fibromuscular tube that connects the urinary bladder to the exterior of the body. Its primary purpose is to transport urine from the bladder during the process of urination, medically known as micturition. While both men and women share this basic urinary conduit, the structure diverges significantly between the sexes due to reproductive anatomy. In females, the urethra is short and strictly dedicated to the urinary system. In males, it is much longer, winding through the prostate and penis to serve a dual purpose as a shared passageway for both urine and semen.

Anatomical Differences Between Male and Female Urethra

  • Female Urethra: Measures approximately 4 cm in length. It runs straight downwards and forwards behind the pubic symphysis, opening into the vestibule of the vagina just anterior to the vaginal opening.
  • Male Urethra: Measures about 20 cm in length. It exits the bladder, travels through the prostate gland, passes through the pelvic floor muscles, and runs the entire length of the penis to open at the tip of the glans.

A common clinical observation is that the short length of the female urethra places women at a much higher risk for bacterial urinary tract infections, as external bacteria have a much shorter distance to travel to reach the bladder.

Segments of the Male Urethra

To understand clinical procedures like catheterization, nurses must memorize the four distinct anatomical segments of the male urethra:

  1. Preprostatic segment: A very short portion located right at the neck of the bladder.
  2. Prostatic segment: The widest and most dilatable part, passing directly through the center of the prostate gland.
  3. Membranous segment: The shortest, least dilatable, and most rigid part, passing through the urogenital diaphragm where the external urethral sphincter sits.
  4. Spongy segment: Also called the penile urethra, this is the longest portion, traveling through the corpus spongiosum of the penis to the external urethral meatus.

Histology and Lining Epithelium

The cellular lining of the urethra changes as it travels outward from the bladder. Near the bladder neck, the tube is lined with transitional epithelium, the stretchy tissue capable of withstanding stretching as the bladder fills and empties. As the tube continues outward, it transitions into pseudostratified columnar epithelium or stratified columnar epithelium. Finally, near the external opening or meatus, the lining shifts to stratified squamous epithelium, which is tougher and better suited to protect against friction and the external environment.

Sphincter Mechanisms and Urinary Control

Controlling urination requires the coordinated action of two distinct muscular rings:

  • Internal Urethral Sphincter: Located at the junction of the bladder and the urethra. It is composed of involuntary smooth muscle and stays tightly contracted to keep urine inside the bladder until the brain signals it to relax.
  • External Urethral Sphincter: Located further down within the pelvic floor. It is made of voluntary skeletal muscle, allowing a person to consciously hold urine or initiate a stream.

Damage to these sphincters—often caused by childbirth, prostate surgery, or nerve injury—results in urinary incontinence.

Blood Supply, Innervation, and Lymphatic Drainage

The urethra receives its blood supply from various local branches of the internal iliac artery, including branches of the inferior vesical and middle rectal arteries in males, and the vaginal and internal pudendal arteries in females. Venous blood drains into matching veins that empty into the internal iliac venous system.

Nerve supply is a rich mix of autonomic and somatic fibers. The autonomic nervous system (both sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers via the pelvic and hypogastric plexuses) controls the involuntary internal sphincter and smooth muscle tone. Meanwhile, the pudendal nerve provides somatic motor and sensory innervation to the external urethral sphincter, giving conscious feedback regarding bladder fullness and control.
