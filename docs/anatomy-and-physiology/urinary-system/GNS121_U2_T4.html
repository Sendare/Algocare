Urethra

Introduction and Definition of the Urethra

The **urethra** is a fibromuscular tube that conveys urine from the urinary bladder to the exterior of the body. Serving as the terminal portion of the lower urinary tract, it marks the final pathway for excretion. Beyond its urinary role, the male urethra also functions as a shared conduit for the reproductive system, transmitting semen during ejaculation. Its structural design balances elasticity with muscular control, ensuring both continent storage and efficient expulsion.

Anatomical Structure and Dimensions: Male vs Female

A striking anatomical disparity exists between the male and female urethral tracts, largely dictated by pelvic architecture and reproductive geography. - **Male Urethra**: Measures approximately 18 to 20 centimeters in length, winding through the prostate, pelvic floor, and entire length of the penis. - **Female Urethra**: Measures a mere 4 centimeters in length, following a relatively straight, short course directly anterior to the vagina. In Nigerian clinical practice, this difference carries immense practical significance: the short female urethra predisposes women to ascending urinary tract infections, while the long male tract makes catheterization technically more demanding and prone to false passage creation if proper technique is neglected.

Male Urethra Segments

To master male catheterization and pelvic surgeries, one must visualize the four distinct anatomical segments of the male urethra: 1. **Preprostatic (Intramural) segment**: The short vertical portion immediately leaving the bladder neck, surrounded by the internal urethral sphincter. 2. **Prostatic segment**: The widest and most dilatable part, passing directly through the substance of the prostate gland and receiving the ejaculatory ducts. 3. **Membranous segment**: The shortest, least dilatable, and most vulnerable portion passing through the deep perineal pouch; surrounded by the external sphincter. 4. **Spongy (Penile) segment**: The longest segment, running through the corpus spongiosum of the penis and terminating at the external urethral meatus.

Female Urethra Structure and Relations

The female urethra travels obliquely downward and forward from the internal urethral orifice to the external urethral meatus. It lies embedded in the anterior wall of the vagina, sharing a dense connective tissue septum known as the urethrovaginal septum. Because of this intimate relationship, surgical procedures or trauma to the anterior vaginal wall can directly impact urethral competence and urinary control. The external meatus opens into the vestibule of the vagina, sitting posterior to the clitoris and anterior to the vaginal opening—a critical landmark during sterile female catheterization.

Histology and Lining Epithelium

The microscopic architecture of the urethral wall transitions dramatically from its origin at the bladder to its termination at the skin. Near the bladder neck, the lining consists of **transitional epithelium** (urothelium), which easily withstands stretching. As the tube continues, it shifts into **pseudostratified columnar** or **stratified columnar epithelium**. Finally, near the external urethral meatus, the epithelium transforms into **stratified squamous epithelium**, providing a tough, protective barrier against external friction and pathogens. Beneath the epithelium lies a richly vascular lamina propria containing mucous glands of Littré that lubricate the lumen.

Sphincter Mechanisms and Urinary Control

Maintaining urinary continence relies on two distinct sphincter mechanisms working in concert: - **Internal Urethral Sphincter**: Composed of involuntary smooth muscle located at the vesicourethral junction. Under autonomic control, it remains tonically contracted to keep urine inside the bladder until the micturition reflex is triggered. - **External Urethral Sphincter**: Composed of voluntary skeletal muscle situated within the urogenital diaphragm. Under somatic control via the pudendal nerve, it allows conscious delay of urination, giving patients voluntary command over voiding.

Vascular Supply, Innervation, and Lymphatic Drainage

Blood reaches the urethra through various regional branches including the inferior vesical, middle rectal, and internal pudendal arteries. Venous blood drains into matching plexuses that empty into the internal iliac veins. Nerve supply is a complex mix: sympathetic fibers from the pelvic plexus inhibit bladder contraction and stimulate the internal sphincter, parasympathetic fibers promote voiding, and somatic pudendal nerves supply the external skeletal sphincter. Lymphatic drainage follows local vascular pathways, emptying mainly into internal and external iliac lymph nodes from the proximal parts, while the distal male spongy urethra drains into deep and superficial inguinal nodes.

Physiological Role in Excretion and Reproduction

The urethra acts as a dynamic valve system during daily physiological cycles. During the storage phase, sympathetic tone keeps the bladder relaxed and both urethral sphincters tightly closed. During micturition, parasympathetic signals prompt detrusor contraction while simultaneously relaxing the internal sphincter, allowing the external sphincter to open voluntarily. In males, a sophisticated neural switch prevents the simultaneous passage of urine and semen by closing the bladder neck during ejaculation, ensuring semen is directed forward through the penile urethra.
