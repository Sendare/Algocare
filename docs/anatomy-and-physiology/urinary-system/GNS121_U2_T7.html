Functions of the Kidney

Introduction to Renal Anatomy and General Physiology

The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped organs positioned against the posterior abdominal wall. Despite their modest size, they receive about twenty percent of the resting cardiac output, underscoring their vital role in cleaning the blood. Blood enters through the renal artery and flows down into microscopic functional units called nephrons. Each human kidney contains over a million nephrons, and these tiny structures are where all the filtering, reabsorbing, and secreting happen.

At the start of every nephron lies the glomerulus, a specialized cluster of capillaries enclosed by Bowman's capsule. Blood pressure forces water and small solutes out of the blood and into the capsule, creating the initial filtrate. As this fluid travels through the long renal tubule—which includes the proximal convoluted tubule, the loop of Henle, and the distal convoluted tubule—the body reclaims essential nutrients and water, leaving unwanted waste to form urine.

Regulation of Fluid and Electrolyte Balance

Maintaining precise control over body water and dissolved salts is a primary duty of the kidneys. When you drink plenty of water, the kidneys excrete large amounts of dilute urine. When you are dehydrated, they conserve water, producing a small volume of concentrated urine.

Electrolytes like sodium, potassium, and chloride are actively managed along the renal tubules. Hormones direct this fine-tuning:

  • Antidiuretic hormone (ADH): Acts on the collecting ducts to make them permeable to water, allowing water to be reabsorbed back into the bloodstream.
  • Aldosterone: Promotes sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion in the distal nephron, which indirectly pulls water along with the sodium.
  • Atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP): Released by stretched heart muscle cells to cause the kidneys to excrete extra sodium and water.

Acid-Base Homeostasis and pH Regulation

Normal blood pH must remain tightly bound between 7.35 and 7.45. Even slight shifts outside this range disrupt cellular enzymes and normal metabolism. While the lungs handle volatile acids by breathing out carbon dioxide, the kidneys manage fixed acids and regulate bicarbonate buffers.

Tubular cells accomplish this balance through two main mechanisms:

  1. Hydrogen ion secretion: The renal tubules actively pump excess hydrogen ions into the urine filtrate to get rid of them.
  2. Bicarbonate reabsorption and regeneration: Filtered bicarbonate is reclaimed from the tubular fluid, and new bicarbonate is generated and added directly back to the bloodstream to neutralize circulating acids.

Excretion of Metabolic Wastes and Foreign Substances

Cellular metabolism constantly produces chemical byproducts that would become toxic if allowed to build up. The kidneys clear these substances efficiently from the circulation.

  • Urea: Formed in the liver from the breakdown of amino acids and cleared entirely by renal filtration.
  • Creatinine: A breakdown product of muscle creatine phosphate, used clinically as a reliable marker of kidney filtration performance.
  • Uric acid: Formed from nucleic acid breakdown, cleared through a mix of filtration and tubular secretion.
  • Toxins and drugs: Foreign chemicals, food additives, and medications are actively secreted into the tubule lumen to be eliminated in the urine.

Blood Pressure Regulation via the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System

Blood pressure drops can threaten blood flow to vital organs. When the kidneys sense a drop in blood pressure or low sodium delivery, specialized cells in the juxtaglomerular apparatus release an enzyme called renin.

Renin starts a chemical cascade in the bloodstream:

  1. Renin converts plasmaogen angiotensinogen (made by the liver) into angiotensin I.
  2. As blood passes through the lungs, an enzyme called ACE converts angiotensin I into angiotensin II.
  3. Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor that quickly tightens blood vessels to raise blood pressure.
  4. It also stimulates the adrenal glands to release aldosterone, which prompts the kidneys to hold onto sodium and water, increasing blood volume over the long term.

Hormonal Functions and Erythropoiesis Stimulation

Beyond filtering wastes, the kidneys act as endocrine organs. They monitor the oxygen levels of the blood passing through their tissues. When hypoxia or low oxygen delivery is detected—such as when traveling to high altitudes or during chronic anemia—specialized interstitial cells in the renal cortex respond by synthesizing and releasing erythropoietin (EPO).

EPO travels through the bloodstream directly to the bone marrow. There, it binds to receptor cells and stimulates the proliferation and maturation of erythrocyte precursors, increasing red blood cell production. This feedback loop ensures that the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood matches the metabolic demands of the body. In chronic kidney disease, failing renal tissue cannot produce enough EPO, frequently leading to severe normocytic anemia.

Vitamin D Activation and Calcium Homeostasis

Dietary vitamin D and vitamin D produced by sunlight on the skin are biologically inactive. They must undergo two successive hydroxylations to become fully functional. The liver performs the first step, adding a hydroxyl group to form 25-hydroxyvitamin D. The final, critical activation step occurs in the proximal tubules of the kidneys.

Renal cells convert inactive vitamin D into calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol), the active hormone form. Calcitriol enhances calcium and phosphorus absorption from the gastrointestinal tract, supports bone mineralization, and works alongside parathyroid hormone to keep serum calcium within safe limits.

Gluconeogenesis During Prolonged Fasting

While the liver is the primary organ for making new glucose, the kidneys play a major auxiliary role during periods of prolonged fasting or starvation. When glycogen stores are completely depleted, the renal cortex steps in to synthesize glucose from amino acids and other non-carbohydrate precursors.

During extended fasting, the kidneys can contribute up to twenty percent or more of total systemic glucose production, releasing this newly formed glucose directly into the bloodstream to support glucose-dependent organs like the brain and red blood cells. This metabolic function highlights the broad systemic contributions of renal tissue far beyond simple filtration.
