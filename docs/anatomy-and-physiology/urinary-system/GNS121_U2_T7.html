Functions of the Kidney

Introduction to Renal Anatomy and General Physiology

The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped retroperitoneal organs positioned against the posterior abdominal wall. Each kidney consists of an outer renal cortex and an inner renal medulla, which houses cone-shaped structures called renal pyramids. The functional microscopic unit of the kidney is the nephron, numbering over one million per organ. Every nephron features a glomerulus—a specialized capillary bed enclosed by Bowman's capsule—followed by a long tubular system including the proximal convoluted tubule, the loop of Henle, the distal convoluted tubule, and the collecting duct. Blood enters via the renal artery, undergoes filtration at the glomerulus, and the resulting filtrate is modified through tubular reabsorption and secretion to ultimately form urine. In Nigerian clinical practice, assessing baseline kidney architecture and function via urinalysis and serum creatinine is vital for detecting silent pathologies like hypertensive nephropathy early.

Regulation of Fluid and Electrolyte Balance

Kidneys maintain strict control over body water and mineral ions, adjusting output to match daily intake. When fluid volume drops or plasma osmolality rises, osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus trigger thirst and release antidiuretic hormone (ADH) from the posterior pituitary. ADH acts on the principal cells of the collecting ducts, inserting aquaporins to increase water reabsorption, producing concentrated urine. Conversely, excess water suppresses ADH, yielding dilute urine. Electrolytes such as sodium, potassium, and chloride are dynamically reabsorbed or secreted along different segments of the nephron. Aldosterone promotes sodium reabsorption and potassium excretion in the distal tubules, protecting vascular volume. Fluid overload or electrolyte derangements, such as severe hyperkalemia, pose immediate life-threatening cardiac risks frequently managed in emergency units.

Acid-Base Homeostasis and pH Regulation

Plasma pH must remain tightly regulated between 7.35 and 7.45. The kidneys serve as the primary long-term regulator of acid-base balance by controlling bicarbonate ($ ext{HCO}_3^-$) reabsorption and hydrogen ion ($ ext{H}^+$) excretion.

  • Proximal tubule: Reabsorbs the bulk of filtered bicarbonate through indirect mechanisms involving carbonic anhydrase.
  • Distal nephron: Actively secretes excess hydrogen ions into the tubular lumen, combining them with buffers like ammonia and phosphate for excretion.

Renal compensation for respiratory or metabolic acid-base disorders develops slowly over hours to days, contrasting with rapid respiratory adjustments.

Excretion of Metabolic Wastes and Foreign Substances

The kidneys act as the ultimate filtration plant for clearing nitrogenous and xenobiotic wastes from the bloodstream.

  • Urea: A byproduct of protein metabolism synthesized in the liver and cleared by glomerular filtration.
  • Creatinine: A breakdown product of creatine phosphate in skeletal muscle, serving as a reliable clinical marker for glomerular filtration rate.
  • Uric acid: The end product of purine nucleotide degradation, filtered and partially reabsorbed or secreted.
  • Xenobiotics: Foreign substances, including prescribed medications and toxins, cleared via active tubular secretion.

Accumulation of these waste products leads to uremia, a clinical syndrome affecting multiple organ systems when renal function severely declines.

Blood Pressure Regulation via the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System

Blood pressure control relies heavily on the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). Specialized juxtaglomerular cells in the afferent arterioles sense drops in blood pressure, sympathetic stimulation, or low sodium delivery, releasing the enzyme renin. Renin cleaves plasmaogen angiotensinogen into angiotensin I. As angiotensin I circulates through the lungs, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) transforms it into angiotensin II. This active peptide causes intense vasoconstriction, increases thirst, and stimulates the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone. The collective outcome restores blood pressure and volume. Pharmacological interventions targeting this cascade, such as ACE inhibitors, form the cornerstone of hypertension and chronic kidney disease management in clinical practice.

Hormonal Functions and Erythropoiesis Stimulation

Beyond filtration and fluid control, the kidneys function as endocrine organs. Peritubular interstitial fibroblasts sense tissue hypoxia when arterial oxygen content falls. In response, they synthesize and secrete erythropoietin (EPO) into the bloodstream. EPO travels to the bone marrow, binding to receptor sites on erythroid progenitor cells to stimulate their proliferation and differentiation into mature red blood cells. Chronic kidney disease frequently impairs EPO synthesis, resulting in normocytic normochromic anemia, a predictable complication managed with recombinant erythropoietin therapy and iron supplementation.

Vitamin D Activation and Calcium Homeostasis

Calcium balance requires the synergistic action of the parathyroid glands, bone, intestine, and kidneys. Dietary vitamin D or sun-exposed precursors undergo initial hydroxylation in the liver, but the final, rate-limiting activation step occurs in the proximal tubules of the kidney. Here, the enzyme 1-alpha-hydroxylase converts inactive vitamin D into calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxycholecalciferol), the biologically active form. Calcitriol enhances dietary calcium and phosphate absorption from the gastrointestinal tract and works with parathyroid hormone to regulate bone mineralization. Renal failure disrupts this activation pathway, leading to hypocalcemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism.

Gluconeogenesis During Prolonged Fasting

While the liver serves as the primary site for glucose production during fasting, the kidneys make a substantial metabolic contribution. During prolonged fasting, starvation, or severe metabolic acidosis, the renal cortex activates gluconeogenic pathways. Renal cells take up circulating amino acids—particularly glutamine—and synthesize new glucose molecules to release into the systemic circulation. This renal gluconeogenesis accounts for up to twenty percent of total glucose production during extended fasting states, helping maintain stable blood glucose levels for vital organs like the brain and red blood cells.
