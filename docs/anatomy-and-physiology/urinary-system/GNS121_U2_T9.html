Physiology of Micturition

Urinary Tract Anatomy and Functional Units

Micturition is the physiological process of expelling urine from the urinary bladder. The functional anatomy of the lower urinary tract consists of the detrusor muscle, the trigone, the internal urethral sphincter, and the external urethral sphincter. The detrusor muscle is a specialized network of smooth muscle fibers forming the main wall of the bladder. It possesses unique viscoelastic properties, allowing it to stretch and accommodate increasing volumes of urine without a sharp rise in intravesical pressure.

The bladder neck transitions into the urethra. The internal urethral sphincter is an involuntary smooth muscle continuation of the detrusor at the bladder outlet. Just distal to this lies the external urethral sphincter, a voluntary skeletal muscle ring embedded within the urogenital diaphragm. Understanding these distinct tissue types is vital because involuntary smooth muscle responds to autonomic control, whereas skeletal muscle obeys somatic motor commands.

Neural Regulation and Innervation

Three distinct nerve sets coordinate bladder function: parasympathetic, sympathetic, and somatic pathways.

  • Parasympathetic (Pelvic Nerve): Originates from spinal segments S2 to S4. These fibers use acetylcholine to stimulate muscarinic receptors on the detrusor muscle, causing it to contract.
  • Sympathetic (Hypogastric Nerve): Originates from T11 to L2. These fibers relax the detrusor muscle and contract the internal urethral sphincter via beta-3 and alpha-1 adrenergic receptors, respectively, promoting urine storage.
  • Somatic (Pudendal Nerve): Originates from S2 to S4. These motor fibers innervate the external urethral sphincter, allowing conscious control over its tone.

The Storage Phase: Filling and Continence

During the storage phase, the bladder acts as a low-pressure reservoir. As urine trickles in from the ureters, the sympathetic nervous system dominates. Beta-3 adrenergic receptors on the detrusor muscle cause it to relax, preventing premature contractions while volume rises. Simultaneously, alpha-1 receptors stimulate the smooth muscle of the internal urethral sphincter to remain tightly closed.

At the same time, somatic signals via the pudendal nerve maintain constant tonic contraction of the external urethral sphincter. Afferent stretch receptors in the bladder wall send low-level impulses through pelvic nerve pathways to the spinal cord, signaling that filling is underway without yet triggering a conscious urge to void.

The Micturition Reflex

When bladder volume reaches roughly 200 to 400 milliliters, wall stretch increases significantly. Afferent mechanoreceptors fire at a higher frequency, sending signals up the pelvic nerve to the sacral spinal cord. These signals travel via ascending tracts to the pontine micturition center in the brainstem.

Once the brain gives the green light, parasympathetic outflow surges while sympathetic and somatic inputs are actively inhibited. Acetylcholine binds to muscarinic receptors on the detrusor, causing a coordinated, powerful contraction. At the same time, the internal and external sphincters relax, allowing urine to flow freely through the urethra.

Central Nervous System Control and Voluntary Voiding

While the micturition reflex is an autonomic spinal loop, higher brain centers exert critical oversight. The pontine micturition center acts as the primary relay switch, coordinating the timing of voiding. The frontal cortex and basal ganglia provide inhibitory or facilitative control, allowing humans to delay urination until an appropriate time and place are available.

When a person decides to urinate, the cerebral cortex signals the pontine center to turn off sympathetic and somatic guarding reflexes. The external urethral sphincter relaxes voluntarily via pudendal nerve inhibition, and the detrusor contracts. If higher brain centers are damaged by stroke or spinal injury, this coordinated control is lost, often resulting in uninhibited bladder spasms.

Clinical Implications and Common Dysfunctions

Disruptions along the neurological or muscular pathways of micturition lead to distinct clinical presentations. Neurogenic bladder disorders occur when spinal cord injuries disconnect the brain from sacral reflex centers. A lesion above the sacral cord often results in a hyperreflexic bladder, where the bladder empties automatically whenever it fills partially.

Conversely, lower motor neuron or peripheral nerve damage creates an atonic, flaccid bladder that overfills and overflows. Obstructions like benign prostatic hyperplasia cause lower urinary tract symptoms by forcing the detrusor to work against high resistance, leading to muscle hypertrophy and eventual failure.
