Physiology of Micturition

Urinary Tract Anatomy and Functional Units

Micturition is the physiological process of emptying the urinary bladder. The functional anatomy supporting this process includes the kidneys, ureters, urinary bladder, and urethra. The bladder wall contains a specialized smooth muscle known as the detrusor muscle, arranged in interwoven bundles that contract in unison to expel urine. The base of the bladder features the trigonum, a smooth triangular region defined by the two ureteral orifices and the internal urethral orifice. Urine transport from the renal pelvis occurs via peristaltic waves in the ureters, entering the bladder obliquely to create a physiological one-way valve that prevents vesicoureteral reflux. At the bladder outlet, two distinct sphincters maintain continence: the internal urethral sphincter, composed of involuntary smooth muscle continuous with the detrusor, and the external urethral sphincter, formed by striated muscle under voluntary control within the urogenital diaphragm.

Neural Regulation and Innervation of the Bladder

Precise neural control coordinates both the storage and voiding phases, relying on three major nerve sets branching from the autonomic and somatic nervous systems:

  • Sympathetic Innervation: Originating from spinal segments T11 to L2 via the hypogastric nerves, sympathetic stimulation promotes urine storage by relaxing the detrusor muscle and contracting the internal urethral sphincter.
  • Parasympathetic Innervation: Arising from sacral segments S2 to S4 via the pelvic nerves, parasympathetic outflow drives the voiding phase by stimulating powerful contractions of the detrusor muscle.
  • Somatic Innervation: Carried by the pudendal nerve originating from S2 to S4, somatic fibers innervate the skeletal muscle of the external urethral sphincter, allowing conscious control over the initiation and cessation of urination.

The Storage Phase: Filling and Continence

During the storage phase, the bladder acts as a low-pressure reservoir, accommodating increasing volumes of urine without significant elevation in intravesical pressure. This property, known as compliance, relies on the viscoelastic properties of the smooth muscle and sympathetic inhibition. As urine accumulates, stretch receptors in the bladder wall fire at a low baseline frequency. Afferent impulses travel to the spinal cord, activating sympathetic pathways that inhibit parasympathetic neurons in the pelvic ganglion while exciting the internal sphincter. Concurrently, tonic impulses traveling down the pudendal nerve keep the external urethral sphincter tightly contracted. Voluntary override from higher cerebral centers can keep this continence mechanism active long past the initial sensation of fullness.

The Micturition Reflex and Its Integration

When bladder volume reaches approximately 200 to 400 milliliters, stretch receptor firing increases markedly. Afferent signals ascend through the pelvic nerves to the sacral micturition center. In infants, this triggers an uninhibited spinal reflex arc: efferent parasympathetic signals cause the detrusor muscle to contract while sympathetic inhibition and pudendal nerve relaxation open the urethral sphincters. As the human nervous system matures, this spinal reflex comes under the control of the pontine micturition center in the brainstem. The pons acts as a switching station, coordinating a synergistic response that simultaneously contracts the bladder and relaxes the urinary sphincters to ensure efficient emptying.

Central Nervous System Control and Voluntary Voiding

Higher brain centers exert inhibitory and facilitatory control over the brainstem and sacral micturition centers. The frontal cortex and basal ganglia monitor bladder fullness and assess whether social and environmental conditions are appropriate for voiding. When a person decides to urinate, signals from the frontal cortex release the tonic inhibition maintained over the pontine micturition center. This green light initiates the reflex cascade, while voluntary relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles and the external sphincter allows urine to pass smoothly. If conditions are unsuitable, cortical pathways send excitatory signals to the pudendal nerve, reinforcing external sphincter contraction to override the urge.

Clinical Implications and Common Voiding Dysfunctions

Disruptions anywhere along the neural or muscular axis of micturition result in distinct clinical presentations. Neurogenic bladder dysfunction arises from conditions like stroke, spinal cord injury, or diabetic neuropathy, leading to either failure to store or failure to empty. Urinary retention often occurs when bladder outlet obstruction—such as benign prostatic hyperplasia—forces the detrusor muscle to hypertrophy against high resistance until it eventually fails. Conversely, detrusor overactivity produces urgency and urge incontinence. Clinical evaluation frequently involves urodynamic testing to measure pressure-volume relationships, helping nurses and physicians differentiate between obstructive and neurogenic etiologies.
