Kidneys

Gross Anatomy and Location of the Kidneys

The kidneys are a pair of reddish-brown, bean-shaped organs positioned retroperitoneally in the posterior abdominal wall, roughly between the twelfth thoracic and third lumbar vertebrae. Because the large right lobe of the liver sits directly above the right kidney, the right kidney is positioned slightly lower than the left. An adult human kidney measures about 10 to 12 centimeters in length, 5 to 7 centimeters in width, and roughly 3 centimeters in thickness.

A cross-section reveals two main internal regions: the outer renal cortex and the inner renal medulla. The renal medulla is divided into cone-shaped tissue masses known as renal pyramids. The tips of these pyramids, called renal papillae, drain urine into funnel-shaped structures termed minor and major calyces, which ultimately merge into the renal pelvis. From the renal pelvis, urine flows down the ureter to the urinary bladder. Surrounding the entire organ is a tough fibrous capsule that protects it from trauma and infection.

The Nephron: The Functional Unit

  • Renal Corpuscle: Composed of the glomerulus, a network of capillary loops, and Bowman's capsule, a double-walled epithelial cup that surrounds the glomerulus and catches the initial filtrate.
  • Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Lined with simple cuboidal epithelium featuring dense microvilli, creating a brush border that massively increases surface area for reabsorbing water, ions, and nutrients.
  • Loop of Henle: A U-shaped hairpins structure with a descending limb permeable to water and an ascending limb actively pumping out sodium and chloride ions.
  • Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Shorter than the PCT, responsible for fine-tuning electrolyte balance and secreting specific waste products under hormonal control.
  • Collecting Duct: Receives fluid from multiple nephrons and travels down toward the renal papilla, playing a major role in final water conservation.

Renal Blood Supply and Circulation

The kidneys receive a massive share of cardiac output—roughly 20 to 25 percent at rest—highlighting their critical filtering role. Blood enters directly from the abdominal aorta via the renal artery. As this artery enters the renal hilum, it branches progressively into segmental, interlobar, arcuate, and interlobular arteries.

From the interlobular arteries, tiny afferent arterioles feed into the capillary beds of the glomerulus. Unlike most capillary beds in the body that drain into venules, the glomerular capillaries drain into efferent arterioles. This unique arrangement allows the kidney to maintain high hydrostatic pressure inside the glomerulus to drive filtration. The efferent arteriole then branches into a second capillary network—the peritubular capillaries or the vasa recta—which surrounds the renal tubules to reabsorb and secrete substances before blood finally leaves via the renal vein.

Three Steps of Urine Formation

  1. Glomerular Filtration: Fluid and small solutes are forced out of the glomerular capillaries and into Bowman's capsule by hydrostatic pressure, creating a protein-free filtrate.
  2. Tubular Reabsorption: As the filtrate flows through the renal tubules, essential substances like glucose, amino acids, water, and vital ions are moved from the tubular lumen back into the peritubular capillary blood.
  3. Tubular Secretion: Additional waste products, excess hydrogen ions, potassium, and certain drugs are actively transported from the peritubular blood directly into the tubule to be eliminated in the final urine.

Fluid, Electrolyte, and Acid-Base Balance

The kidneys act as the body's master chemists, adjusting water and electrolyte output to match daily intake. Sodium, potassium, calcium, and phosphate levels are tightly regulated through hormonal feedback loops, particularly involving aldosterone and parathyroid hormone. When blood volume drops, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system triggers sodium and water retention in the distal tubules.

Acid-base regulation relies on the selective excretion of hydrogen ions and the reabsorption or generation of bicarbonate ions in the tubular fluid. If blood pH drops toward acidosis, the kidneys excrete more acid and hold onto bicarbonate to buffer the blood. Conversely, during alkalosis, bicarbonate is excreted in large amounts. While the lungs respond to pH changes within minutes, renal compensation is more powerful and takes hours to days to reach full effect.

Endocrine Functions of the Kidneys

Beyond filtration and waste removal, the kidneys function as endocrine organs releasing vital hormones into the bloodstream:

  • Erythropoietin (EPO): Released in response to low blood oxygen levels, stimulating bone marrow stem cells to produce red blood cells.
  • Renin: An enzymatic hormone released by juxtaglomerular cells when blood pressure falls, initiating the cascade that produces angiotensin II.
  • Calcitriol: The active hormonal form of vitamin D, synthesized by the kidneys to promote calcium absorption in the gastrointestinal tract.

Clinical Assessment and Laboratory Indicators

Evaluating renal health in clinical practice relies on a combination of patient history, physical examination, and diagnostic lab work. Clinicians frequently monitor serum creatinine and Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN). Creatinine is a breakdown product of muscle metabolism filtered almost exclusively by the glomerulus, making it a reliable marker for estimating the Glomerular Filtration Rate (GFR).

Urine analysis provides immediate clues regarding kidney status. The presence of protein (proteinuria) or red blood cells (hematuria) often points to glomerular membrane damage. A common pitfall in clinical assessment is relying solely on BUN, which can rise independently of kidney injury due to dehydration, high protein intake, or gastrointestinal bleeding.
