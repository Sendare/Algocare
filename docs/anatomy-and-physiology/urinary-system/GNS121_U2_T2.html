Ureters

Introduction to the Ureters and Their General Overview

The ureters are paired, muscular tubular structures that transport urine from the renal pelvis of the kidneys down to the urinary bladder. Each ureter measures approximately 25 to 30 centimeters in length in an adult and runs in a retroperitoneal position along the posterior abdominal wall. Their primary role is dynamic drainage rather than passive flow, ensuring waste fluids continuously move away from the upper urinary tract to prevent backpressure and kidney damage.

Anatomical Course and Key Constrictions

As the ureters descend toward the pelvis, they cross the pelvic brim and enter the lesser pelvis before obliquely piercing the posterior wall of the bladder. Along this path, they encounter three distinct anatomical narrowings where kidney stones often become lodged:

  • Ureteropelvic junction (UPJ): The point where the renal pelvis narrows into the proximal ureter.
  • Pelvic brim crossing: The location where the ureters cross over the common iliac artery bifurcation.
  • Vesicoureteral junction (VUJ): The site where the ureters pass obliquely through the muscular wall of the bladder.

Histology and Wall Layers

The wall of the ureter is composed of three distinct tissue layers that provide both strength and flexibility for propulsion:

  1. Mucosa: The innermost layer, lined by transitional epithelium that stretches easily as urine volume changes, resting on a lamina propria.
  2. Muscularis: Composed of inner longitudinal and outer circular smooth muscle layers in the upper ureters, with a third outer longitudinal layer appearing near the bladder.
  3. Adventitia: The outer fibrous connective tissue layer that anchors the ureter to surrounding retroperitoneal structures.

Physiology of Urine Transport

Urine movement through the ureters relies heavily on active smooth muscle contraction rather than simple gravity. Pacemaker cells in the renal pelvis initiate rhythmic peristaltic contractions every few seconds, sending urine jets into the bladder. The oblique angle at which the ureters enter the bladder acts as a one-way valve, preventing urine from flowing backward toward the kidneys when bladder pressure rises.

Blood Supply and Nerve Control

The ureters receive a rich, segmental blood supply from nearby vessels including the renal, gonadal, and internal iliac arteries, which run within the surrounding adventitia. Venous drainage generally mirrors the arterial supply. Autonomic nerve fibers from nearby plexuses innervate the smooth muscle and transmit visceral pain sensations, which are typically referred to the lower back and groin during renal colic.

Clinical Conditions Affecting the Ureters

Pathologies involving the ureters often stem from obstruction or congenital abnormalities. Urolithiasis, or kidney stones, can cause severe flank pain known as renal colic when lodged in a ureteral constriction. Vesicoureteral reflux occurs when the one-way valve mechanism fails, allowing urine to wash back up toward the kidneys and predisposing the patient to recurrent urinary tract infections and potential renal scarring.
