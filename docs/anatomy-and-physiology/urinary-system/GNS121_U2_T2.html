Ureters

Introduction and Anatomical Overview of the Ureters

The ureters are paired, muscular, thick-walled tubular structures responsible for transporting urine from the renal pelves of the kidneys down to the urinary bladder. Each tube measures approximately 25 to 30 centimeters in length in an adult, though this varies slightly depending on individual body height and pelvic dimensions. Originating at the ureteropelvic junction (UPJ) near the lower pole of each kidney, they descend retroperitoneally along the posterior abdominal wall, crossing the pelvic brim to enter the pelvic cavity before inserting obliquely into the base of the bladder. Their primary function is not merely passive drainage, but an active, rhythmic propulsion of liquid waste that prevents urinary stasis and subsequent ascending infection.

Anatomical Course and Important Abdominal Relations

Throughout their descent, the ureters lie retroperitoneally, resting directly upon the anterior surface of the psoas major muscles. As they travel inferiorly, they cross anterior to the common iliac or external iliac vessels to enter the true pelvis.

In biological females, a uniquely critical relationship exists within the broad ligament of the uterus: the ureter passes directly inferior to the uterine artery, famously remembered by the anatomical mnemonic 'water under the bridge.' Accidental ligation or transection of the ureter during total abdominal hysterectomy is a well-documented surgical hazard due to this close proximity.

Histological Layers of the Ureter Wall

  • Mucosa: Lined by transitional epithelium (urothelium) resting on a lamina propria. This specialized epithelium accommodates stretching and provides an impermeable barrier against toxic urine components.
  • Muscularis: Composed of inner longitudinal and outer circular smooth muscle layers in the upper two-thirds, with an additional outer longitudinal layer in the lower third. This robust arrangement generates coordinated peristalsis.
  • Adventitia: An outer layer of fibroareolar connective tissue that anchors the ureter to surrounding retroperitoneal structures and contains its blood and nerve supply.

Physiology of Urine Transport: Peristalsis and Valves

Urine transport relies primarily on active peristaltic contractions initiated by pacemaker cells located within the minor calyces and renal pelvis. These rhythmic contractions generate wave-like movements that force urine down the tubular lumen at rates of one to five waves per minute, independent of posture or gravity.

As the ureter approaches the bladder, it pierces the muscular wall at an oblique angle. This tunnel-like intramural passage creates a physiological one-way valve mechanism. When the bladder fills and internal pressure rises, the ureteral walls are compressed shut, preventing the dangerous backflow of urine—a condition known as vesicoureteral reflux.

Vascular Supply and Innervation

Blood supply to the ureters is exceptionally segmental, arriving via branches from multiple neighboring vessels depending on the anatomical level. The upper ureter receives blood from the renal arteries, the middle section from the gonadal (testicular or ovarian) arteries and the abdominal aorta, and the pelvic portion from the internal iliac, superior vesical, and middle rectal arteries.

Venous blood mirrors this pattern, draining into corresponding renal, gonadal, and internal iliac veins. Autonomic innervation is supplied by both sympathetic and parasympathetic fibers forming the renal, aortic, and hypogastric plexuses, which modulate peristaltic frequency and transmit visceral pain sensations during obstruction.

Clinical Significance: Anatomical Constrictions and Calculi

The ureter features three notable physiological narrowings along its course where kidney stones (renal calculi or ureterolithiasis) frequently lodge, causing intense loin-to-groin pain and urinary obstruction:

  1. Ureteropelvic Junction (UPJ): Where the renal pelvis transitions into the proximal ureter.
  2. Pelvic Brim: Where the ureter crosses anterior to the common iliac vessels.
  3. Vesicoureteral Junction (UVJ): Where the ureter pierces the wall of the urinary bladder.

When a calculus becomes impacted at any of these sites, smooth muscle spasm occurs above the obstruction, generating severe renal colic that radiates characteristically into the lower abdomen and genitalia.
