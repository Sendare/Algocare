Functions of Hormones

Introduction to Hormonal Communication in the Body

The endocrine system acts as the body's chemical messaging network, coordinating complex physiological processes over extended periods. Unlike the rapid, electrical nerve impulses of the nervous system, hormones travel via the bloodstream to reach distant target cells. These chemical messengers influence virtually every cell, organ, and function of the human body, maintaining internal stability despite external environmental changes. - **Endocrine Signaling**: Hormones are secreted directly into the extracellular fluid, diffuse into local blood vessels, and circulate systemically. - **Paracrine Signaling**: Chemical messengers affect neighboring cells without entering the bloodstream. - **Autocrine Signaling**: A cell releases a chemical that binds to receptors on its own surface, altering its own activity.

Mechanism of Hormone Action and Receptor Binding

Hormones circulate throughout the entire body, yet they only affect specific target cells. This selectivity relies entirely on specialized protein receptors. When a hormone encounters a cell possessing its specific receptor, binding occurs, triggering a cascade of intracellular events. - **Water-Soluble Hormones**: These include peptide and amine hormones that cannot cross the lipid bilayer. They bind to extracellular membrane receptors, activating second messenger systems such as cyclic AMP (cAMP) inside the cell. - **Lipid-Soluble Hormones**: Steroid and thyroid hormones easily diffuse across the plasma membrane. They bind to intracellular receptors located in the cytoplasm or nucleus, directly altering gene transcription and protein synthesis.

Regulation of Metabolism and Energy Balance

Cellular metabolism is tightly governed by hormonal inputs that determine whether the body stores nutrients or breaks them down for immediate energy. The thyroid gland, pancreas, and adrenal glands are primary drivers of this continuous metabolic balancing act. Insulin, secreted by the beta cells of the pancreatic islets, promotes the uptake of glucose into muscle and adipose tissue, lowering blood sugar after a meal. Conversely, glucagon stimulates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver when blood glucose levels drop. Thyroid hormones—thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3)—diffuse into cells to elevate basal metabolic rate, increasing oxygen consumption and heat production across nearly all body tissues.

Fluid Balance, Electrolyte Regulation, and Blood Pressure

Maintaining internal hydration and ion concentrations requires a sophisticated hormonal network acting primarily on the kidneys. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH), released from the posterior pituitary, promotes water reabsorption in the renal collecting ducts, concentrating urine and preventing dehydration. When blood pressure or blood sodium drops, the kidneys release renin, initiating the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). Aldosterone acts on the distal tubules of the nephron to reabsorb sodium and water while excreting potassium, which expands blood volume and raises systemic blood pressure.

Growth, Development, and Cellular Reproduction

Physical maturation and tissue growth depend heavily on the synergistic actions of growth hormone (GH), thyroid hormones, and insulin-like growth factors. Growth hormone, produced by the anterior pituitary, stimulates protein synthesis, cell division, and elongation of long bones during childhood and adolescence. Beyond childhood stature, hormones continue to regulate tissue repair, bone remodeling, and cellular turnover throughout adulthood. Disruption in this delicate balance leads to distinct clinical abnormalities, such as gigantism when hypersecreted before epiphyseal plate closure, or acromegaly when excess GH occurs in adulthood.

Stress Response and Adaptation

When the body encounters physical or psychological stress, it mounts an immediate and coordinated hormonal defense. The acute phase is mediated by the sympathetic nervous system and the adrenal medulla, which release epinephrine and norepinephrine into the bloodstream. This triggers the classic fight-or-flight response: increased heart rate, dilated airways, and redirected blood flow to skeletal muscles. For prolonged stress, the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis takes over. Cortisol, the primary glucocorticoid, is released from the adrenal cortex. Cortisol mobilizes energy stores by stimulating gluconeogenesis, breaking down muscle protein, and releasing fatty acids, while simultaneously suppressing non-essential functions like immune responses and inflammation.

Feedback Loops and Endocrine Homeostasis

Endocrine glands do not operate unchecked; they rely on feedback mechanisms to maintain stability and prevent runaway hormone production. Negative feedback is the primary regulatory mechanism in the endocrine system. When hormone levels rise above a set point, the target organ response or the hormone itself inhibits further release from the governing gland. Positive feedback is far less common because it amplifies changes rather than reversing them. A classic physiological example occurs during childbirth, where uterine contractions stimulate oxytocin release, which in turn causes stronger contractions until delivery is complete.
