Tissues and Membranes

Introduction and Characteristics of Body Tissues

A tissue is not just a random collection of cells. It is a group of similar cells working together to perform a specific, shared job in the body. If you look under a microscope, you will see that these cells are often held closely together by intercellular junctions or embedded in a non-living material called the extracellular matrix.

The human body is built from millions of these cells, but they are organized into teams. The study of these tissues is called histology. Without tissues, organs like the heart or stomach could not divide their workload. Understanding how cells group together helps nurses visualize how injuries happen and how the body repairs itself after trauma or surgery.

The Four Primary Tissue Types

All organs in the human body are constructed from combinations of just four primary tissue types. Each type has a distinct structure tailored to its overall job.

  • Epithelial tissue: Covers body surfaces, lines hollow organs and cavities, and forms glands. It acts as a protective barrier and manages absorption and secretion.
  • Connective tissue: The most abundant and widely distributed tissue type. It binds structures together, supports the body, protects delicate organs, and stores fat.
  • Muscle tissue: Specialized for contraction. It shortens forcefully to produce movement of body parts, pump blood, and move food through the gut.
  • Nervous tissue: Composed of neurons and supporting cells. It generates and transmits electrical signals quickly across the body to coordinate activities.

Epithelial Tissue: Structure and Classification

Epithelium forms the outer layer of the skin and lines internal passageways like the respiratory and digestive tracts. Because these surfaces face harsh environments, epithelial cells fit tightly together to form continuous sheets. They have two distinct surfaces: an apical surface that faces the open space or outside world, and a basal surface attached to a basement membrane.

Epithelial tissues are classified using two main features: the number of cell layers and the shape of the cells.

Connective Tissue Diversity

Connective tissue binds body parts together and provides structural support. Unlike epithelium, connective tissues usually have a rich blood supply and are made mostly of extracellular matrix rather than tightly packed cells. The matrix can be liquid, gel-like, or rigid.

Important types include bone with its hard calcified matrix, blood with its fluid plasma matrix, adipose tissue for fat storage and insulation, and fibrous connective tissue found in tendons and ligaments. A common clinical misconception is that all connective tissues heal quickly; in reality, structures like tendons and ligaments have poor blood supplies and take a very long time to repair after an injury.

Muscle and Nervous Tissues

Muscle and nervous tissues are known as the excitable tissues because they can change their electrical state to perform their functions. Muscle cells contract when stimulated. There are three types: skeletal muscle (voluntary, striated, attached to bones), cardiac muscle (involuntary, striated, found only in the heart wall), and smooth muscle (involuntary, non-striated, found in the walls of hollow internal organs like the stomach and blood vessels).

Nervous tissue consists of neurons, which transmit nerve impulses across long distances, and glial cells, which protect, nourish, and support the neurons. Together, these tissues allow the human body to sense its environment and react swiftly.

Classification and Functions of Body Membranes

Body membranes are thin sheets of tissue that cover surfaces, line body cavities, and surround organs. They are generally grouped into two major categories: epithelial membranes and connective tissue membranes.

Epithelial membranes combine an epithelial layer with an underlying connective tissue layer:

  • Mucous membranes (Mucosae): Line body cavities that open to the outside world, such as the digestive and respiratory tracts. They secrete mucus to keep surfaces moist and trap foreign particles.
  • Serous membranes (Serosae): Line closed internal body cavities that do not open to the exterior, like the thoracic and abdominopelvic cavities. They secrete a watery fluid that reduces friction between moving organs.
  • Cutaneous membrane: The skin, which covers and protects the body surface.

Connective tissue membranes contain only connective tissue without any epithelium. A primary example is synovial membranes, which line the joint cavities and secrete lubricating fluid to ease joint movement.

Clinical Implications for Nursing Practice

Understanding tissues and membranes is vital for clinical nursing. When a patient develops a pressure ulcer (bedsore), epithelial and connective tissues break down due to prolonged pressure and restricted blood flow. Recognizing how different tissues heal guides wound care and nutritional management.

Furthermore, nurses must monitor fluid shifts across serous and mucous membranes. For instance, an infection in a serous membrane leads to fluid accumulation, a condition known as effusion. Knowing which tissues regenerate easily—such as epithelial tissue—versus those that heal by forming scar tissue—like cardiac muscle—helps nurses explain prognoses to patients and anticipate long-term recovery needs.
