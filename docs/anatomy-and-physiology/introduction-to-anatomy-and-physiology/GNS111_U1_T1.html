Organization of the Human Body

Introduction to Anatomy and Physiology

To understand how the human body works, we must study its structure and function. Anatomy is the study of body structures and their physical relationships to one another. For example, looking at how bones connect to form a joint is an anatomical study. Physiology, on the other hand, looks at how those structures actually work to keep the body alive. While anatomy deals with the body's physical design, physiology deals with chemistry, physics, and biological processes. These two disciplines are inseparable because structure determines function; a bone is shaped the way it is so it can support weight and protect delicate organs inside.

Levels of Structural Organization

The human body is organized in a clear hierarchy, building from the smallest chemical building blocks up to a complex, functioning living being.

  1. Chemical Level: Atoms combine to form molecules like water, proteins, and DNA.
  2. Cellular Level: Molecules group together to form the basic living units of the body, known as cells.
  3. Tissue Level: Similar cells with a common function group together to form tissues, such as muscle tissue.
  4. Organ Level: Two or more tissue types combine to form an organ, like the stomach or heart.
  5. Organ System Level: Multiple organs work closely together to achieve a common health goal, such as the digestive system.
  6. Organismal Level: All the organ systems work together to sustain the life of the whole human body.

Characteristics of Living Human Organisms

Living human beings display several essential life processes that separate them from non-living matter. Metabolism includes all chemical reactions taking place in the body, combining the breakdown of nutrients with the building of new structures. Responsiveness is the ability to sense changes in the internal or external environment and react to them quickly. Movement involves not just walking, but also the internal transport of blood, food, and cellular materials. Growth, reproduction, and differentiation—where unspecialized cells become specialized tissues—are also vital life functions. Finally, excretion removes metabolic waste products so they do not poison the body.

Anatomical Terminology and Body Planes

Medical communication requires a standard point of reference. The anatomical position describes a person standing upright, facing forward, with upper limbs at the sides and palms facing forward. When describing body locations, terms like anterior (front) and posterior (back), or superior (toward the head) and inferior (away from the head) keep healthcare notes clear and accurate.

To view internal structures, the body can be sliced along imaginary flat surfaces called planes:

  • Sagittal plane: Divides the body vertically into right and left parts.
  • Frontal plane: Divides the body vertically into front and back parts.
  • Transverse plane: Divides the body horizontally into upper and lower parts.

Body Cavities and Membranes

The human body is internally divided into protected spaces called cavities that house delicate organs. The two main divisions are the dorsal body cavity, which contains the cranial cavity and spinal column, and the ventral body cavity, which contains the thoracic and abdominopelvic cavities. The thoracic cavity holds the heart and lungs, protected by the ribcage, while the abdominopelvic cavity contains digestive and reproductive organs.

Delicate organs inside the ventral cavity are wrapped in thin sheets of tissue called serous membranes. These membranes produce a slippery fluid that reduces friction when organs move, such as when the lungs expand during breathing or the heart beats.

Homeostasis and Control Systems

Maintaining a stable internal environment despite changing external conditions is known as homeostasis. The body achieves this through continuous feedback loops. A negative feedback loop works to reverse a directional change, bringing the body back to its normal set point—this is how body temperature and blood sugar are regulated. If body temperature rises, negative feedback triggers sweating to cool the skin down.

Conversely, a positive feedback loop enhances or accelerates the original change rather than reversing it. This occurs during processes like blood clotting or childbirth, where a response must push an event to completion before stopping.
