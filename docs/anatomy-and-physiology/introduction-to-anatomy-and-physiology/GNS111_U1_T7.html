Human Genetics

Introduction to Human Genetics and DNA Structure

Genetics is the study of heredity and how biological traits are passed from parents to offspring. At the core of this science is DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid), the master molecule holding the instructions for building and maintaining the human body. DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder, a structure known as a double helix.

The rungs of this ladder consist of four nitrogenous bases:

  • Adenine (A) always pairs with Thymine (T)
  • Cytosine (C) always pairs with Guanine (G)

This specific pairing rule is the foundation of genetic replication. Every human cell contains this complete code, wrapped tightly into structures called chromosomes to fit inside the microscopic cell nucleus.

Chromosomes, Genes, and the Human Genome

Inside almost every human cell, DNA is organized into compact packages called chromosomes. Normal somatic cells contain 46 chromosomes, arranged in 23 pairs. Half of these come from the biological mother and the other half from the father. The first 22 pairs are called autosomes, while the 23rd pair determines biological sex: XX for females and XY for males.

A gene is a specific segment of DNA along a chromosome that codes for a particular protein, determining traits like eye color or blood type. The complete set of genetic material in an organism is called the human genome.

Mendelian Inheritance and Punnett Squares

Gregor Mendel discovered the basic rules of inheritance using pea plants, which also apply to many human traits and single-gene disorders. Traits are determined by alleles, which are different versions of the same gene. Dominant alleles express their trait even if only one copy is present, while recessive alleles require two copies to show up physically.

A Punnett square is a simple grid tool nurses and geneticists use to predict the probability of an offspring inheriting a specific trait. It maps out the parental alleles on the top and side, combining them in the grid boxes to reveal all possible genetic outcomes for the children.

Non-Mendelian Inheritance Patterns

Not all human traits follow simple dominant and recessive rules. Incomplete dominance occurs when neither allele is fully dominant, resulting in a blended phenotype, such as wavy hair. In codominance, both alleles are expressed equally and independently, like the AB blood type where both A and B proteins show up on red blood cells.

Other traits are polygenic, meaning they are controlled by the combined interaction of multiple genes working together. Height, skin color, and eye color are classic examples of polygenic traits, creating a wide spectrum of physical appearances rather than strict categories.

Chromosomal Abnormalities and Mutations

A mutation is a permanent alteration in the DNA sequence that makes up a gene. While some mutations are harmless or repaired by the cell, others cause structural changes. Chromosomal abnormalities often happen during cell division when chromosomes fail to separate correctly, a process known as non-disjunction.

Non-disjunction can lead to cells having an abnormal number of chromosomes. For instance, having an extra chromosome is called trisomy, while missing one is called monosomy. These major numerical shifts frequently result in significant physical and developmental challenges for the affected individual.

Common Genetic Disorders in Clinical Practice

Nurses frequently care for patients living with inherited or acquired genetic conditions. Down syndrome, caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (Trisomy 21), presents with characteristic facial features and varying degrees of cognitive delay.

Other notable conditions include sickle cell anemia, an autosomal recessive blood disorder that alters hemoglobin structure, and cystic fibrosis, which affects mucus production in the lungs and digestive system. Recognizing these patterns helps nurses anticipate complications, provide proper care, and support affected families.

Genetic Counseling and Nursing Assessment

Nurses play a vital role in taking detailed family health histories to identify clients at risk for hereditary conditions. A thorough genogram (family tree) helps track diseases across multiple generations. When genetic risks are identified, nurses often refer patients for genetic counseling.

Genetic counselors help families understand diagnostic test results, recurrence risks, and management options without making decisions for them. Nurses provide emotional support, reinforce educational information provided by the specialist team, and advocate for patient choices during emotionally difficult diagnostic processes.
