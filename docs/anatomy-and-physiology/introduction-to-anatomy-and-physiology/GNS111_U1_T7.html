Human Genetics

Introduction to Human Genetics and DNA Structure

Genetics is the foundational study of heredity and variation in living organisms. At the core of this discipline is **deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)**, the macromolecule that stores hereditary instructions for building and maintaining human tissues. DNA is organized as a double helix composed of repeating nucleotide units. Each nucleotide contains a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine, thymine, cytosine, and guanine. Base pairing follows strict biochemical rules. Adenine exclusively binds to thymine via two hydrogen bonds, while cytosine pairs with guanine through three hydrogen bonds. This complementary pairing ensures accurate replication during cell division and preserves genetic fidelity across generations. When genetic information is expressed, a segment of DNA is transcribed into ribonucleic acid (RNA), which is subsequently translated into functional proteins responsible for cellular structure and enzymatic activity.

Chromosomes, Genes, and the Human Genome

The human genome comprises approximately 3 billion base pairs of DNA packed tightly into the nucleus of somatic cells. This massive amount of data is organized into **chromosomes**, discrete structures formed by DNA tightly wound around histone proteins. Human somatic cells contain 46 chromosomes, arranged into 23 homologous pairs. Twenty-two pairs are autosomes, while the final pair consists of the sex chromosomes—XX in females and XY in males. A **gene** is a specific functional locus along a chromosome that codes for a polypeptide or functional RNA molecule. Although humans possess tens of thousands of genes, only a small fraction of the total genome directly codes for proteins; the remainder includes regulatory sequences, structural elements, and non-coding regions. Variations in gene sequences among individuals account for inherited diversity and phenotypic differences.

Mendelian Inheritance and Punnett Squares

Gregor Mendel established the fundamental laws of inheritance through his experiments with pea plants, which laid the groundwork for modern genetics. Traits are determined by **alleles**, which are alternative forms of a gene. When an individual inherits two identical alleles for a characteristic, they are **homozygous**; inheriting two different alleles makes them **heterozygous**. - **Dominant allele**: Expressed phenotypically even when paired with a recessive allele, typically represented by an uppercase letter. - **Recessive allele**: Masked in the presence of a dominant allele and only expressed when homozygous, represented by a lowercase letter. - **Genotype**: The exact genetic constitution of an organism. - **Phenotype**: The observable physical or biochemical characteristics resulting from the genotype and environment. Clinicians use **Punnett squares** as a predictive grid tool to calculate the probability of offspring inheriting specific genotypes based on parental allele combinations.

Non-Mendelian Inheritance Patterns

While many traits follow strict dominant and recessive rules, several genetic traits display complex inheritance patterns that deviate from classic Mendelian ratios. - **Incomplete dominance**: The heterozygous phenotype is an intermediate blend of the two homozygous phenotypes, such as pink flowers resulting from red and white parent crosses. - **Codominance**: Both alleles are fully and simultaneously expressed in the heterozygote, as seen in the AB blood group system where A and B antigens are both present. - **Polygenic inheritance**: A single phenotypic trait is controlled by the additive effect of multiple genes, resulting in continuous variation like human height or skin color. - **X-linked recessive inheritance**: Traits caused by mutant genes on the X chromosome, affecting males more frequently because they possess only a single X chromosome.

Chromosomal Abnormalities and Mutations

Mutations represent permanent alterations in the DNA nucleotide sequence. They can arise spontaneously during replication or be induced by environmental mutagens like radiation and chemical agents. Structural chromosomal changes include deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations, where segments of chromosomes break and rearrange abnormally. Numerical abnormalities usually stem from **nondisjunction**, the failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during meiosis. This error generates gametes with an abnormal number of chromosomes. Fertilization involving these aberrant gametes leads to conditions such as **monosomy** (missing a chromosome) or **trisomy** (possessing an extra chromosome). Routine laboratory karyotyping allows cytogeneticists to visualize chromosomes and identify these large-scale structural and numerical anomalies.

Common Genetic Disorders in Clinical Practice

Healthcare professionals frequently encounter genetic disorders that manifest across pediatric and adult care settings. Understanding their pathophysiological basis aids in clinical management and anticipatory guidance. - **Down syndrome (Trisomy 21)**: Characterized by an extra copy of chromosome 21, resulting in characteristic facial features, intellectual disability, and increased risk for congenital heart defects. - **Sickle cell anemia**: An autosomal recessive disorder caused by a single nucleotide substitution in the beta-globin gene, producing abnormal hemoglobin that causes red blood cells to sickle under low oxygen tension. - **Cystic fibrosis**: An autosomal recessive condition affecting chloride ion transport, leading to thick, viscous mucus accumulation in the respiratory and digestive tracts. - **Turner syndrome**: A sex chromosome abnormality where phenotypic females are missing all or part of one X chromosome (45,X), leading to short stature and gonadal dysgenesis.

Genetic Counseling and Nursing Assessment

Nurses play an instrumental role in identifying hereditary risk factors and supporting families navigating genetic conditions. A comprehensive genetic assessment begins with constructing a detailed **pedigree chart** spanning at least three generations to trace the transmission of inherited traits or disorders. Key components of genetic nursing assessment include evaluating family medical history, identifying red flags such as early-onset cancer or multiple miscarriages, and providing psychological support. When genetic risks are identified, nurses facilitate referrals to specialized genetic counselors. These counselors help patients understand diagnostic testing options, interpret complex risk probabilities, and make informed reproductive or medical decisions without coercion.
