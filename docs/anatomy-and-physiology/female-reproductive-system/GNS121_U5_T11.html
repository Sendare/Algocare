Pelvic Floor

Anatomical Boundaries and Structure

The pelvic floor forms the inferior boundary of the abdominopelvic cavity, stretching like a muscular hammock from the pubic bone anteriorly to the coccyx posteriorly, and laterally to the ischial tuberosities. This funnel-shaped structure separates the true pelvis above from the perineum below. It features two primary gaps: the anterior urogenital hiatus, which transmits the urethra and vagina, and the posterior rectoanal hiatus for the anal canal. Understanding these bony and muscular boundaries is essential because increased intra-abdominal pressure—frequently seen in chronic cough, heavy lifting, or repeated childbirth—pushes directly downward against this structural sling.

Muscles of the Pelvic Diaphragm

The pelvic diaphragm is the primary muscular layer supporting the pelvic organs. It consists mainly of the levator ani muscle group and the coccygeus muscle.

  • Pubococcygeus: The most medial and dynamic portion of the levator ani, wrapping around the urethra, vagina, and rectum to maintain continence.
  • Iliococcygeus: The posterolateral portion that arises from the arcus tendineus levatoris ani and inserts into the coccyx and anococcygeal raphe.
  • Puborectalis: Forms a crucial U-shaped muscular sling behind the anorectal junction, creating the acute anorectal angle necessary for fecal continence.
  • Coccygeus: Located posteriorly, extending from the ischial spine to the lateral margin of the sacrum and coccyx, assisting in supporting the pelvic viscera.

Fascial Layers and Ligamentous Supports

Muscles alone cannot maintain pelvic organ position against gravity and intra-abdominal pressure; they rely heavily on endopelvic fascia and specialized condensations of connective tissue. The endopelvic fascia suspends the uterus and vagina from the bony pelvic walls. Key supports include the pubocervical fascia supporting the bladder anteriorly, the rectovaginal fascia supporting the rectum posteriorly, and the cardinal and uterosacral ligaments anchoring the cervix and upper vagina. When these fascial attachments tear or stretch—often during difficult vaginal deliveries—organs lose their suspension and begin to descend into the vaginal canal.

Physiological Functions

The pelvic floor is a versatile muscular system serving four critical physiological roles: support, continence, reproduction, and sexual function.

  1. Support: Elevates and maintains pelvic viscera in their correct anatomical positions.
  2. Continence: Maintains tonic contraction of urethral and anal sphincters at rest, relaxing voluntarily only during elimination.
  3. Reproduction: Provides a rigid yet flexible canal that directs the fetal head downward during parturition.
  4. Sexual Function: Contributes to orgasmic response through rhythmic, involuntary contractions of the superficial and deep perineal muscles.

Neuromuscular Control and Blood Supply

Precise neural control is required for the pelvic floor to alternate between resting continence and active relaxation. The primary nerve supply originates from the pudendal nerve (sacral roots S2-S4), which provides somatic motor and sensory innervation to the perineum and external sphincters. Direct branches from the sacral plexus also innervate the levator ani muscles. Vascular supply is delivered predominantly by branches of the internal iliac artery, including the inferior vesical, middle rectal, and internal pudendal arteries, ensuring rich perfusion to these metabolically active postural muscles.

Common Dysfunctions and Pathologies

Pelvic floor dysfunction manifests in two main forms: hypotonicity (weakness) and hypertonicity (excessive tension). Hypotonicity leads to pelvic organ prolapse—such as cystocele, rectocele, or uterine prolapse—and stress urinary incontinence. Conversely, hypertonic muscles fail to relax properly, resulting in dyspareunia (painful intercourse), chronic pelvic pain syndrome, and obstructive voiding or constipation. Obstetric trauma, chronic straining, aging, and surgical interventions are primary contributing factors to these pathological states.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Evaluation

Assessing the pelvic floor requires a sensitive, comprehensive nursing approach that balances clinical thoroughness with patient privacy. The nurse begins by taking a detailed history regarding urinary leakage, bowel habits, sexual comfort, and obstetric background. During physical evaluation, inspection checks for visible prolapse, skin integrity, and atrophy. Digital palpation—often performed by trained clinicians or specialized nurses—assesses resting muscle tone, voluntary contraction strength using the Oxford grading scale, and the patient's ability to isolate pelvic floor muscles without contracting abdominal or gluteal muscle groups.

Nursing Management and Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation of the pelvic floor focuses on restoring muscle strength, endurance, and coordination. Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), commonly known as Kegel exercises, is the first-line non-surgical intervention. Nurses play a vital role in teaching correct technique, ensuring patients isolate the levator ani rather than bearing down. For patients unable to contract these muscles voluntarily, biofeedback, electrical stimulation, and vaginal cones serve as effective adjuncts. Lifestyle modifications—such as weight management, treating chronic cough, and avoiding constipation—are also reinforced to reduce ongoing downward strain.
