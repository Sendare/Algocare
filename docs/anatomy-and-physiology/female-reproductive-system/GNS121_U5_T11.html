Pelvic Floor

Anatomical Boundaries and Structure of the Pelvic Floor

The pelvic floor sits at the base of the abdominopelvic cavity, forming a supportive basin that holds the pelvic organs in place. Picture a strong, muscular hammock stretching across the bottom of the pelvis. Its boundaries are defined by bony landmarks: the pubic symphysis anteriorly, the ischial tuberosities laterally, and the coccyx posteriorly. This anatomical arrangement allows the structure to counteract downward pressure from the abdomen while maintaining openings for the urethra, vagina, and rectum.

Muscles of the Pelvic Diaphragm

The main muscular component of the pelvic floor is the pelvic diaphragm, a funnel-shaped structure primarily made up of the levator ani and coccygeus muscles.

  • Levator ani: This is a broad, continuous sheet of muscle divided into three main parts: the pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, and puborectalis. Together, they wrap tightly around the urethra, vagina, and anal canal to maintain closure.
  • Puborectalis: Acts like a sling around the rectum, creating an angle that helps maintain fecal continence.
  • Coccygeus: Located posteriorly, this smaller muscle assists the levator ani in supporting the pelvic viscera and pulling the coccyx forward.

Fascia and Ligamentous Support

Muscles alone cannot hold pelvic organs in position; they rely heavily on a complex network of pelvic fascia and ligaments. The endopelvic fascia wraps around the bladder, uterus, and rectum, anchoring them securely to the pelvic walls. Key condensations of this fascia, such as the uterosacral and cardinal ligaments, prevent uterine prolapse by anchoring the cervix and upper vagina to the sacrum and lateral pelvic walls. When these connective tissues stretch or tear during childbirth, the organs begin to descend.

Physiological Functions of the Pelvic Floor

A healthy pelvic floor performs several vital day-to-day functions beyond basic organ support:

  • Continence: It maintains resting tone around the urethral and anal sphincters to prevent involuntary leakage of urine and stool.
  • Voiding and Defecation: The muscles must relax completely to allow normal urination and bowel movements.
  • Sexual Function: Rhythmic contractions of these muscles enhance sexual sensation and orgasmic response.
  • Core Stability: It works alongside the diaphragm, transversus abdominis, and deep back muscles to stabilize the core and manage internal pressure during lifting.

Nerves and Blood Supply

The primary nerve supply to the pelvic floor comes from the pudendal nerve, which originates from the sacral plexus (S2 to S4). This nerve provides both motor control to the urethral and anal sphincters and sensory feedback from the perineum. Blood is supplied mainly by branches of the internal iliac artery, ensuring high metabolic activity for muscles that must maintain constant resting tone.

Common Pelvic Floor Dysfunctions

Pelvic floor disorders occur when muscles and connective tissues become either too weak or too tight. Weakness often leads to pelvic organ prolapse, where the bladder (cystocele), uterus, or rectum (rectocele) bulges into the vagina. It also causes stress urinary incontinence. Conversely, hypertonic or overly tight pelvic floor muscles can cause chronic pelvic pain, painful intercourse, and difficulty with urination or bowel emptying.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Evaluation

Assessing the pelvic floor requires a sensitive, thorough nursing approach. Nurses begin with a detailed health history, screening for urinary leakage, bowel difficulties, pain during intercourse, and a feeling of pelvic heaviness. During physical examination, the clinician evaluates muscle strength, endurance, and coordination through palpation. Asking patients about lifestyle factors like chronic coughing, heavy lifting, or constipation helps identify root causes of ongoing strain.

Nursing Management and Rehabilitation

Nursing care for pelvic floor dysfunction focuses on restoring strength, coordination, and function. Pelvic floor muscle training, commonly known as Kegel exercises, is the cornerstone of non-surgical management. Nurses teach patients how to isolate the correct muscles without squeezing their thighs, buttocks, or holding their breath. For patients with tight muscles, relaxation techniques and biofeedback are utilized. When conservative methods fail, patients may be referred for surgical repair.
