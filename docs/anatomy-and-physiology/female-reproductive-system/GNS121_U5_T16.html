Breasts

Introduction to Breast Anatomy and Location

The breasts, medically known as the mammary glands, sit on the anterior chest wall. In adult humans, they extend vertically from the second to the sixth ribs and horizontally from the lateral border of the sternum to the mid-axillary line. Resting primarily over the pectoralis major muscle, about two-thirds of the breast tissue lies on this muscle, while the remaining portion extends toward the armpit.

Gross Anatomy and Surface Landmarks

On the external surface, the prominent pigmented area is the areola, which contains small sebaceous prominences called Montgomery glands that lubricate the nipple during lactation. Centrally positioned within the areola is the nipple, a cylindrical elevation containing multiple tiny openings where milk exits. The color of the areola varies from pink to dark brown, often darkening permanently during pregnancy.

Internal Structure: Glandular Tissue and Lobes

Inside the breast, the functional tissue is arranged into 15 to 20 segments called lobes. Each lobe radiates outward from the nipple like slices of a pie. These lobes are further subdivided into smaller units called lobules, which contain the milk-producing alveoli. Surrounding these glandular structures is a cushion of adipose, or fatty, tissue that determines the overall size and shape of the breast.

The Lactiferous Duct System

Milk travels from the producing alveoli through a branching network of tubes.

  • Alveoli: Microscopic milk-secreting sacs lined with secretory cells.
  • Lactiferous ducts: Larger tubes that collect milk from each lobe.
  • Lactiferous sinus: A dilated region near the base of the nipple where milk temporarily pools before release.
  • Nipple pores: The tiny external openings at the tip of the nipple.

Supporting Structures: Cooper's Ligaments

The breast is supported against gravity by suspensory ligaments of the breast, commonly known as Cooper's ligaments. These fibrous connective tissue bands run from the deep fascia of the chest wall, through the breast tissue, and attach firmly to the dermis of the skin. Over time, stretching or weakening of these ligaments can lead to sagging, which is a normal part of aging or post-lactation changes.

Blood Supply, Lymphatic Drainage, and Innervation

Blood reaches the breasts primarily through branches of the internal mammary artery and the lateral thoracic artery. Drainage of lymph fluid is critically important in nursing because about 75% of the lymph from the breast flows toward the axillary lymph nodes in the armpit, serving as the main pathway for the spread of breast cancer cells. Sensation is supplied by cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves.

Physiological Changes Across the Lifespan

Breast tissue transforms across different life stages. At birth, both male and female infants may have temporarily enlarged breasts due to maternal hormones passing through the placenta. During puberty, rising estrogen stimulates duct growth and fat deposition. Throughout adult reproductive years, cyclical hormonal fluctuations cause monthly swelling and tenderness. After menopause, declining estrogen causes the glandular tissue to shrink and get replaced by fat.

Hormonal Regulation of Lactation

Two main hormones drive milk production and release after childbirth. Prolactin, secreted by the anterior pituitary gland, stimulates the alveolar cells to manufacture milk. Meanwhile, oxytocin, released from the posterior pituitary gland in response to suckling, causes the tiny muscles around the alveoli to squeeze, forcing milk down the ducts in what is known as the let-down reflex.
