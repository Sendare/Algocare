Breasts

Anatomical Location and Surface Landmarks

The human breasts, or mammary glands, sit on the anterior thoracic wall, predominantly overlying the pectoral muscles. Anatomically, they extend vertically from the second to the sixth ribs and horizontally from the lateral border of the sternum to the mid-axillary line. - **Base**: Rests primarily on the pectoralis major muscle and partly on the serratus anterior. - **Retromammary space**: A loose connective tissue plane between the breast and deep pectoral fascia, allowing the normal breast to move freely over the chest wall. Fixation of the breast in this space often signals deep malignant invasion. - **Nipple (Papilla)**: A cylindrical prominence located near the center of the breast, containing the openings of the lactiferous ducts. - **Areola**: The pigmented circular zone surrounding the nipple, studded with small sebaceous elevations called Montgomery's tubercles, which secrete protective lipids during lactation.

Internal Glandular Architecture and Lobes

Internally, the functional tissue of the non-lactating breast consists of glandular parenchyma embedded within a supportive matrix of adipose and fibrous connective tissue. The gland is divided into 15 to 20 distinct lobes arranged radially around the nipple. Each lobe functions as a separate functional unit and subdivides into numerous smaller lobules. These lobules contain the milk-secreting alveoli lined with secretory epithelium. During pregnancy and lactation, these glandular elements undergo massive proliferation and hypertrophy, replacing much of the fatty stroma to prepare for milk production. Conversely, after menopause, glandular tissue regresses significantly and is largely replaced by adipose tissue.

The Lactiferous Duct System

Milk synthesized within the alveolar epithelium travels through a branching hierarchy of drainage channels toward the exterior. Each of the 15 to 20 lobes drains via a single major **lactiferous duct**. As these ducts converge near the areola, each expands slightly into a dilated storage reservoir termed the **lactiferous sinus**. From this ampulla, the duct narrows again to form a fine terminal pore that pierces the surface of the nipple. While older anatomical models emphasized the storage role of the sinus, modern imaging reveals that these segments act more as continuous conduits rather than large stagnant pools.

Suspensory Ligaments and Stromal Support

The structural integrity and characteristic contour of the breast depend heavily on its suspensory framework, known universally as **Cooper's ligaments** (suspensory ligaments of the breast). These fibrous bands originate from the deep fascia of the pectoral muscles, course through the breast parenchyma, and insert firmly into the dermis of the skin. They act as natural internal bra straps resisting the downward pull of gravity. In malignant conditions such as scirrhous carcinoma, tumor infiltration and fibrosis shorten these ligaments, pulling inward on the overlying skin to create the characteristic 'peau d'orange' or dimpling sign observed during clinical evaluation.

Vascular Supply, Lymphatic Drainage, and Innervation

Blood reaches the mammary glands through multiple pathways, reflecting their expansive embryological origin. The primary arterial inflow stems from branches of the **internal thoracic artery** (internal mammary artery), supplemented by lateral thoracic and intercostal branches. Venous return mirrors arterial pathways, draining into the axillary and internal thoracic veins. Lymphatic drainage is clinically critical due to its role in breast cancer metastasis. More than 75 percent of the lymph from the breast drains toward the ipsilateral **axillary lymph nodes**. The remaining drainage travels primarily to parasternal nodes. Cutaneous innervation is supplied by the lateral and anterior cutaneous branches of the intercostal nerves (specifically spinal segments T3 through T5), with the fourth intercostal nerve providing the primary sensory supply to the nipple-areolar complex.

Hormonal Regulation and Physiological Lifespan Changes

Breast tissue undergoes continuous morphological evolution driven by systemic endocrine signals. At puberty, rising circulating estrogen stimulates the elongation and branching of the ductal tree, while progesterone promotes the budding of immature alveoli. During pregnancy, a synergistic cocktail of hormones—including estrogen, progesterone, prolactin, and human placental lactogen—triggers full lobulo-alveolar development. Following childbirth, the sharp drop in circulating placental estrogen and progesterone removes their inhibitory block, allowing anterior pituitary **prolactin** to drive milk synthesis. **Oxytocin**, released from the posterior pituitary in response to infant suckling, stimulates the contraction of myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli to execute the milk ejection reflex.
