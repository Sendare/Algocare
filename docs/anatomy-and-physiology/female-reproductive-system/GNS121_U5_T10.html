Bony Pelvis

Anatomical Structure and Bones of the Pelvis

The bony pelvis forms a sturdy, basin-shaped ring of bone that connects the spine to the lower limbs. It protects the pelvic viscera, supports the weight of the upper body, and provides attachment sites for powerful muscles of the back, abdomen, and lower extremities. Understanding this framework is foundational for obstetric care and physical assessment. - **Ilium**: The largest, flaring upper component of the hip bone, featuring the prominent iliac crest that serves as a landmark for clinical injections and bone marrow aspirations. - **Ischium**: The posteroinferior portion that bears body weight when sitting, highlighted by the noticeable ischial tuberosity and the ischial spine. - **Pubis**: The anteroinferior section that meets its counterpart at the midline to form the pubic symphysis. - **Sacrum**: A wedge-shaped bone composed of five fused vertebrae wedged between the two hip bones, forming the posterior wall. - **Coccyx**: The terminal tailbone made of three to five fused vertebrae, providing attachment for pelvic floor muscles.

Joints and Ligaments of the Pelvic Girdle

The stability of the pelvic girdle relies on strong, fibrous joints and heavy-duty ligaments that limit excessive movement. Unlike more mobile joints in the limbs, pelvic joints are designed for weight-bearing transfer and slight yielding during childbirth. | Joint / Ligament | Location and Structure | Primary Function | | :--- | :--- | :--- | | **Sacroiliac Joint** | Between the auricular surfaces of the sacrum and ilium | Transfers upper body weight to the lower limbs | | **Pubic Symphysis** | Secondary cartilaginous joint joining the pubic bones anteriorly | Absorbs shock and allows minor widening during pregnancy | | **Sacrospinous Ligament** | Connects sacrum to ischial spine | Converts the greater sciatic notch into a foramen | | **Sacrotuberous Ligament** | Connects sacrum to ischial tuberosity | Prevents anterior tilting of the sacrum under load | During pregnancy, hormones like relaxin increase joint laxity, softening these ligaments to expand the pelvic dimensions. This adaptive physiological loosening can lead to pelvic girdle pain and altered gait in expectant mothers.

Division: False versus True Pelvis

An imaginary oblique plane called the linea terminalis, or pelvic brim, divides the bony pelvis into two distinct compartments with entirely different anatomical and clinical roles. The false pelvis, or greater pelvis, lies superior to the pelvic brim. It is bounded by the flaring iliac wings laterally and the abdominal wall anteriorly. Although classified as part of the pelvis, it structurally supports the lower abdominal organs and enlarges during pregnancy to accommodate the growing gravid uterus. The true pelvis, or lesser pelvis, lies inferior to the pelvic brim. This bony canal has a definite inlet, cavity, and outlet. It constitutes the true birth canal through which the fetus must pass during vaginal delivery. Its dimensions and shape dictate the progress and mechanism of labor, making accurate clinical assessment critical for midwives and obstetric nurses.

Pelvic Inlet, Cavity, and Outlet

Navigating the true pelvis requires understanding its three distinct anatomical regions: the inlet, the cavity, and the outlet. Each presents specific planes and diameters that dictate fetal engagement and descent. The pelvic inlet is the entry gate bounded by the sacral promontory posteriorly, the arcuate lines laterally, and the pubic crest anteriorly. Its shape varies, but in normal female pelves, it is transversely oval. Key diameters measured here include the conjugate diameter and transverse diameter. The pelvic cavity is a curved canal, wide and roomy, situated between the inlet above and the outlet below. Because the anterior wall is short (formed by the pubic symphysis) and the posterior wall is long (formed by the sacrum), the fetus must rotate during descent. The pelvic outlet is the diamond-shaped exit bounded by the pubic arch anteriorly, the ischial tuberosities laterally, and the coccyx posteriorly. An adequate subpubic angle is essential for the fetal head to clear the perineum without excessive soft-tissue trauma.

Classification of Pelvic Types (Caldwell-Moloy)

Pelvic architecture varies widely among individuals. The Caldwell-Moloy classification categorizes female pelves into four primary morphological types based on the shape of the pelvic inlet and the characteristics of the midpelvis. 1. **Gynecoid Pelvis**: The classic female type with a rounded or transversely oval inlet and wide subpubic arch. It is the most favorable type for normal vaginal delivery. 2. **Android Pelvis**: Heart-shaped or wedge-shaped inlet resembling a male pelvis, featuring prominent ischial spines and a narrow forepelvis. It frequently results in arrested descent and operative intervention. 3. **Anthropoid Pelvis**: An oval inlet with a longer anteroposterior diameter than transverse diameter. Women with this shape often deliver occiput posterior positions successfully. 4. **Platypelloid Pelvis**: A flat, kidney-shaped inlet with a wide transverse diameter but a very short anteroposterior diameter, causing difficulty at the pelvic inlet. Most women present with mixed pelvic traits rather than a pure single type, influencing clinical decision-making during labor progress.

Sexual Dimorphism: Differences Between Male and Female Pelvis

The human pelvis displays marked sexual dimorphism, reflecting evolutionary adaptations for childbearing in females versus greater upper body muscle mass and narrow leverage in males. Forensic scientists, anatomists, and nurses use these skeletal markers for identification and assessment. The female pelvis is wider, shallower, and lighter. The iliac wings flare out more extensively, creating a broader hip span. The pelvic inlet is spacious and oval, while the subpubic angle is wide and obtuse, typically greater than 90 degrees. The ischial spines are blunter and directed outward, enlarging the pelvic outlet. Conversely, the male pelvis is heavier, thicker, and deeper. Its iliac crests are more vertical, and the false pelvis is deep. The true pelvis is narrow and funnel-shaped with a heart-shaped inlet. The subpubic angle is acute, forming an inverted 'V' shape, and the prominent ischial spines project inward, encroaching on the pelvic cavity.

Clinical Significance in Obstetrics and Nursing Care

Clinical evaluation of the bony pelvis directly impacts maternal and neonatal outcomes. Midwives and nurses utilize pelvic assessment to anticipate dystocia—difficult labor—caused by cephalopelvic disproportion, where the fetal head is too large to pass through the maternal pelvis. Clinical pelvimetry involves manual palpation of accessible landmarks during vaginal examination, such as checking the prominence of the ischial spines, estimating the diagonal conjugate, and assessing the subpubic angle. Although imaging techniques like pelvimetry via CT or MRI provide precise measurements, they are used sparingly due to radiation or cost concerns. Nursing care during labor in patients with borderline pelvic dimensions involves monitoring contraction patterns, encouraging frequent maternal position changes to facilitate fetal alignment, and remaining vigilant for signs of obstructed labor. Early recognition of pelvic abnormalities prevents uterine rupture and severe birth asphyxia in clinical settings across Nigeria and globally.
