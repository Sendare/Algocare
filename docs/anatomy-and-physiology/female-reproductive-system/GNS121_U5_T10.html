Bony Pelvis

Introduction to the Bony Pelvis

The bony pelvis is a sturdy, basin-shaped bony ring that connects the spinal column to the lower limbs. For nursing students studying obstetric and medical-surgical nursing, a solid grasp of pelvic anatomy is foundational. The pelvis serves multiple critical functions: it bears the weight of the upper body while standing, transfers that weight to the lower extremities, and protects internal abdominopelvic organs such as the urinary bladder, parts of the intestines, and reproductive organs. Furthermore, in obstetrics, it serves as the bony canal through which the fetus must pass during vaginal delivery. Understanding its structural layout is vital for assessing labor progress, identifying potential cephalopelvic disproportion, and delivering safe, effective patient-centered nursing care across various healthcare settings in Nigeria and globally.

Anatomical Structure and Bones of the Pelvis

The bony pelvis is formed by four bones: the two hip bones (also known as the coxal or innominate bones) laterally and anteriorly, and the sacrum and coccyx posteriorly. Each hip bone in an adult is a fusion of three distinct bones: the ilium, the ischium, and the pubis, which meet at a cup-shaped socket called the acetabulum. The ilium forms the superior, fan-shaped portion, the ischium forms the posteroinferior part providing the ischial tuberosities upon which we sit, and the pubis forms the anteroinferior part meeting at the pubic symphysis. Posteriorly, the sacrum is composed of five fused vertebrae, articulating with the ilium at the sacroiliac joints, and is terminated inferiorly by the small, triangular coccyx, commonly called the tailbone. Recognizing these individual landmarks is essential for accurate physical assessment and understanding pelvic mechanics during childbirth.

Joints and Ligaments of the Pelvic Girdle

The stability of the pelvic girdle relies heavily on strong joints and a robust network of ligaments. Anteriorly, the two hip bones join at the pubic symphysis, a secondary cartilaginous joint reinforced by superior and inferior pubic ligaments. Posteriorly, the pelvis connects to the axial skeleton via the paired sacroiliac joints, which are strong weight-bearing synovial joints supported by interosseous, dorsal, and ventral sacroiliac ligaments. Additionally, major accessory ligaments—such as the sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments—span from the sacrum and coccyx to the ischial tuberosity and spine, respectively. These ligaments convert the greater and lesser sciatic notches into foramina, allowing critical neurovascular structures to pass while preventing excessive upward tilting of the sacrum. During pregnancy, hormones like relaxin cause these ligaments to lax, increasing pelvic mobility to facilitate childbirth, which nurses must account for when managing maternal mobility and comfort.

Division of the Pelvis: False versus True Pelvis

An imaginary oblique plane passing from the sacral promontory posteriorly to the upper margin of the pubic symphysis anteriorly—known as the pelvic brim or linea terminalis—divides the bony pelvis into two distinct regions: the false pelvis and the true pelvis. The false pelvis (greater pelvis) lies superior to the pelvic brim and is actually part of the abdominal cavity, bounded by the iliac wings; it supports the abdominal viscera and during pregnancy, helps lift the enlarging uterus. The true pelvis (lesser pelvis) lies inferior to the pelvic brim and forms a deep, bony canal that represents the true birth canal. It contains the pelvic cavity and pelvic organs, and its dimensions are of paramount importance in obstetrics because any restriction in the true pelvis can obstruct fetal descent during labor.

Pelvic Inlet, Cavity, and Outlet

The true pelvis is conceptualized as a curved canal defined by three key planes: the inlet, the cavity, and the outlet. The pelvic inlet (superior strait) is the entrance to the true pelvis; its shape (typically oval or round) and diameters (such as the obstetric conjugate) are critical for determining if a fetus can enter the pelvis. The pelvic cavity is the curved space between the inlet and the outlet, where the fetus undergoes internal rotation during labor. The pelvic outlet (inferior strait) is the exit of the true pelvis, bounded by the pubic arch anteriorly, ischial tuberosities laterally, and the coccyx posteriorly. Nurses and midwives monitor pelvic dimensions and planes during labor to assess progress and detect cephalopelvic disproportion early, ensuring timely intervention.

Classification of Pelvic Types (Caldwell-Moloy)

The Caldwell-Moloy classification categorizes female pelvic types into four primary shapes based on the morphology of the pelvic inlet: gynecoid, android, anthropoid, and platypelloid. The gynecoid pelvis is the classic female type, characterized by a rounded or slightly oval inlet with wide subpubic angles, highly favorable for normal vaginal delivery. The android pelvis resembles a male pelvis, featuring a heart-shaped inlet, prominent ischial spines, and a narrow pubic arch, which frequently causes arrested labor and difficult deliveries. The anthropoid pelvis has an anteroposteriorly elongated, oval-shaped inlet, allowing the fetus to easily pass in an occipito-posterior position. The platypelloid pelvis is flat with a transverse-oval inlet and a wide transverse diameter, often resulting in obstructed labor at the inlet. Understanding these classifications helps nurses anticipate delivery complications.

Sexual Dimorphism: Differences Between Male and Female Pelvis

Sexual dimorphism is markedly visible in the human pelvis, as the female pelvis is specifically adapted for childbearing, whereas the male pelvis is adapted for heavier muscular build and greater physical strength. Overall, the female pelvis is wider, shallower, and lighter than the male pelvis. Specific differences include: the female pelvic inlet is wider and oval or round, whereas the male inlet is heart-shaped and narrow; the female subpubic angle is wide and obtuse (greater than 90 degrees), while the male angle is narrow and acute (less than 90 degrees); the female sacrum is wider, shorter, and less curved; and the ischial spines in females are farther apart, increasing the transverse diameter of the pelvic outlet. Nurses must be aware of these anatomical differences when interpreting pelvic x-rays, performing physical assessments, or studying forensic and obstetric anatomy.

Clinical Significance in Obstetrics and Nursing Care

In clinical practice and nursing care, the bony pelvis holds profound significance, particularly in maternal and child health. Nurses must perform pelvic assessments (pelvimetry) when indicated to identify potential cephalopelvic disproportion, where the fetal head is too large to fit through the maternal pelvis. Recognizing contracted pelves helps prevent prolonged labor, uterine rupture, obstetric fistulas—a major public health concern in resource-limited settings—and fetal distress. Furthermore, nurses provide essential intrapartum care by encouraging optimal maternal positions (such as upright or squatting positions) that maximize pelvic diameters and facilitate fetal descent. Postpartum care also involves assessing pelvic pain, symphysis pubis dysfunction, and promoting mobility exercises to restore pelvic stability and maternal well-being after delivery.
