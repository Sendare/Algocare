Bony Pelvis

Anatomy and Bones of the Bony Pelvis

The bony pelvis is a sturdy, basin-shaped ring of bone that connects the spine to the lower limbs, supporting the upper body weight and protecting internal abdominal and reproductive organs. It is formed by four distinct bones.

  • Ilium: The large, fan-shaped upper bone that forms the flared hip region.
  • Ischium: The lower and back portion of the hip bone, featuring a rough prominence called the ischial tuberosity that supports your weight when sitting.
  • Pubis: The lower and front portion that meets at the midline to form a flexible joint.
  • Sacrum and Coccyx: The sacrum is a wedge-shaped bone made of five fused vertebrae at the back of the pelvis, ending in the small, segmented tailbone known as the coccyx.

Together, these bones fuse during late adolescence to form two large hip bones (coxal bones) which articulate with the sacrum behind and each other in front.

Joints and Ligaments of the Pelvic Girdle

The joints of the pelvis provide strong stability while allowing minor adjustments during childbirth. The main articulations include the paired sacroiliac joints connecting the sacrum to the ilium, and the pubic symphysis at the front where the two pubic bones meet.

Strong ligaments reinforce these joints. The sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments bridge the sacrum to the ischium, converting notches into large foramina that let major nerves and blood vessels pass to the lower limbs. Hormones like relaxin loosen these ligaments during pregnancy, increasing pelvic mobility to help the baby pass through, though this flexibility can sometimes cause back pain for expectant mothers.

False Pelvis Versus True Pelvis

An imaginary plane called the pelvic brim (or linea terminalis) divides the bony pelvis into two distinct regions with very different functions.

  • False Pelvis (Greater Pelvis): Situated above the pelvic brim. It is actually part of the abdominal cavity, supporting the abdominal organs and the growing uterus during pregnancy.
  • True Pelvis (Lesser Pelvis): Situated below the pelvic brim. This is the true bony canal through which a baby must travel during birth. It contains the pelvic cavity, bladder, rectum, and reproductive organs.

Planes of the True Pelvis: Inlet, Cavity, and Outlet

The true pelvis is shaped like a curved funnel, consisting of three main regions that nurses and midwives assess during labor: the pelvic inlet, cavity, and outlet.

The pelvic inlet is the upper opening where the baby enters the true pelvis. Its shape can be round, oval, or heart-like. The pelvic cavity is the curved middle space where the baby rotates to fit the birth canal. Finally, the pelvic outlet is the lower diamond-shaped exit bounded by the coccyx, ischial tuberosities, and the lower pubic bone.

A common clinical mistake is assuming a wide inlet guarantees an easy birth; the ischial spines inside the cavity and the outlet dimensions are equally critical for successful delivery.

Pelvic Types: The Caldwell-Moloy Classification

The Caldwell-Moloy classification categorizes female pelves into four main types based on the shape of the pelvic inlet. Knowing these shapes helps predict how a labor might progress.

  • Gynecoid: The classic female pelvis with a rounded or slightly oval inlet. It is the most favorable shape for normal vaginal delivery.
  • Android: A heart-shaped, male-like pelvis with prominent ischial spines. Labor in this pelvis often results in a difficult delivery or requires intervention.
  • Anthropoid: An oval-shaped pelvis with a long front-to-back diameter, allowing the baby to deliver easily, often facing forward.
  • Platypelloid: A flat, wide, kidney-bean-shaped pelvis with a short front-to-back diameter, which frequently causes arrest of labor at the inlet.

Sexual Dimorphism: Male Versus Female Pelvis

The human pelvis shows clear sexual dimorphism because the female pelvis is specifically adapted for childbearing, whereas the male pelvis is built for strength and muscle attachment.

The female pelvis is wider, shallower, and lighter. Its inlet is round or oval, the pubic arch is wide (greater than 90 degrees), and the sacrum is shorter and less curved. In contrast, the male pelvis is narrower, deeper, and heavier. It features a heart-shaped inlet, a narrow pubic arch (less than 90 degrees), and prominent ischial spines that project inward into the pelvic cavity.

Obstetrical Assessment and Nursing Care

Nurses and midwives play a vital role in assessing pelvic dimensions to ensure safe deliveries. During prenatal visits, clinicians evaluate the pelvic shape and check for adequate space. Pelvimetry—the measurement of pelvic dimensions—can be done via clinical digital examination or imaging studies if obstruction is suspected.

Nurses must monitor labor progress carefully, watching for signs of cephalopelvic disproportion (CPD), where the baby's head is too large to fit through the maternal pelvis. Recognizing prolonged labor or failure to descend helps prevent severe complications like uterine rupture or birth asphyxia, ensuring prompt referral for surgical intervention when necessary.
