Physiology of Lactation

Mammary Gland Anatomy and Functional Units

The mature female breast is a specialized cutaneous gland designed for milk production and nourishment. Within each breast lie 15 to 20 lobes separated by adipose and connective tissue. These lobes branch into smaller lobules containing the functional units known as alveoli.

  • Alveoli: Hollow, spherical cavities lined with a single layer of secretory epithelial cells that synthesize and secrete milk into the lumen.
  • Myoepithelial cells: Basket-like cells surrounding each alveolus that contract in response to oxytocin to force milk into the ductal system.
  • Lactiferous ducts: Collecting tubes that converge toward the nipple, temporarily widening into lactiferous sinuses just beneath the areola to store milk.
  • Areola: The pigmented skin surrounding the nipple containing Montgomery glands, which secrete lipid substances to lubricate and protect the nipple during feeding.

Mammogenesis: Structural Development During Pregnancy

Breast development begins long before childbirth, escalating dramatically during gestation to prepare the tissue for lactation. This process, termed mammogenesis, is orchestrated by a synergistic hormonal milieu driven primarily by estrogen, progesterone, human placental lactogen, and prolactin.

During the first and second trimesters, high circulating levels of estrogen stimulate rapid elongation and branching of the ductal tree. Simultaneously, progesterone acts synergistically with estrogen to promote proliferation of alveolar buds at the terminal ends of the ducts. Human placental lactogen, secreted by the syncytiotrophoblast, further enhances lobulo-alveolar growth. By the end of pregnancy, the glandular tissue supersedes the adipose tissue, leaving the breasts fully primed for secretory activity, although high progesterone levels from the placenta actively suppress copious milk production until delivery occurs.

Lactogenesis: The Onset of Milk Secretion

The transition from pregnancy to lactation occurs in distinct phases known as lactogenesis. Lactogenesis I begins during mid-pregnancy, marked by cytological and metabolic differentiation of alveolar cells, allowing them to produce small amounts of colostrum. However, systemic progesterone maintains inhibition.

Lactogenesis II is triggered abruptly following the delivery of the placenta. The sudden withdrawal of estrogen and progesterone removes the block on the alveolar epithelium, allowing prolactin to interact unhindered with its receptors. Within two to four days postpartum, copious milk secretion begins, commonly referred to as the milk coming in. Successful initiation relies heavily on early, frequent infant suckling and effective removal of colostrum to prevent feedback inhibition of lactation.

Hormonal Regulation: Prolactin and Oxytocin

Two major pituitary hormones govern the entire lifecycle of lactation: prolactin and oxytocin.

Prolactin is an anterior pituitary hormone responsible for milk synthesis. Throughout pregnancy, its levels rise steadily, restrained only by the hypothalamus releasing dopamine (prolactin-inhibiting factor). After childbirth, suckling stimulates sensory nerve endings in the nipple, sending neural signals to the hypothalamus to suppress dopamine release, which results in a massive secretory surge of prolactin into the bloodstream.

Oxytocin is synthesized in the hypothalamus and stored in the posterior pituitary gland. Released in response to tactile, visual, or auditory cues associated with the baby, oxytocin travels via the bloodstream to bind with receptors on the myoepithelial cells, prompting immediate milk ejection.

The Milk Ejection Reflex and Galactopoiesis

Once milk synthesis is established, two concurrent processes keep the supply flowing: the milk ejection reflex (let-down) and galactopoiesis.

The milk ejection reflex is a neuroendocrine loop. Infant suckling sends afferent signals through the spinal cord to the paraventricular and supraoptic nuclei of the hypothalamus, triggering pulsatile releases of oxytocin from the posterior pituitary. Within a minute, milk is forced from the alveoli into the larger ducts. This reflex can be conditioned by sights, sounds, or emotional stress, meaning anxiety or pain can inhibit oxytocin release via sympathetic vasoconstriction.

Galactopoiesis refers to the maintenance of established milk production. It relies entirely on autocrine control—the principle of supply and demand. As milk is removed from the gland, a whey protein called feedback inhibitor of lactation (FIL) is cleared; if milk accumulates, FIL builds up and slows down further secretion.

Composition of Breast Milk Across Stages

Breast milk is a dynamic biological fluid that changes in composition to match the evolving nutritional and immunological needs of the infant.

  • Colostrum: Secreted during the first few days postpartum, this thick, yellowish fluid is rich in secretory IgA, lactoferrin, white blood cells, and growth factors, serving as the newborn's first immunologic defense.
  • Transitional milk: Produced from roughly day four to day two weeks postpartum, showing a gradual decline in protein and immunoglobulins alongside an increase in fats, lactose, and total caloric density.
  • Mature milk: Established by the end of the second week, appearing whiter and thinner. It is categorized into foremilk (watery, quenches thirst at the start of a feed) and hindmilk (rich in fats, satisfies hunger at the end of a feed).

Common Clinical Challenges in Lactation

Understanding the physiology of lactation helps nurses identify and manage common postpartum complications.

  • Breast engorgement: Occurs around day three when vascular congestion and milk accumulation peak. Poor latch or infrequent feeding worsens this. Warm compresses before feeding and cold compresses afterward help alleviate discomfort.
  • Plugged ducts: Results from incomplete emptying of a specific duct, leading to a palpable tender lump. Management focuses on frequent feeding, starting feeds on the affected side, and gentle massage toward the nipple.
  • Mastitis: An inflammation of breast tissue that can progress to infection if milk stasis persists. It presents with localized pain, erythema, fever, and flu-like symptoms. Effective milk removal remains paramount alongside medical treatment.
