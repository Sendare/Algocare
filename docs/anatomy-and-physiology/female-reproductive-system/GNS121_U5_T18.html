Physiology of Lactation

Mammary Gland Anatomy and Development

The female breast is a specialized cutaneous gland designed to nourish the newborn. Structurally, it consists of 15 to 20 lobes arranged radially around the nipple, separated by adipose and connective tissue. Each lobe contains smaller lobules made up of clusters of alveoli, which are the actual milk-producing milk glands.

  • Alveoli: Tiny, hollow, spherical cavities lined with a single layer of secretory epithelial cells that synthesize milk components.
  • Myoepithelial cells: Basket-like cells surrounding the alveoli that contract in response to oxytocin to push milk into the ducts.
  • Lactiferous ducts: Tubules that converge from each lobe to transport milk toward the nipple, where they widen slightly into lactiferous sinuses just beneath the areola.

During pregnancy, high levels of estrogen and progesterone stimulate mammogenesis, causing extensive branching of the ductal system and proliferation of alveolar structures to prepare the breast for lactation.

Hormones of Pregnancy and Lactogenesis

The onset of milk production, known as lactogenesis, occurs in distinct phases. Lactogenesis I begins during mid-pregnancy, when high circulating levels of estrogen and progesterone stimulate the growth of the glandular tissue while simultaneously inhibiting actual copious milk secretion. Despite high prolactin levels during pregnancy, milk production is held in check by these placental hormones.

Following the delivery of the placenta at birth, a sharp, sudden drop in estrogen and progesterone occurs. This hormonal withdrawal removes the block on prolactin, triggering Lactogenesis II, which is the onset of copious milk production usually occurring within 2 to 4 days postpartum.

A common clinical misunderstanding is that milk volume depends entirely on maternal fluid intake; in reality, the primary driver of milk volume in the early days is this hormonal shift followed by frequent nipple stimulation.

Prolactin and the Supply-Demand Loop

Once lactation is established, maintaining milk production—known as galactopoiesis—relies heavily on the hormone prolactin. Secreted by the anterior pituitary gland, prolactin drives the synthesis of milk proteins, lactose, and lipids in the alveolar epithelial cells.

Every time an infant suckles at the breast, sensory nerve endings in the nipple send neural signals to the mother's hypothalamus. In response, the hypothalamus reduces its release of dopamine (which normally acts as a prolactin-inhibiting factor), causing a surge of prolactin into the bloodstream. This surge replenishes the milk supply for the next feeding, creating a classic endocrine feedback loop driven by infant demand.

The Milk Ejection Reflex and Oxytocin

Producing milk is only half the process; the infant must also be able to get it out. The milk ejection reflex, often called the let-down reflex, is mediated by the neurohypophyseal hormone oxytocin.

When the infant suckles, neural impulses travel from the nipple through the spinal cord to the hypothalamus. The posterior pituitary responds by releasing oxytocin into the bloodstream. Oxytocin travels to the breast and causes the myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli to contract, forcing milk down into the large ducts.

Interestingly, this reflex is conditioned and can be easily disrupted by maternal stress, pain, or anxiety, which trigger epinephrine release and vasoconstriction that block oxytocin flow.

Composition of Breast Milk Stages

Breast milk changes its biochemical composition over time to match the growing infant's exact nutritional and immunological needs.

  • Colostrum: Secreted during the first few days postpartum, this thick, yellowish fluid is rich in immunoglobulin A (IgA), white blood cells, and proteins, acting as the baby's first immune defense.
  • Transitional milk: Produced from about day 4 to day 14, blending colostrum properties with increasing amounts of lactose, fat, and calories.
  • Mature milk: Established around two weeks postpartum, divided into foremilk (watery, thirst-quenching milk at the start of a feed) and hindmilk (fat-rich, calorie-dense milk that releases later in the feed).

Clinical Implications and Challenges

Understanding the physiology of lactation helps nurses manage common breastfeeding complications. When milk is not regularly removed from the alveoli, engorgement occurs, causing breast tissue to swell painfully. This increased intraductal pressure can eventually compress capillaries, decrease prolactin delivery, and signal the gland to slow production via a localized protein called Feedback Inhibitor of Lactation (FIL).

Prolonged stasis can also lead to blocked ducts and mastitis, an infection of the breast tissue. Effective nursing management focuses on correcting infant latch techniques, encouraging frequent emptying of the breasts, and reassuring mothers that supply responds directly to consistent removal.
