Changes in Pelvic Floor During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Anatomy and Function of the Pelvic Floor

The pelvic floor is a hammock-like structure made up of muscles, ligaments, and fascia that stretches across the base of the pelvis from the pubic bone in front to the coccyx at the back. Its primary components include the levator ani muscle group—consisting of the pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, and puborectalis muscles—and the coccygeus muscle.

These structures work together to support the pelvic organs, including the bladder, uterus, and bowels, while also controlling urinary and fecal continence. During childbearing, this muscular sling undergoes profound anatomical stretching and hormonal softening to accommodate a growing fetus and allow for vaginal delivery.

Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

Throughout pregnancy, rising levels of hormones such as relaxin and progesterone cause the connective tissues, ligaments, and muscles of the pelvic floor to soften and loosen. This laxity is necessary to prepare the pelvis for childbirth, but it also means the pelvic floor must bear an increasing downward weight from the enlarging uterus, amniotic fluid, and fetus.

As the pregnancy progresses toward term, this constant gravitational pressure gradually stretches and weakens the muscle fibers, even before the physical trauma of labor occurs.

Trauma and Impact of Labour

Childbirth represents the most significant physical challenge to the pelvic floor. As the fetus descends through the birth canal, the levator ani muscles undergo extreme stretching—sometimes up to three times their original length.

  1. Perineal tearing: Spontaneous lacerations of the skin, fascia, and muscles of the perineum can occur as the baby's head crowns.
  2. Episiotomy: A surgical cut made in the perineum to widen the outlet, which also severs muscle and tissue fibers.
  3. Nerve compression: Prolonged pressure from the fetal head can stretch and damage the pudendal nerve, leading to temporary or prolonged sensory and motor deficits.

Postnatal Recovery and Involution

During the puerperium, the body initiates repair processes to restore pelvic structures to their pre-pregnant state, a process known as involution. Hormonal levels drop sharply after delivery of the placenta, causing relaxin to clear from the system and allowing connective tissues to regain their tone.

However, full recovery takes many weeks or months. Edema, bruising, and surgical wounds from tears or episiotomies gradually heal, while the stretched muscle fibers slowly rebuild their strength, provided they are not subjected to early heavy lifting or excessive strain.

Clinical Assessment of the Postnatal Pelvic Floor

Nurses and midwives assess the postnatal pelvic floor to detect trauma, monitor healing, and identify early signs of dysfunction. Assessment includes inspecting the perineum for the healing of tears or episiotomies using the REEDA scale (Redness, Edema, Ecchymosis, Discharge, Approximation).

Palpation and structured questioning can evaluate muscle tone, voluntary contraction strength, and pain levels. Nurses should also screen routinely for urinary incontinence, pain during intercourse, or feelings of pelvic heaviness, as patients may feel embarrassed to report these symptoms spontaneously.

Common Childbirth-Related Pelvic Floor Dysfunctions

When pelvic floor muscles and supportive fascia fail to recover properly, several long-term dysfunctions can emerge:

  • Pelvic Organ Prolapse: The descent of pelvic organs—such as the bladder, uterus, or bowel—into or through the vaginal canal due to weak support.
  • Urinary Incontinence: Involuntary leakage of urine, often triggered by coughing, sneezing, or jumping (stress urinary incontinence).
  • Anal Incontinence: Difficulty controlling gas or stool, frequently linked to anal sphincter tears during delivery.
  • Dyspareunia: Pain during sexual intercourse, often resulting from tight scar tissue, perineal trauma, or nerve hypersensitivity.

Nursing Care and Rehabilitation Strategies

Proactive nursing interventions significantly improve long-term pelvic floor health. Nurses educate new mothers on performing Pelvic Floor Muscle Training (PFMT)—commonly known as Kegel exercises—which involves contracting and relaxing the levator ani muscles to improve strength and circulation.

Education should also cover lifestyle adjustments, such as avoiding heavy lifting during the early puerperium, preventing constipation with high-fiber diets and adequate hydration to reduce straining, and encouraging gradual return to physical activity. Early and correct rehabilitation prevents chronic morbidity and enhances the mother's quality of life.
