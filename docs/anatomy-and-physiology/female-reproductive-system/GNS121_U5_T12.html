Changes in Pelvic Floor During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Anatomy of the Pelvic Floor

The pelvic floor is a hammock-like structure of muscles, ligaments, and fascia stretching across the base of the pelvis. It supports the pelvic organs, including the bladder, uterus, and bowel, while maintaining continence.

  • Levator ani muscle: The primary muscular component, consisting of the puborectalis, pubococcygeus, and iliococcygeus muscles.
  • Coccygeus muscle: Assists the levator ani in supporting the posterior pelvic viscera.
  • Perineal body: A fibromuscular node situated between the vagina and the anus that provides crucial structural integrity.

Fascial layers and ligaments, such as the endopelvic fascia and arcus tendineus fasciae pelvis, anchor these muscles to the bony pelvis. Proper anatomical tone is essential for normal urinary, fecal, and sexual function throughout a woman's life.

Physiological Changes in the Pelvic Floor During Pregnancy

Pregnancy induces profound biochemical and mechanical alterations in the pelvic floor. Elevated circulating levels of progesterone and relaxin cause softening of the connective tissues, ligaments, and extracellular matrix. This laxity is a necessary adaptation to prepare the pelvis for childbirth, but it also reduces structural support.

As the fetus grows, the constant downward gravitational pull and increasing intra-abdominal pressure place chronic strain on the levator ani muscles. Over nine months, this sustained load leads to muscle fiber elongation and basal fatigue. Pregnant women frequently experience symptoms of pelvic heaviness or mild stress urinary incontinence due to these hormonal and mechanical shifts before labour even begins.

Impact of Labour and Childbirth on Pelvic Floor Structures

Childbirth represents the most significant mechanical trauma to the pelvic floor. During the second stage of labour, the fetal head descends, forcing the levator ani muscles to stretch up to three times their resting length.

  • Myofascial stretching: Extreme elongation can cause microscopic tears in muscle fibers.
  • Nerve compression: Prolonged pressure from the presenting part can stretch and injure the pudendal nerve, leading to transient or permanent denervation.
  • Perineal lacerations: Spontaneous tears or surgical incisions (episiotomies) frequently compromise the perineal body and superficial muscles.

Risk factors for severe trauma include instrument-assisted delivery, macrosomia, and prolonged second stage labour.

Pelvic Floor Recovery and Involution During the Puerperium

The puerperium is the postpartum period when maternal anatomical structures return close to their pre-pregnant state, a process known as involution. Hormonal levels drop sharply after placental delivery, initiating tissue repair.

Edema and swelling in the perineal tissues gradually subside within the first two weeks. Muscle fibers begin repairing micro-tears and regaining resting tone, aided by the reduction in intra-abdominal pressure. However, complete recovery of muscle strength and fascial integrity is rarely instantaneous and can take anywhere from six months to a full year. Incomplete involution, exacerbated by heavy lifting or chronic constipation, increases the long-term risk of pelvic organ prolapse.

Clinical Assessment of the Postnatal Pelvic Floor

Postnatal evaluation of the pelvic floor is typically performed during the routine six-week postpartum check. The nurse or midwife conducts a focused history regarding urinary habits, bowel function, and dyspareunia before proceeding to a physical examination.

Visual inspection of the perineum evaluates the healing of any lacerations or episiotomy repairs, noting signs of infection, gaping, or scar tissue formation. A digital vaginal examination assesses muscle tone, resting tension, and voluntary contraction strength, often graded using the Modified Oxford Scale from zero to five. Palpation also helps identify areas of localized tenderness, trigger points, or fascial defects that require targeted intervention.

Common Pelvic Floor Dysfunctions Associated with Childbirth

When pelvic floor muscles and connective tissues fail to recover adequately after childbirth, several distinct dysfunctions manifest.

  • Stress urinary incontinence: Involuntary leakage of urine during coughing, sneezing, or physical exertion due to poor urethral support.
  • Pelvic organ prolapse: Descent of the bladder (cystocele), uterus, or bowel (rectocele) into or through the vaginal canal resulting from fascial rupture.
  • Anal incontinence: Inability to control gas or stool, often linked to direct sphincter trauma or pudendal neuropathy.
  • Sexual dysfunction: Dyspareunia caused by tight, painful scar tissue or loss of vaginal sensation from muscle denervation.

These conditions profoundly impact physical comfort, emotional well-being, and social confidence.

Nursing Care and Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation Strategies

Nurses and midwives play a pivotal role in promoting recovery and preventing long-term morbidity through education and rehabilitation. Early mobilization post-delivery encourages circulation and reduces edema, while instruction on proper posture and lifting mechanics protects vulnerable tissues.

Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), commonly known as Kegel exercises, is the cornerstone of rehabilitation. Patients must be taught to isolate the correct muscle group without contracting their abdominal, gluteal, or adductor muscles. Nurses should advise starting exercises gently as soon as the catheter is removed or spontaneous voiding is established, progressing frequency and hold times gradually. For women with significant trauma or severe weakness, referral to a specialized women's health physiotherapist for biofeedback or electrical stimulation is indicated.
