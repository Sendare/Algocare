Changes in Pelvic Floor During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Anatomy of the Pelvic Floor

The pelvic floor is a complex, bowl-shaped muscular structure composed of the levator ani muscle group (which includes the iliococcygeus, pubococcygeus, and puborectalis muscles), the coccygeus muscle, and associated connective tissue fascia. Situated at the base of the true pelvis, it stretches like a supportive hammock from the pubic bone anteriorly to the coccyx posteriorly, and laterally to the ischial tuberosities. The primary function of this robust anatomical framework is to provide essential structural support to abdominopelvic organs, including the bladder, uterus, and bowel, preventing their prolapse against the continuous force of gravity and intra-abdominal pressure. Additionally, the pelvic floor muscles play an active, critical role in maintaining urinary and fecal continence by wrapping tightly around the urethra, vagina, and anal canal to sustain resting sphincter tone, while also allowing for voluntary relaxation during elimination and childbirth. For nursing students preparing for clinical practice in Nigeria, thorough comprehension of these foundational anatomical relationships is vital, as any compromise to this muscular sling directly leads to postpartum complications such as pelvic organ prolapse or stress urinary incontinence, conditions frequently encountered in maternal and gynecological healthcare settings.

Physiological Changes in the Pelvic Floor During Pregnancy

Pregnancy induces profound physiological and anatomical alterations throughout the maternal body, with the pelvic floor undergoing significant stress and adaptation. Under the primary influence of circulating reproductive hormones—most notably relaxin and progesterone—the connective tissues, ligaments, and fascial supports of the pelvic girdle undergo extensive softening and increased laxity. This hormonal cascade is a necessary physiological preparation designed to enhance pelvic mobility, allowing the pelvis to slightly expand and accommodate the growing fetus throughout gestation. Simultaneously, as the gravid uterus increases in size and weight week by week, a persistent, cumulative mechanical load is placed directly upon the underlying pelvic floor muscles. This sustained downward pressure stretches and weakens the muscular fibers, often leading to chronic fatigue of the levator ani complex even before the onset of labor. Pregnant women frequently experience symptoms such as pelvic heaviness or mild stress urinary incontinence due to this progressive weight and ligamentous laxity. Recognizing these normal physiological adjustments helps nursing students differentiate between expected gestational changes and early pathological weakening, enabling them to provide targeted antepartum health education focused on posture, body mechanics, and early pelvic floor muscle awareness.

Impact of Labour and Childbirth on Pelvic Floor Structures

Labour and childbirth represent the most extreme biomechanical challenge to the pelvic floor structures. During the second stage of labour, as the fetal head descends through the birth canal, the levator ani muscles and associated fascial supports are subjected to massive stretching—often lengthening up to three to four times their resting length. This intense stretching, combined with the powerful downward maternal pushing forces, can result in microtrauma, muscle fiber tearing, or detachment from their pelvic bone insertions. Furthermore, the neuropraxia of the pudendal nerve due to prolonged compression against the bony pelvis can significantly impair muscle function postpartum. Obstetric interventions, such as the use of forceps, vacuum extraction, prolonged second-stage labor, and midline episiotomies, drastically increase the risk of severe perineal and pelvic floor trauma, including third- and fourth-degree obstetric anal sphincter injuries (OASIS). When these delicate fascial planes and muscular attachments are disrupted without adequate repair or healing, the long-term structural integrity of the pelvic floor is severely compromised. For nursing and midwifery students in Nigeria, understanding the precise mechanisms of intrapartum pelvic trauma is crucial for identifying high-risk deliveries, providing skillful intrapartum support, and facilitating prompt referral for specialized repair.

Pelvic Floor Recovery and Involution During the Puerperium

Following childbirth, the maternal body initiates a complex physiological process known as the puerperium, during which the pelvic floor structures undergo involution and recovery. Involution involves the repair of microtrauma, the reduction of edema, the clearance of localized inflammation, and the gradual restoration of muscle tone and fascial tension. The high levels of pregnancy hormones, such as relaxin and estrogen, rapidly decline after delivery, allowing the stretched connective tissues and ligaments to begin regaining their baseline firmness over a period of approximately six weeks. However, complete recovery is a gradual process that can take several months, and structural restoration depends heavily on factors such as maternal age, parity, infant birth weight, and the presence or absence of traumatic lacerations. Breastfeeding mothers experience prolonged low estrogen levels due to lactation, which can lead to temporary vulvovaginal atrophy and mucosal dryness, further influencing local tissue healing. Nurses play an essential role during the puerperium by educating postnatal mothers about physiological healing milestones, encouraging adequate rest, monitoring for signs of abnormal healing or hematoma formation, and introducing gentle rehabilitation practices to support optimal muscular involution.

Clinical Assessment of the Postnatal Pelvic Floor

Clinical assessment of the postnatal pelvic floor is a critical component of comprehensive postpartum nursing care. This assessment begins with a thorough health history, where the nurse actively screens for subjective symptoms such as urinary leakage, urgency, fecal incontinence, dyspareunia, or a heavy, dragging sensation in the vagina indicative of pelvic organ prolapse. Physical assessment typically involves a careful inspection of the perineum to evaluate the healing of lacerations, episiotomies, or surgical repairs, checking for signs of infection, gaping wound edges, or abnormal edema. With appropriate specialized training and patient consent, nurses or midwives may also perform a digital vaginal palpatory assessment to evaluate resting muscle tone, voluntary contraction strength, and endurance of the levator ani muscles, often utilizing standardized scoring systems like the Modified Oxford Scale. It is imperative that these assessments are conducted with high levels of clinical sensitivity, privacy, and empathy, as postnatal women may feel vulnerable or embarrassed discussing intimate pelvic health concerns. Documentation of findings provides a baseline to monitor recovery progress and ensures that timely referrals to physiotherapists or medical specialists are initiated when deviations from normal healing are identified.

Common Pelvic Floor Dysfunctions Associated with Childbirth

When the biomechanical limits of the pelvic floor are exceeded during pregnancy and childbirth, various forms of pelvic floor dysfunction frequently manifest. Stress urinary incontinence (SUI), characterized by the involuntary leakage of urine during episodes of increased intra-abdominal pressure such as coughing, sneezing, laughing, or lifting, is one of the most common acute and long-term consequences of childbirth trauma. Pelvic organ prolapse (POP)—wherein structures such as the bladder (cystocele), rectum (rectocele), or uterus descend into or through the vaginal canal—occurs due to significant fascial and ligamentous breakdown. Fecal incontinence or flatal incontinence can also arise if the internal or external anal sphincter complexes sustained direct lacerations during delivery that healed suboptimally. Additionally, many women experience postpartum sexual dysfunctions, notably dyspareunia (painful intercourse), which can stem from tight or poorly healed scar tissue, vaginal dryness from lactation, or persistent hypertonicity of the pelvic floor muscles. Recognizing these pathologies is essential for nursing students, as these conditions significantly impair the physical, psychological, and social well-being of women in their communities, often remaining underreported due to cultural stigma.

Nursing Care and Pelvic Floor Rehabilitation Strategies

Comprehensive nursing care and targeted pelvic floor rehabilitation strategies are paramount in preventing, mitigating, and treating childbirth-related pelvic floor dysfunctions. Nurses and midwives play an indispensable role in promoting recovery by providing proactive, evidence-based health education starting from the antenatal period and continuing through postnatal follow-up. A cornerstone of rehabilitation is the instruction and reinforcement of pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT), commonly known as Kegel exercises, which teach women how to voluntarily isolate, contract, and relax the levator ani muscles to improve strength, endurance, and neuromuscular coordination. Nurses must instruct patients on correct technique—emphasizing that they should contract the muscles as if trying to stop the flow of urine without holding their breath or contracting abdominal, gluteal, or thigh muscles. For women with significant trauma, complex dysfunctions, or persistent weakness, timely referral to specialized pelvic floor physiotherapists for advanced modalities such as biofeedback, electrical stimulation, or individualized rehabilitation programs is necessary. By delivering compassionate guidance and structured support, nursing professionals empower women to reclaim their pelvic health, improve their quality of life, and prevent long-term morbidities.
