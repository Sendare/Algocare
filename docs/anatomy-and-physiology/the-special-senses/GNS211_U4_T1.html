The Eye

Accessory Structures and Protection of the Eye

The eyeball does not sit unprotected in the skull. Several accessory structures shield the delicate globe from injury, dust, and drying.

  • Eyelids and Eyelashes: These structures block foreign particles and reflexively snap shut when danger approaches.
  • Conjunctiva: A clear mucous membrane lining the inner eyelids and the white of the eye. It secretes mucus to keep the eyeball moist, though it lacks pain receptors deep down, making it prone to unnoticed infections like conjunctivitis.
  • Lacrimal Apparatus: The tear-producing system. Tears contain lysozyme, an enzyme that destroys bacteria, protecting the eye from infection while washing away debris.
  • Extrinsic Eye Muscles: Six skeletal muscles attach to the outside of the eyeball, allowing smooth, precise movement in all directions so both eyes focus together.

Tunics of the Eyeball: Fibrous and Vascular Layers

The wall of the eyeball is built from three distinct layers, often called tunics.

The outer fibrous tunic provides shape and protects inner parts. It consists of the tough, white sclera, which forms the bulk of the outer globe, and the clear cornea at the front, which lets light enter without distortion.

The middle vascular tunic, or uvea, supplies blood to the eye tissues. It includes the choroid (a dark, blood-rich membrane), the ciliary body (which controls lens shape), and the colorful iris (which adjusts the size of the pupil to control light entry).

Internal Cavities, Humors, and Pressure

The eye is divided into two main cavities that hold fluids, known as humors, which maintain its shape and internal pressure.

  1. Anterior Segment: Located in front of the lens, this space contains aqueous humor, a watery fluid continuously made and drained to supply nutrients to the cornea and lens.
  2. Posterior Segment: Located behind the lens, this large cavity is filled with vitreous humor, a thick, jelly-like substance that supports the retina against the back wall of the eye.

If the drainage of aqueous humor becomes blocked, fluid builds up, raising intraocular pressure. This condition can compress blood vessels and damage the optic nerve, leading to glaucoma.

Refraction and Accommodation

To see clearly, light rays must be bent—or refracted—so they land precisely on the back of the eye. The cornea does most of the fixed bending, while the flexible lens makes fine adjustments.

When viewing distant objects, the ciliary muscle relaxes, pulling the suspensory ligaments tight, which flattens the lens. When shifting focus to something close, the ciliary muscle contracts, releasing tension on the ligaments. This allows the lens to bulge into a rounder shape, a process called accommodation.

The Retina and Photoreception

The retina is the innermost, light-sensitive layer of the eye containing two main types of photoreceptor cells:

  • Rods: Highly sensitive to light and dark, functioning well in dim light but yielding only shades of gray.
  • Cones: Responsible for sharp color vision, requiring bright light to operate effectively.

Light hits these cells at the back of the retina, sparking chemical changes that convert light energy into electrical signals. These signals pass through bipolar cells and ganglion cells, gathering at the optic disc—commonly called the blind spot because it contains no photoreceptors—before heading to the brain.

The Visual Pathway to the Brain

Once visual signals leave the retina via the optic nerve, they travel backward toward the brain.

At the optic chiasm, nerve fibers from the inner half of each retina cross over to the opposite side of the brain, while fibers from the outer half stay on the same side. This crossing allows both sides of the brain to receive visual information from both eyes, creating depth perception.

The pathway continues as the optic tracts to the thalamus, and finally to the occipital lobe at the back of the brain, where visual processing turns electrical impulses into the images we consciously see.
