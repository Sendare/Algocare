Mechanism of Sight

Anatomy and Optical Structures of the Eye

Vision begins when light enters the eye through the transparent cornea, the primary refracting surface. Passing through the anterior chamber filled with aqueous humor, light reaches the pupil, an adjustable aperture regulated by the iris. Behind the pupil sits the lens, which provides fine-focusing adjustments for near and distant objects. The innermost layer of the eyeball is the retina, packed with specialized photoreceptors that convert light energy into electrical neural impulses.

Light Refraction and Focusing

As light rays travel from air into the dense media of the eye, they bend—a process called refraction. The cornea accomplishes the fixed bending of light, while the flexible lens allows for variable focusing. For clear vision, light must converge precisely onto the fovea centralis of the retina. Errors in this optical system lead to common refractive anomalies like myopia, where images focus in front of the retina.

Accommodation and the Pupillary Light Reflex

When shifting gaze from a distant board to a close textbook, the eye undergoes accommodation. Ciliary muscles contract, releasing tension on suspensory ligaments, allowing the lens to thicken for near vision. Simultaneously, the pupillary light reflex protects the retina from sudden, intense illumination by triggering parasympathetic constriction of the circular iris muscles via the oculomotor nerve.

Phototransduction in Rods and Cones

  • Rods: Specialized for low-light, monochromatic night vision; contain the pigment rhodopsin.
  • Cones: Responsible for high-acuity daylight and color vision; concentrated heavily within the fovea centralis.
  • Dark Current: In darkness, photoreceptors continuously release glutamate because sodium channels remain open.
  • Bleaching: Light exposure alters photopigments, closing sodium channels and hyperpolarizing the cell to halt neurotransmitter release.

Neural Processing and Visual Pathways

Electrical signals pass from photoreceptors through bipolar and amacrine cells to retinal ganglion cells. Axons of these ganglion cells bundle together to form the optic nerve. At the optic chiasm, nasal fibers cross over while temporal fibers remain uncrossed, ensuring that visual information from the left visual field is processed in the right occipital lobe.

Visual Adaptation

Transitioning from bright sunlight into a dimly lit room requires dark adaptation, during which retinal cells slowly regenerate photopigments depleted by bright light. Conversely, stepping into glaring sunlight triggers light adaptation, where rods are temporarily saturated and bleached while cones quickly take over visual processing. These adjustments prevent sensory overload and optimize vision across vast lighting ranges.
