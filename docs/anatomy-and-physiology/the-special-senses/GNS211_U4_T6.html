Mechanism of Sight

Introduction to Visual Physiology and Anatomy of the Eye

Sight begins when light bounces off objects in the environment and enters the eye. The outermost layer of the eye is the tough, white sclera, which protects the inner structures. At the very front sits the clear, dome-shaped cornea, responsible for bending incoming light rays. Just behind the cornea lies the iris, the colored muscle that controls the size of the central opening known as the pupil. Deep inside, the delicate retina lines the back wall, packed with specialized light-sensitive cells that translate optical images into electrical signals for the nervous system.

Light Refraction and the Pathway of Light

Before light can stimulate the back of the eye, it must pass through several transparent structures that bend or refract the rays.

  1. Cornea: Provides the primary fixed bending power, focusing light inward.
  2. Aqueous humor: Fills the anterior chamber, supplying nutrients while maintaining pressure and clarity.
  3. Lens: A flexible structure that fine-tunes the focus for near or distant objects.
  4. Vitreous humor: A clear gel filling the large rear cavity, holding the retina in place as light travels through to the back.

If any of these media are cloudy or misshapen, light rays fail to converge sharply on the retina, resulting in blurry vision.

Pupillary Reflex and Accommodation

The eye constantly adjusts to changing light levels and viewing distances through automatic reflexes. The pupillary light reflex protects delicate retinal cells by constricting the pupil in bright light and dilating it in dim light. Meanwhile, accommodation is the process where the eye changes the shape of the lens to keep objects in sharp focus. When viewing something close up, the ciliary muscles contract, causing the lens to bulge. When looking far away, those muscles relax, pulling the lens into a flatter shape.

Phototransduction in the Retina: Rods and Cones

Phototransduction is the molecular machinery that converts physical light into chemical and electrical language. This task falls to the photoreceptors nestled in the deep layers of the retina.

  • Rods: Highly sensitive to low light levels, enabling night and peripheral vision without picking up fine details or color.
  • Cones: Responsible for sharp daytime vision and rich color perception, requiring bright light to function properly.

Inside these cells, specialized light-absorbing pigments, such as rhodopsin in rods, change shape when struck by a photon. This chemical shift alters the electrical charge of the cell membrane, starting the neural message.

Neural Processing and the Visual Pathway to the Brain

Once photoreceptors generate an electrical signal, it passes through bipolar and ganglion cells in the retina before traveling onward. The axons of the ganglion cells bundle together to form the optic nerve, exiting the eye at the optic disc. These two optic nerves meet and partially cross over at the optic chiasm, ensuring that visual information from each eye reaches both hemispheres of the brain. The signals then travel along the optic tracts to the thalamus and finally land in the occipital lobe, where the brain interprets the final image.

Visual Adaptation and Color Vision

The visual system easily adjusts to extreme changes in illumination through adaptation. Dark adaptation occurs slowly over twenty to thirty minutes as rod pigments regenerate after entering a dark space. Light adaptation happens much faster, taking only seconds as the eye is suddenly flooded with photons. Meanwhile, color vision relies on three distinct types of cones sensitive to red, green, or blue wavelengths of light. The brain compares the firing rates of these three cone populations to create the full spectrum of colors we experience daily.
