The Nose

Introduction and Overview of the Nose

The nose is the primary organ of smell and functions as the initial anatomical segment of the human respiratory system. As nursing students studying for clinical examinations in Nigeria, understanding the structure and function of the nose is vital because it serves as the primary gateway for air entering the lower respiratory tract. Anatomically, the nose is divided into the external nose and the internal nasal cavity, both of which are specialized to perform critical protective, conditioning, and sensory roles. Air entering through the nostrils is first filtered, warmed, and humidified before it reaches the delicate tissues of the lungs. Furthermore, the nasal passages house the olfactory receptors responsible for the sense of smell, which plays a major role in human safety, nutrition, and environmental awareness. The internal nasal cavity is richly vascularized and communicates directly with the paranasal sinuses and the nasopharynx via the posterior apertures known as choanae. Pathologies affecting this region can drastically impair breathing, olfaction, and systemic oxygenation, making nursing assessments of the nose a standard component of head-to-neck physical examinations. Recognizing normal nasal anatomy allows nurses to quickly identify deviations such as septal deviation, nasal polyps, mucosal pallor indicative of allergies, or acute inflammation during clinical rotations.

Gross Anatomy of the External Nose and Nasal Cavity

The external nose presents a prominent triangular structure on the face, supported superiorly by bone and inferiorly by cartilage. The bony framework consists of the nasal bones, the frontal processes of the maxilla, and the nasal part of the frontal bone. The cartilaginous framework is composed of the lateral nasal cartilages, major alar cartilages, and minor alar cartilages, which give shape and flexibility to the nostrils, or nares. Inside the skull, the nasal cavity is divided down the midline into right and left chambers by the nasal septum, a structure formed by the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone, the vomer bone, and septal cartilage. The lateral walls of each nasal cavity feature three shelf-like bony projections known as the superior, middle, and inferior nasal conchae or turbinates. These conchae dramatically increase the surface area of the nasal cavity, ensuring maximum contact between inspired air and the vascular mucous membrane. Beneath each concha lies a corresponding passage called a meatus, designated as superior, middle, or inferior, which receives drainage from the paranasal sinuses and the nasolacrimal duct. A thorough grasp of this gross anatomical layout is essential for nursing students when performing procedures such as nasogastric tube insertion, nasal suctioning, or administering nasal drops, as improper technique can easily damage the fragile conchae or the highly vascular nasal septum.

Histology of the Nasal Mucosa and Respiratory Epithelium

The internal lining of the nasal cavity is primarily composed of pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium, widely known as respiratory epithelium. This specialized tissue is interspersed with numerous goblet cells that secrete mucus to trap incoming dust, pollen, bacteria, and other airborne particulate matter. Interspersed among the epithelial cells are millions of beating cilia that coordinate a sweeping motion, moving the trapped mucus and debris posteriorly toward the pharynx where it can be swallowed and destroyed by gastric acid in the stomach. This protective mechanism is often referred to as the mucociliary escalator. The submucosa beneath the respiratory epithelium is exceptionally rich in blood vessels, particularly capacitance vessels and venous plexuses. This rich vascular network is crucial for warming inspired air as it passes through the nasal passages. In the roof of the nasal cavity, a specialized area called the olfactory epithelium contains bipolar receptor cells that mediate the sense of smell, differing histologically from the surrounding respiratory epithelium. For nursing students, understanding this histology is important when evaluating clients exposed to dry air, smoke, or toxic fumes, as these environmental hazards can paralyze or destroy cilia, leading to chronic respiratory infections, crusting, and a breakdown of the body's primary mucosal defense line.

Paranasal Sinuses and Their Functional Relationship

The paranasal sinuses are air-filled, mucosa-lined extensions of the nasal cavity located within the bones of the skull, specifically the frontal, sphenoid, ethmoid, and maxilla bones. The largest of these are the maxillary sinuses, followed by the frontal sinuses, ethmoidal air cells, and the sphenoid sinuses. These sinuses communicate directly with the nasal cavity through small openings or ostia that drain into the nasal meatuses. The primary functions of the paranasal sinuses include reducing the relative weight of the skull, adding resonance to the voice, and producing mucus that helps keep the nasal cavity moist and clean. Because the drainage ostia of the sinuses are relatively small, they are highly susceptible to blockage when the nasal mucosa becomes inflamed due to allergies, viral upper respiratory infections, or bacterial invasion. When these ostia become obstructed, mucus accumulates within the sinus cavities, creating an ideal environment for microbial proliferation, which results in sinusitis. Nurses must assess clients presenting with facial pain, headache, purulent nasal discharge, and maxillary tenderness for potential sinus involvement, and understand that humidification and decongestants help restore sinus drainage by reducing mucosal swelling around the ostia.

Physiology of Respiration, Air Filtration, and Conditioning

Respiration relies heavily on the nose to condition inspired air before it reaches the delicate alveoli in the lungs. As a person breathes in, air is channeled through the narrow passages created by the nasal conchae, forcing turbulent airflow. This turbulence ensures maximum contact between the air molecules and the warm, moist mucous membrane lining the nasal walls. The dense network of superficial blood vessels in the nasal submucosa transfers body heat to the incoming air, warming it to near body temperature, which prevents thermal injury to the lower respiratory airways. Simultaneously, serous and mucous secretions from glands and goblet cells evaporate into the airstream, humidifying the air up to 100 percent relative humidity and preventing the delicate alveolar membranes from drying out. Furthermore, large particulate matter, dust, and pollen are mechanically filtered out by nasal hairs located in the vestibule, while smaller particles are trapped by the sticky mucus layer on the respiratory epithelium. For nursing professionals, this physiological conditioning process highlights why mouth breathing—often seen in clients with nasal obstructions, severe septal deviations, or nasal polyps—leads to dry throats, frequent lower respiratory irritation, and increased susceptibility to infections because the air bypasses natural filtration and humidification.

Olfaction: Anatomy and Physiology of the Sense of Smell

Olfaction is the special sensory process that enables the perception of smell, initiated within the roof of the nasal cavity. The olfactory region contains specialized pseudostratified epithelium housing millions of bipolar olfactory receptor cells, supporting cells, and basal stem cells. The dendrites of these bipolar receptor cells terminate in tiny knobs covered by cilia that extend into the layer of mucus, while their unmyelinated axons bundle together to form the olfactory nerve (cranial nerve I). Odorant molecules dissolved in the nasal mucus bind to specific receptor proteins on these olfactory cilia, triggering a biochemical cascade and action potentials that travel along the olfactory nerves. These nerve fibers pass upward through the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone to synapse in the olfactory bulb. From the olfactory bulb, neural signals travel along the olfactory tracts directly to the limbic system, amygdala, and cerebral cortex, bypassing the thalamus. This direct connection explains why smells often trigger strong emotional memories and physiological responses like salivation or nausea. Nurses should monitor for deficits in olfaction, known as anosmia, which can result from head trauma shearing the cribriform plate, chronic rhinitis, or viral infections, as loss of smell significantly impacts a client's nutritional intake and ability to detect environmental hazards like smoke or gas leaks.

Clinical Correlations and Common Pathologies

Clinical disorders of the nose are among the most common reasons clients seek healthcare, spanning simple allergic rhinitis to life-threatening conditions. Allergic rhinitis involves an IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reaction to airborne allergens like pollen, dust mites, or mold, resulting in mucosal edema, sneezing, and clear rhinorrhea. Epistaxis, or nosebleeds, frequently occurs due to trauma or dryness affecting Kiesselbach's plexus, an anastomotic vascular network located on the anterior inferior nasal septum. Nurses managing epistaxis must position the client sitting upright and leaning forward to prevent blood aspiration, applying direct digital pressure to the soft lower portion of the nose for at least 10 to 15 minutes. Nasal polyps are benign, inflammatory growths of the nasal mucosa often associated with chronic rhinosinusitis or asthma, which can severely obstruct nasal breathing. Furthermore, nasal fractures resulting from facial trauma require careful assessment for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) rhinorrhea—a clear, watery fluid leak that tests positive for glucose, indicating a fracture of the cribriform plate and a risk of meningitis. Recognizing these pathologies equips nursing students to deliver safe, evidence-based care, perform targeted physical assessments, and educate clients on preventative management.
