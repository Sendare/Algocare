The Nose

Introduction and Overview of the Nose

The human nose is the prominent initial segment of the respiratory tract and the primary organ of olfaction. Serving as more than a simple facial feature, it acts as a sophisticated environmental conditioning chamber for every breath we take. As air enters the nasal passages, the nose immediately goes to work filtering out particulate matter, adjusting the temperature to match body core warmth, and adding moisture. This remarkable organ is divided structurally into an external visible portion and an internal nasal cavity that extends deep into the skull, communicating directly with the nasopharynx. Understanding the nose requires looking closely at how its bony and cartilaginous framework supports both breathing and smelling while protecting the delicate lower airway tissues from trauma and infection.

Gross Anatomy of the External Nose and Nasal Cavity

The external nose features a rigid upper bony bridge and a flexible lower cartilaginous framework that gives the structure its shape and resilience. Superiorly, the nasal bones, frontal process of the maxilla, and nasal part of the frontal bone form the root and bridge. Inferiorly, a collection of lateral, major alar, and minor alar cartilages shape the tip and mobile nostrils, known as the nares.

Inside, the nasal cavity is partitioned down the midline by the nasal septum—composed of the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone, the vomer, and septal cartilage. Lateral walls feature three horizontal bony projections called nasal conchae or turbinates: superior, middle, and inferior. These turbinates curl into the cavity to create turbulent airflow pathways, increasing the mucosal surface area available to process incoming air. Beneath each concha lies a corresponding meatus, which serves as a drainage channel for paranasal sinuses and the nasolacrimal duct.

Histology of the Nasal Mucosa and Respiratory Epithelium

The respiratory region of the nasal cavity is lined by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium, frequently called respiratory epithelium. This specialized tissue layer contains millions of beating hair-like cilia covered by a blanket of mucus. Goblet cells scattered throughout the epithelium, along with underlying submucosal seromucous glands, continuously secrete this protective mucus layer.

  • Cilia: Beat in a coordinated, synchronized wave-like motion toward the pharynx.
  • Mucus Blanket: Traps inhaled dust, bacteria, pollen, and other airborne foreign particles.
  • Lamina Propria: A highly vascularized connective tissue layer beneath the epithelium that warms passing air.

This continuous movement forms the mucociliary escalator, sweeping trapped debris backward toward the throat where it is swallowed and destroyed by stomach acid. A common clinical mistake is assuming thick nasal discharge always indicates a bacterial infection; inflammation from simple allergies can also stimulate excessive mucus production.

Paranasal Sinuses and Their Functional Relationship

The paranasal sinuses are air-filled, mucosa-lined extensions of the nasal cavity located within the bones of the skull. The four pairs of sinuses are the maxillary, frontal, sphenoid, and ethmoidal sinuses. They drain their secretions through small openings, called ostia, directly into the nasal meatuses.

These hollow spaces lighten the weight of the skull, give resonance to the voice, and produce mucus that helps keep the nasal passages moist. Because their drainage ostia are very narrow, they easily become blocked when the nasal mucosa swells from allergies or a cold, leading to painful pressure buildup known as sinusitis.

Physiology of Respiration, Air Filtration, and Conditioning

As air flows through the nasal cavity during inspiration, it undergoes three vital processes: filtration, humidification, and warming. Large particulate matter is initially trapped by coarse hairs, called vibrissae, located inside the vestibule. Finer dust particles and microorganisms are caught in the sticky mucous blanket covering the nasal conchae.

Warming and humidification happen simultaneously thanks to the extensive vascular network running through the lamina propria and turbinates. Arterial blood flowing close to the surface transfers body heat to the incoming air stream, bringing it close to 37 degrees Celsius by the time it reaches the pharynx. Simultaneously, water evaporates from the wet mucosal surface, saturating the air with moisture up to nearly 100% relative humidity. This conditioning step prevents the delicate alveolar membranes in the lungs from drying out and failing.

Olfaction: Anatomy and Physiology of the Sense of Smell

The sense of smell relies on a specialized patch of tissue called the olfactory epithelium, located on the roof of the nasal cavity near the superior nasal concha. This area contains three main cell types: olfactory receptor cells, supporting sustentacular cells, and basal stem cells that continually regenerate sensory neurons.

Olfactory receptors are bipolar neurons featuring tiny hair-like cilia projecting into the nasal mucus. When volatile chemical odor molecules dissolve in this mucus layer, they bind to specific receptor proteins on the cilia. This binding triggers an electrical nerve impulse that travels up through tiny holes in the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone. The nerve fibers synapse directly within the olfactory bulb, sending signals along cranial nerve I to the brain's temporal lobes for conscious interpretation and emotional processing.

Clinical Correlations and Common Pathologies

The nose is vulnerable to a variety of clinical conditions that disrupt its normal filtering, breathing, and smelling roles. Epistaxis, or nosebleeds, occurs very commonly due to the rich vascular network in the anterior nasal septum, a site known as Kiesselbach's plexus. Most nosebleeds stop easily with direct pinching pressure, though posterior bleeds require medical intervention.

Allergic rhinitis causes sneezing, itching, and watery congestion when environmental triggers like pollen or dust mites provoke an immune response in the nasal mucosa. Chronic obstruction can stem from a deviated nasal septum or from benign growths called nasal polyps, which develop from chronic mucosal inflammation. Additionally, severe head trauma can fracture the cribriform plate, tearing the meninges and causing clear cerebrospinal fluid to leak out of the nose, a dangerous condition known as cerebrospinal fluid rhinorrhea.
