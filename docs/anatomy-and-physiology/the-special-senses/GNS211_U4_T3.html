The Nose

Introduction and Functional Overview of the Nose

The nose is much more than a prominent facial landmark; it serves as the primary portal of entry for the respiratory tract and houses the specialized organ of smell. Anatomically divided into the external nose and the internal nasal cavity, this structure performs critical homeostatic functions before air ever reaches the delicate alveolar membranes of the lungs. Incoming air encounters a complex labyrinth designed to warm, humidify, and filter particulate matter. Beyond respiration and olfaction, the nasal cavities and their associated air-filled spaces contribute significantly to vocal resonance and facial expression. Understanding this dual role in breathing and sensory perception forms the foundation for managing various otorhinolaryngological conditions seen in clinical practice across Nigeria, such as allergic rhinitis driven by heavy dust and harmattan winds.

Gross Anatomy of the External Nose and Nasal Cavity

The external nose features a skeletal framework composed of both bone and hyaline cartilage. The superior third forms the bony bridge consisting of the nasal bones, frontal process of the maxilla, and nasal part of the frontal bone. The inferior two-thirds rely on flexible lateral, septal, and greater/lesser alar cartilages, which maintain patent airway openings during forceful inspiration.

Internally, the nasal cavity is split symmetrically into left and right chambers by the nasal septum—a structure formed by the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone, the vomer, and septal cartilage. The lateral walls feature three bony shelves known as nasal conchae (superior, middle, and inferior). These scroll-like projections drastically increase the mucosal surface area, creating turbulent airflow patterns that ensure maximum contact between the air and the vascular mucous membrane.

Histology of the Nasal Mucosa and Respiratory Epithelium

Most of the respiratory tract, including the nasal cavity, is lined by pseudostratified ciliated columnar epithelium containing mucus-secreting goblet cells. This specialized cellular arrangement is commonly referred to as the respiratory epithelium. Cilia beat in a coordinated, metachronal wave, propelling a sheet of mucus toward the nasopharynx where it is swallowed and destroyed by gastric acid.

The lamina propria beneath this epithelium contains a rich network of capacitance vessels and seromucous glands. The abundance of superficial blood vessels allows the nose to rapidly transfer heat to incoming cold air, making epistaxis (nosebleeds)—particularly from Kiesselbach's plexus on the anterior septum—a frequent clinical presentation in dry, arid weather.

Paranasal Sinuses and Their Functional Relationship

  • Maxillary Sinuses: The largest of the paranasal sinuses, located within the maxillary bones, draining high on the medial wall into the middle meatus.
  • Frontal Sinuses: Situated within the frontal bone above the orbits, draining via the frontonasal duct into the middle meatus.
  • Ethmoidal Air Cells: A collection of small honeycomb-like cavities located between the nasal cavity and the orbits, divided into anterior, middle, and posterior groups.
  • Sphenoidal Sinuses: Positioned centrally within the body of the sphenoid bone, lying just inferior to the pituitary gland and draining into the sphenoethmoidal recess.

These air-filled extensions of the respiratory mucosa lighten the skull, provide structural resonance for the voice, and buffer the brain against facial trauma. Each sinus communicates directly with the nasal cavity through small openings called ostia. Blockage of these ostia during upper respiratory infections frequently precipitates painful inflammation known as sinusitis.

Olfaction: Anatomy and Physiology of the Sense of Smell

The olfactory region occupies the roof of the nasal cavity, covering the superior nasal concha and the opposing nasal septum. Specialized olfactory receptor cells are bipolar neurons whose peripheral processes project into the mucus layer as olfactory hairs, while their central axons bundle together to form Cranial Nerve I (the olfactory nerve). These nerve bundles traverse the porous cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone to synapse in the olfactory bulb.

Odorant molecules dissolved in the mucus bind to specific G-protein coupled receptors on the cilia, initiating an electrical action potential. This sensory information travels directly to the limbic system and olfactory cortex without preliminary relay through the thalamus, explaining why certain scents instantly trigger vivid emotional memories and strong autonomic responses.

Clinical Correlations and Common Pathologies

Nasal pathologies frequently disrupt upper airway patency and sensory functions. Allergic rhinitis causes sneezing, clear rhinorrhea, and mucosal edema triggered by airborne allergens like pollen and dust mites. Conversely, infectious rhinitis (the common cold) is typically viral, involving localized inflammation and hypersecretion.

Structural deviations, such as a deviated nasal septum, can narrow one nasal passage, increasing airway resistance and predisposing individuals to chronic mouth breathing, snoring, and obstructive sleep apnea. Nasal polyps—benign, inflammatory growths arising from the nasal or sinus mucosa—can cause progressive nasal obstruction and hyposmia (reduced sense of smell). Assessment of these conditions requires careful inspection using a penlight and nasal speculum, keeping anatomical landmarks in clear view.
