The Tongue

Introduction to the Tongue: Anatomy and Gross Structure

The tongue is a remarkable, muscular organ located primarily within the oral cavity, playing an essential role in digestion, taste, and communication. Grossly, the tongue is divided into distinct anatomical regions: the apex (tip), the body (anterior two-thirds), and the root (posterior one-third). The superior surface, known as the dorsum, is characterized by a V-shaped groove called the sulcus terminalis, which separates the anterior oral part from the posterior pharyngeal part. At the apex of the sulcus terminalis lies the foramen cecum, an embryonic remnant of the thyroglossal duct. The inferior surface of the tongue features the lingual frenulum, a fold of mucous membrane that anchors the tongue to the floor of the mouth, limiting excessive posterior movement. Understanding these surface landmarks is critical for nursing students during comprehensive oral cavity assessments, as abnormalities, lesions, or frenulum restrictions (such as ankyloglossia or tongue-tie) can significantly impact neonatal feeding and adult speech articulation. Furthermore, the mucosa of the dorsum is rough due to numerous small projections called papillae, which are vital for mechanical processing and sensory detection. Proper documentation of tongue appearance, moisture, and mobility during physical examinations helps identify systemic conditions, dehydration, or local pathology.

Musculature of the Tongue: Intrinsic and Extrinsic Muscles

The musculature of the tongue is complex and divided into two primary categories: intrinsic and extrinsic muscles. Intrinsic muscles originate and insert entirely within the tongue substance, consisting of superior longitudinal, inferior longitudinal, transverse, and vertical muscles. These intrinsic fibers allow the tongue to alter its shape—making it shorter, narrower, broader, or curled—which is indispensable for manipulating food and forming words. Conversely, extrinsic muscles originate from external structures (such as the mandible, hyoid bone, and styloid process) and insert into the tongue. The major extrinsic muscles include the genioglossus, hyoglossus, styloglossus, and palatoglossus. The genioglossus is particularly prominent; it protrudes the tongue forward, while the styloglossus retracts and elevates it. The hyoglossus depresses the tongue and pulls its sides down, and the palatoglossus elevates the posterior part of the tongue during swallowing. For nursing and clinical practice, assessing tongue movement provides immediate insight into cranial nerve integrity. Weakness or asymmetrical deviation when the patient sticks out their tongue often points to localized or central neurological lesions, particularly affecting the hypoglossal nerve. Mastering these muscle actions enables healthcare providers to accurately diagnose cranial nerve neuropathies and design targeted speech or swallowing rehabilitation plans.

Papillae and the Physiology of Taste Perception

The dorsal surface of the tongue is covered by specialized mucosa featuring four main types of papillae: circumvallate, fungiform, foliate, and filiform. Circumvallate papillae are large, dome-shaped structures arranged in a V-shape just anterior to the sulcus terminalis and contain numerous taste buds. Fungiform papillae are mushroom-shaped projections scattered predominantly across the tip and sides of the tongue, also housing taste buds. Foliate papillae appear as short vertical folds on the posterolateral margins of the tongue and contain sensitive taste receptors. In stark contrast, filiform papillae are small, thread-like structures covering the entire anterior two-thirds of the tongue; they lack taste buds entirely but provide a rough abrasive surface that helps grip and manipulate food during chewing. Taste perception (gustation) begins when chemical tastants dissolve in saliva and bind to specialized receptor cells located within the taste buds of circumvallate, fungiform, and foliate papillae. These receptors transduce chemical signals into electrical impulses sent to the brain. Nurses should note that conditions like glossitis, nutritional deficiencies, or certain medications can lead to atrophy of these papillae, resulting in a smooth, painful, 'bald' tongue and alterations in taste perception.

Innervation: Sensory and Motor Nerve Supply

The tongue possesses a rich and intricate nerve supply, divided neatly into sensory (taste and general sensation) and motor components. Motor innervation to almost all intrinsic and extrinsic muscles of the tongue is supplied by the hypoglossal nerve (Cranial Nerve XII), with the sole exception of the palatoglossus muscle, which is innervated by the vagus nerve (Cranial Nerve X) via the pharyngeal plexus. For sensory innervation, the tongue is divided into distinct anatomical zones. General sensation (touch, pain, temperature) for the anterior two-thirds of the tongue is supplied by the lingual nerve, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (Cranial Nerve V3). Taste sensation from this same anterior two-thirds travels via the chorda tympani branch of the facial nerve (Cranial Nerve VII). Meanwhile, both general sensation and taste for the posterior one-third of the tongue are supplied primarily by the glossopharyngeal nerve (Cranial Nerve IX), with a small contribution near the epiglottis supplied by the internal laryngeal branch of the vagus nerve (Cranial Nerve X). This complex overlapping innervation is clinically vital; nurses test cranial nerves V, VII, IX, X, and XII during neurological assessments to pinpoint deficits affecting taste, chewing, swallowing, and speech.

Blood Supply and Lymphatic Drainage

The tongue is a highly vascular organ, ensuring rapid healing of mucosal injuries and high metabolic activity. The primary arterial supply is derived from the lingual artery, a direct branch of the external carotid artery. The lingual artery branches into the deep lingual artery, which supplies the apex, and the dorsal lingual arteries, which supply the root and posterior regions. Venous return mirrors this arterial supply, with blood draining via the deep lingual veins, dorsal lingual veins, and ultimately emptying into the internal jugular vein through the lingual vein and facial-jugular system. The superficial nature of the veins on the ventral surface of the tongue makes it an anatomical site for rapid sublingual medication absorption, such as nitroglycerin in cardiac emergencies. Lymphatic drainage of the tongue is equally important, particularly in the context of oral cancers like squamous cell carcinoma. The tip of the tongue drains directly to the submental lymph nodes. The anterior two-thirds drain into the submandibular and deep cervical lymph nodes, while the posterior one-third drains directly into the superior and inferior deep cervical lymph nodes. Understanding these distinct lymphatic drainage pathways allows nurses and healthcare providers to accurately stage head and neck tumors and palpate for regional lymphadenopathy during clinical examinations.

Functions of the Tongue in Mastication, Swallowing, and Speech

Beyond taste perception, the tongue is a multi-functional organ critical for everyday survival activities, namely mastication, swallowing (deglutition), and speech articulation. During mastication, the intrinsic and extrinsic muscles coordinate seamlessly to position food between the teeth for grinding, mix it thoroughly with saliva to form a cohesive bolus, and sweep the oral cavity clean of food particles. Once mastication is complete, the tongue transitions into the voluntary phase of swallowing: it presses upward and backward against the hard palate, squeezing and propelling the food bolus posteriorly into the pharynx. Furthermore, the tongue acts as a primary articulator in human speech. By rapidly changing shape and altering its points of contact against the teeth, alveolar ridge, hard palate, and soft palate, the tongue modulates airflow to produce precise consonant and vowel sounds. For nursing professionals caring for patients recovering from a stroke (cerebrovascular accident), traumatic brain injury, or head and neck surgeries, tongue dysfunction directly impairs all three of these domains. Dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), dysarthria (slurred or distorted speech), and ineffective chewing frequently result from lingual muscle weakness or nerve paralysis, necessitating careful swallowing screenings, texture-modified diets, and speech-language pathology referrals to prevent aspiration pneumonia.
