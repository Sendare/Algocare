The Tongue

Introduction to the Tongue: Anatomy and Gross Structure

The tongue is a mobile muscular organ situated in the floor of the oral cavity. It plays a central role in several daily physiological activities including digestion, phonation, and taste. Anatomically, the tongue is divided into two primary parts: the anterior two-thirds (oral part) and the posterior one-third (pharyngeal part). These regions are separated by a V-shaped groove on the dorsal surface known as the **sulcus terminalis**, at the apex of which lies a small pit called the **foramen cecum**, a developmental remnant of the thyroglossal duct. The inferior surface of the tongue features a midline mucosal fold called the **frenulum**, which anchors the tongue to the floor of the mouth while permitting controlled movement.

Musculature of the Tongue: Intrinsic and Extrinsic Muscles

Tongue movement and shape changes rely on a complex arrangement of skeletal muscles divided into intrinsic and extrinsic groups. - **Intrinsic muscles**: Confined entirely within the tongue substance; responsible for altering its shape (making it long, narrow, short, or flat). They include the superior longitudinal, inferior longitudinal, transverse, and vertical muscles. - **Extrinsic muscles**: Originate from external bony structures and insert into the tongue; responsible for moving the organ as a whole within or outside the oral cavity. They include: - **Genioglossus**: Protrudes the tongue forward. - **Hyoglossus**: Depresses and retracts the tongue. - **Styloglossus**: Elevates and retracts the tongue. - **Palatoglossus**: Elevates the posterior tongue and closes the oropharyngealisthmus.

Papillae and the Physiology of Taste Perception

The dorsal surface of the tongue is covered in specialized mucosal projections called **papillae**, which house the taste buds responsible for gustation. There are four main types: - **Fungiform papillae**: Mushroom-shaped projections scattered mostly across the anterior two-thirds of the tongue. - **Vallate (circumvallate) papillae**: Large, dome-shaped papillae arranged in a V-shape immediately anterior to the sulcus terminalis. - **Foliate papillae**: Series of short vertical folds located on the posterolateral borders of the tongue. - **Filiform papillae**: Small, thread-like structures covering the entire anterior surface; they lack taste buds but provide a rough frictional surface essential for manipulating food during mastication.

Innervation: Sensory and Motor Nerve Supply

Nerve supply to the tongue is anatomically divided due to its dual embryonic origin from different pharyngeal arches. - **Motor Innervation**: All intrinsic and extrinsic muscles of the tongue are supplied by the **hypoglossal nerve (Cranial Nerve XII)**, with one exception—the **palatoglossus**, which is innervated by the pharyngeal plexus via the **vagus nerve (Cranial Nerve X)**. - **Sensory Innervation (Taste)**: - Anterior two-thirds: **Facial nerve (Cranial Nerve VII)** via the chorda tympani. - Posterior one-third: **Glossopharyngeal nerve (Cranial Nerve IX)**. - Extreme posterior root: **Vagus nerve (Cranial Nerve X)**. - **Sensory Innervation (General/Pain/Temperature)**: - Anterior two-thirds: **Lingual nerve**, a branch of the mandibular division of the trigeminal nerve (Cranial Nerve V3). - Posterior one-third: Glossopharyngeal nerve (Cranial Nerve IX).

Blood Supply and Lymphatic Drainage

The tongue is a highly vascular organ, ensuring rapid healing of mucosal injuries and effective systemic drug absorption when sublingual medications are administered. - **Arterial Supply**: The primary arterial source is the **lingual artery**, a direct branch of the external carotid artery. It branches into the deep lingual artery and sublingual artery. - **Venous Drainage**: Deoxygenated blood drains via the deep lingual, sublingual, and dorsal lingual veins, ultimately emptying into the **internal jugular vein** via the facial and lingual veins. - **Lymphatic Drainage**: - Tip of the tongue: Submental lymph nodes. - Anterior two-thirds: Submandibular and deep cervical lymph nodes. - Posterior one-third: Superior deep cervical (jugulodigastric) lymph nodes directly.

Functions of the Tongue in Mastication, Swallowing, and Speech

Beyond taste perception, the tongue is a mechanical workhorse. During **mastication**, the intrinsic and extrinsic muscles coordinate seamlessly to position food between the teeth while protecting the buccal mucosa from injury. Once chewing is complete, the tongue changes shape to gather the masticated food into a cohesive bolus. During the oral phase of **swallowing** (deglutition), the tongue acts as a mechanical ramp, pressing upward and backward against the hard palate to propel the bolus into the pharynx. For **speech (phonation)**, the tongue acts as a versatile articulator. By dynamically modifying the shape and volume of the oral cavity and creating precise constrictions against the teeth, alveolar ridge, and palate, it allows humans to produce distinct vowel sounds and consonant phonemes.
