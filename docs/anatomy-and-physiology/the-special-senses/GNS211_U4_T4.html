The Tongue

Gross Anatomy and Structural Features of the Tongue

The tongue is a muscular, mobile organ sitting inside the floor of the mouth, playing a vital role in digestion, taste, and speech. Anatomically, it is divided into two main parts: the oral body (the anterior two-thirds) and the pharyngeal root (the posterior one-third). These regions are separated on the dorsal surface by a V-shaped groove known as the sulcus terminalis, at the apex of which sits a small pit called the foramen caecum—an embryological remnant of the thyroid gland's descent. The undersurface, or ventral surface, of the tongue features a midline mucous membrane fold called the frenulum, which anchors the tongue to the floor of the mouth while allowing free movement of the anterior tip.

Intrinsic and Extrinsic Musculature

Tongue movement relies on two distinct groups of skeletal muscles working in harmony.

  1. Intrinsic Muscles: These muscles originate and insert entirely within the tongue substance. They include the superior longitudinal, inferior longitudinal, transverse, and vertical muscles. Their primary job is to change the shape of the tongue—making it curl, shorten, lengthen, or flatten.
  2. Extrinsic Muscles: These muscles originate from bones outside the tongue (such as the mandible, hyoid bone, and styloid process) and insert into it. They include the genioglossus, hyoglossus, styloglossus, and palatoglossus. Their main function is to move the tongue bodily in space—protruding, retracting, depressing, and elevating it.

Lingual Papillae and Taste Perception

The dorsal surface of the tongue appears velvety because of tiny projections called papillae, most of which contain taste buds. There are four main types of papillae:

  • Vallate (circumvallate) papillae: Large, dome-shaped structures arranged in a V-row just in front of the sulcus terminalis, packed with numerous taste buds.
  • Fungiform papillae: Mushroom-shaped dots scattered mostly across the tips and sides of the tongue, appearing reddish due to a rich vascular core.
  • Foliate papillae: Leaf-like folds located along the posterolateral borders of the tongue.
  • Filiform papillae: Small, cone-shaped projections covering the entire anterior surface. Unlike the others, they do NOT contain taste buds; instead, they provide rough friction to help handle food during chewing.

Nerve Supply: Sensory and Motor Innervation

The tongue has a complex nerve supply reflecting its dual embryological origin.

  • Motor Innervation: Almost all intrinsic and extrinsic muscles are supplied by the hypoglossal nerve (CN XII), except for the palatoglossus, which is supplied by the pharyngeal plexus (CN X).
  • General Sensory Innervation: Touch, pain, and temperature for the anterior two-thirds come from the lingual nerve (a branch of CN V3), while the posterior one-third is supplied by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX) and a small root area by the vagus nerve (CN X).
  • Special Sensory (Taste) Innervation: Taste from the anterior two-thirds is carried by the chorda tympani branch of the facial nerve (CN VII), and taste from the posterior third is carried by the glossopharyngeal nerve (CN IX).

Blood Supply and Lymphatic Drainage

Because the tongue is metabolically active and highly vascular, it receives a rich blood supply primarily from the lingual artery, a direct branch of the external carotid artery. Venous return mirrors this arterial supply, draining through the lingual veins which eventually empty into the internal jugular vein, often via the facial or superior thyroid veins.

Lymphatic drainage is also crucial, especially regarding oral cancer metastasis:

  • The tip drains into the submental lymph nodes.
  • The anterior lateral two-thirds drain into the submandibular and deep cervical nodes.
  • The posterior third drains directly into the superior deep cervical (jugulodigastric) nodes.

Functional Roles in Mastication, Swallowing, and Speech

Beyond taste, the tongue is an indispensable mechanical tool for everyday living. During mastication (chewing), the tongue constantly manipulates food, pushing it between the molar teeth while mixing it with saliva to form a cohesive food bolus. During swallowing (deglutition), the intrinsic and extrinsic muscles elevate the tongue against the hard palate, squeezing the bolus backward into the pharynx. Finally, for speech articulation, the tongue rapidly changes shape and position—contacting the teeth, alveolar ridge, and soft palate—to shape airflow into distinct vowel and consonant sounds.
