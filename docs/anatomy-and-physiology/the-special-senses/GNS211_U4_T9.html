Mechanism of Smell

Introduction to Olfaction and the Olfactory System

Olfaction is the chemical sense responsible for the perception of smells. Unlike vision or hearing, which rely on physical waves of light and sound, olfaction requires direct chemical interaction between volatile molecules in the air and biological receptors inside the nasal cavity. This sensory modality plays a critical role in human survival, influencing nutritional intake by detecting spoiled food, warning against environmental hazards such as smoke or gas leaks, and contributing significantly to the sensation of taste during eating.

Anatomy of the Olfactory Epithelium and Receptor Cells

The olfactory epithelium is a specialized patch of pseudostratified columnar epithelium located on the roof of the nasal cavity, covering an area of about five square centimeters in humans. It houses three main cell types: - **Olfactory receptor cells**: Specialized bipolar neurons embedded directly within the epithelial layer, featuring fine cilia projecting into the mucous layer. - **Supporting cells**: Columnar cells that provide metabolic and physical support, akin to glial cells in the central nervous system. - **Basal cells**: Stem cells located near the basement membrane that continually divide and differentiate to replace damaged olfactory neurons, a rare example of adult neurogenesis.

Olfactory Transduction: How Odorants are Detected

Olfactory transduction translates chemical signals into electrical activity. When volatile odorant molecules dissolve in the mucus lining of the nasal cavity, they bind to specific G-protein coupled receptors located on the cilia of olfactory receptor cells. This binding activates a specialized G-protein known as Golf, which in turn stimulates the enzyme adenylyl cyclase. Adenylyl cyclase increases intracellular concentrations of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). The rise in cAMP opens cyclic nucleotide-gated ion channels, allowing an influx of sodium and calcium ions into the cell. This inward current depolarizes the membrane, generating a receptor potential that can trigger an action potential if threshold is reached.

Neural Pathways and the Olfactory Bulb

Unmyelinated axons of olfactory receptor cells bundle together to form the olfactory nerve, which penetrates the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone to synapse within the olfactory bulb. Inside the olfactory bulb, these incoming terminals converge onto specialized spherical structures called glomeruli. Each glomerulus receives input from receptor cells expressing the exact same type of odorant receptor, creating an organized sensory map. Mitral and tufted cells within the bulb then process this integrated information and relay it onward through the olfactory tract toward higher cortical structures.

Central Processing of Odor Information in the Brain

Unlike other sensory systems that relay primarily through the thalamus before reaching the cortex, olfactory information travels directly to limbic and cortical structures. The olfactory tract projects to the primary olfactory cortex, which includes the piriform cortex, amygdala, and entorhinal cortex. This direct wiring explains why smells evoke powerful emotional memories and visceral responses so rapidly. The amygdala processes emotional significance, the hippocampus contributes to odor memory, and signals eventually reach the orbitofrontal cortex for conscious identification and appreciation of complex aromas.

Olfactory Adaptation and Fatigue

Olfactory adaptation refers to the rapid decrease in sensitivity to a continuous, unchanging odorant over time. This protective neural mechanism prevents sensory overload, allowing the nervous system to detect new, potentially more critical environmental changes. Adaptation occurs at multiple levels, including receptor phosphorylation which reduces G-protein coupling, and calcium-mediated feedback loops within the receptor cell cilia that shut down transduction channels despite the continued presence of the stimulating molecule.

Clinical Correlates: Disorders of Smell

Disorders of the olfactory system can significantly impact nutritional status and safety. Clinicians evaluate these deficits using standardized terms: - **Anosmia**: Complete loss of the sense of smell, often caused by head trauma shearing the olfactory nerve filaments across the cribriform plate, or by severe viral upper respiratory infections. - **Hyposmia**: Partial reduction in the ability to smell, frequently seen with normal aging or chronic nasal congestion. - **Parosmia (Dysosmia)**: Distortion in the perception of smell, where a familiar pleasant scent is perceived as foul or noxious. - **Phantosmia**: Perceiving an odor when no physical source is present, sometimes associated with temporal lobe seizures or psychiatric conditions.
