Mouth

Introduction and Overview of the Oral Cavity

The oral cavity serves as the primary entrance to the digestive tract and plays a vital role in communication, respiration, and initial digestion. Anatomically partitioned into the vestibule and the oral cavity proper, this space is lined with stratified squamous epithelium that withstands the mechanical friction of chewing. Beyond nutrition, the mouth acts as a secondary airway when nasal passages are obstructed and houses structures essential for speech articulation. In clinical settings across Nigeria, assessing the oral cavity is often the first step in a physical examination, providing immediate visual clues about hydration status, nutritional deficiencies, and systemic diseases.

Anatomical Boundaries and Structures of the Mouth

The boundaries of the oral cavity are defined by distinct muscular and skeletal structures that create a closed chamber during rest and manipulation.

  • Lips and Cheeks: Form the anterior and lateral walls, containing the orbicularis oris and buccinator muscles which keep food between the teeth.
  • Hard and Soft Palates: Form the roof of the mouth, separating the oral cavity from the nasal passages; the hard palate features bony support, while the soft palate ends in the uvula.
  • Floor of the Mouth: Consists of the mylohyoid muscles and supports the tongue.
  • Fauces: The arched opening at the back of the oral cavity that leads directly into the pharynx.

The Tongue: Structure, Function, and Taste Sensation

The tongue is a mobile muscular organ composed of intrinsic and extrinsic muscles covered in specialized mucous membrane. Its dorsal surface is dotted with papillae—some of which contain taste buds sensitive to sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami stimuli. Beyond gustation, the tongue manipulates food during mastication, gathers the chewed material into a bolus, and initiates swallowing by pushing it backward. It is also indispensable for phonation, shaping sounds produced by the larynx into intelligible speech. Cranial nerve XII (hypoglossal) provides motor innervation to the tongue, while sensory innervation is shared among cranial nerves V, VII, IX, and X.

Dentition: Structure and Types of Teeth

Human dentition is specialized for mechanical digestion, breaking down whole foods into smaller, manageable particles. Humans undergo two sets of teeth during their lifetime: deciduous (milk teeth) numbering 20, and permanent teeth numbering 32.

Structurally, each tooth consists of a visible crown, a neck, and roots anchored in the alveolar bone. The outer layer of the crown is covered by enamel, the hardest substance in the human body, protecting the underlying dentin and dental pulp.

Salivary Glands and Saliva Production

Saliva is secreted by three pairs of major salivary glands—parotid, submandibular, and sublingual—along with numerous minor glands scattered across the oral mucosa. Daily production ranges from one to one point five liters, driven by autonomic nervous system regulation. Parasympathetic stimulation promotes the secretion of a watery, enzyme-rich fluid, whereas sympathetic stimulation yields a thicker, mucous secretion.

Saliva is composed of water, electrolytes, mucus, antibacterial compounds like lysozyme, and the digestive enzyme salivary amylase, which initiates the breakdown of complex carbohydrates. Beyond digestion, saliva lubricates the oral cavity, maintains oral pH, and facilitates taste perception by dissolving food molecules.

Physiology of Mastication and Swallowing

Digestion begins in the oral cavity through coordinated mechanical and chemical processes. Mastication (chewing) breaks food down into smaller pieces, increasing surface area for enzymatic action. The process relies on the muscles of mastication—masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid, and lateral pterygoid—innervated by the mandibular branch of the trigeminal nerve.

Once mastication is complete, swallowing (deglutition) transitions from a voluntary act to an involuntary reflex. The tongue pushes the food bolus into the pharynx, triggering the swallowing reflex managed by the swallowing center in the medulla oblongata. Protective mechanisms, including the closure of the epiglottis and elevation of the soft palate, ensure food enters the esophagus safely without aspiration into the respiratory tract.

Clinical Significance and Common Oral Pathologies in Nursing Practice

The oral cavity serves as a mirror for overall health, often displaying early signs of systemic illness, nutritional deficiencies, and immune suppression. Nurses frequently encounter common conditions such as oral candidiasis (thrush), which presents as white, curdy patches on the mucosa often seen in immunocompromised patients or following broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Dental caries, gingivitis, and periodontitis are prevalent inflammatory conditions linked to poor oral hygiene and bacterial plaque accumulation. Furthermore, nutritional deficits like iron or vitamin B deficiencies can manifest as painful glossitis or angular cheilitis at the corners of the mouth. Maintaining oral hygiene is a fundamental nursing intervention that prevents ventilator-associated pneumonia in bedridden patients and promotes patient comfort.
