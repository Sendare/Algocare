Physiology of Digestion

Introduction to the Digestive System and Digestion

The human digestive system is fundamentally a continuous muscular tube, stretching from the oral cavity to the anus, supplemented by several exocrine and endocrine accessory organs. Its primary biological mandate is breaking down ingested macromolecules—proteins, carbohydrates, and lipids—into absorbable units capable of crossing cellular membranes to enter systemic circulation. This journey involves two parallel mechanisms: mechanical digestion, which physically fragments large food particles to increase surface area, and chemical digestion, which uses enzymatic hydrolysis to cleave molecular bonds. The entire gastrointestinal tract shares a uniform four-layered histological architecture from the esophagus downward: the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, and serosa. Each layer is specially modified across different anatomical segments to handle propulsion, secretion, or protection against self-digestion.

Ingestion and Mechanical Digestion in the Oral Cavity

Digestion begins in the oral cavity through mastication and salivation. Teeth tear, grind, and crush food, mixing it with saliva secreted by the parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands. Saliva is roughly 99.5 percent water, yet its remaining components initiate chemical processing immediately. Salivary amylase begins the breakdown of complex starches into maltose and maltotriose, while lingual lipase starts lipid hydrolysis, a process that continues later in the acidic environment of the stomach. Additionally, saliva contains mucus for lubrication, lysozyme and immunoglobulins for pathogen defense, and electrolytes that maintain oral pH. Once mastication is complete, the tongue rolls the processed food into a cohesive mass called a bolus, preparing it for the swallowing phase.

Deglutition and Esophageal Motility

Swallowing, or deglutition, is a complex reflex involving coordinated actions of the tongue, soft palate, pharynx, and esophagus. It occurs in three distinct phases:

  1. Voluntary buccal phase: The tongue pushes the bolus backward toward the oropharynx.
  2. Involuntary pharyngeal phase: The soft palate seals the nasopharynx, the larynx elevates, and the epiglottis folds down over the glottis to prevent aspiration into the respiratory tree.
  3. Involuntary esophageal phase: The upper esophageal sphincter relaxes, and the bolus enters the esophagus, propelled downward by peristalsis—sequential waves of circular and longitudinal smooth muscle contraction.

The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxes briefly to permit bolus entry into the stomach, then tightly constricts afterward to prevent the retrograde reflux of acidic gastric contents.

Gastric Secretions and Chemical Digestion in the Stomach

The stomach acts as a muscular mixing basin and temporary holding reservoir. Its mucosa is pitted with gastric glands containing several specialized cell types:

  • Mucous neck cells: Produce protective alkaline mucus guarding the gastric lining against self-digestion.
  • Parietal cells: Secretes hydrochloric acid (HCl), establishing a harsh acidic environment (pH 1.5–3.5) that denatures proteins and activates enzymes, alongside intrinsic factor necessary for vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum.
  • Chief cells: Release pepsinogen, which is converted into active pepsin upon contact with HCl to initiate protein cleavage.
  • G cells: Enteroendocrine cells releasing the hormone gastrin into the bloodstream to stimulate acid and enzyme secretion.

Mechanical churning combined with these secretions converts the solid bolus into a semi-liquid paste called chyme, which is gradually metered out into the duodenum through the pyloric sphincter.

Role of Accessory Organs: Liver, Gallbladder, and Pancreas

Chemical digestion in the small intestine relies heavily on secretions delivered from three major accessory digestive organs. The liver continuously synthesizes bile, an alkaline fluid containing water, electrolytes, bile salts, and bilirubin. While the liver produces bile, the gallbladder stores, concentrates, and releases it into the cystic duct in response to cholecystokinin stimulation. Bile salts are amphipathic molecules that perform emulsification, breaking large fat globules into microscopic droplets, vastly increasing the surface area for lipid-digesting enzymes. Concurrently, the pancreas contributes an alkaline juice rich in bicarbonate—which neutralizes acidic chyme arriving from the stomach—and a suite of powerful pancreatic enzymes including trypsinogen, pancreatic amylase, and pancreatic lipase.

Enzymatic Digestion and Nutrient Absorption in the Small Intestine

The small intestine—comprising the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum—is the primary site for both final chemical digestion and nutrient absorption. Its inner mucosal surface is massively amplified by circular folds, villi, and microscopic microvilli that form a brush border. Pancreatic enzymes break down complex nutrients further, while brush border enzymes complete the conversion of carbohydrates into monosaccharides and proteins into single amino acids.

Absorption occurs across the enterocytes via active transport or facilitated diffusion. Monosaccharides and amino acids enter the capillary blood within the villi and travel via the hepatic portal system directly to the liver. Conversely, digested lipids combine with bile salts to form micelles, diffuse into enterocytes, get reassembled into triglycerides, packaged into water-soluble chylomicrons, and enter the lymphatic lacteals rather than blood capillaries.

Water Absorption and Formation of Feces in the Large Intestine

By the time indigestible food residues reach the cecum and large intestine, most nutrients and water have already been reclaimed. The large intestine—consisting of the cecum, colon, rectum, and anal canal—specializes in absorbing remaining water, compacting waste, and hosting resident gut microbiota that ferment leftover carbohydrates and synthesize essential vitamins like Vitamin K and biotin. As water is continuously absorbed down osmotic gradients, liquid chyme transitions into semi-solid feces. Slow, rhythmic contractions called haustrations mix the contents, while mass movements propel feces toward the rectum. Distension of the rectal wall triggers the defecation reflex, coordinating involuntary relaxation of the internal anal sphincter and voluntary relaxation of the external anal sphincter to expel waste.

Neural and Hormonal Regulation of Gastrointestinal Functions

Gastrointestinal activity is tightly regulated by local neural networks and circulating hormones to ensure efficient processing. The enteric nervous system, often called the "brain of the gut," contains millions of neurons organized into the myenteric plexus (controlling motility) and submucosal plexus (controlling secretion). This intrinsic system can operate independently, though it is modulated by parasympathetic fibers (which stimulate digestion) and sympathetic fibers (which inhibit it). Additionally, enteroendocrine cells release key hormones into the bloodstream:

  • Gastrin: Stimulates gastric acid secretion and motility.
  • Secretin: Triggers pancreatic bicarbonate release to buffer acidic chyme.
  • Cholecystokinin (CCK): Stimulates gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme secretion while suppressing gastric emptying.
