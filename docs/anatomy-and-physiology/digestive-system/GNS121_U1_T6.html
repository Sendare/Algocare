Physiology of Digestion

Introduction to the Digestive System and Digestion

The human digestive system is essentially a long, continuous muscular tube called the gastrointestinal tract, stretching from the mouth to the anus, alongside several vital accessory organs. Its primary purpose is to break down large, complex food molecules into smaller, absorbable nutrients that cells can use for energy, growth, and repair.

Digestion relies on two main processes working together:

  • Mechanical digestion: Physical breakdown of food into smaller pieces through chewing, churning, and segmentation, which increases the surface area available for chemical attack.
  • Chemical digestion: Enzymatic breakdown of complex food polymers—like carbohydrates, proteins, and fats—into their basic chemical building blocks.

Once food is broken down into simple molecules, it undergoes absorption, passing through the epithelial lining of the intestinal wall into the blood and lymph vessels. Any material that cannot be digested or absorbed is eventually eliminated from the body through the process of defecation.

Ingestion and Mechanical Digestion in the Oral Cavity

Digestion starts the moment food enters the oral cavity. Ingestion is the active process of taking food and liquids into the body through the mouth. Once inside, the teeth and tongue perform mechanical digestion by masticating the food, crushing and tearing it into smaller, swallowable fragments.

At the same time, the three pairs of major salivary glands—parotid, submandibular, and sublingual—secrete saliva into the mouth. Saliva is composed mostly of water, which moistens the food and dissolves food molecules so we can taste them. Saliva also contains important enzymes:

  • Salivary amylase: Begins the chemical breakdown of complex starches into simpler sugars like maltose.
  • Lingual lipase: Activated later in the acidic environment of the stomach, it assists in breaking down dietary fats.

Mucus in saliva lubricates the chewed food, binding it together into a smooth, slippery mass called a bolus that is ready to be swallowed safely.

Deglutition and Esophageal Motility

Swallowing, medically known as deglutition, is a complex reflex that moves a food bolus from the mouth through the pharynx and into the esophagus. It occurs in three distinct phases: the voluntary oral phase, the involuntary pharyngeal phase, and the involuntary esophageal phase.

During the pharyngeal phase, the respiratory passages are temporarily blocked to prevent aspiration. The soft palate rises to seal the nasopharynx, and the epiglottis folds downward over the larynx, covering the glottis.

Once inside the esophagus, the food bolus travels downward not by gravity alone, but through coordinated waves of muscular contraction and relaxation known as peristalsis. Circular muscles behind the bolus contract while circular muscles ahead of it relax, pushing the food forward. At the junction between the esophagus and the stomach, the lower esophageal sphincter relaxes briefly to let the bolus pass into the stomach, then tightly closes again to prevent acidic gastric contents from splashing back upward into the esophagus.

Gastric Secretions and Chemical Digestion in the Stomach

The stomach acts as a muscular storage tank and processing station where chemical digestion truly intensifies. Its thick muscular wall churns the food and mixes it with powerful gastric juice, transforming the bolus into a creamy, semi-fluid paste called chyme.

Gastric juice is produced by specialized cells within the gastric glands:

  • Mucus neck cells: Secrete an alkaline mucus layer that protects the stomach lining from digesting itself.
  • Parietal cells: Produce hydrochloric acid, which creates a strongly acidic environment to kill ingested microbes and denature proteins, and intrinsic factor, which is essential for vitamin B12 absorption in the small intestine.
  • Chief cells: Secrete pepsinogen, an inactive enzyme that is converted into active pepsin by hydrochloric acid to break down proteins.
  • G cells: Release the hormone gastrin into the bloodstream to stimulate further acid and enzyme secretion.

Very few substances are absorbed directly through the stomach wall, though water, certain ions, alcohol, and some lipid-soluble drugs can pass into the bloodstream at this stage.

Role of Accessory Organs: Liver, Gallbladder, and Pancreas

Although food never directly enters the liver, gallbladder, or pancreas, these accessory organs produce and store essential secretions that empty into the duodenum to complete digestion.

The liver produces bile, a greenish-yellow fluid containing bile salts. Bile acts as a detergent, performing emulsification—breaking large fat globules into tiny droplets suspended in water, which massively increases the surface area for lipid-digesting enzymes. The gallbladder simply stores, concentrates, and releases this bile when fatty foods enter the small intestine.

The pancreas has both endocrine and exocrine functions, but its exocrine portion produces pancreatic juice. This fluid is packed with bicarbonate to neutralize acidic chyme from the stomach, alongside powerful digestive enzymes:

  • Pancreatic amylase for carbohydrates
  • Trypsin and chymotrypsin for proteins
  • Pancreatic lipase for fats
  • Nucleases for nucleic acids

Enzymatic Digestion and Nutrient Absorption in the Small Intestine

The small intestine is the absolute workhorse of the digestive tract. It is divided into the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, and it is here that the vast majority of chemical digestion and nutrient absorption takes place.

The internal structure of the small intestine is specially adapted for maximum absorption. Its lining features circular folds called plicae circulares, which are covered in tiny finger-like projections called villi. The microscopic cell membranes of these villi feature even smaller projections known as microvilli, collectively forming the brush border. This multi-layered structure creates a massive surface area roughly the size of a tennis court.

Enzymes embedded in the brush border complete the breakdown of carbohydrates into monosaccharides and proteins into amino acids. Monosaccharides and amino acids are then actively transported across the intestinal epithelium directly into the blood capillaries within each villus. Conversely, broken-down lipids are reassembled inside the mucosal cells into lipoproteins called chylomicrons, which enter the lymphatic vessels or lacteals because they are too large to pass directly into blood capillaries.

Water Absorption and Formation of Feces in the Large Intestine

By the time chyme reaches the large intestine—which includes the cecum, colon, rectum, and anal canal—almost all nutrients have already been absorbed. The primary job of the large intestine is to reclaim remaining water and electrolytes, turning liquid residue into semi-solid feces.

The large intestine houses trillions of resident bacteria known as the gut microbiota. These beneficial microorganisms ferment remaining undigested carbohydrates, producing gases and synthesizing vital vitamins such as vitamin K and certain B vitamins, which the host body then absorbs.

As water is gradually extracted through the colon wall, the remaining waste material condenses into feces, consisting of water, undigested fiber, bacteria, and sloughed-off epithelial cells. Peristaltic waves push these feces into the rectum, triggering the defecation reflex. This reflex relaxes the internal anal sphincter while conscious control allows voluntary relaxation of the external anal sphincter to expel the waste.

Neural and Hormonal Regulation of Gastrointestinal Functions

Digestive activities are tightly controlled by both the nervous system and local hormones to ensure secretions and motility match the presence and type of food consumed.

The digestive tract has its own dedicated nervous network called the enteric nervous system, often referred to as the 'second brain.' It controls local peristalsis and secretion independently, though it also communicates with the central nervous system via the vagus nerve. The parasympathetic nervous system generally stimulates digestion, while the sympathetic nervous system inhibits it during stress or physical exertion.

Endocrine cells scattered throughout the stomach and small intestine release key regulatory hormones into the bloodstream:

  • Gastrin: Stimulates gastric glands to secrete more hydrochloric acid and pepsinogen.
  • Secretin: Stimulates the pancreas to release bicarbonate-rich fluid to neutralize stomach acid.
  • Cholecystokinin (CCK): Stimulates the release of enzyme-rich pancreatic juice and causes the gallbladder to contract and release bile.
