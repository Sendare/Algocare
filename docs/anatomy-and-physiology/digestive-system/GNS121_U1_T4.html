Intestines

Introduction to Intestinal Anatomy

The intestines form a long, continuous muscular tube stretching from the pyloric sphincter of the stomach to the anus. This vital organ system is roughly nine meters long in an adult human and packs tightly into the abdominal cavity. Its primary duties include completing food breakdown, absorbing essential nutrients and water, and expelling waste products. Clinicians divide the intestines into two major sections based on diameter and function: the small intestine and the large intestine. Each section possesses unique structural features tailored to its specific workload.

Structure and Function of the Small Intestine

The small intestine is the longest segment of the gastrointestinal tract, divided sequentially into the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum. The duodenum receives acidic chyme from the stomach along with digestive enzymes and bile from the pancreas and liver. The jejunum handles the bulk of chemical digestion and nutrient absorption. The ileum absorbs remaining nutrients, particularly vitamin B12 and bile salts, before connecting to the large intestine at the ileocecal valve.

To maximize absorption, the internal lining features circular folds called plicae circulares, covered in tiny finger-like projections known as villi. Each villus cell further bristles with microscopic microvilli, creating a massive surface area equivalent to a tennis court.

Structure and Function of the Large Intestine

  • Cecum and Appendix: A pouch-like beginning of the large intestine where the appendix hangs.
  • Colon: Divided into ascending, transverse, descending, and sigmoid sections, responsible for absorbing water and electrolytes.
  • Rectum: A straight muscular tube that stores solid fecal waste before defecation.
  • Anal Canal: The terminal opening controlled by internal and external sphincters.

The large intestine has a wider diameter but is much shorter than the small intestine. Its outer longitudinal muscle forms three distinct bands called taeniae coli, which gather the colon into pouches known as haustra.

Histology and Microscopic Architecture

The intestinal wall shares a general four-layer plan throughout its length: the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, and serosa.

The mucosa contains epithelial cells interspersed with goblet cells that secrete protective mucus. Deep pits within the mucosal lining, known as crypts of Lieberkühn, secrete intestinal juices and house stem cells for constant tissue regeneration.

The submucosa houses blood vessels, lymphatics, and the submucosal nerve plexus. Beneath this lies the muscularis layer, arranged in inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle layers that drive peristalsis. The outermost layer, the serosa, provides a smooth, slippery membrane that reduces friction against other abdominal organs.

Physiology of Digestion and Nutrient Absorption

Chemical digestion relies on enzymes breaking complex food molecules down into tiny monomers. Carbohydrates split into monosaccharides, proteins break down into amino acids, and fats turn into fatty acids and monoglycerides.

Once broken down, nutrients cross the intestinal epithelium. Water-soluble nutrients like sugars and amino acids pass directly into the capillary blood within the villi, heading straight to the liver via the portal vein. Fats require a different pathway. Because fats do not mix easily with water, bile salts package them into tiny droplets called micelles. Enterocytes absorb them, reassemble them into triglycerides, and bundle them with proteins into chylomicrons, which enter specialized lymphatic vessels called lacteals.

Water and Electrolyte Balance in the Bowel

The digestive tract handles an enormous volume of fluid daily, including dietary intake and gastrointestinal secretions from salivary glands, stomach, pancreas, and liver. The small intestine absorbs the vast majority of this fluid, leaving about one to two liters to enter the large intestine.

The colon is exceptionally efficient at reclaiming water and electrolytes, particularly sodium and chloride. As sodium is actively pumped out of the lumen, water follows passively down an osmotic gradient. If food moves through the colon too quickly, insufficient water is reabsorbed, resulting in diarrhea. Conversely, sluggish movement allows excessive water extraction, leading to constipation.

Blood Supply, Innervation, and Lymphatic Drainage

The intestines receive a rich, redundant blood supply from two main branches of the abdominal aorta: the superior mesenteric artery supplying the small intestine and proximal colon, and the inferior mesenteric artery supplying the distal colon and rectum.

Nervous control is largely autonomous through the enteric nervous system, often called the body's second brain. It features two networks of neurons: the myenteric plexus for motility and the submucosal plexus for secretions. Parasympathetic input via the vagus nerve and sacral nerves enhances digestive activity, while sympathetic input from the thoracic spinal cord slows it down during stress. Lymphatic drainage parallels the blood vessels, filtering fluid through regional lymph nodes before emptying into the thoracic duct.

Clinical Correlations and Nursing Implications

Disruptions in intestinal function present frequently in clinical practice. Nurses must monitor bowel sounds, track abdominal distension, and record elimination patterns.

Conditions like paralytic ileus occur after abdominal surgery when peristalsis temporarily halts, requiring nursing vigilance to withhold oral intake until bowel sounds and passing flatus return. Inflammatory bowel diseases, such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, cause chronic pain, malabsorption, and altered fluid balance. Nurses play a crucial role in managing hydration, monitoring electrolyte labs, administering prescribed therapies, and educating patients on dietary modifications.
