Oesophagus

Anatomical Overview and Structural Layers

The oesophagus is a muscular, collapsible tube measuring about twenty-five centimetres in length, serving as the main conduit for food and liquids from the pharynx down to the stomach. It begins at the level of the sixth cervical vertebra, lying just behind the trachea, and descends through the superior and posterior mediastinum before piercing the diaphragm at the oesophageal hiatus.

Microscopically, the wall of the oesophagus features four distinct tissue layers from the inside out:

  • Mucosa: Lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, providing a tough protective surface against friction from passing food boluses.
  • Submucosa: Contains mucus-secreting glands that lubricate the lumen to ease food passage.
  • Muscularis propria: Composed of an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer, transitioning from skeletal muscle in the upper third to smooth muscle in the lower third.
  • Adventitia: A loose connective tissue layer anchoring the oesophagus to surrounding structures, lacking the serosal covering found on abdominal organs.

Anatomical Constrictions and Relations

Along its course through the neck, thorax, and abdomen, the oesophagus encounters four normal anatomical narrowings where foreign bodies are most likely to lodge or where instruments can cause accidental perforation during endoscopy.

  1. Pharyngooesophageal junction: Formed by the cricopharyngeus muscle at the upper oesophageal sphincter.
  2. Aortic arch crossing: Compressed where the aortic arch crosses the anterior and left side of the tube.
  3. Left main bronchus compression: Narrows where the left main bronchus crosses anteriorly.
  4. Diaphragmatic hiatus: Constricted as it passes through the muscular opening of the diaphragm.

In the thorax, the oesophagus maintains close spatial relations. Anteriorly, it relates to the trachea and heart's left atrium. Posteriorly, it rests against the vertebral column, thoracic duct, and descending aorta. Understanding these close anatomical neighbours helps explain why an enlarged left atrium can cause dysphagia.

Vascular Supply and Lymphatic Drainage

Blood supply to the oesophagus is segmented due to its long vertical course through multiple body cavities. The cervical portion receives blood from the inferior thyroid arteries. The thoracic portion gets branches directly from the descending thoracic aorta and bronchial arteries. The abdominal portion is supplied by branches of the left gastric artery and the inferior phrenic artery.

Venous drainage follows a dual pattern of immense clinical significance. The upper and middle thoracic veins drain into the systemic circulation via the azygos and hemiazygos systems. Conversely, the lower oesophageal veins drain into the portal venous system via the left gastric vein. This junction creates an important portosystemic anastomosis. When portal pressure rises in conditions like liver cirrhosis, these submucosal veins swell into fragile varices prone to life-threatening haemorrhage. Lymphatic drainage follows a longitudinal network, emptying into deep cervical, posterior mediastinal, and gastric lymph nodes.

Nerve Supply and Physiology of Swallowing

Control of oesophageal motility depends on both extrinsic neural inputs and an intrinsic enteric nervous system. The vagus nerve provides parasympathetic innervation, driving peristalsis and sphincter relaxation. Sympathetic fibers from the cervical and thoracic sympathetic chains modulate vascular tone and decrease glandular secretion.

Deglutition, or swallowing, occurs in three distinct phases:

  1. Oral phase: Voluntary movement of the chewed food bolus to the back of the pharynx by the tongue.
  2. Pharyngeal phase: Involuntary reflex action where the soft palate rises, the larynx elevates, the epiglottis folds down over the airway, and the upper oesophageal sphincter relaxes.
  3. Oesophageal phase: Coordinated peristaltic waves propel the bolus down toward the stomach.

Primary peristalsis is a continuation of the swallowing reflex, sweeping down the tube. If any food particles remain behind, local distension triggers secondary peristalsis independently of the brainstem, clearing the lumen entirely.

Oesophageal Sphincters and Reflux Prevention

Two muscular ring valves guard the entry and exit points of the oesophagus, ensuring unidirectional movement of food and protecting adjacent tissues from damage.

The Upper Oesophageal Sphincter (UOS) consists chiefly of the cricopharyngeus muscle. It remains tonically contracted at rest to prevent air from entering the gastrointestinal tract during inspiration, relaxing reflexively only during swallowing, belching, or vomiting.

The Lower Oesophageal Sphincter (LOS) is a high-pressure zone located at the gastro-oesophageal junction. Unlike a true anatomical sphincter with a distinct muscle ring, it functions primarily as a physiological sphincter maintained by intrinsic smooth muscle tone and reinforced externally by the right crus of the diaphragm. When the LOS loses its resting tone, gastric contents flow backward into the lower oesophagus, leading to mucosal irritation and classic symptoms of heartburn.

Clinical Disorders and Pathophysiology

Disorders of the oesophagus frequently manifest as dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), odynophagia (painful swallowing), or retrosternal chest pain.

  • Gastro-oesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD): Occurs from transient relaxations or weakness of the lower oesophageal sphincter, exposing the fragile squamous epithelium to corrosive gastric acid and causing chronic heartburn or mucosal erosion.
  • Achalasia: A motility disorder characterized by failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter to relax and the absence of normal peristalsis in the lower smooth muscle segment, causing food to back up and dilate the upper tube.
  • Oesophageal Varices: Dilated submucosal veins in the lower third caused by portal hypertension, carrying a severe risk of massive upper gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Hiatal Hernia: Protrusion of a portion of the stomach upward through the diaphragmatic hiatus into the thorax, which often worsens acid reflux.
