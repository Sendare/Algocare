Oesophagus

Anatomical Overview and Structural Divisions

The oesophagus is a muscular, collapsible tube measuring approximately 25 centimetres in length in the average adult. It acts as a primary conduit, connecting the pharynx to the stomach and facilitating the unidirectional passage of chewed food and liquids. Topographically, the organ is divided into three distinct segments based on its location: cervical, thoracic, and abdominal.

  • Cervical segment: Extends from the lower border of the cricoid cartilage down to the thoracic inlet, lying posterior to the trachea.
  • Thoracic segment: The longest portion, descending through the superior and posterior mediastinum behind the left main bronchus and the pericardium.
  • Abdominal segment: A very short terminal section measuring roughly one to two centimetres that passes through the diaphragm to empty into the gastric cardia.

Throughout its descent, the tube maintains a slight leftward deviation in the neck before returning to the midline in the thorax, finally curving anteriorly and to the left as it approaches the stomach.

Histology and Wall Architecture

The wall of the oesophagus is composed of four distinct histological layers from the lumen outward: the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria, and adventitia.

Notably, the muscularis propria transitions uniquely along its length: the upper third consists entirely of striated skeletal muscle under voluntary control, the middle third features a mixed composition of striated and smooth muscle, and the lower third is composed exclusively of involuntary smooth muscle.

Natural Anatomical Constrictions

Along its vertical pathway, the oesophagus encounters four distinct anatomical narrowings where external structures press against its walls. Clinically, these sites represent zones of heightened vulnerability where swallowed foreign bodies tend to lodge and where corrosive chemical ingestions cause the most severe localized tissue damage and subsequent stricture formation.

  1. Pharyngooesophageal constriction: Located at the upper oesophageal sphincter, formed by the cricopharyngeus muscle.
  2. Aortic constriction: Caused by the crossing of the aortic arch over the anterior and left lateral aspect of the tube.
  3. Bronchial constriction: Formed where the left main bronchus crosses anteriorly to enter the left lung.
  4. Diaphragmatic constriction: Created as the muscular hiatus of the diaphragm pinches the terminal pathway just above the stomach.

Vascular Supply and Venous Drainage

Blood delivery to the oesophagus is segmented according to its anatomical regions. The cervical portion receives arterial blood from the inferior thyroid arteries. The thoracic section is supplied directly by multiple small oesophageal branches arising from the descending thoracic aorta and intercostal arteries. Finally, the abdominal segment receives its blood supply from the left gastric artery, a direct branch of the celiac trunk, and the left inferior phrenic artery.

Venous drainage mirrors this regional layout but holds immense clinical importance due to portal-systemic anastomoses. Blood from the upper and middle segments drains into the azygos and hemiazygos veins, which empty into the superior vena cava. Conversely, veins from the lower mucosal segment drain into the left gastric vein, a tributary of the portal vein system. In conditions of portal hypertension such as liver cirrhosis, this junctional zone becomes a critical collateral pathway, engorging the submucosal vessels into fragile varices prone to massive upper gastrointestinal haemorrhage.

Neural Regulation and Motility Control

Coordinated movement along the oesophagus relies on both extrinsic neural pathways and an intrinsic enteric nervous system. The extrinsic control is managed by the vagus nerve (cranial nerve X), which provides parasympathetic innervation. Preganglionic vagal fibres synapse within the myenteric plexus, embedded between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers of the wall. Sympathetic input originates from cervical and thoracic sympathetic ganglia, which primarily modulate blood vessel tone and reduce secretory activity.

The intrinsic control operates through the enteric nervous system, specifically the myenteric plexus (Auerbach's plexus). This local network coordinates the sequential contraction of circular muscle behind a food bolus and simultaneous relaxation of circular muscle ahead of it, ensuring smooth, unidirectional propulsion toward the stomach without requiring conscious thought.

Mechanics of Swallowing and Peristalsis

Deglutition, or swallowing, occurs in three structured phases: the voluntary oral phase, the involuntary pharyngeal phase, and the oesophageal phase. Once a food bolus enters the upper oesophagus, the oesophageal phase takes over entirely via two distinct types of peristaltic waves.

Primary peristalsis is a continuation of the swallowing reflex initiated in the brainstem. A wave of contraction sweeps down the entire length of the tube, clearing the lumen in a matter of seconds. If a portion of the bolus adheres to the mucosal wall, localized distension triggers secondary peristalsis. This reflex is mediated locally by the enteric nervous system without brainstem intervention, continuing until the remaining material is successfully pushed into the stomach.

Function of Upper and Lower Sphincters

The upper and lower oesophageal sphincters act as high-pressure muscular valves that maintain luminal closure at rest, preventing both air intake during inspiration and the reflux of acidic gastric contents into the pharynx.

The upper oesophageal sphincter is a functional high-pressure zone formed primarily by the cricopharyngeus muscle. It remains tonically contracted during quiet respiration to prevent swallowed air from entering the gastrointestinal tract, relaxing only briefly during the pharyngeal phase of swallowing.

The lower oesophageal sphincter sits at the gastrooesophageal junction. Its resting tone is maintained by intrinsic smooth muscle contraction working in synergy with the right crus of the diaphragm, which pinches the tube externally. Transient inappropriate relaxation of this lower valve constitutes the primary mechanism behind gastrooesophageal reflux disease.

Clinical Pathophysiology and Common Disorders

Pathological conditions affecting the oesophagus frequently disrupt swallowing, mucosal integrity, or sphincter tone. Gastrooesophageal reflux disease arises when frequent transient relaxations of the lower sphincter expose the squamous lining to corrosive gastric acid, leading to heartburn, mucosal erosion, and potentially Barrett's oesophagus, a metaplastic change predisposing to malignancy.

Motility disorders also present significant clinical challenges. Achalasia is characterized by the degeneration of inhibitory neurons in the myenteric plexus, resulting in failure of the lower sphincter to relax during swallowing alongside a complete lack of coordinated peristalsis. This causes progressive dysphagia for both solids and liquids, causing marked dilatation of the organ above the obstructed junction.
