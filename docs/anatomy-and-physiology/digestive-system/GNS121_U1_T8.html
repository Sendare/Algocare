Metabolism

Introduction and Pathways of Metabolism

Metabolism refers to every single chemical reaction that happens inside the living body to keep us alive, growing, and repairing tissues. These chemical processes are divided into two opposing but cooperative sides: anabolism and catabolism.

  • Anabolism: The building-up side of metabolism. It takes small, simple molecules and uses energy to build larger, complex structures. Think of it like laying bricks to build a house. Examples include building muscle tissue from amino acids or storing glucose as glycogen.
  • Catabolism: The breaking-down side of metabolism. It takes large, complex molecules and breaks them down into smaller pieces, which releases the energy stored inside them. Think of this like tearing down an old wall to get usable firewood. Examples include digesting food or breaking down stored fat for fuel.

Together, these pathways ensure the body always has the building blocks and energy it needs for daily survival.

Role of ATP in Cellular Energy

Adenosine triphosphate, universally known as ATP, is the ultimate energy currency of the cell. Whenever a cell needs to do work—whether that is pumping ions across a membrane, contracting a muscle fiber, or building a new protein—it spends ATP.

Structurally, ATP consists of an adenosine molecule attached to a chain of three phosphate groups. The magic lies in the bonds holding those phosphate groups together, particularly the final two. These are high-energy bonds. When the cell needs energy, it breaks off the very last phosphate group, turning ATP into adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and releasing a burst of usable energy.

Cells do not keep massive stockpiled reserves of ATP because it is unstable for long-term storage. Instead, they constantly regenerate ATP from ADP by using the energy released during catabolic processes like glucose breakdown.

Carbohydrate Metabolism

Carbohydrates are the body's preferred and most immediate source of fuel. When we eat carbohydrates, digestion breaks them down into simple sugars, primarily glucose. Once glucose enters the bloodstream, it is shuttled into cells with the help of the hormone insulin.

Inside the cell, glucose goes through a series of steps to yield ATP:

  1. Glycolysis: Takes place in the cytoplasm, breaking one glucose molecule into two pyruvate molecules while generating a small amount of ATP without needing oxygen.
  2. Krebs Cycle (Citric Acid Cycle): Takes place inside the mitochondria, processing pyruvate further and releasing carbon dioxide.
  3. Electron Transport Chain: Also in the mitochondria, this final step produces the vast majority of cellular ATP, requiring oxygen to function properly.

If the body has more glucose than it immediately needs, it converts the extra sugar into glycogen through glycogenesis, storing it mainly in the liver and skeletal muscles. When blood sugar drops, glycogen is broken back down into glucose via glycogenolysis.

Lipid Metabolism

Lipids, primarily in the form of triglycerides, serve as the body's largest and most efficient long-term energy reserve. Gram for gram, fats provide more than double the amount of energy compared to carbohydrates or proteins.

When energy is needed, triglycerides are broken down through lipolysis into glycerol and fatty acids. Glycerol can enter the standard glycolysis pathway, while fatty acids undergo a process called beta-oxidation inside the mitochondria. Beta-oxidation chops the long fatty acid chains into two-carbon pieces that feed directly into the Krebs cycle, generating large amounts of ATP.

When carbohydrate supplies are very low—such as during starvation or uncontrolled diabetes—the liver processes excess fatty acids into ketone bodies. While the brain and heart can use ketones for fuel, high levels of ketones can acidify the blood, leading to a dangerous condition called ketoacidosis.

Protein Metabolism

Proteins are rarely used primarily for energy because their main job is building and maintaining body structures, enzymes, hormones, and antibodies. However, during times of prolonged starvation or extreme caloric restriction, the body will break down muscle and other tissues for fuel.

Unlike carbohydrates and fats, proteins contain nitrogen. When amino acids are broken down for energy or converted into other molecules, the nitrogen-containing amine group must be removed first in a process called deamination. This creates toxic ammonia. To protect the body, the liver swiftly converts this harmful ammonia into urea, which is then filtered out of the blood by the kidneys and excreted in the urine.

The remaining carbon skeletons from the broken-down amino acids can enter cellular respiration pathways to generate ATP or be converted into glucose or fats.

Basal Metabolic Rate and Energy Balance

Energy balance is the relationship between the calories taken into the body through food and the calories expended through daily living. Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) represents the minimum number of calories your body needs just to keep you alive while resting in a warm, fasting state—powering involuntary functions like breathing, heart beating, and brain activity.

Total daily energy expenditure is made up of three main components:

  1. BMR: Makes up the largest share (about 60-75%) of total daily calorie burning.
  2. Physical Activity: Includes all voluntary movements, from simple fidgeting to intense gym workouts.
  3. Thermic Effect of Food: The energy required by the body to digest, absorb, and process the nutrients you eat.

When calorie intake matches total energy expenditure, body weight remains stable. Consuming more calories than you burn leads to weight gain, while a sustained calorie deficit forces the body to burn stored fat and glycogen, resulting in weight loss.

Clinical Implications and Metabolic Disorders

Metabolic processes can sometimes falter due to genetic defects, hormonal imbalances, or lifestyle factors. Understanding these disorders helps nurses recognize clinical signs and provide proper patient care.

  • Diabetes Mellitus: A major metabolic disorder involving insulin. Type 1 diabetes is characterized by an autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing pancreatic beta cells, while Type 2 involves insulin resistance where cells fail to respond properly to insulin.
  • Phenylketonuria (PKU): A genetic metabolic disorder where the body lacks the enzyme needed to break down the amino acid phenylalanine, leading to toxic buildup and potential intellectual disability if untreated.
  • Metabolic Syndrome: A cluster of conditions—including high blood pressure, high blood sugar, excess waist fat, and abnormal cholesterol levels—that significantly increase the risk of heart disease and stroke.

Nurses monitor metabolic status through blood glucose checks, lipid panels, and electrolyte tests to catch imbalances early and guide therapeutic interventions.
