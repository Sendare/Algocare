Metabolism

Introduction to Metabolism and Energy Transformation

Metabolism encompasses every chemical reaction occurring within the human body to sustain life. These integrated biochemical pathways allow cells to harvest energy from ingested nutrients, synthesize structural components, and eliminate metabolic waste products. The body constantly shifts between energy storage and energy release depending on nutritional status and physical demand. Understanding these foundational processes is essential for recognizing how nutritional imbalances manifest clinically.

Anabolism and Catabolism: Dual Pathways

Metabolic processes are broadly divided into two opposing yet complementary categories: - **Anabolism**: Constructive pathways that build complex molecules from simpler precursors, such as synthesizing muscle protein from amino acids or storing glucose as glycogen. These reactions require an input of energy. - **Catabolism**: Destructive pathways that break down complex macromolecules into simpler units, such as splitting triglycerides into fatty acids or glycogen into glucose. These reactions release stored energy. Cellular homeostasis depends on a dynamic equilibrium between these two forces.

The Central Role of ATP

Adenosine triphosphate (ATP) serves as the primary energy currency of the cell. Structurally, ATP consists of an adenosine molecule attached to three phosphate groups. The high-energy bonds linking these phosphate groups store packets of chemical energy released during catabolism. When a cell requires energy for work—such as muscle contraction, active transport, or synthesis reactions—the terminal phosphate bond is cleaved, converting ATP to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate. This constant phosphorylation and dephosphorylation cycle underpins cellular vitality.

Carbohydrate Metabolism

Carbohydrates are the body's most accessible fuel source. Dietary carbohydrates are broken down into monosaccharides, primarily glucose, which enters the bloodstream. Cells take up glucose to generate ATP through glycolysis, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation. When glucose supply exceeds immediate energy demands, the liver and skeletal muscles convert it into glycogen via glycogenesis. Conversely, when blood glucose drops, glycogenolysis releases stored glucose back into circulation, and gluconeogenesis synthesizes new glucose from non-carbohydrate sources like amino acids and glycerol.

Lipid Metabolism

Lipids provide the most concentrated form of stored energy in the human body. Triglycerides are broken down through lipolysis into glycerol and free fatty acids. These fatty acids undergo beta-oxidation within the mitochondrial matrix, splitting into two-carbon acetyl groups that feed directly into the Krebs cycle to yield massive amounts of ATP. When carbohydrate intake is deficient, excess acetyl-CoA is shunted into hepatic ketogenesis, producing ketone bodies that serve as alternative fuel for the brain and heart.

Protein Metabolism

Unlike carbohydrates and fats, proteins are not stored in a specialized reserve pool; functional proteins exist primarily as structural tissue, enzymes, and hormones. Dietary proteins are hydrolyzed into amino acids, which are absorbed and utilized for anabolism. When amino acids exceed synthetic needs, they undergo deamination in the liver, where the amino group is removed and converted into urea for renal excretion. The remaining carbon skeleton is then converted into glucose or acetyl-CoA for energy production.

Basal Metabolic Rate and Energy Balance

Basal metabolic rate (BMR) represents the minimum energy expenditure required to maintain vital autonomic functions in a resting, postabsorptive state. Several variables influence BMR, including body composition, total surface area, age, sex, and thyroid hormone levels. Energy balance is achieved when total caloric intake equals total energy expenditure, which comprises BMR, the thermic effect of food, and physical activity. Positive energy balance results in weight gain through fat storage, whereas negative energy balance leads to weight loss.

Clinical Implications and Metabolic Disorders

Disruptions in metabolic pathways result in distinct clinical syndromes. Inborn errors of metabolism, such as phenylketonuria, arise from genetic enzyme deficiencies. Acquired metabolic disorders are far more prevalent globally. Type 2 diabetes mellitus involves cellular resistance to insulin, impairing glucose uptake and disrupting carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism. Similarly, starvation and uncontrolled diabetes can trigger diabetic ketoacidosis, a life-threatening state of metabolic acidosis driven by unchecked fat breakdown and ketone accumulation.
