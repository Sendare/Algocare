Stomach

Anatomical Location and Gross Regions of the Stomach

The stomach is a J-shaped, muscular enlargement of the gastrointestinal tract located in the upper left quadrant of the abdomen, just beneath the diaphragm. It acts as an expandable holding tank between the esophagus and the small intestine, allowing us to eat meals faster than we can digest them. Food enters the stomach from the esophagus through the cardiac sphincter, also called the lower esophageal sphincter, and leaves through the pyloric sphincter into the duodenum.

Grossly, the stomach is divided into four main regions:

  • Cardia: The small area immediately surrounding the superior opening where food enters from the esophagus.
  • Fundus: The dome-shaped region tucked beneath the diaphragm, sitting superior and lateral to the cardiac region.
  • Body: The large central portion of the stomach between the fundus and the curved inferior region.
  • Pylorus: The funnel-shaped terminal region that connects the stomach to the small intestine, consisting of a wider antrum and a narrow pyloric canal.

Histology and Microscopic Structure of the Stomach Wall

The wall of the stomach shares the general four-layer plan of the alimentary canal—mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, and serosa—with specialized adaptations for digestion and mechanical churning.

The mucosa is deeply folded into temporary ridges called rugae, which flatten out as the organ fills with food. Under the microscope, the mucosal surface is dotted with millions of microscopic pits called gastric pits, at the base of which lie tubular gastric glands. Unlike most of the digestive tract, the muscular layer of the stomach, the muscularis externa, features three layers of smooth muscle instead of two: an inner oblique layer, a middle circular layer, and an outer longitudinal layer. This unique extra third layer allows the stomach to contract in multiple directions, churning food vigorously and mixing it with powerful digestive juices to create a semi-liquid paste called chyme.

Gastric Secretions and Cellular Sources

The gastric glands secrete about two to three liters of gastric juice daily. This fluid is a mixture of water, mucus, hydrochloric acid, and important enzymes produced by distinct specialized cells residing within the gastric pits.

  • Mucus neck cells: Produce alkaline mucus that forms a protective coating to shield the stomach lining from self-digestion by acid.
  • Parietal cells: Large cells that secrete hydrochloric acid, which creates an acidic environment to kill swallowed microbes and activate protein-digesting enzymes, alongside intrinsic factor necessary for vitamin B12 absorption in the small intestine.
  • Chief cells: Secrete pepsinogen, the inactive precursor to the protein-splitting enzyme pepsin, which is activated upon contact with hydrochloric acid.
  • Enteroendocrine cells: Release local hormone messengers such as gastrin directly into the tissue fluid and blood to regulate digestion.

Physiology of Gastric Motility and Digestion

Mechanical and chemical digestion happen simultaneously inside the stomach. As food arrives, the stomach undergoes receptive relaxation, reflexively stretching to accommodate the volume without a dramatic spike in internal pressure.

Peristaltic waves begin gently near the fundus and grow stronger as they approach the pylorus. Each wave pushes chyme toward the closed pyloric valve; most of the mixture is blocked and squirted backward into the body of the stomach in a process called retropulsion, which thoroughly mashes solid particles. Only a small, well-liquefied milliliter or two of chyme passes through the pylorus into the duodenum with every single contraction. Carbohydrate digestion initiated by salivary amylase continues briefly in the stomach until gastric acid halts it, while protein digestion kicks into high gear thanks to pepsin.

Neural and Hormonal Regulation of Gastric Activity

Control of gastric secretion and motility is divided into three overlapping phases—cephalic, gastric, and intestinal—preventing chaos and ensuring digestive efficiency.

The cephalic phase is triggered by the sight, smell, thought, or taste of food. Signals sent via the vagus nerve prep the stomach by ramping up glandular secretions even before food arrives. Once food enters the organ, the gastric phase takes over, driven by stomach distension and rising pH. Stretch receptors and chemical sensors stimulate local enteric nerves and release gastrin, which powerfully spikes acid and enzyme output. Finally, the intestinal phase slows things down. As acidic chyme enters the duodenum, nervous reflexes and hormones like secretin and cholecystokinin inhibit gastric emptying, giving the small intestine adequate time to process incoming nutrients.

Clinical Correlations and Common Pathologies

Disruptions in the delicate balance between aggressive gastric juices and protective mucosal defenses lead to several common clinical conditions.

  • Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD): Erosion of the mucosal lining, most frequently caused by infection with the bacterium *Helicobacter pylori* or prolonged use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) that block protective prostaglandins.
  • Gastritis: Inflammation of the gastric mucosa, presenting as burning upper abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting.
  • Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD): Occurs when the lower esophageal sphincter relaxes inappropriately, allowing acidic stomach contents to splash upward and damage the esophageal lining.

A common clinical misconception is that stress and spicy foods are the primary causes of stomach ulcers; while they worsen symptoms, nearly all ulcers stem from *H. pylori* bacterial infection or medication side effects.
