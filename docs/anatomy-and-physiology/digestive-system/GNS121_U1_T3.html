Stomach

Anatomical Location and Gross Structure

The stomach is a muscular, J-shaped expansion of the gastrointestinal tract positioned in the upper left quadrant of the abdomen, directly inferior to the diaphragm. It occupies the epigastric, umbilical, and left hypochondriac regions. Anatomically, the organ is partitioned into four primary sections: the **cardia** surrounding the superior opening (gastroesophageal junction), the dome-shaped **fundus** projecting superiorly, the central **body** acting as the main mixing chamber, and the funnel-shaped **pylorus** leading into the duodenum. Two distinct borders help maintain its characteristic shape. The **lesser curvature** forms the shorter, concave medial border anchored by the lesser omentum, while the **greater curvature** forms the longer, convex lateral border attached to the greater omentum. Entry and exit are strictly controlled by two muscular sphincters: the **lower esophageal sphincter** at the top, preventing retrograde reflux of acidic chyme into the esophagus, and the **pyloric sphincter** at the bottom, regulating the rate of gastric emptying into the small intestine.

Microscopic Anatomy and Histology of the Gastric Wall

The stomach wall shares the standard four-layer tubular gut plan consisting of mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, and serosa, but exhibits specialized adaptations for digestion. The mucosa is thrown into longitudinal folds called **rugae**, which flatten as the stomach fills. Deep gastric pits indent the mucosal surface, opening into branched gastric glands. The muscularis externa is uniquely modified to include an innermost **oblique layer** of smooth muscle running alongside the standard circular and longitudinal layers. This third layer allows the stomach to churn, pummel, and physically break down food into a creamy paste known as chyme. The outermost layer is a serosa continuous with the visceral peritoneum.

Gastric Glands and Cellular Secretions

- **Mucose neck cells**: Produce a protective, alkaline mucus that coats the gastric epithelium to prevent self-digestion by acid and enzymes. - **Parietal cells**: Large, pyramidal cells located primarily in the middle zones of gastric glands that secrete hydrochloric acid and **intrinsic factor**, a glycoprotein essential for vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum. - **Chief cells**: Secrete **pepsinogen**, the inactive precursor of the proteolytic enzyme pepsin, alongside gastric lipase. - **Enteroendocrine cells**: Specialized cells, such as G-cells, scattered throughout the glands that release regulatory hormones like **gastrin** directly into the interstitial fluid and bloodstream.

Physiology of Motility, Digestion, and Absorption

Gastric motility is driven by pacesetter cells called interstitial cells of Cajal, which generate slow-wave electrical potentials. Peristaltic waves begin gently near the fundus and increase in force as they approach the pylorus. This rhythmic action pulverizes food and mixes it thoroughly with gastric juices. Chemical digestion focuses primarily on proteins, initiated by pepsin, and minimal lipid breakdown via gastric lipase. Carbohydrate digestion continues briefly from salivary amylase until gastric acid inactivates it. Absorption across the gastric mucosa is remarkably limited due to tight junctions and the thick mucus barrier, though water, certain ions, alcohol, and lipid-soluble medications like aspirin are absorbed in small quantities.

Neural and Hormonal Regulation of Gastric Secretion

Gastric activity is regulated across three overlapping phases: cephalic, gastric, and intestinal. 1. **Cephalic phase**: Triggered by the sight, smell, taste, or thought of food, mediated via the vagus nerve to prep the stomach. 2. **Gastric phase**: Activated by food entering the stomach through distension and rising pH, stimulating gastrin release and vigorous acid secretion. 3. **Intestinal phase**: Controlled by partially digested food entering the duodenum, which initially stimulates minor gastric secretion before triggering enterogastric reflexes and hormones (like secretin and CCK) to inhibit gastric emptying. Histamine, acetylcholine, and gastrin act synergistically on parietal cells to maximize acid output, while somatostatin acts as a paracrine inhibitor to shut down secretion when acidity rises too high.

Clinical Correlations and Common Pathologies

Disruptions in gastric homeostasis lead to notable clinical conditions. **Peptic ulcer disease** occurs when mucosal defenses are overwhelmed by aggressive factors such as *Helicobacter pylori* infection or prolonged nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug usage, potentially causing epigastric pain or life-threatening bleeding. **Gastritis** represents inflammation of the gastric mucosa, presenting with nausea and dyspepsia. In clinical pharmacology, proton pump inhibitors block the H+/K+ ATPase pump on parietal cells to effectively suppress acid production. Another critical clinical entity is **pernicious anemia**, an autoimmune condition destroying parietal cells, halting intrinsic factor production and causing vitamin B12 deficiency.
