Blood Groups

Introduction to Blood Groups

Blood groups are classification systems based on the presence or absence of inherited antigenic substances on the surface of red blood cells. These antigens can be proteins, carbohydrates, glycoproteins, or glycolipids, depending on the blood group system. The human body's immune system recognizes its own red blood cell antigens as self and mounts an aggressive defense against foreign antigens. Understanding these classifications prevents fatal immune reactions during blood transfusions, organ transplantation, and pregnancy. While over 30 human blood group systems are formally recognized by the International Society of Blood Transfusion, the **ABO system** and the **Rhesus system** remain clinically paramount in daily nursing practice across Nigerian healthcare facilities.

The ABO Blood Group System

The ABO system categorizes blood into four main types based on the presence or absence of **A** and **B** antigens on red blood cells, coupled with naturally occurring antibodies in the plasma. - **Group A**: Features A antigens on red blood cells and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. - **Group B**: Features B antigens on red blood cells and anti-A antibodies in the plasma. - **Group AB**: Features both A and B antigens on red blood cells with neither antibody in the plasma. - **Group O**: Lacks both A and B antigens on red blood cells, while containing both anti-A and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. Landsteiner's law dictates that normal healthy individuals possess plasma antibodies directed against the ABO antigens absent from their own red blood cells.

The Rhesus Factor System

The Rhesus (Rh) system is the second most important blood group classification, clinically driven primarily by the **RhD antigen**. Individuals who express the D antigen on their red blood cells are classified as **Rh-positive**, whereas those lacking the antigen are **Rh-negative**. Unlike the ABO system, individuals with Rh-negative blood do not naturally possess circulating anti-Rh antibodies in their plasma. These antibodies only develop after deliberate or accidental sensitization, such as exposure to Rh-positive red blood cells through a mismatched transfusion or fetomaternal hemorrhage during childbirth.

Blood Typing and Crossmatching Procedures

Before any blood component is transfused, rigorous laboratory testing ensures safety. **Forward grouping** tests the patient's red blood cells against known commercial antisera (anti-A, anti-B, anti-D) to identify surface antigens. **Reverse grouping** (or back typing) tests the patient's serum against known type A and type B red blood cells to confirm the presence of expected plasma antibodies. The **crossmatch** serves as the final functional safety check. A major crossmatch mixes the recipient's serum with the donor's red blood cells in vitro to detect any potential hemolytic incompatibilities before the transfusion bag ever reaches the ward.

Principles of Blood Transfusion Compatibility

Transfusion safety relies on ensuring that donor red blood cells will not be attacked by the recipient's plasma antibodies. **Type O negative** packed red blood cells lack A, B, and RhD antigens, making them the universal red cell donor for emergency situations when there is no time for complete crossmatching. Conversely, **Type AB positive** individuals are universal recipients for red blood cells because their plasma contains no antibodies against A, B, or Rh antigens. For plasma transfusions, the rules invert: Type AB is a universal plasma donor, while Type O is a universal plasma recipient.

Clinical Implications of Blood Incompatibility

Blood incompatibility triggers severe pathological consequences when mismatched cells interact with host antibodies. An **acute hemolytic transfusion reaction** occurs within minutes when recipient antibodies coat and destroy donor red blood cells, releasing free hemoglobin into the bloodstream, which can precipitate acute kidney injury, disseminated intravascular coagulation, and shock. In obstetrics, **hemolytic disease of the newborn** arises when an Rh-negative mother is sensitized by an Rh-positive fetus during a prior pregnancy or delivery. Her IgG anti-D antibodies cross the placenta in subsequent pregnancies, attacking and destroying the fetal red blood cells, leading to severe anemia and hydrops fetalis unless prevented with **Rho(D) immune globulin** prophylaxis.
