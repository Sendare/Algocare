Blood Groups

Introduction to Blood Groups

Blood groups are classifications of human blood based on the presence or absence of specific inherited protein molecules, called antigens, on the surface of red blood cells. These antigens can be proteins, carbohydrates, or glycoproteins. Our immune system uses them to recognize our own cells as "self" and foreign cells as "nonself." When the body encounters foreign red blood cell antigens, it produces antibodies to attack and destroy them. Understanding these markers prevents fatal immune reactions during blood transfusions.

The ABO Blood Group System

The ABO system categorizes blood into four major types based on two antigens (A and B) and two corresponding antibodies found in blood plasma.

  • Type A: Red blood cells carry A antigens; plasma contains anti-B antibodies.
  • Type B: Red blood cells carry B antigens; plasma contains anti-A antibodies.
  • Type AB: Red blood cells carry both A and B antigens; plasma contains neither antibody.
  • Type O: Red blood cells carry neither antigen; plasma contains both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.

An individual's plasma naturally contains antibodies against any ABO antigens that are missing from their own red blood cells.

The Rhesus Factor

The Rhesus (Rh) system is another crucial protein marker system on red blood cells, most notably the D antigen. If the D antigen is present on your red blood cells, your blood is Rh-positive. If it is absent, your blood is Rh-negative. Unlike the ABO system, people with Rh-negative blood do not automatically have antibodies against the Rh factor in their plasma from birth. Instead, antibodies only develop after actual exposure to Rh-positive blood, such as through a mismatched transfusion or during childbirth with an Rh-positive baby.

Blood Typing and Crossmatching Procedures

Before any transfusion takes place, laboratory scientists perform blood typing and crossmatching to ensure compatibility.

  1. Forward typing: Patient red blood cells are mixed with known commercial antibodies (anti-A and anti-B) to see if they clump together, or agglutinate.
  2. Reverse typing: Patient serum is mixed with known A and B red blood cells to check which natural antibodies are present.
  3. Crossmatching: Donor red blood cells are mixed directly with the recipient's serum in a test tube to catch any hidden antibody reactions before the blood goes into the patient's vein.

Principles of Blood Transfusion Compatibility

Safe transfusion relies on ensuring the donor's red blood cells do not get attacked by the recipient's plasma antibodies.

  • Universal Red Cell Donor: Type O-negative red blood cells lack A, B, and Rh antigens, meaning recipient antibodies have nothing to attack.
  • Universal Plasma Donor: Type AB individuals can donate plasma safely to any group because their plasma contains no anti-A or anti-B antibodies.
  • Universal Red Cell Recipient: Type AB-positive individuals can receive red blood cells from any group because their plasma has no natural antibodies to attack incoming foreign antigens.

Clinical Implications of Blood Incompatibility

When incompatible blood is transfused, a severe acute hemolytic transfusion reaction occurs. Recipient antibodies coat the donor red blood cells, causing them to rupture, or hemolyze, inside the blood vessels. Free hemoglobin spills into the bloodstream, clogging kidney tubules and triggering widespread inflammation. Common symptoms include fever, chills, lower back pain, chest tightness, and dark urine. Immediate nursing actions include stopping the transfusion at once, keeping the intravenous line open with normal saline using new tubing, and notifying the physician.
