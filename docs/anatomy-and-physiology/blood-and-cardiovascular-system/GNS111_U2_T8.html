Pulse

Understanding Pulse and Cardiovascular Dynamics

A pulse is the palpable bound or expansion of an arterial wall that occurs with each heartbeat. When the left ventricle of the heart contracts, it forces a wave of blood into the already full arterial system. This pressure wave travels much faster than the actual flow of blood, creating a rhythmic tap that nurses can feel wherever a major artery crosses a bone or firm tissue.

Studying the pulse gives direct insight into how well the heart is pumping and how elastic the blood vessels are. It reflects cardiovascular efficiency, cardiac output, and peripheral tissue perfusion. Nurses monitor this vital sign routinely to establish a baseline, track response to medications, or detect sudden clinical deterioration.

Anatomical Sites for Pulse Assessment

Nurses assess pulses at various parts of the body depending on the clinical situation, patient age, and accessibility.

  • Temporal: Located over the temporal bone of the head, useful when facial or head injuries are evaluated.
  • Carotid: Located in the neck along the medial edge of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Used heavily during emergency CPR assessments.
  • Apical: Located at the apex of the heart (5th intercostal space, left midclavicular line). Assessed with a stethoscope for a full minute, especially when giving heart medications.
  • Brachial: Located in the inner aspect of the biceps or medial antecubital fossa. Standard site for measuring blood pressure in infants.
  • Radial: Located on the thumb side of the inner wrist. The most common site for routine pulse checks in conscious adults.
  • Femoral: Located in the groin groin crease beneath the inguinal ligament, used during major circulatory assessments.
  • Popliteal: Located behind the knee joint in the popliteal fossa, checked when assessing lower leg blood flow.
  • Dorsalis Pedis: Located on the top surface of the foot, crucial for checking circulation to the lower extremities.

Factors Affecting Pulse Rate and Rhythm

Many physiological and environmental variables alter a person's pulse. A nurse must account for these factors before deciding if an abnormal reading requires intervention.

  • Age: Newborns have very rapid resting heart rates, which gradually slow down as they grow into adulthood.
  • Exercise: Physical exertion increases metabolic demand, prompting the heart to beat faster to supply more oxygen.
  • Fever and Infection: Rising body temperature increases metabolic rate, typically raising the heart rate significantly.
  • Stress and Emotion: Sympathetic nervous system activation releases adrenaline, causing the heart rate to climb.
  • Medications: Drugs like digoxin or beta-blockers slow the pulse down, while stimulants like epinephrine speed it up.
  • Blood Loss: Severe hemorrhage reduces blood volume, causing the body to compensate by increasing the heart rate.

Techniques and Guidelines for Measuring Pulse

Accurate pulse measurement is a foundational clinical skill. Nurses should use the pads of the first two or three fingers—never the thumb, because the thumb has its own distinct pulse that can be confused with the patient's heartbeat.

When a pulse is regular, count the beats for 30 seconds and multiply by two. If the pulse is irregular, weak, or if the patient is unstable, count for a full 60 seconds to catch dropped beats or subtle variations. Rate is recorded in beats per minute (bpm). Alongside rate, the nurse must evaluate rhythm (regular or irregular) and volume/strength, often graded on a scale from 0 (absent) to 3+ (bounding).

Characteristics of Normal and Abnormal Pulses

A normal resting pulse in an adult typically ranges from 60 to 100 beats per minute with a regular, steady rhythm. Deviations from this range carry specific clinical terminology.

  • Tachycardia: A resting heart rate above 100 beats per minute.
  • Bradycardia: A resting heart rate below 60 beats per minute, though well-conditioned athletes may naturally have lower resting rates.
  • Arrhythmia: A pulse with an irregular rhythm, where beats occur at unpredictable intervals.
  • Bounding Pulse: A very strong, forceful pulse (3+) often caused by exercise, anxiety, or high blood pressure.
  • Thready Pulse: A weak, barely perceptible pulse (1+) often seen in shock, dehydration, or heart failure.

Clinical Implications and Nursing Management

Abnormal pulse findings often serve as an early warning sign of clinical deterioration. A sudden rise in heart rate can indicate internal bleeding, sepsis, pain, or respiratory distress before blood pressure drops. Conversely, a dangerously low heart rate can cause dizziness, fainting, or inadequate organ perfusion.

When a nurse identifies an abnormal pulse, immediate action involves checking for associated symptoms like chest pain, shortness of breath, or pale skin. The nurse must review the patient's current medication chart, check recent lab results, and notify the physician promptly if the findings are acute or worsening.
