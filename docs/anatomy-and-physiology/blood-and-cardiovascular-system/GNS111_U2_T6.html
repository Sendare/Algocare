Blood Pressure

Introduction to Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is the lateral force exerted by circulating blood on the walls of the body's arteries. It is generated primarily by the pumping action of the heart combined with the resistance offered by the vascular bed. Clinically, it is expressed as two values: **systolic blood pressure**, representing the peak pressure in the arteries during ventricular contraction, and **diastolic blood pressure**, representing the lowest pressure during ventricular relaxation. In Nigerian clinical practice, measuring this parameter remains a foundational baseline skill for assessing cardiovascular health in both emergency and routine outpatient settings. Understanding this physiological dynamic allows clinicians to evaluate perfusion adequacy to vital organs such as the brain, kidneys, and heart.

Physiology and Mechanisms of Blood Pressure Regulation

Blood pressure is governed by a delicate balance between cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance. If either component increases without a compensatory change, blood pressure rises. - **Autonomic Nervous System**: Rapidly adjusts vascular tone and heart rate through sympathetic stimulation (raising pressure) and parasympathetic action (lowering pressure). - **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS)**: A hormonal cascade responding to lowered renal blood flow, retaining sodium and water while vasoconstricting vessels to restore volume and pressure. - **Baroreceptors**: Specialized stretch receptors located in the carotid sinuses and aortic arch that detect pressure fluctuations and signal the brainstem to modify vasomotor activity. - **Kidneys**: Provide long-term regulation by excreting or retaining fluid to alter total blood volume.

Classification and Normal Ranges of Blood Pressure

Clinical classification standardizes how readings are interpreted to guide treatment decisions. Normal adult blood pressure generally falls below 120/80 mmHg. Techniques for Accurate Blood Pressure Assessment

Inaccurate blood pressure measurement leads to misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment. To secure a reliable reading, the patient should rest quietly for at least five minutes with their feet flat on the floor and arm supported at heart level. Selecting the correct cuff size is non-negotiable; using a cuff that is too small produces falsely elevated readings, while an oversized cuff yields falsely low numbers. The bladder of the cuff must encircle at least eighty percent of the arm. Inflate the cuff beyond the point where the radial pulse disappears, then deflate it gradually while listening with the stethoscope over the brachial artery. Note the first tapping sound as the systolic pressure and the disappearance of sounds as the diastolic pressure.

Hypertension: Causes, Risk Factors, and Pathophysiology

Hypertension is widely categorized into primary (essential) hypertension, which has no identifiable secondary cause and accounts for the vast majority of cases, and secondary hypertension, caused by an underlying medical condition such as renal artery stenosis or endocrine disorders. Non-modifiable risk factors include advancing age, family history, and genetic predisposition. Modifiable drivers feature high dietary sodium intake, sedentary lifestyle, obesity, chronic stress, and excessive alcohol consumption. Pathophysiologically, sustained high pressure damages the endothelial lining of blood vessels, promoting plaque accumulation, arterial stiffening, and increased resistance that further amplifies the hypertensive state.

Hypotension: Causes and Clinical Implications

Hypotension occurs when systemic blood pressure falls below normal limits, typically defined as a systolic reading under 90 mmHg or a diastolic reading under 60 mmHg. While some healthy athletes maintain low baselines without symptoms, acute hypotension deprives vital organs of oxygenated blood. Common causes include hypovolemia from dehydration or hemorrhage, severe systemic infection, cardiac pump failure, and autonomic dysfunction. Orthostatic hypotension is a specific form characterized by a significant drop in blood pressure when moving from a lying or sitting position to a standing posture, frequently causing dizziness, lightheadedness, and risk of falls.

Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Management

Managing blood pressure disorders requires a dual approach combining lifestyle modifications and medication regimens. Non-pharmacological strategies form the foundation of care, including adopting the DASH diet rich in fruits and vegetables, restricting sodium, engaging in regular aerobic exercise, achieving weight loss, and moderating alcohol intake. When lifestyle changes are insufficient, pharmacotherapy is initiated. Commonly prescribed drug classes include: - **Diuretics**: Lower blood volume by promoting renal sodium and water excretion. - **ACE Inhibitors**: Prevent the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II to induce vasodilation. - **Calcium Channel Blockers**: Relax vascular smooth muscle by blocking calcium influx. - **Beta-Blockers**: Reduce cardiac output and heart rate by blocking sympathetic stimulation.

Complications of Untreated Blood Pressure Disorders

Chronic, untreated hypertension exerts relentless mechanical stress on the cardiovascular system and target organs. Over time, this damage manifests as left ventricular hypertrophy as the heart muscle thickens to overcome high peripheral resistance. Brain tissue suffers from accelerated atherosclerosis and microvascular aneurysm formation, vastly increasing the risk of ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke. Renal vessels experience sclerosis, progressively destroying nephrons and culminating in chronic kidney disease. Conversely, severe hypotension can cause acute ischemic injury to the kidneys and brain due to prolonged hypoperfusion, leading to shock and multi-organ failure if not promptly corrected.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Education

Nurses play a pivotal role in monitoring hemodynamic stability, managing therapies, and empowering patients to control their vascular health. Clinical responsibilities include performing serial blood pressure measurements, tracking daily fluid balance, and monitoring for adverse drug reactions like electrolyte imbalances or dizziness. Patient education should emphasize the silent nature of hypertension, reinforcing that individuals often feel completely fine despite dangerously high readings. Nurses must teach patients how to properly measure blood pressure at home, adhere strictly to prescribed medication schedules without abrupt cessation, and recognize warning signs of cardiovascular distress.
