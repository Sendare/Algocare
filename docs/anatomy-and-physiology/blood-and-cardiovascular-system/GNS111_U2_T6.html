Blood Pressure

Introduction to Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is the physical force exerted by circulating blood against the inner walls of the body's arteries. As the heart pumps, it drives blood through the systemic circulation, creating this pressure. Two main values define a routine reading. Systolic pressure measures the peak force created when the ventricles contract and push blood out. Diastolic pressure measures the resting force in the arteries when the heart relaxes between beats. Clinicians record these values in millimeters of mercury (mmHg) as a fraction, with the systolic number written over the diastolic number.

Physiology and Mechanisms of Blood Pressure Regulation

Maintaining stable blood pressure requires a complex interplay between cardiac output, peripheral vascular resistance, and blood volume. Cardiac output is the amount of blood pumped by the heart per minute, determined by heart rate and stroke volume. Peripheral vascular resistance refers to the opposition to blood flow caused by the constriction or dilation of blood vessels.

Several rapid and long-term systems control these variables:

  • Baroreceptors: Specialized nerve endings in the carotid sinuses and aortic arch detect pressure changes and signal the brainstem to adjust heart rate and vessel tone.
  • Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS): A slower hormonal cascade activated by the kidneys when blood flow drops, leading to sodium and water retention alongside vasoconstriction.
  • Kidneys: Regulate long-term pressure by excreting or conserving fluid to manage total blood volume.

Classification and Normal Ranges of Blood Pressure

Blood pressure classifications help clinicians identify healthy states and spot early warning signs of cardiovascular disease. A normal reading for a resting adult generally falls below 120/80 mmHg. When readings creep above this threshold, patients are categorized into elevated blood pressure, stage 1 hypertension, or stage 2 hypertension.

Techniques for Accurate Blood Pressure Assessment

Obtaining an accurate blood pressure reading requires careful attention to preparation and technique. Errors in measurement can easily lead to misdiagnosis or inappropriate treatment adjustments. Before inflating the cuff, ensure the patient has rested quietly for at least five minutes with their feet flat on the floor and arm supported at heart level.

The cuff size is critical. Using a cuff that is too small falsely elevates the reading, while a cuff that is too large gives a falsely low result. Inflate the cuff until the pulse disappears, then slowly deflate it while listening for Korotkoff sounds through a stethoscope. The first tapping sound indicates the systolic pressure, and the disappearance of sounds marks the diastolic pressure.

Hypertension: Causes, Risk Factors, and Pathophysiology

Hypertension, commonly known as high blood pressure, is often categorized as either primary or secondary. Primary hypertension has no single identifiable cause and develops gradually over many years, linked to genetics, aging, and lifestyle habits. Secondary hypertension appears suddenly and stems from an underlying medical condition, such as renal artery stenosis, endocrine disorders, or certain medications.

Over time, sustained high pressure damages the delicate endothelial lining of blood vessels, accelerating atherosclerosis. This narrowing and stiffening forces the heart to work harder, eventually leading to left ventricular hypertrophy and heart failure.

Hypotension: Causes and Clinical Implications

Hypotension occurs when blood pressure drops below normal limits, typically defined as readings lower than 90/60 mmHg. While some healthy individuals naturally run low readings without symptoms, acute drops can deprive vital organs of oxygen and nutrients. Common causes include dehydration, acute blood loss, severe infection, cardiac arrhythmias, and certain antihypertensive medications.

A frequent clinical presentation is orthostatic hypotension, characterized by a significant drop in blood pressure when a person stands up quickly. Patients often report dizziness, lightheadedness, or fainting spells, which greatly increase their risk for falls and injuries.

Pharmacological and Non-Pharmacological Management

Managing blood pressure involves a combination of lifestyle modifications and targeted medications. Initial therapy for hypertension often emphasizes non-pharmacological approaches, including adopting the DASH diet, reducing sodium intake, maintaining a healthy weight, exercising regularly, and limiting alcohol consumption.

When lifestyle changes are insufficient, healthcare providers prescribe antihypertensive medications. Common drug classes include:

  • Diuretics: Help the kidneys eliminate excess sodium and water to reduce blood volume.
  • ACE inhibitors: Prevent the conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II, causing blood vessels to dilate.
  • Beta-blockers: Lower heart rate and reduce cardiac output.
  • Calcium channel blockers: Relax and widen blood vessels by preventing calcium from entering muscle cells.

Complications of Untreated Blood Pressure Disorders

Untreated or poorly controlled blood pressure inflicts silent damage across multiple organ systems over many years. Chronic hypertension forces the heart to strain against high resistance, leading to coronary artery disease, heart failure, and myocardial infarction. In the brain, weakened vessels can rupture or block, resulting in transient ischemic attacks or debilitating strokes.

Kidneys are also highly vulnerable. The delicate filtering units experience nephrosclerosis, which gradually impairs renal function and can progress to chronic kidney disease or total kidney failure. Vision loss can also occur if retinal blood vessels burst or sustain damage from high pressure.

Nursing Interventions and Patient Education

Nurses play a central role in monitoring blood pressure, assessing treatment effectiveness, and educating patients. Routine assessments must follow standardized protocols to catch abnormal trends early. When caring for patients on antihypertensive medications, nurses monitor for adverse effects like dizziness, electrolyte imbalances, or orthostatic hypotension.

Education empowers patients to take charge of their health. Nurses teach individuals how to measure their blood pressure at home correctly, keep a detailed log of readings, recognize red flag symptoms, and understand the importance of taking prescribed medications consistently without stopping them abruptly.
