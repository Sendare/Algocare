The Heart

Anatomy and Chambers of the Heart

The human heart is a hollow, muscular organ roughly the size of a closed fist, located safely in the middle of the chest cavity behind the breastbone. It works tirelessly as a double pump to circulate blood throughout the body. The internal structure is divided right down the middle by a thick muscular wall called the septum, separating the organ into two distinct sides that never mix their contents.

Each side contains two chambers: a smaller, thin-walled upper chamber called an atrium, and a larger, muscular lower chamber called a ventricle.

  • Right Atrium: Receives used, deoxygenated blood coming back from the entire body via the large veins known as the venae cavae.
  • Right Ventricle: Pushes that deoxygenated blood out through the pulmonary artery straight to the lungs to pick up fresh oxygen.
  • Left Atrium: Collects newly oxygenated blood returning from the lungs through the pulmonary veins.
  • Left Ventricle: Pumps oxygen-rich blood out through the aorta to supply the brain, organs, and tissues everywhere else.

Because the left ventricle has to push blood all the way around the body, its muscular wall is significantly thicker than that of the right ventricle.

Heart Valves and Blood Flow Direction

To ensure blood only moves forward and never flows backward, the heart relies on a set of specialized fibrous flaps called heart valves. These valves act like one-way doors that open and close passively in response to pressure changes inside the chambers.

  1. Tricuspid Valve: Located between the right atrium and the right ventricle, featuring three cusps.
  2. Mitral (Bicuspid) Valve: Located between the left atrium and the left ventricle, featuring two cusps.
  3. Aortic Valve: Situated at the base where the aorta leaves the left ventricle.
  4. Pulmonary Valve: Situated at the base where the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle.

The tricuspid and mitral valves are known as atrioventricular (AV) valves. They are anchored securely to the ventricle walls by fibrous cords called chordae tendineae, which attach to small finger-like muscles named papillary muscles. When ventricles squeeze hard, these cords stop the valve flaps from blowing backward into the atria. The aortic and pulmonary valves are called semilunar valves because their pockets look like half-moons.

The Cardiac Cycle and Heart Sounds

The cardiac cycle describes all the mechanical and electrical events that happen during one complete heartbeat. It is split into two main phases: systole (when the heart muscle contracts and pumps blood) and diastole (when the heart muscle relaxes and fills with blood).

Starting with atrial diastole and ventricular filling, blood passively flows from the atria into the ventricles. Near the end of this phase, the atria contract to squeeze the final bit of blood downward. Next, ventricular systole begins. As the ventricles contract, pressure inside them shoots up, snapping the AV valves shut to prevent backflow. This sudden closure produces the first heart sound, known clinically as S1 or the "lub" sound.

As ventricles continue squeezing, pressure exceeds that in the aorta and pulmonary artery, forcing the semilunar valves open. Once the ventricles empty, they relax during ventricular diastole. Pressure drops below that of the great arteries, causing the semilunar valves to snap shut, which creates the second heart sound, S2 or the "dub" sound.

Conducting System of the Heart

Unlike skeletal muscles that need a nerve signal from the brain to move, the heart can generate its own electrical impulses through a specialized network called the cardiac conduction system. This built-in wiring allows the heart to beat rhythmically on its own.

  • Sinoatrial (SA) Node: Known as the natural pacemaker of the heart, located in the upper wall of the right atrium. It fires electrical signals faster than any other part of the heart.
  • Atrioventricular (AV) Node: Located near the bottom of the right atrium, this node pauses the electrical signal briefly to give ventricles enough time to fill with blood.
  • Bundle of His: The pathway that carries the electrical signal from the atria down into the muscular septum.
  • Bundle Branches: Left and right pathways that sprint down either side of the septum toward the apex of the heart.
  • Purkinje Fibers: Tiny electrical fibers that spread the signal upward through the ventricular walls, causing a synchronized, powerful squeeze from the bottom up.

Electrocardiography and Heart Rhythms

An electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) is a painless medical test that records the electrical activity of the heart over time using electrodes placed on the skin. It does not measure mechanical pumping strength directly, but rather shows the electrical waves spreading through the heart muscle.

A normal single heartbeat tracing consists of three main graphical features:

  • P wave: Represents electrical depolarization spreading through the atria, causing them to contract.
  • QRS complex: Represents electrical depolarization racing through the ventricles, causing a large ventricular squeeze. This wave is tall because the muscle mass of the ventricles is large.
  • T wave: Represents ventricular repolarization, which is the electrical recovery phase as the muscle relaxes.

Healthcare workers inspect an ECG to spot abnormal rhythms, electrolyte imbalances, or damage from a heart attack. For example, if a section of the heart muscle lacks blood flow, specific segments of the ECG tracing will look abnormally elevated or depressed.

Coronary Circulation and Blood Supply

Even though the heart pumps gallons of blood every day, the chambers are filled with blood that the heart muscle itself cannot absorb for nourishment. The myocardium relies entirely on its own dedicated highway system of blood vessels known as the coronary circulation.

Two main vessels, the left coronary artery and the right coronary artery, branch directly off the base of the aorta just above the aortic valve. They wrap around the outside of the heart like a crown, branching into smaller capillaries that deliver oxygen and nutrients to every single muscle fiber. Once oxygen is used up, deoxygenated blood is collected by cardiac veins that empty into a large channel on the back of the heart called the coronary sinus, which dumps the blood straight back into the right atrium.

When a coronary artery becomes blocked by a blood clot or fatty plaque, oxygen cannot reach that part of the muscle. This lack of blood flow is called ischemia, and if it lasts too long, the affected heart muscle tissue dies, resulting in a myocardial infarction, commonly known as a heart attack.
