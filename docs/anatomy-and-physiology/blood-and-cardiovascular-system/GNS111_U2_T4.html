The Heart

Anatomical Structure and Chambers of the Heart

The human heart is a hollow, muscular organ roughly the size of a clenched fist, situated centrally in the thoracic cavity within the mediastinum. Its base lies superiorly beneath the second rib, while the apex points inferiorly, anteriorly, and to the left, resting near the fifth intercostal space. Internally, the heart is divided into four distinct chambers: two thin-walled atria that receive returning blood, and two thick-walled ventricles that pump blood outward.

  • Right Atrium: Receives deoxygenated blood from the superior vena cava, inferior vena cava, and coronary sinus.
  • Right Ventricle: Pumps deoxygenated blood through the pulmonary semilunar valve into the pulmonary trunk.
  • Left Atrium: Receives oxygenated blood from the four pulmonary veins originating from the lungs.
  • Left Ventricle: Pumps oxygenated blood through the aortic semilunar valve into the systemic aorta.

Muscular walls separate these chambers. The interatrial septum divides the right and left atria, whereas the interventricular septum separates the ventricles. The wall of the left ventricle is notably thicker than that of the right because it must generate enough pressure to force blood through the entire systemic circulation, whereas the right ventricle only needs to overcome the low-resistance pulmonary circuit.

Heart Valves and Direction of Blood Flow

Unidirectional blood flow through the heart is maintained by four distinct valves. These structures open and close passively in response to pressure gradients created during myocardial contraction and relaxation.

Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between the atria and ventricles. The tricuspid valve sits on the right side, possessing three cusps, while the bicuspid or mitral valve sits on the left with two cusps. Chordae tendineae, anchored to papillary muscles projecting from the ventricular walls, attach to the AV valve cusps. During ventricular contraction, these fibrous cords tense up to prevent the cusps from everting back into the atria.

Semilunar valves guard the exits from the ventricles. The pulmonary valve is located at the base of the pulmonary artery, and the aortic valve sits at the base of the aorta. Each consists of three pocket-like cusps that catch backflowing blood, snapping shut to prevent regurgitation into the relaxing ventricles.

Coronary Circulation and Blood Supply

Although the heart chambers are constantly filled with blood, the myocardium is too thick to absorb nutrients and oxygen by simple diffusion from the inside out. Instead, the heart relies on its own dedicated vascular network known as the coronary circulation.

Arterial blood supply originates from the base of the aorta just above the aortic valve via the left and right coronary arteries.

  • Left Coronary Artery: Branches into the anterior interventricular artery and the circumflex artery, supplying the left atrium and most of the left ventricle.
  • Right Coronary Artery: Supplies the right atrium, parts of both ventricles, and crucially, the nodes of the heart's conduction system in many individuals.

Deoxygenated blood from the myocardium is collected by cardiac veins, which converge into the coronary sinus—a large venous channel situated in the posterior sulcus that empties directly into the right atrium.

The Conducting System of the Heart

The heart possesses specialized autorhythmic cardiac muscle fibers that generate and rapidly propagate electrical impulses independently of the central nervous system. This intrinsic conduction system ensures coordinated and efficient mechanical contractions.

  1. Sinoatrial (SA) Node: Located in the posterior wall of the right atrium near the superior vena cava, it sets the baseline pace as the natural pacemaker of the heart.
  2. Atrioventricular (AV) Node: Situated in the lower floor of the right atrium, it briefly delays the electrical impulse to allow the atria to finish emptying their blood into the ventricles.
  3. Bundle of His (AV Bundle): The sole electrical bridge transmitting impulses from the atria down into the interventricular septum.
  4. Right and Left Bundle Branches: Course down the interventricular septum toward the apex.
  5. Purkinje Fibers: Large terminal fibers that rapidly spread the excitation wave upward through the ventricular myocardium.

The Cardiac Cycle

The cardiac cycle encompasses all the mechanical and electrical events occurring during one single heartbeat. It is divided into two primary phases: systole (contraction and emptying) and diastole (relaxation and filling).

  • Atrial Systole: The atria contract, pushing the remaining blood volume through open AV valves into the ventricles.
  • Ventricular Systole (Isovolumetric Contraction): Ventricles begin to contract, raising pressure and snapping the AV valves shut (creating the first heart sound, S1). Because all valves are closed momentarily, blood volume remains unchanged.
  • Ventricular Systole (Ventricular Ejection): Pressure in the ventricles exceeds arterial pressure, forcing open the semilunar valves and driving blood into the aorta and pulmonary artery.
  • Ventricular Diastole (Isovolumetric Relaxation): Ventricles relax, pressure drops below arterial pressure, and the semilunar valves snap shut (creating the second heart sound, S2).
  • Ventricular Diastole (Passive Filling): As ventricular pressure drops below atrial pressure, AV valves open, and blood flows passively from atria into ventricles.

Cardiac Output and Regulation

Cardiac output (CO) is the volume of blood pumped by each ventricle per minute. It is calculated by multiplying heart rate (HR) by stroke volume (SV)—the volume of blood ejected per beat.

$$\text{CO} = \text{HR} \times \text{SV}$|

Stroke volume is determined by three main factors:

  • Preload: The degree of stretch on the ventricular myocardium before it contracts, largely dependent on venous return.
  • Contractility: The forcefulness of contraction of cardiac muscle fibers at any given fiber length, influenced by sympathetic nervous activity and calcium ions.
  • Afterload: The arterial pressure that ventricles must overcome to eject blood.

Autonomic regulation modulates these variables. Sympathetic stimulation increases heart rate and contractility, while parasympathetic stimulation via the vagus nerve decreases heart rate.

Electrocardiography Principles

An electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) is a graphical recording of the electrical activity generated by the heart during the cardiac cycle, captured using electrodes placed on the skin surface. A standard diagnostic ECG tracing consists of distinct waves representing specific electrical events:

  • P Wave: Represents depolarization of the atria, spreading from the SA node across atrial muscle tissue.
  • QRS Complex: Represents depolarization of the ventricles. Because the ventricular muscle mass is large, this deflection is prominent and also conceals atrial repolarization on the tracing.
  • T Wave: Represents repolarization of the ventricles, returning them to their resting electrical state.

Analysis of these waves, their amplitude, and the intervals between them provides crucial clinical insight into conduction abnormalities, myocardial ischemia, and chamber hypertrophy.
