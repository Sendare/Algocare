Blood

Introduction and Overview of Blood in Nursing Care

Blood is a specialized fluid connective tissue that circulates continuously through the cardiovascular system, making it essential for sustaining life. For nurses, understanding blood is not just a theoretical laboratory science; it is a daily clinical reality. Every shift involves monitoring vital signs that reflect blood pressure and volume, checking laboratory reports for abnormal cell counts, or administering blood products to restore lost volume and oxygen-carrying capacity. Blood transports oxygen from the lungs to every tissue, carries away metabolic waste products like carbon dioxide and urea, delivers hormones from endocrine glands to their target organs, and defends the body against invading pathogens. Because blood touches every single organ system, changes in its composition or volume often serve as the earliest warning signs of systemic disease, trauma, or infection.

Composition of Blood

If you spin a tube of anticoagulated blood in a centrifuge, it separates into distinct layers based on weight and density. The top layer is a pale yellow liquid called plasma, which makes up roughly 55 percent of total blood volume. Sandwiched between the plasma and the heavy red blood cells below is a thin, whitish layer called the buffy coat, containing white blood cells and platelets. The bottom layer consists of packed red blood cells, known as the hematocrit, which typically accounts for about 45 percent of the blood volume in a healthy adult.

  • Plasma: The liquid extracellular matrix containing water, proteins, electrolytes, and nutrients.
  • Buffy Coat: A thin layer comprising leukocytes (white blood cells) and thrombocytes (platelets).
  • Erythrocytes: Red blood cells packed at the bottom of the centrifuged tube, responsible for oxygen transport.

Plasma and Its Proteins

Plasma is roughly 90 percent water, providing the fluid highway that keeps blood moving smoothly through vessels. Dissolved within this water are essential solutes, including respiratory gases, nutrients, electrolytes, metabolic waste products, and vital plasma proteins. These proteins are manufactured primarily by the liver and play massive roles in fluid balance and immunity.

  • Albumin: The most abundant plasma protein, responsible for maintaining colloid osmotic pressure, which draws fluid back into capillaries and prevents tissue swelling (edema).
  • Globulins: Divided into alpha, beta, and gamma types; alpha and beta transport lipids and fat-soluble vitamins, while gamma globulins act as antibodies.
  • Fibrinogen: An essential soluble clotting protein that is converted into insoluble strands of fibrin during blood coagulation.

Cellular Components of Blood

The formed elements of blood consist of three main categories, each with specialized structures tailored to their unique functions.

  • Erythrocytes (Red Blood Cells): Biconcave discs lacking a nucleus and mitochondria, packed with hemoglobin to bind and carry oxygen efficiently.
  • Leukocytes (White Blood Cells): Nucleated cells that form our primary defense mechanism against infection, inflammation, and foreign invaders.
  • Thrombocytes (Platelets): Small cell fragments derived from bone marrow megakaryocytes that initiate the clotting process to stop bleeding.

Red blood cells have a normal lifespan of about 120 days before old cells are broken down in the spleen and liver. White blood cells are split into granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) and agranulocytes (lymphocytes, monocytes), each responding to different types of threats like bacteria, viruses, or parasites.

Hemopoiesis and Blood Cell Formation

Blood cells do not live forever, meaning the body must constantly manufacture millions of new ones every single minute. This continuous production process is called hemopoiesis (or hematopoiesis). It takes place primarily in the red bone marrow, which is found in spongy bone areas like the pelvis, sternum, ribs, and proximal ends of long bones.

All blood cells originate from a single common type of stem cell known as a hemocytoblast or multipotent hematopoietic stem cell. Depending on chemical signals called growth factors and cytokines, these stem cells divide and differentiate down two distinct developmental pathways:

  • Myeloid line: Gives rise to erythrocytes, platelets, neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils, and monocytes.
  • Lymphoid line: Gives rise to specialized lymphocytes (T cells, B cells, and natural killer cells) that govern adaptive immunity.

For example, when the kidneys detect low blood oxygen levels, they release the hormone erythropoietin (EPO), which travels to the bone marrow and stimulates the accelerated production of red blood cells.

Hemostasis and Coagulation Pathways

When a blood vessel is injured, the body must act swiftly to prevent fatal blood loss through a three-step process known as hemostasis.

  1. Vascular Spasm: The damaged blood vessel immediately constricts, reducing blood flow through the opening.
  2. Platelet Plug Formation: Platelets rush to the site, stick to the exposed collagen fibers of the broken wall, and clump together to form a temporary seal.
  3. Coagulation (Blood Clotting): A complex cascade of plasma clotting factors converts liquid blood into a solid gel meshwork.

The coagulation cascade involves two pathways that meet at a common pathway: the intrinsic pathway (triggered by internal vessel damage) and the extrinsic pathway (triggered by tissue trauma outside the vessel). Both pathways activate factor X, which turns prothrombin into thrombin. Thrombin then converts soluble fibrinogen into sticky fibrin threads, weaving a tight net that traps blood cells and stabilizes the clot. Once healing occurs, the body dissolves the clot through a process called fibrinolysis.

Clinical Assessment and Common Blood Disorders

Nurses frequently review laboratory tests to evaluate a patient's hematologic status. The Complete Blood Count (CBC) measures red blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, white blood cell count, and platelets. Additional tests like the Prothrombin Time (PT/INR) and Activated Partial Thromboplastin Time (aPTT) monitor how well clotting pathways are functioning, especially for patients taking anticoagulant medications like warfarin or heparin.

Common blood disorders seen in clinical practice include:

  • Anemia: A reduction in oxygen-carrying capacity due to low red blood cells or hemoglobin, leading to fatigue, pallor, and shortness of breath.
  • Polycythemia: An abnormal excess of red blood cells that makes blood thick and sluggish, increasing clot risks.
  • Thrombocytopenia: A dangerously low platelet count that puts the patient at high risk for spontaneous bleeding and bruising.
  • Leukemia: A form of blood cancer characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation of abnormal white blood cells in the bone marrow.
