Blood

Introduction and Overview of Blood in Nursing Care

Blood is a specialized bodily fluid that plays a vital role in maintaining homeostasis, transporting essential nutrients and oxygen, and protecting the body against pathogens. For nursing students in Nigeria, understanding blood is fundamental, as it directly impacts patient assessment, medication administration, intravenous therapy, and transfusion safety. Blood circulates continuously through the cardiovascular system, driven by the pumping action of the heart. It interacts with every organ system, making it a primary indicator of overall health and disease states. Nurses must monitor blood parameters, such as hemoglobin levels, packed cell volume, and white blood cell counts, to evaluate a patient's clinical status. Pathological conditions affecting blood components can lead to severe complications like hypoxia, hemorrhage, or infection. In clinical practice across Nigerian healthcare settings, nurses frequently encounter conditions like severe anemia due to malaria or malnutrition, sickle cell crisis, and blood loss from postpartum hemorrhage or trauma. Recognizing the physiological role of blood allows nurses to anticipate clinical needs, administer prescribed blood products safely, and educate patients and their families about preventive health measures, including proper nutrition and infection control.

Composition

Blood is composed of two main portions: a liquid extracellular matrix called plasma and formed elements, which are living blood cells. When whole blood is centrifuged in a laboratory tube, the heavier formed elements pack to the bottom, while the lighter plasma remains at the top. Formed elements make up roughly 45 percent of total blood volume, a measurement clinically referred to as the hematocrit or packed cell volume (PCV). Plasma constitutes the remaining 55 percent of blood volume. Between the plasma and the red blood cells lies a thin, whitish layer called the buffy coat, which contains leukocytes (white blood cells) and platelets. Understanding this composition is crucial for nurses when interpreting laboratory results, such as a complete blood count (CBC). Variations in these proportions provide immediate diagnostic clues; for instance, a low hematocrit indicates anemia, whereas an elevated white blood cell count typically points toward infection or inflammation. In nursing practice, recognizing these baseline fractions helps clinicians understand how fluid shifts, dehydration, or blood loss alter the patient's circulating volume and oxygen-carrying capacity, guiding prompt and accurate therapeutic interventions.

Plasma

Plasma is the straw-colored, sticky fluid portion of blood that is about 90 percent water. It serves as the suspending medium for the formed elements and contains over a hundred different dissolved solutes, including nutrients, gases, hormones, wastes, and plasma proteins. Plasma proteins are the most abundant solutes by weight and are mostly produced by the liver. They include albumin, which maintains osmotic pressure and prevents fluid from leaking out of blood vessels; globulins, which act as transport proteins and antibodies (immunoglobulins) essential for immunity; and fibrinogen, which plays a critical role in blood clotting. Electrolytes such as sodium, potassium, calcium, and chloride are also dissolved in plasma, helping to maintain blood pH, osmotic balance, and membrane potentials. Nurses must monitor plasma-related lab values, such as serum proteins and electrolytes, particularly when caring for patients with edema, liver disease, burns, or renal failure. Understanding plasma dynamics helps nurses manage intravenous fluid therapy effectively, ensuring that crystalloids and colloids are administered appropriately to restore intravascular volume and correct electrolyte imbalances without causing fluid overload.

Blood Cells

The formed elements of blood consist of erythrocytes (red blood cells), leukocytes (white blood cells), and thrombocytes (platelets). Erythrocytes are specialized biconcave discs lacking a nucleus and mitochondria, packed with hemoglobin to transport oxygen from the lungs to body tissues and assist in carbon dioxide return. Their unique shape maximizes surface area for gas exchange and allows them to deform as they pass through narrow capillaries. Leukocytes are nucleated cells that form part of the immune system, defending the body against infections, foreign invaders, and abnormal cells; they are further divided into granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) and agranulocytes (lymphocytes, monocytes). Thrombocytes are cell fragments derived from megakaryocytes and are essential for hemostasis, adhering to sites of vascular injury to form platelet plugs. As nursing students, identifying the distinct functions of these cells is critical for interpreting differential white blood cell counts, managing patients with bleeding disorders, and caring for individuals with anemia or thrombocytopenia. Nurses must observe patients for clinical signs of cell deficiencies, such as pallor and fatigue in anemia, petechiae in thrombocytopenia, and fever in leukopenia.

Hemopoiesis and Blood Cell Formation

Hemopoiesis (or hematopoiesis) is the physiological process of blood cell formation, occurring continuously throughout adult life in the red bone marrow. All formed elements originate from a single common type of stem cell known as the hemocytoblast or hematopoietic stem cell. Depending on chemical signals called colony-stimulating factors and growth factors like erythropoietin, these stem cells differentiate into myeloid or lymphoid stem cell lines. The myeloid line gives rise to erythrocytes, platelets, and all leukocytes except lymphocytes, which develop primarily from the lymphoid line. Erythropoiesis, the specific production of red blood cells, is stimulated by erythropoietin released by the kidneys in response to hypoxia, such as low blood oxygen levels or high altitudes. Nurses must understand this process when caring for patients with chronic kidney disease, who often develop severe anemia because failing kidneys cannot produce adequate erythropoietin. Similarly, nurses administer recombinant erythropoietin to stimulate red blood cell production in specific clinical contexts. Recognizing how bone marrow depression caused by chemotherapy or infections impairs hemopoiesis helps nurses anticipate pancytopenia and implement appropriate protective isolation and supportive care.

Hemostasis and Coagulation Pathways

Hemostasis is a multistep physiological process that stops bleeding when a blood vessel is injured, preventing excessive blood loss. It involves three rapid, sequential steps: vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and coagulation (blood clotting). Vascular spasm occurs immediately as smooth muscle in the damaged vessel wall constricts to reduce blood flow. Next, platelets adhere to exposed collagen fibers at the injury site, releasing chemical messengers that aggregate more platelets to form a temporary platelet plug. The final step, coagulation, transforms liquid blood into a gel through a complex cascade of plasma proteins called clotting factors, culminating in the conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands that reinforce the platelet plug. The coagulation cascade proceeds via two pathways—the extrinsic pathway, triggered by tissue factor outside the blood vessels, and the intrinsic pathway, triggered by factors within the blood—both converging at a common pathway to produce a stable clot. Nurses must understand these pathways when administering anticoagulants like heparin or warfarin, monitoring clotting times such as PT/INR and aPTT, and watching for signs of hemorrhage or thrombosis in surgical and medical patients.

Clinical Assessment and Common Blood Disorders

Clinical assessment of blood involves evaluating diagnostic test results and observing patient symptoms to detect hematological disorders early. Common screening tools include the complete blood count (CBC), hemoglobin concentration, packed cell volume, and coagulation profiles. Common blood disorders seen in nursing practice include anemia, characterized by a reduced oxygen-carrying capacity leading to fatigue, pallor, and shortness of breath; polycythemia, an abnormal excess of red blood cells; leukemias, which are malignancies of white blood cells; and bleeding disorders such as hemophilia and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Sickle cell disease, a genetic hemoglobinopathy prevalent in Nigeria, causes abnormal sickling of red blood cells during hypoxia or dehydration, resulting in painful vaso-occlusive crises and chronic hemolytic anemia. Nurses play a crucial role in assessing these patients, administering prescribed treatments such as blood transfusions, IV fluids, and pain relief, and monitoring for transfusion reactions. Patient education regarding hydration, infection prevention, and avoiding triggers of sickle cell crises is an essential nursing responsibility to improve patient outcomes and quality of life.
