Blood

Introduction and Overview of Blood in Nursing Care

Blood is a specialized fluid connective tissue that circulates continuously through the cardiovascular system, serving as the primary transport medium for maintaining homeostasis. In clinical practice, monitoring blood parameters provides critical insight into a patient's oxygenation, metabolic state, immune competence, and clotting ability. Nurses encounter blood data constantly, from evaluating routine complete blood count (CBC) laboratory results to managing complex transfusion therapies. Understanding the physiological role of blood enables early recognition of life-threatening imbalances such as severe hemorrhage, profound anemia, or systemic sepsis. This fluid medium essentially links every organ system, acting as a dynamic mirror of overall health status.

Composition of Blood

Whole blood separates into two distinct components when centrifuged: a liquid extracellular matrix called plasma and formed elements consisting of cellular structures. Plasma makes up roughly 55% of total blood volume, while the formed elements comprise the remaining 45%, a proportion quantified clinically as the hematocrit.

  • Plasma: A pale yellow fluid composed of about 90% water and 10% dissolved solutes, including proteins, electrolytes, nutrients, and waste products.
  • Erythrocytes: Red blood cells specialized for the transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide.
  • Leukocytes: White blood cells responsible for immune defense and inflammatory responses.
  • Thrombocytes: Platelets that play a vital role in hemostasis and blood clotting.

Plasma Proteins and Solutes

Plasma is far more than simple saltwater; it contains vital dissolved proteins synthesized primarily by the liver. These proteins maintain osmotic pressure, transport molecules, and defend the body against pathogens.

In addition to proteins, plasma transports essential electrolytes like sodium, potassium, and calcium, alongside metabolic waste products destined for renal or hepatic excretion.

Blood Cells: Erythrocytes, Leukocytes, and Platelets

The formed elements of blood execute specialized survival functions. Erythrocytes are biconcave discs lacking a nucleus and organelles, an adaptation maximizing their surface area for carrying hemoglobin and squeezing through narrow capillaries. Leukocytes are divided into granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils, basophils) and agranulocytes (lymphocytes, monocytes), each orchestrating specific immune defenses against bacteria, viruses, and parasites. Platelets are cytoplasmic fragments pinched off from large bone marrow megakaryocytes; their primary task is adhering to damaged vascular endothelium to initiate primary hemostasis.

Hemopoiesis and Blood Cell Formation

All formed elements originate from a common stem cell population in the red bone marrow known as hemocytoblasts or pluripotent hematopoietic stem cells. Through continuous cell division and differentiation, these stem cells give rise to specialized precursor cell lines. Erythropoiesis is regulated tightly by the hormone erythropoietin (EPO), which is secreted primarily by the kidneys in response to reduced tissue oxygenation—a physiological reality frequently altered in chronic renal failure. Leukopoiesis and thrombopoiesis are similarly stimulated by specific growth factors and cytokines responding to body demands.

Hemostasis and Coagulation Pathways

Hemostasis is the dynamic physiological process that halts bleeding after vascular injury while maintaining blood in a fluid state elsewhere. The process occurs in three rapid sequential steps: vascular spasm, platelet plug formation, and blood coagulation. Coagulation involves two converging pathways—the intrinsic and extrinsic pathways—that merge into a common pathway culminating in the conversion of soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands. This mesh reinforces the initial platelet plug. Crucially, natural anticoagulant mechanisms and fibrinolysis prevent aberrant clotting throughout the vascular tree.

Clinical Assessment and Common Blood Disorders

Nurses must recognize common hematological disorders and interpret baseline laboratory diagnostics. Anemia represents a reduced oxygen-carrying capacity due to low hemoglobin or red cell count, often linked to iron deficiency or chronic disease. Leukemia involves malignant overproduction of abnormal white blood cells, compromising immune function. Clotting disorders include hemophilia, characterized by missing clotting factors, and deep vein thrombosis driven by stasis or hypercoagulability. Nurses monitor vital signs, review laboratory trends like hemoglobin and platelet counts, and observe patients for signs of abnormal bleeding or fatigue.
