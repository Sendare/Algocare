Responding to Adverse Events, Errors and Harm

Defining Adverse Events, Medical Errors, and Patient Harm

Patient safety terminology forms the foundation of quality improvement in clinical practice. Healthcare settings can be unpredictable, making clear definitions essential for accurate reporting and analysis.

  • Adverse Event: An unintended injury or complication caused by healthcare management rather than the patient's underlying disease, resulting in measurable harm or prolonged hospital stay.
  • Medical Error: A failure of a planned action to be completed as intended, or the use of a wrong plan to achieve an aim, which may or may not cause harm.
  • Patient Harm: Physical, emotional, or psychological injury or damage resulting from medical care.

The Role of a Just Culture in Reporting and Responding

Blaming individuals for system-level failures discourages reporting and hides safety vulnerabilities. A just culture balances individual accountability with organizational learning. It creates an environment where healthcare workers feel safe reporting mistakes, near misses, and unsafe conditions without fear of unfair punishment.

However, a just culture does not mean immunity for reckless behavior. It distinguishes between human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless behavior. Human error involves an inadvertent slip or lapse, requiring a system fix. At-risk behavior involves a choice where the risk is not recognized or is mistakenly believed to be justified. Reckless behavior involves conscious disregard of a substantial and unjustifiable risk.

Immediate Actions and Patient Safety Stabilization

When an adverse event or medical error occurs, the immediate priority is always the patient's physical and psychological safety. Nurses must act quickly to mitigate harm and stabilize the clinical situation.

  1. Assess the Patient: Check vital signs, perform a focused physical assessment, and determine the current level of harm.
  2. Provide Intervention: Administer emergency treatments, antidotes, or supportive care as required by the patient's condition.
  3. Notify the Provider: Inform the attending physician or primary care team immediately about the event and the patient's status.
  4. Secure the Environment: Remove faulty equipment from service, label it clearly, and preserve any relevant medications or packaging for investigation.

The Root Cause Analysis (RCA) Process

A Root Cause Analysis (RCA) is a structured problem-solving method used to identify the core underlying reasons for an adverse event. Instead of stopping at the surface mistake made by an individual, an RCA digs deeper into organizational processes, environmental factors, communication gaps, and equipment design.

The multidisciplinary team reviews the timeline of events, interviews staff involved, and asks 'why' repeatedly to uncover systemic vulnerabilities. The final output is an action plan with measurable steps to redesign workflows, update policies, or replace faulty technology, ensuring the same error does not happen again.

Open Disclosure and Communicating with Patients and Families

Open disclosure is the open, transparent conversation between healthcare providers and patients or their families when things go wrong in care. It involves acknowledging the event, expressing sincere regret, explaining what happened, and detailing steps being taken to prevent recurrence.

Delaying or withholding information damages trust and worsens psychological distress. Nurses play a vital role in supporting the patient during these discussions, ensuring communication is delivered in clear, jargon-free language. Empathy, active listening, and a calm demeanor help families process the shock of an unexpected adverse event.

Documentation, Incident Reporting, and Legal Considerations

Accurate record-keeping is critical for both clinical continuity and legal protection. Following an adverse event, nurses must document objective clinical facts in the patient's health record.

Crucially, incident reports (or adverse event reports) are administrative documents used for quality improvement and are kept separate from the permanent medical record. Nurses should never reference the filing of an incident report inside the patient's progress notes, as this can create confusion during legal discovery. Documentation must remain factual, professional, and free of personal opinions, emotional language, or finger-pointing.

Supporting the Second Victim: Healthcare Provider Care

A second victim is a healthcare provider who is traumatized by involvement in an unanticipated adverse event, medical error, or patient injury. Nurses and doctors experiencing this phenomenon often suffer from intense guilt, anxiety, self-doubt, and burnout.

Without adequate support, second victims may leave the profession or make further errors due to stress. Institutional peer support programs, counseling services, and an empathetic leadership approach help staff process their emotional response. Acknowledging that healthcare workers are human and prone to error fosters a healthier, more resilient workforce.

Implementing System Changes to Prevent Recurrence

The final and most critical step in responding to an adverse event is translating findings into sustainable system changes. Collecting incident reports and conducting root cause analyses serves no purpose if the insights are not applied to clinical practice.

System improvements often involve changing standard operating procedures, upgrading technology, introducing double-check protocols for high-risk tasks, or redesigning physical medication rooms to minimize distractions. Evaluating the effectiveness of these changes over time ensures that patient safety remains dynamic and continuous.
