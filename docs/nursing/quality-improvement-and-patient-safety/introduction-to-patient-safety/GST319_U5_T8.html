Design Principles to Reduce Human Error

Understanding Human Error in Healthcare

Medical errors rarely stem from individual carelessness or lack of dedication among nurses and doctors. Instead, accidents usually happen because the clinical environment, equipment, or institutional workflows allow them to occur. When designing safer systems, healthcare managers look at human factors engineering, which studies how people interact with technology, procedures, and physical spaces. Acknowledging that humans naturally experience fatigue, memory lapses, and distraction is the first step toward building a safer hospital ward.

Slips, Lapses, and Mistakes

Errors manifest in different ways depending on whether the breakdown happens during planning or execution.

  • Slips: Observable errors of execution where the plan was correct, but the physical action failed, such as accidentally grabbing the wrong IV line because two look identical.
  • Lapses: Internal memory failures that are not always visible to others, like forgetting to administer a scheduled dose of antibiotics because an emergency interrupted the task.
  • Mistakes: Flaws in the conscious plan itself, where a nurse follows a guideline incorrectly because they misunderstood the patient's actual clinical condition.

Standardization and Simplification

Complex, cluttered, and non-standardized environments invite mistakes. Standardization ensures that identical items, equipment, and protocols look and function the same way across every ward in a hospital. For example, if every emergency crash cart in a tertiary facility has the exact same drawer layout, any nurse can find airway supplies quickly during a code blue without searching.

Simplification removes unnecessary steps from clinical procedures. Reducing the number of handoff transitions or cutting down redundant documentation steps minimizes cognitive overload, allowing nurses to focus directly on patient monitoring and direct care delivery.

Using Forcing Functions and Constraints

A forcing function is a design feature that prevents a user from taking an action unless a specific safety condition is met first. It acts as an absolute physical barrier to error.

  • Mechanical design: Enteral feeding tubes and intravenous lines often have incompatible nozzle sizes to make it physically impossible to connect a liquid feed into a venous line.
  • Digital constraints: Electronic health record systems can lock a medication order entry screen if the dosage entered falls outside safe clinical parameters, forcing the clinician to review the weight-based limits before proceeding.

Redundancies and Safety Checklists

Complex medical procedures carry inherent risks that a single person can easily miss during a busy shift. Redundancy introduces overlapping safety checks so that if one person or system misses a flaw, a second barrier catches it before harm occurs. Surgical safety checklists are a classic example, requiring the whole theatre team to pause and verbally verify patient identity, surgical site, and planned procedure just before incision.

While checklists require discipline to complete, they protect against human memory lapses, especially during high-stress situations or routine tasks where staff might otherwise skip vital steps.

Evaluating Human-Machine Interfaces

Medical technology should match how nurses naturally think and work. Poorly designed infusion pumps, confusing monitor alarms, and cluttered computer screens contribute heavily to clinical fatigue and alarm fatigue. When alarms sound constantly for non-critical issues, staff may ignore them entirely, missing genuine emergencies.

Designers improve human-machine interfaces by using clear visual displays, logical button layouts, and distinct, actionable auditory signals. Equipment that requires minimal memorization and provides immediate, understandable feedback helps nurses execute their duties safely and efficiently.
