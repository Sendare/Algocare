The Spinal Cord

Gross Anatomy and Protective Structures

The spinal cord is a delicate cylinder of nervous tissue that runs from the brainstem down through the vertebral canal. In adults, it typically extends from the foramen magnum of the skull down to the level of the first or second lumbar vertebra (L1-L2). It is housed within the bony vertebral column, which provides sturdy structural protection against physical trauma.

At its inferior end, the cord tapers into a conical structure called the conus medullaris. Extending from this tip is the filum terminale, a strand of fibrous tissue that anchors the spinal cord to the coccyx. Because the bony vertebral column grows faster than the nervous tissue during development, the lower lumbar and sacral nerve roots angle sharply downward inside the canal, forming a bundle known as the cauda equina, which resembles a horse's tail.

Spinal Meninges and Cerebrospinal Fluid

Three protective membrane layers called meninges wrap around the spinal cord, continuous with those covering the brain.

  • Dura mater: The tough, fibrous outermost layer forming a strong protective tube.
  • Arachnoid mater: The delicate, web-like middle layer.
  • Pia mater: The innermost transparent layer that clings tightly to the surface of the spinal cord.

Between the dura mater and the bone of the vertebral vertebrae lies the epidural space, which contains fat and blood vessels and serves as the common site for administering epidural anesthesia during labor. Between the arachnoid and pia mater is the subarachnoid space, filled with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) that cushions the cord against shock.

Internal Grey and White Matter Organization

A cross-section of the spinal cord reveals an inner core of grey matter shaped roughly like a butterfly or the letter H, surrounded by outer white matter.

The grey matter consists primarily of neuronal cell bodies, neuroglia, and unmyelinated axons. It is subdivided into horns: the posterior (dorsal) horns contain sensory nuclei, the anterior (ventral) horns contain motor neuron cell bodies, and the lateral horns (present in thoracic and lumbar segments) house autonomic motor neurons.

The white matter is arranged into columns or funiculi—posterior, lateral, and anterior—containing myelinated nerve fibers traveling up and down the central nervous system.

Ascending and Descending Neural Tracts

White matter pathways transmit signals between the brain and the body via two main route types:

  • Ascending tracts: Sensory pathways that carry information up toward the brain. Examples include the spinothalamic tracts (pain and temperature) and the dorsal columns (fine touch and proprioception).
  • Descending tracts: Motor pathways that carry commands down from the brain. Examples include the corticospinal tracts, which control voluntary movement of skeletal muscles.

Damage to these tracts disrupts communication, leading to sensory deficits or paralysis below the level of the lesion.

Each spinal nerve is formed by the union of a posterior (sensory) root—which features a swelling called the dorsal root ganglion containing sensory cell bodies—and an anterior (motor) root. A dermatome is a specific strip of skin supplied by a single spinal nerve, which helps clinicians map nerve root damage.

Spinal Reflexes and Reflex Arcs

A spinal reflex is a fast, predictable, automatic response to a stimulus that bypasses conscious thought to protect the body from harm. The pathway it travels is called a reflex arc, consisting of five basic components:

  1. Receptor: Detects the environmental stimulus.
  2. Sensory neuron: Conducts the nerve impulse toward the spinal cord.
  3. Integration center: One or more synapses within the grey matter of the cord.
  4. Motor neuron: Transmits the output impulse away from the cord.
  5. Effector: A muscle or gland that responds to the motor signal.

The knee-jerk patellar reflex is a classic example of a simple monosynaptic stretch reflex.

Clinical Assessment and Common Injuries

Injuries to the spinal cord can result from trauma, tumors, or vascular accidents, leading to profound clinical challenges. Transection or severe compression of the cord causes paralysis and sensory loss below the injury site. Quadriplegia (tetraplegia) involves impairment of all four limbs, typically resulting from cervical cord damage, while paraplegia affects the lower body, occurring from thoracic, lumbar, or sacral injuries.

During acute spinal trauma, patients may experience spinal shock, a temporary loss of all reflex activity and flaccid paralysis below the lesion. Nurses must carefully monitor respiratory status in high cervical injuries, as innervation to the diaphragm via the phrenic nerve (C3-C5) may be compromised.
