Parasympathetic Nervous System

Introduction to the Parasympathetic Nervous System

The parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) is one of the two main divisions of the autonomic nervous system, alongside the sympathetic nervous system. While the sympathetic branch prepares the body for physical exertion or danger, the parasympathetic branch conserves energy and maintains routine bodily functions. It dominates during quiet, restful states, allowing the body to feed, rest, and digest without immediate stress. Nurses often describe this system using the phrase 'rest and digest' to contrast it with the 'fight or flight' response.

Anatomical Organization and Cranio-Sacral Outflow

Parasympathetic nerve fibers emerge from the central nervous system in a pattern known as cranio-sacral outflow. This means the preganglionic neurons originate in two specific regions:

  1. Brainstem: Fibers travel through cranial nerves III (oculomotor), VII (facial), IX (glossopharyngeal), and X (vagus).
  2. Sacral spinal cord: Fibers emerge from spinal segments S2 through S4 to innervate pelvic organs.

The vagus nerve (cranial nerve X) is the most prominent component, supplying about 75% of all parasympathetic activity to thoracic and abdominal viscera, including the heart, lungs, and stomach.

Ganglia and Neurotransmitter Pathways

Unlike the sympathetic system, which has ganglia located close to the spinal cord, parasympathetic ganglia are situated terminal or intramural—very close to or directly inside the walls of the target organs.

Transmission along this pathway involves a two-neuron chain:

  • Preganglionic neurons release acetylcholine (ACh), which binds to nicotinic receptors on the postganglionic neuron.
  • Postganglionic neurons also release acetylcholine (ACh) at the target organ, acting on muscarinic receptors.

Because both preganglionic and postganglionic fibers release acetylcholine, the entire parasympathetic pathway is described as cholinergic.

Cholinergic Receptors: Nicotinic and Muscarinic

The effects of acetylcholine depend entirely on the specific receptor subtype present on the target cell membrane:

  • Nicotinic receptors: Found on postganglionic neurons in all autonomic ganglia and at the neuromuscular junction. They are ligand-gated ion channels that always produce an excitatory response when stimulated.
  • Muscarinic receptors: Found on all target organs innervated by parasympathetic postganglionic fibers (such as the heart, smooth muscle, and glands). They are G-protein coupled receptors that can either stimulate or inhibit target activity depending on the tissue type.

Physiological Effects on Target Organs

Stimulation of the parasympathetic system produces targeted responses designed to conserve energy and promote internal maintenance. Below is a comparison of key organ responses:

Organ / SystemParasympathetic Effect
HeartDecreases heart rate and reduces contractility
PupilsConstricts pupils (miosis) for near vision
LungsConstricts bronchioles and increases secretion
Digestive TractStimulates peristalsis, motility, and secretion
BladderContracts the detrusor muscle and relaxes sphincters
GlandsStimulates tear, saliva, and digestive enzyme production

Role in Homeostasis and the Rest-and-Digest Response

Homeostasis relies on a continuous, balanced tug-of-war between the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions. While the sympathetic system manages crisis intervention, the parasympathetic system restores internal equilibrium once the crisis passes. It lowers metabolic demand, lowers blood pressure, and diverts blood flow away from skeletal muscles back toward the gastrointestinal tract. This ongoing restorative care keeps organ systems from burning out under chronic stress.

Clinical Implications and Pharmacological Agents

Drugs that mimic or block parasympathetic activity are widely used in clinical nursing practice. Cholinergics (or parasympathomimetics) stimulate the system and are used to treat conditions like urinary retention or glaucoma. Conversely, anticholinergics (parasympatholytics) block muscarinic receptors. Nurses must monitor patients taking anticholinergic medications for classic side effects such as dry mouth, blurred vision, constipation, and urinary retention—often remembered by the teaching phrase 'can't see, can't pee, can't spit, can't shit.'
