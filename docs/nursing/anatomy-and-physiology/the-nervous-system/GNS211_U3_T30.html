Sympathetic Nervous System

Introduction to the Sympathetic Nervous System

The sympathetic nervous system is a core division of the autonomic nervous system, preparing the body to handle sudden stress, physical exertion, or perceived danger. Operating mostly outside conscious control, it orchestrates widespread adjustments that prioritize immediate survival over routine body maintenance. When activated, energy stores mobilize, breathing accelerates, and blood flow shifts dynamically toward skeletal muscles.

Anatomical Organization and Pathways

Sympathetic pathways originate in the thoracolumbar region of the spinal cord, specifically from segments T1 to L2. Preganglionic neurons emerge from the lateral gray horn and synapse in ganglia located close to the spinal column.

  • Sympathetic chain ganglia: Series of nodes running alongside the vertebral column, allowing widespread signal distribution.
  • Collateral ganglia: Prevertebral ganglia situated closer to target abdominal organs, such as the celiac and mesenteric ganglia.
  • Adrenal medulla: Receives direct preganglionic fibers without a intervening synapse, acting as a modified sympathetic ganglion.

Neurotransmitters and Receptors

Chemical signaling in the sympathetic nervous system relies primarily on acetylcholine and norepinephrine. Preganglionic fibers release acetylcholine to stimulate postganglionic neurons through nicotinic receptors. Most postganglionic fibers then release norepinephrine onto target organs, interacting with adrenergic receptors.

The Fight-or-Flight Response Mechanism

The fight-or-flight mechanism coordinates a rapid biochemical cascade through neural pathways and hormonal release from the adrenal medulla. Epinephrine and norepinephrine flood the bloodstream, amplifying and prolonging neural signals. Liver glycogen breaks down into glucose to fuel muscles, while sweat gland secretion increases to cool the body during impending exertion.

Effects on Major Body Organs

Target organs respond distinctly based on their specific adrenergic receptor profiles. The cardiovascular system experiences elevated output, while the respiratory system widens airways to maximize oxygen intake. Conversely, digestive and urinary processes slow down sharply as blood is diverted away from the gastrointestinal tract.

Clinical Implications and Pharmacological Agents

Drugs frequently target the sympathetic nervous system to treat conditions like hypertension, asthma, and heart failure. Sympathomimetics mimic natural transmitters, while sympatholytics block them. A common clinical misconception is that beta-blockers affect only the heart, whereas non-selective agents can also cause bronchoconstriction by blocking pulmonary receptors.
