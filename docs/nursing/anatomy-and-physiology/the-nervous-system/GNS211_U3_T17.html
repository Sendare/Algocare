Wakefulness and Sleep

Introduction and Neural Control of Wakefulness

Wakefulness is maintained by continuous sensory input and active neural stimulation projecting upward to the cerebral cortex. The core driver of this state is the Reticular Activating System (RAS), a complex network of nuclei located throughout the brainstem. Neurons of the RAS release activating neurotransmitters that bombard the thalamus and cortex, keeping the brain alert and responsive to the environment. When these ascending pathways quiet down, sensory filters close, and the brain transitions into sleep.

Stages of Sleep: NREM and REM

Sleep is divided into two distinct physiological states: Non-Rapid Eye Movement (NREM) sleep and Rapid Eye Movement (REM) sleep. NREM sleep is further split into three stages (N1, N2, N3), progressing from light sleep to deep, slow-wave sleep where tissue repair and physical restoration occur. REM sleep features active brain waves, dreaming, and muscle atonia, preventing the sleeper from acting out dreams.

Brain Waves Measured by Electroencephalogram

An Electroencephalogram (EEG) records electrical voltage fluctuations along the scalp, reflecting cortical electrical activity. Different states of consciousness display distinct wave patterns:

  • Beta waves: High frequency, low amplitude, seen during active concentration and wakefulness.
  • Alpha waves: Slower frequency, seen during relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed.
  • Theta waves: Characteristic of light NREM sleep stages.
  • Delta waves: Very slow, high amplitude waves dominating deep NREM sleep.

Circadian Rhythms and the Suprachiasmatic Nucleus

Biological clocks govern the roughly 24-hour cycle of physiological processes known as circadian rhythms. The master pacemaker orchestrating this cycle is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) located in the hypothalamus. Light entering the retina stimulates the SCN, which suppresses melatonin release from the pineal gland during daytime. Darkness signals the SCN to allow melatonin synthesis, promoting drowsiness.

Neurotransmitters of the Sleep-Wake Cycle

Sleep and wakefulness depend on a delicate balance of neurochemical messengers. Wakefulness is promoted by monoamines and peptides such as acetylcholine, norepinephrine, serotonin, histamine, and orexin. Conversely, sleep initiation relies heavily on inhibitory signaling, predominantly through gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), which quiets the arousal centers in the brainstem and diencephalon.

Physiological Changes During Sleep

The body undergoes profound autonomic and metabolic shifts during sleep. During NREM sleep, sympathetic tone decreases while parasympathetic activity dominates, leading to reduced heart rate, lowered blood pressure, and decreased metabolic rate. During REM sleep, autonomic activity becomes erratic, causing fluctuations in heart rate, irregular breathing, and loss of thermoregulation.

Clinical Sleep Disorders and Nursing Implications

Sleep disturbances negatively impact recovery, immune function, and mental health. Common disorders include insomnia (difficulty falling or staying asleep), obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) (repetitive upper airway collapse during sleep), and narcolepsy (sudden daytime sleep attacks). Nurses must screen for sleep hygiene issues, monitor patients for nocturnal oxygen desaturation, and provide safe environments for those with excessive daytime somnolence.
