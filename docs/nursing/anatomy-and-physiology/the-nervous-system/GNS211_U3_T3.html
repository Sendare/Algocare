Central Nervous System

Introduction and Organization of the Central Nervous System

The central nervous system acts as the main processing hub for the human body, consisting of the brain and the spinal cord. It receives sensory input from both internal organs and the external environment, integrates this information, and issues motor commands to muscles and glands.

  • Brain: Housed inside the cranial cavity, it coordinates higher functions like memory, reasoning, emotion, and conscious sensation.
  • Spinal Cord: Protected by the vertebral column, it serves as the primary conduit for signals traveling between the brain and the rest of the body, while also managing rapid reflex arcs independently.

Cellular organization relies on two primary cell types: neurons, which transmit electrical impulses, and neuroglia, which support, insulate, and nourish the neurons.

Protection of the Brain: Cranium, Meninges, and Fluid

Because nervous tissue is delicate and has a very limited capacity for regeneration, the brain possesses multiple layers of physical and chemical defense.

  1. Skull Bones: The rigid bony cranium provides tough mechanical protection against blunt trauma.
  2. Meninges: Three connective tissue membranes wrap around the brain and spinal cord:
  • Dura mater: The outermost, tough, leather-like layer adherent to the skull.
  • Arachnoid mater: The web-like middle layer enclosing the subarachnoid space.
  • Pia mater: The delicate innermost layer clinging tightly to every contour of the brain surface.
  1. Cerebrospinal Fluid: A clear liquid cushion filling the ventricles and subarachnoid space to absorb physical shocks.

Major Regions of the Brain

The brain is divided into distinct anatomical regions, each handling specialized biological activities:

  • Cerebrum: The largest part, divided into left and right hemispheres, responsible for higher cognitive functions, voluntary movement, and sensory perception.
  • Diencephalon: Contains the thalamus, hypothalamus, and epithalamus, serving as a relay and control center for autonomic functions and endocrine regulation.
  • Brainstem: Comprising the midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata, it regulates essential survival functions like breathing, heart rate, and blood pressure.
  • Cerebellum: Positioned dorsal to the brainstem, it coordinates smooth skeletal muscle movements, balance, and posture.

Functional Areas of the Cerebral Cortex

The outer gray matter of the cerebrum is organized into specific functional territories. Sensory areas interpret incoming nerve impulses from receptors around the body, allowing conscious awareness of touch, light, sound, and taste. Motor areas control voluntary skeletal muscle contractions, with the primary motor cortex located in the precentral gyrus.

Scattered among these are association areas, which integrate diverse pieces of information to give meaning to experiences, support abstract reasoning, and store memories. The left cerebral hemisphere typically dominates language and speech production, while the right hemisphere excels in spatial perception and artistic ability.

Anatomy and Pathways of the Spinal Cord

The spinal cord extends from the brainstem down through the vertebral canal, stopping around the lumbar region. Internally, it features a butterfly-shaped core of gray matter surrounded by white matter made of myelinated nerve tracts.

  • Ascending Tracts: Sensory pathways carrying signals upward toward the brain.
  • Descending Tracts: Motor pathways carrying instructions downward from the brain to peripheral effectors.

Spinal nerves emerge at regular intervals along the cord, forming vital communication loops with the trunk and limbs. The spinal cord also handles autonomous reflex arcs, executing rapid protective movements without waiting for brain processing.

Cerebrospinal Fluid and the Blood-Brain Barrier

The central nervous system maintains a stable internal environment through specialized barriers and fluids. Cerebrospinal fluid is continuously produced by choroid plexuses within the brain ventricles, circulating nutrients and removing metabolic waste products.

Simultaneously, the blood-brain barrier protects neural tissue from circulating pathogens and sudden chemical fluctuations in the blood. Tight junctions between capillary endothelial cells, reinforced by astrocyte foot processes, severely restrict what substances can pass from the bloodstream into brain tissue. While lipid-soluble molecules like oxygen, carbon dioxide, and alcohol diffuse freely, many polar or large molecules are blocked.
