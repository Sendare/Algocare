Foetal Skull Injuries

Anatomy and Physiology of the Foetal Skull

The foetal skull is structurally adapted to withstand the pressures of labour and pass through the birth canal. It consists of the vault, the face, and the base. The vault is formed by membranous bones that are not yet fused, allowing for overlapping during labour, a process known as moulding.

  • Sutures: Fibrous joints connecting the skull bones. Key ones include the frontal, sagittal, coronal, and lambdoid sutures.
  • Fontanelles: Unossified membrane spaces where sutures intersect. The anterior fontanelle is diamond-shaped and closes by 18 months, while the posterior fontanelle is triangular and closes by 6 to 8 weeks.
  • Bones: Includes the two frontal bones, two parietal bones, two temporal bones, and the occipital bone.

Introduction and Causes of Foetal Skull Injuries

Foetal skull injuries refer to structural damage sustained by the cranium during the antepartum or, more commonly, intrapartum period. While the foetal skull is designed to endure considerable stress, excessive mechanical forces can overwhelm its adaptive capacity.

The primary cause of these injuries is mechanical trauma related to childbirth. Difficult vaginal deliveries, particularly those involving instrument assistance, place abnormal stress on the neonatal head. Cephalopelvic disproportion, prolonged second stage of labour, and abnormal foetal presentations further elevate the likelihood of cranial trauma.

Classification and Types of Injuries

Foetal skull injuries present in various forms, ranging from mild soft tissue swelling to severe bony fractures. Recognizing the specific type helps guide appropriate clinical management.

  • Caput Succedaneum: Edema of the scalp soft tissues, crossing suture lines, caused by pressure from the cervix.
  • Cephalhaematoma: Bleeding between the periosteum and skull bone, confined by suture lines and does not cross them.
  • Subgaleal Haematuria / Haemorrhage: Bleeding in the loose connective tissue beneath the galea aponeurosis, capable of crossing suture lines and holding large blood volumes.
  • Linear Skull Fractures: Simple cracks in the skull bone without depression, often healing without surgical intervention.
  • Depressed Skull Fractures: Inward bending of the skull bone, often resembling a ping-pong ball indentation, requiring careful evaluation.

Mechanisms and Risk Factors

Understanding how injuries occur requires looking at the interplay between maternal pelvic dimensions and foetal head size. Disproportionate forces concentrate stress on specific areas of the cranium.

Risk factors include primiparity, macrosomia, precipitous labour, and malpresentations like brow or face presentation. The application of instruments such as forceps and vacuum extractors significantly increases risk if placement is suboptimal or traction is excessive. Vacuum delivery is particularly associated with scalp lacerations and cephalhaematomas, whereas forceps deliveries carry a higher risk of linear or depressed fractures and facial nerve injury.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis

A thorough physical examination of the newborn head is essential immediately after birth, especially following a difficult delivery. Nurses inspect and palpate the scalp for asymmetry, swelling, bruising, or depression.

Diagnostic evaluation goes beyond visual inspection. Transillumination can differentiate fluid collections. Skull radiography confirms the presence and type of fractures. Cranial ultrasonography or computed tomography may be ordered if intracranial haemorrhage or neurological deficits are suspected. Monitoring vital signs and neurological status, including alertness and reflex responses, helps detect complications early.

Nursing Management and Interventions

Nursing care focuses on observation, comfort, and supporting recovery. Most mild injuries, such as caput succedaneum or small cephalhaematomas, resolve spontaneously without specific treatment.

Nurses must handle the infant's head gently to avoid exacerbating trauma or pain. Vital signs and neurological scores are monitored regularly. If hyperbilirubinemia develops due to the reabsorption of extravasated blood from a large cephalhaematoma or subgaleal bleed, phototherapy may be required. Providing clear, reassuring information to parents helps alleviate anxiety regarding visible swellings or bruising on their newborn.

Potential Complications and Prognosis

While many foetal skull injuries have a benign course, severe cases can lead to notable complications. Subgaleal haemorrhage can result in rapid, massive blood loss leading to hypovolemic shock and anaemia. Depressed fractures can sometimes exert pressure on underlying brain tissue, increasing the risk of seizures or localized neurological deficits.

Infections like cellulitis or abscess formation can develop over areas of scalp lacerations or hematomas. Overall prognosis depends heavily on the extent of injury and whether there is associated intracranial damage. Early identification and prompt management greatly improve outcomes.
