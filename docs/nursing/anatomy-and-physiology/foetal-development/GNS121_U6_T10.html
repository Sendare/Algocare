Foetal Skull

Introduction and Importance of the Foetal Skull in Obstetrics

The foetal skull is the most critical part of the foetus during labour because it is the largest and least compressible structure that must pass through the maternal pelvis. Unlike the flexible limbs and trunk, the rigid head encounters significant resistance along the birth canal. Safe childbirth depends heavily on the interaction between the size, shape, and position of the foetal head and the dimensions of the pelvis. Midwives and obstetricians study the skull anatomy closely to predict how the head will navigate through the pelvic brim, cavity, and outlet. Understanding this bony structure helps clinicians identify malpresentations early, assess the progress of labour, and prevent prolonged or obstructed labour which poses severe risks to both mother and baby in Nigerian clinical settings.

Bones of the Foetal Skull

The vault of the foetal skull consists of several flat membrane bones that are not yet fused together. These individual plates allow the head to adapt to the maternal pelvis during descent.

  • Frontal bones: Two bones that form the forehead, which fuse into a single bone usually by the second year of life.
  • Parietal bones: Two large bones forming the sides and roof of the cranium, meeting at the sagittal suture.
  • Occipital bone: A single bone forming the back and base of the skull, situated below the lambdoid suture.
  • Temporal bones: Two bones forming the lower sides of the skull, housing the structures of the ears.

Sutures of the Foetal Skull

Sutures are membranous spaces that separate the bones of the foetal skull. They provide flexibility, allowing the cranial bones to overlap slightly during labour, a process known as moulding.

  • Sagittal suture: Runs down the midline, separating the two parietal bones from each other.
  • Coronal suture: Separates the frontal bones from the two parietal bones on either side.
  • Lambdoid suture: Separates the occipital bone from the two parietal bones posteriorly.
  • Frontal suture: Runs between the two unjoined halves of the frontal bone in the midline.

Fontanelles and Their Clinical Significance

Fontanelles are unossified membrane-filled spaces where two or more sutures intersect. They provide additional room for brain growth and act as vital landmarks during vaginal examinations to determine the position and attitude of the foetal head.

  • Anterior fontanelle (Bregma): Diamond-shaped space formed by the junction of the sagittal, coronal, and frontal sutures. It remains open until about 18 months after birth.
  • Posterior fontanelle (Lambda): Triangular-shaped space formed by the junction of the sagittal and lambdoid sutures. It closes much earlier, usually by six to eight weeks postpartum.

Regions and Landmarks of the Foetal Skull

To accurately describe foetal positions, obstetricians divide the skull into specific regions and reference points.

  • Vertex: The area bounded by the anterior fontanelle, the two parietal eminences, and the posterior fontanelle. This is the presenting part in the most common type of labour.
  • Occiput: The area behind the posterior fontanelle, overlying the occipital bone.
  • Binciput / Brow: The area between the root of the nose and the anterior fontanelle.
  • Face: The area extending from the root of the nose and supraorbital ridges down to the junction of the chin.

Diameters of the Foetal Skull

The foetal skull presents various transverse and anteroposterior diameters depending on its degree of flexion or extension during labour.

  • Suboccipito-bregmatic: Measures about 9.5 cm from the base of the occiput to the centre of the anterior fontanelle. It is the smallest and most favourable diameter, occurring when the head is fully flexed.
  • Occipito-frontal: Measures about 11.5 cm from the bridge of the nose to the occipital protuberance when the head is midway between flexion and extension.
  • Mentovertical: Measures about 13.5 cm from the chin to the highest point of the vertex, representing a brow presentation which is often too large for safe vaginal delivery.
  • Biparietal: The largest transverse diameter, measuring about 9.5 cm between the two parietal eminences.

Moulding of the Foetal Skull During Labour

Moulding refers to the overlapping of the cranial bones at the sutures in response to the pressure exerted by the uterine contractions and the maternal pelvic bones. This adaptive mechanism reduces the overall dimensions of the foetal head without damaging the brain tissue underneath. Mild moulding is a normal physiological process and usually resolves within a few days after birth. However, excessive or rapid moulding indicates disproportion between the foetal head and the pelvis, signalling a high risk of obstructed labour. Midwives must assess moulding by feeling how closely the parietal bones overlap during vaginal examinations.
