Embryonic Development

Gametogenesis and Fertilization

Embryonic development begins with the production of specialized sex cells through gametogenesis. Spermatogenesis produces four functional spermatozoa in males, while oogenesis yields a single viable secondary oocyte and polar bodies in females due to unequal cytoplasmic division. Fertilization typically occurs in the ampulla of the uterine tube. When a sperm successfully penetrates the secondary oocyte's zona pellucida, cortical granules release enzymes that harden the layer to prevent polyspermy. The fusion of male and female pronuclei restores the diploid chromosome number of 46, creating a unique zygote.

Cleavage and Blastocyst Formation

Following fertilization, the zygote undergoes a series of rapid mitotic cell divisions known as cleavage without increasing the overall cytoplasmic mass. The cells, called blastomeres, become tightly packed through a process known as compaction around the eight-cell stage.

  • Morula: A solid ball of 16 or more cells reaching the uterine cavity around the fourth day.
  • Blastocyst: Fluid accumulates inside the morula, creating a cavity called the blastocoele, separating the structure into an outer trophoblast and an inner cell mass.
  • Embryoblast: The inner cell mass that will eventually develop into the actual embryo proper.
  • Trophoblast: The outer layer of cells that contributes to the formation of the placenta.

Implantation and Placental Development

Around the sixth day after fertilization, the blastocyst sheds its zona pellucida—a step called hatching—and makes contact with the endometrial lining of the uterus. The trophoblast differentiates into two layers: the inner cytotrophoblast and the outer syncytiotrophoblast, which actively erodes maternal tissues to embed the conceptus.

The developing placenta takes over hormonal support of the pregnancy by producing human chorionic gonadotropin, maintaining the corpus luteum until the placenta is fully mature.

Gastrulation and Germ Layer Formation

Gastrulation occurs during the third week of development, transforming the bilaminar embryonic disc into a trilaminar disc. This pivotal phase establishes the primitive streak on the epiblast surface, serving as the landmark for directional cell migration.

Cells invaginate through the primitive node and streak, displacing the hypoblast to form the endoderm, positioning themselves in the middle to form the mesoderm, and leaving the remaining surface cells as the ectoderm. Each of these three primary germ layers gives rise to distinct organ systems and tissues throughout the growing fetus.

Neurulation and Organogenesis

Following gastrulation, the notochord induces the overlying ectoderm to thicken and form the neural plate. Through coordinated cell shape changes, the neural plate edges elevate into neural folds, creating a central neural groove. These folds fuse together to form the neural tube, which ultimately gives rise to the central nervous system, including the brain and spinal cord.

Cells at the border of the neural folds detach to form neural crest cells, a remarkably versatile population that migrates throughout the body to form peripheral nerves, melanocytes, and parts of the craniofacial skeleton. Improper closure of the neural tube results in structural birth defects such as spina bifida.

Embryonic Folding and Body Plan

Concurrent with neurulation, the flat embryonic disc undergoes simultaneous head-tail (cephalocaudal) and lateral folding. This dramatic movement transforms the flat sheet into a three-dimensional cylindrical vertebrate body plan.

Cephalocaudal folding brings the developing heart and brain into their proper anatomical positions on the ventral and anterior surfaces. Lateral folding incorporates part of the yolk sac into the embryo to form the primitive gut tube. These folding movements protect the internal organs and establish the basic external form of the human body before the fetal period begins.

Teratology and Congenital Anomalies

Teratology is the study of structural birth defects and abnormalities caused by environmental agents known as teratogens. These agents include certain medications, maternal infections like rubella, ionizing radiation, and chemical substances.

The impact of a teratogen depends heavily on the timing of exposure. The embryonic period—spanning weeks three through eight—is the time of highest vulnerability because major organ systems are actively forming during organogenesis. Exposures prior to this period often result in an all-or-nothing effect, whereas exposures in the later fetal period typically cause functional impairment or growth restriction rather than gross structural malformations.
