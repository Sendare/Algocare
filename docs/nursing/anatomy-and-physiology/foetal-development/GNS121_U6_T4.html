Placenta Formation

Introduction and Early Embryonic Development

Placenta formation, or placentation, begins soon after fertilization and implantation. Following the fusion of the oocyte and sperm in the fallopian tube, the zygote undergoes rapid mitotic divisions called cleavage as it travels toward the uterine cavity. By day three, it forms a solid ball of cells known as a morula, which subsequently develops into a fluid-filled blastocyst by day five. The blastocyst consists of an inner cell mass that will form the embryo and an outer layer of cells called the trophoblast, which drives the entire placental development process.

Implantation and Decidualization

Implantation occurs around the sixth to tenth day after fertilization. The blastocyst burrows into the functional layer of the endometrium, now termed the decidua. Under the influence of progesterone, endometrial stromal cells undergo decidualization, enlarging and accumulating glycogen and lipids to nourish the early conceptus.

  • Decidua basalis: The region directly beneath the site of blastocyst implantation, forming the maternal component of the placenta.
  • Decidua capsularis: The superficial portion that overlies the conceptus and closes it off from the rest of the uterine cavity.
  • Decidua parietalis: The remaining lining of the uterine cavity that is not in direct contact with the chorionic sac.

Trophoblast Differentiation and Chorionic Villi

As the blastocyst embeds into the endometrium, the trophoblast differentiates into two distinct layers:

  • Cytotrophoblast: The inner layer of mononuclear cells that actively divide and feed cells into the outer layer.
  • Syncytiotrophoblast: The outer multinucleated protoplasmic mass without distinct cell boundaries that actively erodes maternal tissues to bury the conceptus.

Projecting outward from these layers, primary chorionic villi appear as cellular extensions. These villi evolve into secondary and tertiary structures containing fetal capillary loops, establishing the crucial structural framework for nutrient and gas exchange between mother and baby.

Establishment of Maternal-Placental Circulation

By the end of the first trimester, maternal blood vessels are extensively remodeled by invading trophoblasts. Spiral arteries in the myometrium lose their muscular walls, transforming from high-resistance, low-capacity vessels into low-resistance, high-capacity channels. This physiological adaptation allows maternal blood to flow freely into the intervillous spaces, bathing the fetal chorionic villi. Here, maternal and fetal blood remain separated by a thin placental membrane, allowing efficient diffusion of oxygen and nutrients without direct blood mixing.

Structure of the Mature Placenta

At term, the human placenta is a discoid organ weighing about 500 grams. It features two distinct surfaces: the fetal surface, which is smooth and covered by the amnion with the umbilical cord inserting near the center, and the maternal surface, which is rough, dark red, and divided into 15 to 20 bulging areas called cotyledons. Each cotyledon contains several main stem villi and their branches, supplied by branches of the uterine vessels. The structural integrity of these cotyledons is critical; retained fragments following delivery can cause severe postpartum hemorrhage.

Physiological Functions of the Developed Placenta

The mature placenta acts as the lungs, gut, and kidneys for the developing fetus.

Function CategoryMechanism / Description
RespiratoryOxygen and carbon dioxide cross the placental membrane freely via simple diffusion.
NutritiveGlucose, amino acids, vitamins, and electrolytes are transported actively or passively to the fetus.
ExcretoryFetal metabolic waste products like urea, uric acid, and creatinine cross into maternal blood for excretion.
EndocrineSecretes crucial hormones including hCG, human placental lactogen, estrogen, and progesterone to maintain pregnancy.

Common Anomalies in Placental Development

Abnormalities in placental attachment or structure can lead to serious obstetric complications. Placenta previa occurs when the blastocyst implants in the lower uterine segment, partially or completely covering the internal cervical os and causing painless vaginal bleeding in the third trimester. Placenta accreta involves abnormal trophoblastic invasion past the decidua into the myometrium without an intervening decidual layer, which can be further categorized as increta or percreta depending on depth of invasion. These conditions frequently result in life-threatening hemorrhage at delivery.
