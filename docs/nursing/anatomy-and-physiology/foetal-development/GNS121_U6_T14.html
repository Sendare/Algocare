Moulding During Labour

Fetal Skull Anatomy and Sutures

The fetal skull is uniquely adapted for the journey through the birth canal. It consists of several bones that are not yet fused together. These bones are separated by membranous spaces called sutures, and where more than two bones meet, larger spaces form called fontanelles.

  • Frontal bones: Two bones at the front that form the forehead, which fuse together within the first few years of life.
  • Parietal bones: Two large bones on the sides and top of the skull that play the biggest role in overlapping during labour.
  • Occipital bone: The bone at the back of the head forming the base of the skull.
  • Temporal bones: Two bones situated on the lower sides of the skull.

The main sutures include the frontal suture between the two frontal bones, the sagittal suture running between the parietal bones, the coronal suture separating the frontal and parietal bones, and the lambdoid suture separating the parietal bones from the occipital bone. These gaps give the skull flexibility, allowing the bony plates to shift and overlap without damaging the underlying delicate brain tissue.

Definition and Physiological Mechanisms of Moulding

Moulding is the process by which the fetal skull bones overlap and compress in response to the pressure of the birth canal during labour. As the myometrium contracts and pushes the foetus downward, the pelvic floor and cervix exert counter-pressure on the presenting part. Because the fetal skull bones are separated by compressible sutures, they glide over one another. Typically, the margins of the parietal bones slide beneath the frontal and occipital bones, and one parietal bone may override the other.

This overlapping reduces the various diameters of the fetal head, most notably the suboccipitobregmatic or biparietal diameters, facilitating descent. Moulding is a normal, physiological mechanism of adaptation. Once the baby is born, the elastic nature of the scalp and sutures allows the head to gradually return to its normal shape within a few days.

Grading and Classification of Moulding

Midwives and obstetricians grade moulding during vaginal examinations to monitor fetal progress and well-being. The classification ranges from zero to three based on how easily the bones can be felt and whether they can be separated.

GradeClinical DescriptionStatus
Grade 0Bones are meeting edge-to-edge with no overlap.Normal, minimal pressure
Grade 1Bones are touching each other without overlapping.Mild pressure
Grade 2Bones are overlapping but can be easily separated by finger pressure (reducible).Moderate pressure
Grade 3Bones are overlapping firmly and cannot be separated (fixed/non-reducible).Severe pressure or cephalopelvic disproportion

Grade 3 moulding, especially when accompanied by early decelerations on the fetal monitor, serves as an urgent warning sign of potential distress or obstructed labour.

Assessment, Documentation, and Clinical Significance

Monitoring moulding is an essential component of intrapartum care using the partograph. During every vaginal examination, the midwife assesses the position of the fetal skull sutures and records the degree of moulding.

Documentation uses standard symbols: '+' for separating, '++' for reducible overlap, and '+++' for fixed overlap. Tracking this on the partograph helps differentiate between normal progress and prolonged labour. While mild moulding is a normal sign of adaptation, rapid or excessive moulding—especially high up in the pelvis—warns the healthcare team of obstructed labour before catastrophic complications occur. Integrating moulding assessments with fetal heart rate monitoring ensures timely intervention, such as deciding on an assisted vaginal delivery or an emergency caesarean section.

Complications and Pathological Moulding

While physiological moulding protects the foetus, pathological moulding can cause serious harm. When pressure on the fetal head is excessive or prolonged—frequently due to cephalopelvic disproportion or malpositions like occipito-posterior positions—it can tear the supporting intracranial membranes.

The falx cerebri and tentorium cerebelli are particularly vulnerable to tearing under extreme shearing forces, leading to intracranial hemorrhage. Furthermore, severe localized pressure can interrupt cerebral blood flow, causing ischemic injury or localized necrosis of the scalp tissues. Recognizing pathological moulding early prevents severe birth asphyxia, neonatal trauma, and long-term neurological impairment.
