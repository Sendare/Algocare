Functions of the Kidney

Introduction to Renal Anatomy and General Physiology

The kidneys are paired, bean-shaped organs located retroperitoneally in the posterior abdominal wall. Each kidney contains over a million functional units called nephrons. Blood enters the kidney through the renal artery, branching into increasingly smaller vessels until it reaches a capillary tuft called the glomerulus, where blood filtration begins. The primary role of the kidney is to maintain the body's internal environment through filtration, reabsorption, secretion, and excretion. Understanding this structural layout is vital because every functional process, from blood pressure control to waste removal, depends on the specialized cells lining the nephron tubules.

Regulation of Fluid and Electrolyte Balance

The kidneys maintain homeostasis by precisely adjusting the volume and concentration of extracellular fluid. When body water is low, the posterior pituitary releases antidiuretic hormone (ADH), which acts on the collecting ducts to increase water reabsorption, producing concentrated urine. Conversely, excess water suppresses ADH, leading to dilute urine. Electrolytes such as sodium, potassium, and chloride are managed similarly; sodium is actively reabsorbed in the proximal tubules and loop of Henle, driven by the sodium-potassium ATPase pump, while potassium secretion is regulated by aldosterone in the distal nephron.

Acid-Base Homeostasis and pH Regulation

Blood pH must remain tightly regulated between 7.35 and 7.45. The kidneys achieve this by regulating hydrogen ions and bicarbonate.

  • Hydrogen Ion Excretion: Renal tubular cells actively secrete excess hydrogen ions into the tubular lumen to be eliminated in the urine.
  • Bicarbonate Reabsorption: Filtered bicarbonate is reclaimed into the bloodstream to buffer metabolic acids.
  • Buffer Generation: Renal cells synthesize new bicarbonate from carbon dioxide and water, replenishing buffers lost during daily metabolic activity.

Excretion of Metabolic Wastes and Foreign Substances

Cellular metabolism generates waste products that become toxic if allowed to accumulate. The kidneys filter these substances out of the bloodstream continuously. Urea, derived from protein breakdown in the liver, and creatinine, a byproduct of muscle metabolism, are the primary nitrogenous wastes cleared by the kidneys. In addition, the renal tubules secrete foreign compounds, including ingested drugs, toxins, and food additives, directly into the urine filtrate. Clinical assessment of renal function often relies on measuring how efficiently the kidneys clear creatinine from the blood.

Blood Pressure Regulation via the Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System

When systemic blood pressure drops or sodium delivery to the distal tubule decreases, specialised cells in the juxtaglomerular apparatus release the enzyme renin. Renin converts plasmaogen angiotensinogen into angiotensin I. Angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) then transforms angiotensin I into angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor that raises blood pressure. Angiotensin II also stimulates the adrenal cortex to release aldosterone, promoting renal sodium and water reabsorption. This integrated hormonal loop ensures tissue perfusion is maintained during fluid loss or hemorrhage.

Hormonal Functions and Erythropoiesis Stimulation

Beyond filtration, the kidneys function as endocrine organs. When renal oxygen sensors detect low partial pressure of oxygen in circulating blood, specialized interstitial cells release erythropoietin (EPO). EPO travels through the bloodstream to the bone marrow, stimulating the proliferation and maturation of erythroid progenitor cells into mature red blood cells. Chronic kidney disease frequently impairs EPO synthesis, resulting in normocytic normochromic anemia that requires exogenous synthetic EPO therapy.

Vitamin D Activation and Calcium Homeostasis

The kidneys play a crucial role in mineral metabolism by completing the activation of Vitamin D. Inactive cholecalciferol from sunlight exposure or diet is first hydroxylated in the liver, and then the kidneys perform the final hydroxylation step using the enzyme 1-alpha-hydroxylase to produce calcitriol (1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D). Calcitriol promotes active calcium and phosphate absorption from the gastrointestinal tract. Without functional renal tissue, calcium balance fails, contributing to bone weakening and renal osteodystrophy.

Gluconeogenesis During Prolonged Fasting

Although the liver is the primary site of glucose production, the kidneys contribute significantly to systemic blood glucose during prolonged fasting or starvation. Renal cortical cells possess the necessary enzymes to synthesize glucose from non-carbohydrate precursors, specifically amino acids like glutamine. During periods of extended nutrient deprivation, the kidneys can supply up to twenty percent of total blood glucose, ensuring adequate fuel supply for glucose-dependent tissues such as the brain and renal medulla.
