Nephrons

Introduction to the Nephron and Renal Structure

The nephron serves as the functional and structural unit of the human kidney. Each adult human kidney contains approximately one million of these microscopic tubules. Their primary task involves regulating water and soluble substances by filtering the blood, reabsorbing what the body needs, and excreting the rest as urine.

Inside the kidney, tissue splits into two distinct regions: the outer renal cortex and the inner renal medulla. Nephrons span both zones, with their filtration structures sitting in the cortex and their long tubular loops dipping deep into the medullary pyramids.

Anatomy of the Renal Corpuscle

The renal corpuscle marks the starting point of every nephron and consists of two main structures:

  • Glomerulus: A high-pressure network of fenestrated capillaries tucked inside a cup-like sac. Blood enters through a wider afferent arteriole and leaves through a narrower efferent arteriole, creating the high hydrostatic pressure needed for filtration.
  • Bowman's Capsule: A double-walled epithelial cup that surrounds the glomerulus. Its inner visceral layer contains specialized cells called podocytes with foot processes that form filtration slits, preventing large proteins and blood cells from escaping into the urine.

The Renal Tubule System

Once fluid passes through the renal corpuscle, it travels through a continuous tubular network where composition changes dramatically.

  1. Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Lined with simple cuboidal epithelium featuring microvilli to maximize surface area for active reabsorption.
  2. Loop of Henle: A U-shaped hairpins structure with a descending limb that dips into the medulla and an ascending limb that returns to the cortex.
  3. Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Shorter than the PCT, specialized for fine-tuning ion balance and secretion.
  4. Collecting Duct: Receives fluid from multiple nephrons, descending through the medulla to empty urine into the renal pelvis.

Cortical and Juxtamedullary Nephrons

Nephrons are classified into two main types based on their location within the kidney parenchyma:

Surrounding the long loops of juxtamedullary nephrons are specialized capillary vessels called the vasa recta, which preserve the salt concentration gradient in the medulla.

Mechanisms of Urine Formation: Glomerular Filtration

Urine production begins with glomerular filtration, a passive process where hydrostatic pressure forces water and small solutes across the filtration membrane from the capillary blood into Bowman's capsule.

Three physical barriers form this membrane: the fenestrated capillary endothelium, the basement membrane, and the podocyte filtration slits. Together, they allow water, electrolytes, glucose, amino acids, and small waste products to pass while blocking formed elements like red blood cells and large plasma proteins like albumin.

The volume of filtrate formed by both kidneys each minute is known as the glomerular filtration rate (GFR), which averages about 125 mL per minute in a healthy adult.

Tubular Reabsorption and Secretion Processes

Once filtrate enters the renal tubule, the body must reclaim vital nutrients and water while dumping additional waste.

  • Tubular Reabsorption: Moves valuable substances from the tubule lumen back into the peritubular capillaries. The proximal convoluted tubule reabsorbs nearly all filtered glucose and amino acids, plus a large percentage of water and sodium, using both active and passive transport mechanisms.
  • Tubular Secretion: Moves substances from the surrounding blood capillaries into the tubular lumen. This process eliminates metabolic wastes like urea, creatinine, and excess hydrogen or potassium ions that were missed during initial filtration.

Role of the Loop of Henle in Countercurrent Multiplier Systems

The loop of Henle acts as a countercurrent multiplier, establishing a profound osmotic gradient in the interstitial fluid of the renal medulla. This system relies on the opposite permeabilities of its two limbs:

  • Descending Limb: Highly permeable to water but impermeable to sodium and chloride. As filtrate flows down into the salty medulla, water leaves the tubule via osmosis, concentrating the remaining fluid.
  • Ascending Limb: Impermeable to water but actively transports sodium, potassium, and chloride ions out into the medullary tissue. This salt pumping creates the high solute concentration in the surrounding deep medullary tissue without adding water.

Hormonal Regulation of Nephron Function and Fluid Balance

The final volume and concentration of urine depend heavily on hormonal signals that act on the distal nephron and collecting ducts:

  • Antidiuretic Hormone (ADH): Released from the posterior pituitary when blood volume drops or plasma osmolality rises. ADH inserts aquaporin water channels into the collecting duct cells, allowing water to be reabsorbed and producing concentrated urine.
  • Aldosterone: Secreted by the adrenal cortex when blood pressure or sodium levels fall. It stimulates the distal tubule and collecting duct to reabsorb more sodium and water while excreting potassium.
  • Atrial Natriuretic Peptide (ANP): Released by heart muscle cells when stretched by high blood volume. ANP inhibits sodium reabsorption, promoting water loss to lower blood pressure.
