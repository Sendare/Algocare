Kidneys

Renal Anatomy and Gross Structure

The kidneys are a pair of reddish-brown, bean-shaped organs located retroperitoneally in the posterior abdominal wall, roughly between the twelfth thoracic and third lumbar vertebrae. Because of the large right lobe of the liver, the right kidney sits slightly lower than the left. A protective layer of adipose tissue, surrounded by the renal fascia, cushions each organ against physical trauma.

A coronal section reveals three distinct internal regions:

  • Renal cortex: The outermost layer, appearing granular, which houses the renal corpuscles and receives the majority of the blood supply.
  • Renal medulla: The inner region composed of striated conical structures called renal pyramids. The broad base of each pyramid faces the cortex, while the pointed tip, the renal papilla, points toward the renal sinus.
  • Renal pelvis: A large, funnel-shaped cavity that collects urine from the major calyces and narrows inferiorly to become the ureter, transporting urine to the bladder.

The Nephron and Its Microscopic Segments

The nephron is the structural and functional unit of the kidney, with each human kidney containing over one million of these microscopic structures. Each nephron consists of a renal corpuscle and a long, specialized renal tubule.

  1. Glomerulus: A tuft of high-pressure capillaries enclosed by Bowman's capsule, responsible for initial blood filtration.
  2. Proximal Convoluted Tubule (PCT): Lined with simple cuboidal epithelium featuring dense microvilli, creating a brush border that maximizes reabsorption.
  3. Loop of Henle: Features a descending limb permeable to water and an ascending limb permeable to solutes, establishing a medullary osmotic gradient.
  4. Distal Convoluted Tubule (DCT): Shorter than the PCT, specialized for active secretion and fine-tuning of ion concentrations.
  5. Collecting Duct: Receives fluid from multiple nephrons, making final adjustments to water volume under the influence of antidiuretic hormone.

Renal Blood Supply and Vascularization

The kidneys receive a massive share of the cardiac output—roughly 20 to 25 percent—reflecting their critical filtering role. Blood enters through the renal artery, which branches progressively into segmental, interlobar, arcuate, and interlobular arteries before forming the afferent arterioles.

The unique portal system of the kidney features two capillary beds in series. The afferent arteriole leads into the glomerulus for filtration, and blood exits via the efferent arteriole. This efferent vessel then divides into a second capillary network—either the peritubular capillaries surrounding the cortical tubules or the vasa recta running parallel to the loops of Henle in juxtamedullary nephrons. This dual capillary arrangement allows high-pressure filtration in the first bed and low-pressure reabsorption in the second.

Mechanisms of Urine Formation

Urine production relies on three sequential physiological processes: glomerular filtration, tubular reabsorption, and tubular secretion.

  • Glomerular filtration: Driven by hydrostatic pressure, water and small solutes pass from the glomerular capillaries across the filtration membrane into Bowman's capsule, forming the filtrate.
  • Tubular reabsorption: Essential nutrients, electrolytes, and the majority of water are reclaimed from the filtrate back into the peritubular capillaries, primarily along the proximal convoluted tubule and loop of Henle.
  • Tubular secretion: Excess hydrogen ions, potassium, creatinine, and certain drugs are actively transported from the peritubular capillaries into the renal tubule, ensuring their elimination from the body.

Regulation of Fluid and Electrolyte Balance

The kidneys maintain homeostasis by finely adjusting the volume and ionic composition of urine. When blood pressure or extracellular fluid volume drops, the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system is triggered. Renin converts angiotensinogen to angiotensin I, which is subsequently converted by ACE to angiotensin II, a potent vasoconstrictor that also stimulates aldosterone release from the adrenal cortex to promote sodium and water reabsorption.

Simultaneously, the posterior pituitary gland releases antidiuretic hormone (ADH) when blood osmolarity rises. ADH inserts aquaporin channels into the walls of the collecting ducts, allowing water to be reabsorbed back into the medullary tissue rather than being lost in urine. Conversely, atrial natriuretic peptide is released by heart muscle cells when stretched by high blood volume, inhibiting sodium reabsorption to encourage diuresis.

Endocrine Functions of the Kidneys

Beyond filtration and excretion, the kidneys function as endocrine organs, synthesizing and releasing vital hormones that regulate systemic physiology.

  • Erythropoietin (EPO): Produced by interstitial fibroblasts in the renal cortex in response to hypoxia, EPO stimulates the bone marrow to increase red blood cell production.
  • Calcitriol: The kidneys perform the final hydroxylation step to convert inactive vitamin D into calcitriol, the active hormone required for calcium and phosphate absorption in the gastrointestinal tract.
  • Renin: An enzymatic hormone synthesized by juxtaglomerular cells that initiates the systemic vasoconstriction and fluid retention cascade via angiotensin.

Clinical Assessment and Nursing Evaluation

Evaluating renal function requires a combination of laboratory diagnostics and clinical monitoring. Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen (BUN) are standard blood tests used to gauge waste clearance. Creatinine is a reliable indicator because it is generated at a constant rate from muscle breakdown and filtered almost exclusively by the glomeruli.

Nurses closely monitor urine output as a primary indicator of tissue perfusion, with normal output expected to be at least 0.5 mL/kg/hour. Bedside assessments include checking for peripheral edema, assessing for costovertebral angle tenderness during palpation, and reviewing urinalysis results for proteinuria or hematuria, which indicate damage to the glomerular filtration barrier.
