Acid-Base Balance

Introduction to Acid-Base Balance and pH

Acid-base balance refers to the precise regulation of free hydrogen ions in the body fluids. The term pH stands for potential of hydrogen, measuring the concentration of these ions in a solution. A higher concentration of hydrogen ions yields a lower pH, making the fluid more acidic. Conversely, a lower concentration raises the pH, resulting in an alkaline or basic environment. Normal cellular metabolism constantly produces volatile and non-volatile acids, threatening this delicate balance. Nurses must recognize that even minor deviations from normal pH severely disrupt enzymatic reactions, cardiac contractility, and nervous system function.

Normal Physiological pH Ranges and Homeostasis

Arterial blood pH must be maintained within a strict physiological range of 7.35 to 7.45. A value below 7.35 indicates acidemia, while a value above 7.45 indicates alkalemia. Homeostasis relies on a hierarchy of three defense mechanisms working in sequence: chemical buffer systems react within seconds, the respiratory system responds within minutes, and the renal system takes hours to days for long-term adjustment. Failure of any single tier forces the others to compensate, though complete correction requires resolving the underlying pathology.

Role of Buffer Systems in Body Fluids

  • Bicarbonate buffer system: The primary extracellular buffer composed of carbonic acid and sodium bicarbonate, highly responsive alongside the lungs and kidneys.
  • Phosphate buffer system: Operates mainly within the intracellular fluid and renal tubules, handling fixed acids effectively.
  • Protein buffer system: The most abundant buffer system in the body, utilizing intracellular and plasma proteins like hemoglobin to neutralize excess acid or base.

Respiratory Regulation of Acid-Base Balance

The lungs control acid-base balance by regulating carbon dioxide elimination through ventilation. Chemoreceptors in the brainstem and carotid bodies detect changes in blood carbon dioxide and pH. When arterial carbon dioxide rises, central chemoreceptors stimulate an increase in respiratory rate and depth, blowing off excess carbon dioxide and reducing acid. Conversely, hypoventilation retains carbon dioxide to address alkalosis. This respiratory response acts rapidly, achieving noticeable compensation within minutes to hours, though it cannot fully normalize pH on its own.

Renal Regulation and Bicarbonate Management

The kidneys provide the most powerful and comprehensive regulation of acid-base balance by directly excreting hydrogen ions and reabsorbing or generating new bicarbonate. While lungs manage volatile acids, kidneys handle non-volatile metabolic acids like lactic acid and ketones. Renal tubular cells secrete hydrogen into the urine while reclaiming filtered bicarbonate. They can also synthesize new bicarbonate during prolonged acidosis. This metabolic response is powerful, but unlike buffers or respiration, it requires days to reach maximal effectiveness.

Overview of Acid-Base Imbalances: Acidosis and Alkalosis

Acid-base disorders are categorized into respiratory or metabolic origins, depending on the primary driving factor. Respiratory acidosis stems from alveolar hypoventilation leading to carbon dioxide retention, seen in conditions like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Respiratory alkalosis results from hyperventilation blowing off excessive carbon dioxide, often triggered by anxiety or fever. Metabolic acidosis occurs due to an increase in non-volatile acids or a loss of bicarbonate, common in severe diarrhea or diabetic ketoacidosis. Metabolic alkalosis arises from a loss of acid or gain in base, frequently caused by prolonged vomiting or diuretic overuse.

Arterial Blood Gas (ABG) Interpretation for Nurses

Analyzing an arterial blood gas sample requires a systematic approach to identify primary disturbances and compensation.

  1. Inspect the pH: Determine if the blood is acidemic (< 7.35) or alkalemic (> 7.45).
  2. Check the PaCO2: Assess the respiratory component, noting if it matches the pH direction.
  3. Check the HCO3: Evaluate the metabolic component to see which system aligns with the pH change.
  4. Determine compensation: Look at the uncompromised parameter to see if it is shifting to normalize the pH.

A mnemonic like ROME (Respiratory Opposite, Metabolic Equal) helps nurses quickly link abnormal values to their source.
