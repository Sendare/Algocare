Pulmonary Circulation

Introduction to Pulmonary Circulation

Pulmonary circulation is the movement of blood from the heart to the lungs and back again. Unlike systemic circulation, which supplies oxygen to the entire body, the pulmonary system specifically targets gas exchange. Deoxygenated blood is pumped out of the right side of the heart, travels through the lungs to drop off carbon dioxide and pick up oxygen, and then returns to the left side of the heart. This low-pressure circuit ensures that delicate lung tissues are protected from fluid overload while maximizing respiratory efficiency.

Anatomy of the Pulmonary Vessels and Heart Chambers

The structures involved in pulmonary circulation consist of the right ventricle, the pulmonary trunk, the right and left pulmonary arteries, the lung capillary beds, and four pulmonary veins.

  • Right Ventricle: The muscular chamber that receives deoxygenated blood and generates force to push it through the pulmonary valve.
  • Pulmonary Trunk: A large vessel leaving the right ventricle that quickly splits into left and right branches.
  • Pulmonary Arteries: The only arteries in the adult body carrying deoxygenated blood.
  • Pulmonary Veins: Four vessels that carry newly oxygenated blood back into the left atrium.

The Pathway of Deoxygenated Blood from the Right Ventricle

Blood flow follows a strict anatomical sequence starting in the right ventricle. When this chamber contracts, it opens the pulmonary semilunar valve and propels blood into the pulmonary trunk. The trunk divides into the right and left main pulmonary arteries, heading toward each respective lung. Inside the lungs, these arteries branch repeatedly into smaller lobar arteries, segmental arteries, and eventually microscopic pulmonary arterioles that feed the capillary beds surrounding the alveoli.

Gas Exchange at the Alveolar-Capillary Membrane

Gas exchange occurs across the ultra-thin alveolar-capillary membrane through simple diffusion. Deoxygenated blood arriving at the pulmonary capillaries has a high partial pressure of carbon dioxide and a low partial pressure of oxygen. Alveolar air has a high concentration of oxygen and a low concentration of carbon dioxide. Gases move down their concentration gradients: oxygen diffuses from the alveoli into the capillary blood, binding to hemoglobin, while carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood into the alveoli to be exhaled.

Return of Oxygenated Blood to the Left Atrium

Once gas exchange is complete, oxygen-rich blood collects from the pulmonary capillaries into small venules. These venules merge to form larger pulmonary veins. Unlike the rest of the venous system, the four pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood. Two veins emerge from the left lung and two from the right lung. All four empty directly into the posterior wall of the left atrium, ready to be pumped into systemic circulation via the left ventricle.

Hemodynamics and Pulmonary Blood Pressure Regulation

Pulmonary blood pressure is significantly lower than systemic blood pressure, with a normal mean pulmonary arterial pressure of about 15 mmHg. Low pressure is maintained because the pulmonary blood vessels have large diameters and thin walls that offer very little resistance to flow. Furthermore, a unique physiological response called hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction occurs when local oxygen levels drop: small pulmonary arterioles constrict to redirect blood flow away from poorly ventilated alveoli toward areas that are well-oxygenated.

Clinical Implications and Common Pulmonary Circulation Disorders

Disruptions in pulmonary circulation can lead to severe cardiopulmonary compromise.

  • Pulmonary Embolism: A blood clot, fat globule, or air bubble that travels from the systemic veins and lodges in the pulmonary arterial tree, blocking blood flow.
  • Pulmonary Hypertension: Abnormally high blood pressure in the pulmonary arteries, which increases the workload on the right ventricle and can eventually lead to right-sided heart failure, known as cor pulmonale.
  • Pulmonary Edema: Fluid accumulation in the interstitial spaces and alveoli of the lungs, often secondary to left heart failure, which severely impairs gas exchange.
