Pulse

Introduction to Pulse and Cardiovascular Dynamics

The pulse represents the palpable bounding of blood flow through the peripheral arteries, created by the contraction of the left ventricle. Each time the heart pumps, it ejects a stroke volume of blood into the aorta, generating a pressure wave that travels rapidly along the arterial walls. This wave moves much faster than the actual flowing blood. Assessing this pressure wave gives nurses immediate insight into heart rate, rhythm, and the overall strength of peripheral perfusion.

Anatomical Sites for Pulse Assessment

Nurses assess pulses at various anatomical locations depending on clinical priorities. Peripheral sites are used for routine vital signs and circulation checks, while central sites are vital during emergencies or when peripheral pulses are absent.

  • Radial: Located on the thumb side of the inner wrist, most common for routine checks.
  • Carotid: Located in the neck along the medial edge of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, used during cardiac arrest.
  • Apical: Located at the fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line, assessed with a stethoscope.
  • Brachial: Located in the antecubital fossa, used for blood pressure measurement and in infants.
  • Femoral: Located in the groin fold, used to assess lower limb circulation.
  • Dorsalis pedis: Located on the top of the foot, crucial for checking peripheral vascular disease.

Factors Affecting Pulse Rate and Quality

A patient's pulse is not a static number; it fluctuates in response to physiological and environmental stimuli.

  • Age: Newborns have high resting rates around 120-160 beats per minute, which gradually decrease as they mature into adulthood.
  • Exercise: Physical exertion increases metabolic demand, prompting the sympathetic nervous system to speed up the heart rate.
  • Fever and Infection: Elevated body temperature raises the basal metabolic rate, increasing heart rate.
  • Stress and Pain: Activation of the fight-or-flight response releases epinephrine, causing tachycardia.
  • Medications: Drugs like beta-blockers decrease the pulse, while stimulants increase it.

Techniques and Guidelines for Measuring Pulse

Accurate pulse measurement requires proper technique and clinical focus. When assessing a peripheral pulse, use the pads of the first two or three fingers, never the thumb, because the thumb has its own palpable pulse. Press firmly enough to feel the rebound of the artery, but avoid pressing so hard that the pulse is obliterated. If the rhythm is completely regular, count for 30 seconds and multiply by two. If any irregularity is detected, count for a full 60 seconds to ensure accuracy.

Characteristics of Normal and Abnormal Pulses

Evaluating a pulse involves more than just counting beats per minute. Nurses must assess rate, rhythm, amplitude, and symmetry. Rate refers to speed, classified as normal, tachycardic, or bradycardic. Rhythm describes the pattern of beats, which should be regular and spaced evenly apart. Amplitude measures the strength of the pulse wave, usually graded on a scale from zero (absent) to four (bounding). Symmetry requires comparing corresponding bilateral pulses, such as both radial arteries, to ensure equal blood flow.

Clinical Implications of Pulse Abnormalities

Abnormal pulse findings serve as early warning signs of physiological deterioration. A sudden spike in heart rate can indicate hypovolemic shock, dehydration, or sepsis, as the body tries to maintain cardiac output despite dropping blood volume. Conversely, a weak, thready pulse often points toward poor peripheral perfusion and heart failure. Documenting these variations accurately allows the multidisciplinary team to intervene before critical organ damage occurs.
