Stomach

Anatomical Location and Gross Structure

The stomach is a muscular, J-shaped enlargement of the gastrointestinal tract located in the upper left quadrant of the abdomen, just beneath the diaphragm. It acts as a temporary holding tank for ingested food, allowing the digestive process to occur gradually. Anatomically, the organ features four major regions:

  • Cardia: The small area surrounding the superior opening, or lower esophageal sphincter.
  • Fundus: The dome-shaped portion tucked beneath the diaphragm superior to the cardiac region.
  • Body: The large central portion of the stomach that serves as the main mixing chamber.
  • Pylorus: The funnel-shaped terminal region leading into the pyloric sphincter, which regulates the emptying of chyme into the duodenum.

Histology of the Gastric Wall

The wall of the stomach is composed of four standard layers found throughout the gut tube—mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, and serosa—with specialized adaptations for digestion. The mucosa forms deep depressions called gastric pits, which lead down into branched gastric glands.

The muscularis externa is uniquely modified to include an inner oblique smooth muscle layer in addition to the standard circular and longitudinal layers. This third layer generates the powerful churning motions required to mechanically break down food particles and mix them thoroughly with gastric juices.

Gastric Secretions and Cellular Sources

Gastric glands contain several specialized cell types that secrete components of gastric juice. Understanding these cells helps nurses comprehend conditions like pernicious anemia and peptic ulcers.

  1. Mucous neck cells: Produce an alkaline mucus that protects the gastric epithelium from self-digestion.
  2. Parietal cells: Secretes hydrochloric acid (HCl) for protein denaturation and intrinsic factor, which is vital for vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum.
  3. Chief cells: Release pepsinogen, an inactive enzyme converted to active pepsin by the acidic environment.
  4. Enteroendocrine cells: Secrete hormones like gastrin directly into the blood to regulate digestive activities.

Physiology of Motility and Digestion

Mechanical and chemical digestion occur simultaneously within the stomach. Peristaltic waves begin gently near the body of the stomach and grow much more vigorous as they reach the pylorus. This rhythmic action mixes food with gastric juices, transforming solid boluses into a creamy paste known as chyme.

Chemical digestion focuses primarily on proteins, initiated by the action of pepsin. Carbohydrate digestion continues briefly in the upper stomach via salivary amylase until the low pH inactivates it. Very little nutrient absorption happens across the gastric mucosa, though water, alcohol, and certain lipid-soluble drugs pass through into the bloodstream.

Neural and Hormonal Regulation

Gastric secretion and motility are tightly regulated through three overlapping phases: cephalic, gastric, and intestinal.

  • Cephalic phase: Triggered by the sight, smell, or thought of food, mediated by the vagus nerve to prepare the stomach for arrival.
  • Gastric phase: Activated once food enters the stomach via distension and chemical changes, stimulating gastrin release and vigorous acid secretion.
  • Intestinal phase: Controlled by the movement of chyme into the duodenum, where hormones like secretin and cholecystokinin slow down gastric emptying to allow proper intestinal digestion.

Clinical Correlations and Pathologies

Disruptions in gastric mucosal defense or motility lead to common clinical conditions. Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) involves erosion of the mucosal lining, frequently caused by *Helicobacter pylori* infection or chronic use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) that block protective prostaglandins.

Gastritis represents inflammation of the gastric mucosa, presenting with epigastric pain, nausea, and vomiting. If the pyloric sphincter fails to relax properly in infants, a condition known as pyloric stenosis develops, resulting in projectile vomiting after feeding.
