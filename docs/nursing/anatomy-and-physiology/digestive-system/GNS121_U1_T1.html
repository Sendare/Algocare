Mouth

Anatomical Boundaries and Structures of the Mouth

The oral cavity, commonly known as the mouth, serves as the initial segment of the digestive tract and plays a vital role in speech and respiration. It is bounded by several distinct anatomical structures that maintain its structural integrity and define its space.

  • Lips (Labia): Form the anterior entry point, composed of skeletal muscle and covered by mucous membrane, highly sensitive to touch and temperature.
  • Cheeks (Buccae): Form the lateral walls, containing the buccinator muscle which keeps food between the teeth during chewing.
  • Hard and Soft Palates: Form the roof of the mouth. The anterior hard palate provides a bony surface for the tongue to press against during swallowing, while the muscular soft palate and uvula move upward during swallowing to seal off the nasopharynx.
  • Mylohyoid Muscle: Forms the muscular floor of the oral cavity, supporting the tongue and associated structures.
  • Fauces: The arched opening at the back of the oral cavity connecting to the oropharynx, marked by the palatine tonsils.

A common misconception in basic assessment is confusing the vestibule with the oral cavity proper. The oral vestibule is the narrow space between the lips/cheeks and the teeth, whereas the oral cavity proper extends from the teeth backward to the fauces.

The Tongue: Structure, Function, and Taste Sensation

The tongue is a bulky, muscular organ anchored to the floor of the mouth by the lingual frenulum. It consists of intrinsic muscles that change its shape and extrinsic muscles that alter its overall position.

The superior surface and lateral borders are covered in small projections called papillae, many of which house taste buds. The primary taste modalities detected by these receptors are sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami. Cranial nerve VII (facial) innervates the anterior two-thirds of the tongue for taste, while cranial nerve IX (glossopharyngeal) supplies the posterior third.

Beyond taste, the tongue manipulates food during mastication, gathers food into a bolus, and pushes it backward into the pharynx to initiate swallowing. It also acts as a primary articulator for clear speech production.

Dentition: Structure and Types of Teeth

Teeth are hard, calcified structures embedded in the alveolar processes of the maxilla and mandible. Humans develop two successive sets of teeth: deciduous (primary) teeth numbering 20, and permanent teeth numbering 32.

Each tooth consists of an exposed crown, a narrow neck, and one or more roots anchored in bone. Internally, a hard outer layer of enamel protects the underlying dentin, which surrounds the central pulp cavity containing blood vessels and nerves.

Salivary Glands and Saliva Production

Saliva moistens the oral mucosa, dissolves food chemicals for taste, cleanses the teeth, and initiates carbohydrate digestion through the action of the enzyme salivary amylase. Three pairs of major exocrine salivary glands produce the vast majority of this fluid:

  1. Parotid Glands: The largest salivary glands, located anterior to the ears, secreting a watery fluid rich in amylase via Stensen's duct.
  2. Submandibular Glands: Located medial to the mandible, producing a mixed serous and mucous secretion via Wharton's duct.
  3. Sublingual Glands: The smallest major glands lying beneath the tongue, producing primarily thick, mucous secretions.

In addition to these major pairs, hundreds of minor mucous and serous glands are scattered throughout the mucosa of the lips, cheeks, and tongue, maintaining constant baseline oral moisture.

Physiology of Mastication and Swallowing

Ingestion begins mechanical breakdown through mastication (chewing), where teeth tear and crush food while the tongue and cheeks reposition it. Saliva lubricates the particles, binding them into a soft, cohesive mass known as a bolus.

Swallowing, or deglutition, occurs in three distinct phases:

  • Voluntary Oral Phase: The tongue pushes the bolus upward and backward against the hard palate, moving it into the oropharynx.
  • Pharyngeal Phase (Involuntary): Soft palate elevation blocks the nasopharynx, the larynx rises, and the epiglottis folds down over the glottis to prevent aspiration into the trachea while pharyngeal muscles contract.
  • Esophageal Phase (Involuntary): Peristaltic waves propel the bolus down the esophagus toward the stomach, aided by gravity and coordinated muscular contractions.

Clinical Significance and Common Oral Pathologies in Nursing Practice

The oral cavity reflects a patient's overall health and nutritional status. Regular nursing assessment of the lips, mucosa, tongue, and teeth is a fundamental part of daily care.

Common pathologies encountered in clinical practice include:

  • Stomatitis: Inflammation of the oral mucosa, often caused by infections, chemotherapy, or vitamin deficiencies.
  • Oral Candidiasis (Thrush): A fungal infection caused by *Candida albicans*, presenting as creamy white patches on the tongue or buccal mucosa that leave a bleeding surface when scraped.
  • Gingivitis and Periodontitis: Gum inflammation and tissue destruction resulting from poor dental hygiene and bacterial plaque accumulation.
  • Xerostomia: Chronic dry mouth resulting from reduced salivary flow, which increases the risk of dental caries and makes speaking or swallowing difficult.

Nurses must inspect the oral cavity routinely, noting hydration status, lesions, bleeding gums, and oral hygiene, especially in bedridden or immunocompromised patients.
