Physiology of Digestion

Introduction to the Digestive System and Digestion

Digestion breaks down large food molecules into smaller, absorbable units that the body can use for energy, growth, and cellular repair. The human digestive tract is a continuous muscular tube spanning from the mouth to the anus, flanked by several accessory digestive organs. Two primary processes drive this system:

  • Mechanical digestion: Physical breakdown of food via chewing, mixing, and churning.
  • Chemical digestion: Enzymatic breakdown of complex macromolecules into simple monomers.

Nutrient absorption occurs primarily across the mucosal lining of the small intestine into the bloodstream and lymphatic vessels. Waste products not absorbed are compacted and eliminated through the large intestine. Proper coordination between neural networks and hormonal signals ensures that secretions and motility match the ingested meal's composition.

Ingestion and Mechanical Digestion in the Oral Cavity

Ingestion begins the digestive journey inside the oral cavity, where teeth and tongue collaborate to masticate food. Mastication reduces particle size and mixes food with saliva, forming a softened mass called a bolus. Saliva contains water, electrolytes, mucus, and specific enzymes:

  • Salivary amylase: Initiates the chemical breakdown of complex carbohydrates into maltose.
  • Lingual lipase: Begins lipid digestion, though its activity is largely limited until it reaches the acidic stomach environment.
  • Mucus: Lubricates the oral mucosa and food particles to ease swallowing.
  • Lysozyme and immunoglobulins: Provide antimicrobial defense against ingested pathogens.

Deglutition and Esophageal Motility

Swallowing, or deglutition, moves a food bolus from the mouth through the pharynx and esophagus into the stomach. This coordinated act features voluntary and involuntary phases. During the pharyngeal phase, the soft palate elevates to close the nasopharynx, and the epiglottis folds over the laryngeal opening to prevent aspiration.

Once in the esophagus, the bolus is propelled downward by peristalsis, a series of wave-like muscular contractions. The upper esophageal sphincter relaxes to let the bolus enter, and the lower esophageal sphincter relaxes briefly to allow entry into the stomach while preventing gastric reflux.

Gastric Secretions and Chemical Digestion in the Stomach

The stomach acts as a muscular mixing basin and temporary storage reservoir. Specialized cells within the gastric glands secrete gastric juice, transforming the bolus into a semi-liquid paste called chyme.

  • Mucous neck cells: Produce protective alkaline mucus that shields the stomach lining from self-digestion.
  • Parietal cells: Secrete hydrochloric acid, which lowers gastric pH to denature proteins, and intrinsic factor, which is vital for vitamin B12 absorption in the ileum.
  • Chief cells: Secrete pepsinogen, an inactive proenzyme converted into active pepsin by hydrochloric acid.
  • G cells: Release the hormone gastrin into the bloodstream to stimulate acid and enzyme secretion.

Role of Accessory Organs: Liver, Gallbladder, and Pancreas

Digestion in the small intestine relies heavily on secretions delivered from three key accessory organs via ducts into the duodenum:

Pancreatic juice contains sodium bicarbonate, which neutralizes acidic chyme arriving from the stomach, and powerful enzymes like pancreatic amylase, trypsin, and lipase that digest carbohydrates, proteins, and fats.

Enzymatic Digestion and Nutrient Absorption in the Small Intestine

The small intestine is the main site for chemical digestion and nutrient absorption. Its inner wall features circular folds, villi, and microvilli that maximize surface area. Brush border enzymes anchored to the microvilli complete the breakdown of carbohydrates and peptides.

  • Monosaccharides (glucose, galactose, fructose): Absorbed via active transport or facilitated diffusion into capillary blood within villi.
  • Amino acids and small peptides: Transported across the epithelial membrane into the bloodstream.
  • Fatty acids and monoglycerides: Packaged with bile salts into micelles, absorbed into enterocytes, reassembled into triglycerides, and packaged into chylomicrons for transport into the lymphatic system.

Water Absorption and Formation of Feces in the Large Intestine

Material entering the large intestine consists of indigestible food residues, unabsorbed substances, and bacteria. The large intestine reabsorbs large amounts of water and electrolytes, converting liquid chyme into semi-solid feces. Resident gut bacteria ferment remaining carbohydrates, producing gases and synthesizing essential vitamins like vitamin K.

Peristaltic movements are slower here, featuring mass movements that push fecal matter toward the rectum. As the rectum fills, rectal distension triggers the defecation reflex, relaxing the internal anal sphincter while voluntary control of the external anal sphincter allows elimination.

Neural and Hormonal Regulation of Gastrointestinal Functions

Gastrointestinal activity is strictly regulated by the enteric nervous system, the autonomic nervous system, and gastrointestinal hormones. The enteric nervous system acts as the gut's local brain, controlling motility and secretion independently or alongside central nervous system inputs.

  • Gastrin: Stimulates gastric acid secretion and stomach motility.
  • Secretin: Promotes pancreatic bicarbonate secretion to neutralize acidic chyme.
  • Cholecystokinin (CCK): Stimulates gallbladder contraction to release bile and triggers pancreatic enzyme secretion in response to dietary fats and proteins.
