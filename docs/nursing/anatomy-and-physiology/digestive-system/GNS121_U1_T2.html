Oesophagus

Anatomical Overview and Relations

The oesophagus is a muscular tube, roughly twenty-five centimeters long, connecting the pharynx to the stomach. It begins at the level of the sixth cervical vertebra, passing downwards through the superior and posterior mediastinum. It pierces the diaphragm at the esophageal hiatus, usually at the tenth thoracic vertebra, before terminating at the cardia of the stomach.

In the neck, the trachea lies anterior to the oesophagus, while the vertebral column sits posteriorly. Within the thorax, the aortic arch and left main bronchus cross anteriorly, creating normal anatomical narrowings. The thoracic duct runs posterior to it, and the vagus nerves form a plexus around its muscular walls as it approaches the abdominal cavity.

Histology of the Oesophagal Wall

The wall of the oesophagus consists of four distinct tissue layers from the lumen outward: the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria, and adventitia.

  • Mucosa: Lined by non-keratinized stratified squamous epithelium, providing high resistance to abrasion from coarse food boluses. Beneath this lies the lamina propria and a thin layer of smooth muscle called the muscularis mucosae.
  • Submucosa: Contains connective tissue, blood vessels, lymphatics, and mucous-secreting submucosal glands that lubricate the lumen.
  • Muscularis Propria: Divided into an inner circular layer and an outer longitudinal layer. The upper third features striated skeletal muscle for rapid swallowing, the middle third features a mixed transition of skeletal and smooth muscle, and the lower third is strictly smooth muscle.
  • Adventitia: Outer connective tissue layer blending with surrounding mediastinal structures, lacking the serosal peritoneal covering found in the stomach.

Arterial Supply, Venous Drainage, and Lymphatics

Blood supply to the oesophagus is segmental, reflecting its long vertical course through multiple body cavities. The cervical portion receives blood from the inferior thyroid arteries. The thoracic portion is supplied directly by branches from the descending thoracic aorta and bronchial arteries. The abdominal portion receives blood from the left gastric artery and inferior pharyngeal branches.

Venous drainage follows a critical pattern for clinical nursing. The upper and middle oesophageal veins drain into the systemic circulation via the azygous and hemiazygous systems. The lower oesophageal veins drain into the portal venous system via the left gastric vein. This junction forms a vital portosystemic anastomotic site where portal hypertension causes engorgement, known as oesophageal varices.

Lymphatic drainage flows to the deep cervical nodes from the upper third, posterior mediastinal nodes from the middle third, and gastric and celiac nodes from the lower third.

Nerve Supply and Motility Regulation

Motor and sensory innervation of the oesophagus coordinates the complex process of moving food downward while preventing reflux.

  • Parasympathetic Supply: Provided by the vagus nerves (cranial nerve X). Preganglionic fibers synapse in the myenteric plexus within the muscular wall, stimulating peristalsis and sphincter relaxation.
  • Sympathetic Supply: Originates from the cervical and upper thoracic sympathetic chain (T1-T5), primarily regulating vascular tone and mediating visceral pain.
  • Enteric Nervous System: The myenteric plexus (Auerbach's plexus) and submucosal plexus (Meissner's plexus) generate local rhythmic contractions independently, although central nervous system input modulates their activity for efficient swallowing.

Physiology of Swallowing and Peristalsis

Swallowing, or deglutition, occurs in three distinct phases: the voluntary oral phase, the involuntary pharyngeal phase, and the involuntary oesophageal phase.

Once the food bolus enters the oesophagus, primary peristalsis begins. This is a coordinated wave of circular muscle contraction behind the bolus, coupled with relaxation ahead of it, sweeping downwards toward the stomach at about two to four centimeters per second. If a bolus becomes stuck or moves slowly, local distension triggers secondary peristalsis mediated entirely by the enteric nervous system to clear the lumen completely. Gravity assists this transport, but strong muscular contractions allow people to swallow even when lying flat.

Upper and Lower Oesophagal Sphincters

Two high-pressure zones act as gatekeepers at either end of the oesophagus, preventing air entry during breathing and gastric acid reflux during digestion.

SphincterAnatomical LocationPrimary Function
Upper Oesophagal Sphincter (UOS)Junction of pharynx and oesophagus (cricopharyngeus muscle)Prevents air from entering the gastrointestinal tract during inspiration
Lower Oesophagal Sphincter (LOS)Junction of oesophagus and stomach (T10-T11 level)Prevents acidic gastric contents from refluxing into the lower oesophagus

The LOS maintains constant tonic contraction. It relaxes briefly during swallowing to allow food passage, aided by the pinch-cock action of the right crus of the diaphragm.

Clinical Pathophysiology and Disorders

Pathological conditions of the oesophagus often manifest as dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), odynophagia (painful swallowing), or heartburn.

  • Gastro-oesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD): Occurs due to transient or permanent incompetence of the lower oesophageal sphincter, leading to mucosal injury from gastric acid exposure. Chronic irritation can cause Barrett's oesophagus, a metaplastic change predisposing to malignancy.
  • Achelasia: Characterized by failure of the lower oesophageal sphincter to relax during swallowing, coupled with the absence of normal peristalsis, resulting in proximal dilation (megaoesophagus).
  • Hiatal Hernia: Protrusion of a portion of the stomach upward through the oesophageal hiatus of the diaphragm, which frequently worsens acid reflux symptoms.
