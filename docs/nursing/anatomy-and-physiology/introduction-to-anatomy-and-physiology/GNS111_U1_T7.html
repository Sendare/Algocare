Human Genetics

Introduction to Human Genetics and DNA Structure

Genetics is the study of heredity and how biological traits are passed from parents to offspring. At the core of this science is deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), the hereditary material in humans and almost all other organisms. Every cell in a person's body contains the same DNA, located primarily in the cell nucleus.

  • Double Helix Structure: DNA is shaped like a twisted ladder, known as a double helix.
  • Nucleotides: The building blocks of DNA consisting of a phosphate group, a sugar molecule, and a nitrogenous base.
  • Nitrogenous Bases: Adenine (A) pairs exclusively with thymine (T), and cytosine (C) pairs exclusively with guanine (G).

This specific base pairing forms the genetic code, which dictates protein synthesis and ultimately determines the physical and biochemical traits of the individual.

Chromosomes, Genes, and the Human Genome

DNA does not float freely in the nucleus; it is tightly packed into structures called chromosomes. A normal human somatic cell contains 46 chromosomes, arranged in 23 pairs. Twenty-two of these pairs are autosomes, and one pair consists of the sex chromosomes (XX for females, XY for males).

A gene is a specific functional segment of DNA located at a particular locus on a chromosome. Each gene holds the instructions for making a specific protein. The complete set of genetic material in an organism is called the genome. Variations in these genes, known as alleles, account for the vast diversity of physical traits observed among humans.

Mendelian Inheritance and Punnett Squares

Gregor Mendel established the foundational laws of inheritance using pea plants, which apply to many human traits as well. Mendelian inheritance relies on the concepts of dominant and recessive alleles. A dominant allele expresses its trait even when only one copy is present, while a recessive allele requires two copies for its trait to be visible.

  • Genotype: The actual genetic makeup of an individual (e.g., BB, Bb, bb).
  • Phenotype: The observable physical or biochemical characteristic resulting from that genotype.

Nurses use Punnett squares as a visual tool to calculate the probability of offspring inheriting specific genotypes and phenotypes from parental allele crosses.

Non-Mendelian Inheritance Patterns

Not all human traits follow strict dominant and recessive rules. Non-Mendelian inheritance describes patterns where gene expression is more complex.

  • Incomplete dominance: Neither allele is completely dominant, resulting in a blended phenotype (such as wavy hair from curly and straight parents).
  • Codominance: Both alleles are fully expressed at the same time, as seen in the AB blood group.
  • Polygenic inheritance: Multiple genes contribute to a single phenotypic trait, such as skin color, height, or intelligence.
  • X-linked inheritance: Genes located on the sex chromosomes produce unique inheritance patterns, often affecting males more frequently because they possess only one X chromosome.

Chromosomal Abnormalities and Mutations

Genetic mutations are permanent alterations in the DNA sequence that can occur spontaneously or due to environmental factors called mutagens (such as radiation or certain chemicals). Mutations can involve single nucleotide changes or large-scale structural shifts in chromosomes.

A common cause of chromosomal abnormalities is non-disjunction, which happens when homologous chromosomes fail to separate properly during meiosis. This error leads to gametes with an abnormal number of chromosomes. When fertilization occurs, it results in conditions such as monosomy (missing a chromosome) or trisomy (having an extra chromosome). Structural changes like deletions, translocations, and inversions also disrupt normal development and health.

Genetic Counseling and Nursing Assessment

Nurses play an important role in identifying patients and families who may benefit from genetic evaluation. Taking a thorough family pedigree helps map inherited conditions across generations, identifying patterns of transmission.

Genetic counseling involves explaining inheritance risks, discussing prenatal or diagnostic testing options, and offering emotional support to families facing a diagnosis. Nurses must maintain sensitivity, respect patient autonomy, and ensure informed consent, especially regarding predictive genetic testing for adult-onset conditions. Confidentiality is paramount when managing sensitive genetic health records.
