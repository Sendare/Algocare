Musculoskeletal Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Hormonal Influences and Joint Laxity

Pregnancy triggers profound biochemical shifts that directly target the musculoskeletal framework. The primary driver of joint laxity is relaxin, a peptide hormone secreted by the corpus luteum and placenta. Relaxin remodels collagen fibers within connective tissues, deliberately softening pelvic ligaments and joints to accommodate the growing fetus and ease eventual passage through the birth canal.

Alongside relaxin, elevated levels of progesterone and estrogen contribute to generalized smooth muscle relaxation and joint instability. This systemic loosening is not confined to the pelvis; peripheral joints, including the knees and wrists, also experience increased compliance. A common clinical observation is that pregnant women notice a permanent increase in shoe size due to the flattening of the foot arches under these hormonal influences.

Postural Shifts and Spinal Adaptations

As the uterus expands anteriorly, the maternal center of gravity shifts forward. To maintain upright balance and prevent falling forward, the woman instinctively compensates by hyperextending her back. This compensatory mechanism produces a pronounced inward curve of the lumbar spine known as lordosis.

  • Lumbar Lordosis: Deepening of the lower back curve to counterbalance the heavy gravid uterus.
  • Thoracic Kyphosis: Slumping of the upper back and rounding of the shoulders, which often develops secondary to heavy, enlarged breasts.
  • Cervical Hyperextension: Forward tilting of the head to keep the line of sight level, frequently resulting in neck strain and tension headaches.

Pelvic Girdle Pain and Symphysis Pubis Dysfunction

The pelvic girdle consists of the right and left pubic bones joined anteriorly by the symphysis pubis, alongside the posterior sacroiliac joints. Under the influence of relaxin, the gap at the pubic symphysis widens to allow fetal descent. When this joint becomes excessively mobile or inflamed, it causes Symphysis Pubis Dysfunction (SPD).

Lower Extremity Changes and Gait Alterations

Lower limb mechanics are heavily tested during gestation. The combined effects of increased body weight, altered center of gravity, and joint laxity produce a characteristic wide-based, waddling gait often referred to as the pride of pregnancy walk.

Venous stasis and direct pressure from the enlarged uterus on the inferior vena cava frequently cause lower extremity edema. Furthermore, pregnant women often suffer from painful night muscle cramps in the calves. While historically blamed entirely on calcium depletion, these spasms are now understood to involve a complex mix of fatigue, altered electrolyte handling, and circulatory sluggishness in the lower limbs.

Musculoskeletal Mechanics During Labour

Childbirth places extreme mechanical demands on the maternal skeleton. As the fetus descends through the pelvis, the joints of the pelvic ring undergo further passive stretching. In the lithotomy position or during deep squatting, the sacrum is pushed backward against the iliac bones, a movement known as nutation and counternutation that actually helps increase the internal diameters of the pelvic outlet.

Improper positioning during prolonged labour, particularly when regional anesthesia blocks pain perception, can lead to overstretching or injury of the pelvic floor muscles and sacroiliac ligaments. Healthcare providers must ensure proper alignment and gentle movement during transfers to protect the vulnerable postpartum pelvis.

Postpartum Involution and Recovery

Following delivery, the musculoskeletal system does not snap back into place instantly. Involution of the joint laxity is a gradual process controlled by the clearance of relaxin from the bloodstream, which takes several weeks or even months.

Diastasis recti, the separation of the rectus abdominis muscles along the midline, is common after childbirth due to intense abdominal stretching. Postpartum mothers also face repetitive mechanical strains from lifting, bathing, and breastfeeding newborns. Without proper core rehabilitation, chronic backache can persist well into the postnatal period.

Nursing Assessment and Management

Nurses play a vital role in identifying and mitigating pregnancy-related musculoskeletal discomforts. Assessment includes evaluating posture, checking for asymmetrical gait, palpating the pubic symphysis for tenderness, and screening for numbness in the hands that might indicate carpal tunnel syndrome caused by fluid retention around the median nerve.

Management focuses on non-pharmacological interventions. Nurses should teach proper body mechanics, encourage the use of supportive maternity belts for pelvic girdle pain, recommend warm baths and gentle stretching for leg cramps, and advise on safe lifting techniques to protect the spine during the postpartum phase.
