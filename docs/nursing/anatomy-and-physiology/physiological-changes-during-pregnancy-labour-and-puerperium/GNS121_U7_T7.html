Endocrine Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Introduction and Placental Endocrinology

Pregnancy triggers profound endocrine adjustments to maintain the gestational state, support fetal growth, and prepare the maternal body for birth and lactation. The placenta acts as a major temporary endocrine organ, synthesizing steroid and peptide hormones that dramatically alter maternal physiology.

  • Human Chorionic Gonadotropin (hCG): Produced by the syncytiotrophoblast, stimulates the corpus luteum to produce progesterone in early pregnancy.
  • Human Placental Lactogen (hPL): Also known as chorionic somatomammotropin, promotes maternal insulin resistance to ensure adequate glucose availability for the fetus.
  • Placental Progesterone: Maintains the uterine lining, suppresses myometrial contractions, and prevents maternal immune rejection of the fetus.
  • Placental Estrogen: Stimulates uterine growth, enhances myometrial sensitivity to oxytocin, and promotes breast ductal development.

Pituitary, Thyroid, and Adrenal Adjustments

Maternal systemic glands undergo structural and functional enlargement to cope with the metabolic demands of gestation. The anterior pituitary gland enlarges significantly due to hyperplasia of lactotroph cells, resulting in elevated baseline prolactin levels. Despite this enlargement, the pituitary is vulnerable to ischemic injury if severe postpartum hemorrhage occurs, leading to Sheehan's syndrome.

The thyroid gland also enlarges under the influence of rising estrogen levels and the weak thyrotropic activity of hCG, leading to increased levels of thyroid-binding globulin and total T3 and T4, while free hormone levels remain normal. Adrenal glands increase cortisol production, contributing to the generalized insulin resistance and immunosuppression necessary for pregnancy maintenance.

Metabolic and Calcium Homeostasis Adaptations

Carbohydrate metabolism shifts toward an anabolic state in early pregnancy, followed by a catabolic, insulin-resistant state in the second and third trimesters. This ensures a steady postprandial flux of glucose across the placenta.

Calcium homeostasis is equally dynamic. Fetal skeletal mineralization requires massive calcium transfer from the maternal circulation, peaking in the third trimester. Maternal parathyroid hormone and calcitonin levels adapt to increase intestinal calcium absorption and regulate bone turnover, ensuring the fetus receives adequate minerals without permanently depleting maternal bone density under normal nutritional conditions.

Endocrine Triggers and Mechanisms of Labour

The initiation of parturition is not controlled by a single hormone, but rather by a complex withdrawal of pregnancy-maintaining signals and an increase in stimulatory factors. Near term, the myometrium undergoes a functional shift, becoming highly responsive to uterotonins.

  • Progesterone Withdrawal: Relative or functional reduction in progesterone activity allows myometrial quiescence to end.
  • Estrogen Dominance: Rising estrogen levels increase the synthesis of gap junctions and oxytocin receptors in the myometrium.
  • Prostaglandins: Produced by the deciduas and fetal membranes, these promote cervical ripening and powerful uterine contractions.
  • Oxytocin: Released from the posterior pituitary, it acts synergistically with local prostaglandins to drive forceful expulsion during the second stage of labour.

Hormonal Shifts During Puerperium and Lactation

Following the delivery of the placenta, circulating levels of human placental lactogen, estrogen, and progesterone drop precipitously within days. This abrupt withdrawal of placental steroids removes the inhibition on the anterior pituitary, allowing prolactin to stimulate milk synthesis.

Suckling triggers neural reflexes that prompt the release of oxytocin from the posterior pituitary, causing myoepithelial cell contraction around the alveoli for milk ejection. Meanwhile, elevated prolactin and frequent suckling suppress gonadotropin-releasing hormone, typically inducing lactational amenorrhea, though this is not a reliable standalone contraceptive method.

Clinical Implications for Nursing Care

Understanding endocrine shifts allows nurses to anticipate normal physiological adaptations and identify pathological deviations early. Nurses must monitor blood glucose levels closely in women with gestational diabetes, recognizing that placental hormones intensify insulin resistance as pregnancy advances.

During the postpartum period, nurses must assess for excessive bleeding, monitor uterine involution, and evaluate emotional well-being, as the sharp drop in estrogen and progesterone frequently contributes to postpartum mood disturbances. Furthermore, supporting successful lactation involves assessing infant latch and understanding the neuroendocrine feedback loops of milk production.
