Respiratory Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Anatomical and Hormonal Drivers in Pregnancy

Pregnancy induces profound structural and biochemical modifications across the maternal respiratory system. Rising levels of progesterone act directly on the respiratory center in the medulla oblongata, lowering the threshold for carbon dioxide and driving an increase in minute ventilation. Concurrently, the enlarging uterus exerts upward pressure on the diaphragm, elevating it by approximately four centimeters. Despite this physical restriction, the thoracic cage compensates by expanding its transverse diameter by two centimeters and increasing subcostal angle flaring from 68 to 103 degrees. These changes ensure that maternal oxygen demand is successfully met despite the growing metabolic requirements of the foetoplacental unit.

Pulmonary Volumes and Capacities Alterations

The shifting anatomy and hormonal milieu alter standard lung volumes. While total lung capacity remains relatively unchanged or slightly decreased, specific compartments undergo distinct shifts:

  • Tidal Volume: Increases by about 40 percent due to progesterone stimulation, enhancing the volume of air inhaled per breath.
  • Residual Volume: Decreases by 20 to 25 percent because of the elevated diaphragm and compressed lower thorax.
  • Functional Residual Capacity: Drops significantly by 20 percent, leaving less pulmonary reserve during periods of apnea or airway manipulation.
  • Respiratory Rate: Remains largely unchanged or rises only slightly, meaning increased minute ventilation is achieved through deeper breaths rather than faster breathing.

Arterial Blood Gas and Acid-Base Adjustments

The hyperventilation of pregnancy creates a state of compensatory respiratory alkalosis. Because the mother blows off excess carbon dioxide, her arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide drops from a non-pregnant level of 40 mmHg down to roughly 30 mmHg. To maintain physiological equilibrium, the kidneys compensate by excreting excess bicarbonate into the urine, stabilizing maternal blood pH between 7.40 and 7.47. This mild alkaline state establishes a favorable diffusion gradient across the placenta, facilitating the efficient removal of carbon dioxide from the foetus into the maternal circulation.

Respiratory Adjustments During Labour and Delivery

Labour places intense physical and metabolic demands on the parturient. Uterine contractions, anxiety, and pain trigger marked hyperventilation, causing tidal volume and respiratory rate to spike. During the second stage of labour, voluntary maternal pushing requires sustained breath-holding, which temporarily impairs gas exchange and causes transient maternal and foetal hypoxia. Oxygen consumption can double during active labour. Pain management strategies, particularly epidural analgesia, help control this hyperventilation cycle by reducing pain-induced tachypnoea and stabilizing maternal acid-base status.

Postpartum Respiratory Recovery During the Puerperium

Following delivery of the placenta, the sudden removal of foetoplacental hormones and the rapid decrease in uterine size initiate immediate respiratory recovery. The diaphragm descends back to its pre-pregnant position within weeks, relieving pressure on the lower thorax. Functional residual capacity and residual volume begin returning to baseline values within days, though complete anatomical restoration of the chest wall ligaments may take up to six months. Monitoring respiratory status during the immediate postpartum period remains vital, especially for women recovering from general anesthesia or heavy postpartum blood loss.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Implications for Nigerian Practice

Midwives and nurses working in Nigerian health facilities must understand normal respiratory adaptations to distinguish them from pathology. Physiological dyspnoea of pregnancy—a feeling of breathlessness without wheezing or cyanosis—is common and benign. However, clinicians must maintain high vigilance for respiratory complications such as pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, or cardiac failure, which present with overlapping symptoms like tachypnoea and shortness of breath. Pulse oximetry and careful respiratory rate charting are essential components of routine antenatal and postnatal assessments in busy clinical environments.
