Dermatological Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Physiological Hormonal Triggers and Skin Alterations

Pregnancy induces profound endocrine shifts that directly alter skin homeostasis. Elevated levels of oestrogen, progesterone, and melanocyte-stimulating hormone drive most cutaneous adjustments. Oestrogen increases dermal blood flow and vascular permeability, while melanocyte-stimulating hormone stimulates melanin production. These hormonal surges explain why pregnant women frequently observe sudden changes in skin color, texture, and vascular patterns without any underlying pathology.

Common Physiological Pigmentation Changes

Hyperpigmentation affects up to ninety percent of pregnant women, particularly those with darker complexions. Melanin deposition increases in areas that are naturally darker or subject to friction.

  • Linea Nigra: A dark vertical line appearing on the midline of the abdomen, usually extending from the pubic symphysis to the umbilicus or higher.
  • Melasma: Also known as the mask of pregnancy, characterized by irregular brown patches distributed across the forehead, cheeks, and nose.
  • Areolar Darkening: Pronounced hyperpigmentation of the nipples and surrounding areolae, preparing the breast tissue for lactation.

Vascular and Connective Tissue Manifestations

The cardiovascular adaptations of pregnancy create distinctive surface markings. High circulating oestrogen levels cause capillaries to dilate and proliferate.

Spider angiomas, or telangiectasias, frequently appear on the face, neck, and arms as tiny red arterioles with radiating branches. Palmar erythema manifests as a harmless, non-pruritic red mottling over the palms. Additionally, mechanical stretching combined with hormonal effects on collagen synthesis leads to striae gravidarum—commonly known as stretch marks—over the abdomen, breasts, and thighs.

Hair and Nail Modifications During Pregnancy

Hair growth cycles shift significantly during gestation. High hormone levels prolong the anagen (growth) phase, keeping more hairs on the head and reducing daily shedding. Women often report thicker, more lustrous hair. However, this is temporary; after delivery, the telogen catch-up occurs, leading to noticeable postpartum hair shedding.

Nails may also undergo changes, becoming softer, more brittle, or developing transverse grooves due to altered metabolic rates and increased moisture retention.

Specific Pruritic Dermatoses of Pregnancy

Certain skin conditions are unique to the pregnant state and require careful clinical differentiation. Pruritic urticarial papules and plaques of pregnancy typically present as intensely itchy hives starting within abdominal striae during the third trimester. Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy presents without a primary rash but causes severe, generalized itching—especially on the palms and soles—stemming from retained bile acids, posing a risk to the foetus.

Skin Changes During Labour and Postpartum Recovery

The physical exertion of labour brings immediate cutaneous responses. Diaphoresis is profuse as the body works through uterine contractions and expends high energy. Edema can increase around the perineum and lower extremities due to pushing and fluid shifts.

During the puerperium, the skin begins reverting to its pre-pregnant state. Spider angiomas and palmar erythema gradually fade within weeks. Striae do not disappear entirely but transform from pinkish-red marks into silvery-white, less noticeable scars.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Education

Nurses play a vital role in distinguishing between harmless physiological skin changes and pathological dermatoses requiring medical intervention. Assessment includes inspecting the skin for distribution patterns, checking for jaundice, and evaluating the severity of itching. Patient education reassures mothers that conditions like linea nigra and mild spider veins are normal, while teaching them to report severe generalized itching immediately to protect foetal well-being.
