Haematological Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Introduction to Maternal Haematological Adaptations

Pregnancy induces profound systemic adjustments in the maternal body to support the growing foetus, prepare for blood loss during delivery, and maintain uteroplacental perfusion. The circulatory system undergoes the most striking modifications, collectively termed the hypervolemia of pregnancy. Total blood volume increases by up to forty to fifty percent above pre-pregnant levels, beginning as early as the first trimester and peaking around thirty-four weeks gestation.

Plasma volume expansion vastly outpaces the increase in red blood cell mass. This disparity creates a state of haemodilution, commonly referred to as physiological anaemia of pregnancy. Understanding these baseline shifts is vital for Nigerian nurses evaluating routine full blood count results in antenatal clinics, ensuring normal gestational changes are not misdiagnosed as true pathological anaemia.

Red Blood Cell Dynamics and Iron Metabolism

  • Erythropoiesis: Driven by elevated levels of maternal erythropoietin, red blood cell mass increases by about twenty to thirty percent to enhance oxygen transport.
  • Iron Demand: Total iron requirement during pregnancy is approximately one thousand milligrams, heavily utilized by the expanding red cell mass, foetus, and placenta.
  • Iron Absorption: Dietary iron absorption in the gastrointestinal tract increases significantly, particularly in the later trimesters, though oral supplementation remains routine in resource-limited settings.
  • Serum Ferritin: Storage iron levels progressively decline as pregnancy advances, reflecting the continuous mobilization of maternal stores to meet fetal demands.

Leukocyte Count and Immune Alterations

The maternal immune system undergoes active modulation to tolerate the semi-allogeneic foetus while maintaining defence against pathogens. Total leukocyte counts rise progressively throughout gestation, primarily driven by an increase in circulating neutrophils. This neutrophilia starts early and can reach levels that would normally suggest an acute infection in a non-pregnant individual.

Lymphocyte and monocyte proportions alter slightly, while eosinophil counts remain relatively stable. The elevated white blood cell count during normal pregnancy means clinicians must interpret fever or other clinical signs of sepsis carefully, relying on clinical assessment alongside haematological trends.

Platelet Count and Coagulation Profile Changes

Haemostasis during pregnancy shifts toward a hypercoagulable state to protect the mother from catastrophic haemorrhage during placental separation. Platelet counts often experience a mild, gradual decrease—known as gestational thrombocytopenia—due to hemodilution and increased peripheral consumption, though they generally remain within the normal laboratory range.

Clotting factors, particularly fibrinogen and factors VII, VIII, IX, and X, increase significantly. Concurrently, natural anticoagulant proteins like protein S decrease, while resistance to activated protein C rises. This prothrombotic state safeguards against bleeding at delivery but substantially elevates the risk of venous thromboembolism throughout gestation and the puerperium.

Haematological Adjustments During Labour and Delivery

Labour and delivery place acute metabolic and hemodynamic demands on the maternal cardiovascular and haematological systems. Uterine contractions regularly autotransfuse several hundred millilitres of blood from the intervillous space back into the systemic circulation, causing transient spikes in cardiac output.

Immediately following delivery, blood loss occurs. Vaginal deliveries typically result in a physiological blood loss of up to five hundred millilitres, whereas caesarean sections involve losses of up to one thousand millilitres. Despite these losses, haemoglobin and haematocrit values may paradoxically rise initially due to immediate postpartum plasma volume contraction, before stabilizing or dropping as interstitial fluid shifts back into the vascular space over the first few days.

Postpartum Haematological Changes and Puerperium Recovery

The puerperium is the period following delivery when the maternal anatomy and physiology return close to the non-pregnant state, typically lasting six weeks. Diuresis and diaphoresis occur rapidly during the first few days post-delivery, eliminating the excess plasma volume accumulated during gestation.

Coagulation factors remain elevated for several weeks postpartum, maintaining a heightened risk for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, especially in women who are immobile after surgery. Meanwhile, red blood cell mass and haemoglobin levels gradually recover as bone marrow activity stabilizes, provided adequate iron stores are available.

Clinical Implications and Nursing Care in Nigeria

Managing maternal haematological changes requires targeted nursing interventions tailored to local health realities in Nigeria, where anaemia and postpartum haemorrhage remain major contributors to maternal morbidity and mortality. Antenatal nurses must ensure consistent distribution and compliance with routine iron and folic acid supplementation, while educating mothers on nutrient-rich local foods.

Nurses play a critical role in early detection by monitoring vital signs, assessing lochia, and recognizing signs of postpartum haemorrhage or severe anaemia. Collaborating with medical teams to ensure prompt access to blood transfusion services and promoting early postpartum ambulation are essential life-saving practices in Nigerian maternity wards.
