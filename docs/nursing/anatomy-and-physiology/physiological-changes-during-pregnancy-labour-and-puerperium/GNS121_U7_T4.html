Gastrointestinal Changes During Pregnancy, Labour and Puerperium

Hormonal and Anatomical Drivers of GI Changes

Pregnancy triggers profound systemic adaptations driven primarily by elevated levels of progesterone and oestrogen. Progesterone acts as a smooth muscle relaxant throughout the body, directly impacting the gastrointestinal tract by decreasing peristalsis and delaying gastric emptying. Simultaneously, the physical enlargement of the uterus causes significant anatomical displacement. As the uterus grows out of the pelvis, it pushes the stomach upward and laterally, while displacing the intestines upward and posteriorly. This mechanical shift alters normal intra-abdominal pressure relationships and contributes heavily to common digestive discomforts experienced by expectant mothers.

Upper Gastrointestinal Symptoms: Nausea, Vomiting, and Heartburn

  • Nausea and Vomiting: Often termed morning sickness, this affects up to 80 percent of pregnant women, usually peaking in the first trimester due to rising human chorionic gonadotropin and oestrogen levels.
  • Pyrosis (Heartburn): Relaxation of the lower oesophageal sphincter combined with upward gastric displacement allows acidic stomach contents to reflux easily into the oesophagus.

Intestinal Motility and Constipation

Slowed peristalsis throughout the small and large intestines significantly increases water absorption from faecal matter, leading to a high incidence of constipation among pregnant clients. The growing gravid uterus also exerts mechanical pressure directly on the descending colon and rectum, further obstructing smooth stool elimination. Haemorrhoids frequently develop or worsen as a secondary consequence of this chronic constipation, coupled with increased pelvic venous pressure and engorgement.

Hepatic and Biliary System Adaptations

Liver function tests remain largely within normal limits during pregnancy, though serum alkaline phosphatase levels rise due to placental production. Plasma volume expansion leads to a relative decrease in serum albumin levels. In the gallbladder, progesterone causes smooth muscle relaxation and incomplete emptying, increasing bile stasis. This sluggish bile flow raises the susceptibility of pregnant women to gallstone formation during gestation and the immediate postpartum period.

Gastrointestinal Function During Labour

Gastric motility and secretion nearly cease entirely once active labour is established, driven by pain, anxiety, and the administration of systemic opioid analgesics. Food digestion stops, and any contents remaining in the stomach stay there instead of moving forward. Consequently, vomiting is a frequent occurrence during the transition phase of the first stage of labour. This poses a severe aspiration risk if general anaesthesia becomes necessary during an emergency caesarean section.

Postpartum Recovery and Nursing Care

Following delivery of the placenta, circulating progesterone and oestrogen levels drop sharply, allowing gastrointestinal muscle tone to return toward pre-pregnant baselines over several weeks. Bowel sounds normalise, and peristalsis resumes, although the first postpartum bowel movement may be delayed for a few days due to pre-labour enemas, surgical fasting, or fear of perineal pain from episiotomy sites. Nursing care focuses on encouraging early ambulation, promoting high-fibre diets, ensuring adequate hydration, and administering prescribed stool softeners to prevent straining.
