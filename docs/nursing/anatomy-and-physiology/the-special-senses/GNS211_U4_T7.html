Mechanism of Hearing

Introduction to Auditory Physiology and Sound Waves

Hearing begins with physical vibrations traveling through the air. Sound waves consist of alternating areas of compression (high pressure) and rarefaction (low pressure). The physical properties of these waves determine what we perceive: frequency dictates pitch, measured in Hertz, while amplitude determines loudness, measured in decibels. The human ear acts as a biological transducer, converting mechanical sound energy into electrical nerve impulses that the brain interprets as meaningful sound.

Anatomical Overview of the Ear

The ear is anatomically divided into three distinct compartments:

  • Outer ear: Includes the auricle (pinna) and the external auditory canal, responsible for funneling sound waves toward the tympanic membrane.
  • Middle ear: An air-filled cavity containing the auditory ossicles (malleus, incus, and stapes) and connected to the nasopharynx via the Eustachian tube.
  • Inner ear: A complex labyrinth of fluid-filled bony and membranous channels housing the sensory organs for hearing and balance.

Conducting Sound Through Outer and Middle Ear

Sound waves enter the external auditory canal and strike the tympanic membrane (eardrum), causing it to vibrate. These vibrations transfer to the malleus, rest against the incus, and transmit to the stapes. The middle ear acts as an impedance-matching transformer. Because sound travels from the low-impedance air of the outer ear to the high-impedance fluid of the inner ear, the mechanical advantage provided by the ossicles and the small surface area ratio between the tympanic membrane and the oval window prevents massive energy loss.

Fluid Dynamics and Wave Propagation in the Cochlea

When the stapes footplate pushes against the oval window, it sets up pressure waves in the perilymph of the scala vestibuli. These waves travel through the scala vestibuli, pass around the helicotrema, and travel back through the scala tympani, eventually dissipating at the round window. As the pressure wave moves through the cochlea, it displaces the basilar membrane at a specific location depending on the sound frequency: high frequencies maximally displace the basal end near the base, while low frequencies travel further to the apical end.

Transduction of Sound in the Organ of Corti

The Organ of Corti, resting on the basilar membrane, contains the primary sensory receptors known as hair cells. Stereocilia project from the tops of these hair cells and embed beneath the tectorial membrane. When the basilar membrane vibrates, the shearing motion bends the stereocilia. This mechanical bending opens mechanosensitive ion channels, allowing potassium ions to rush in, depolarizing the cell, and triggering neurotransmitter release onto the fibers of the vestibulocochlear nerve.

Neural Encoding and Auditory Pathway Transmission

Depolarization of hair cells stimulates the spiral ganglion neurons, whose axons form the cochlear branch of cranial nerve VIII (vestibulocochlear nerve). Signals travel to the brainstem, entering the cochlear nuclei. From there, information crosses to the superior olivary complex, which helps localize sound by comparing timing and intensity differences between both ears. The pathway ascends through the lateral lemniscus to the inferior colliculus in the midbrain, then to the medial geniculate nucleus of the thalamus, before finally reaching the primary auditory cortex.

Central Auditory Processing and Perception

Auditory signals terminate in the primary auditory cortex located within the temporal lobe. Here, complex processing occurs, allowing humans to distinguish speech, recognize music, and interpret environmental sounds. Tonotopic organization is maintained right up to the cortex, meaning specific cortical neurons respond preferentially to specific sound frequencies. Damage along this central pathway can lead to central auditory processing disorders, where a patient has normal ear mechanics and hearing sensitivity, but struggles to make sense of what they hear.
