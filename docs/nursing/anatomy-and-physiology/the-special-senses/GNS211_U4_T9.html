Mechanism of Smell

Introduction to Olfaction and the Olfactory System

Olfaction is the chemical sense that allows humans and other animals to detect and identify volatile chemicals in the environment. Unlike vision or hearing, which rely on physical waves of light or sound, the sense of smell responds directly to airborne molecules entering the nasal cavity. This sensory modality plays a critical role in survival, warning humans of hazards like smoke, spoiled food, and gas leaks, while also deeply influencing taste perception and emotional memory.

Anatomy of the Olfactory Epithelium and Receptor Cells

The olfactory epithelium is a specialized patch of mucous membrane located on the roof of the nasal cavity. It contains three main cell types:

  • Olfactory receptor cells: Bipolar neurons responsible for detecting odorants, possessing cilia that extend into the nasal mucus.
  • Supporting cells: Metabolic and physical support structures similar to glial cells.
  • Basal cells: Stem cells that continually divide to regenerate olfactory receptor cells, a rare example of neurogenesis in the adult human nervous system.

Olfactory Transduction: How Odorants are Detected

Odorant molecules dissolve in the mucus layer covering the olfactory epithelium and bind to specific receptor proteins located on the cilia of olfactory receptor cells. This binding activates a G-protein, specifically Golf, which in turn stimulates adenylate cyclase to produce cyclic AMP. The rise in cyclic AMP opens ligand-gated sodium and calcium channels, causing an influx of positive ions, depolarization of the membrane, and the generation of an action potential.

Neural Pathways and the Olfactory Bulb

Unmyelinated axons of olfactory receptor cells bundle together to form the olfactory nerve (Cranial Nerve I). These nerve fibers pass through tiny perforations in the cribriform plate of the ethmoid bone to synapse with mitral and tufted cells within the olfactory bulb. These structures form complex spherical arrangements called glomeruli, where information from thousands of primary receptors converges before heading deeper into the brain.

Central Processing in the Brain

From the olfactory bulb, signals travel along the olfactory tract directly to primary olfactory areas in the brain, notably the piriform cortex and the amygdala, without passing through the thalamus first. This unique anatomical bypass explains why smells evoke powerful emotional and memory responses so rapidly, as the signals project straight to the limbic system and hippocampus.

Olfactory Adaptation and Fatigue

Olfactory adaptation refers to the rapid decline in receptor sensitivity during continuous exposure to a particular odorant. This process protects the nervous system from sensory overload, allowing the brain to notice new changes in the environment. Cellularly, adaptation is largely driven by calcium ion accumulation inside the receptor cell cilia, which inhibits the cyclic AMP pathway until the odorant is removed.

Clinical Correlates: Disorders of Smell

Pathology affecting the olfactory system can significantly impact patient safety and quality of life.

  • Anosmia: Complete loss of the sense of smell.
  • Hyposmia: Reduced ability to smell.
  • Parosmia: Distorted perception of smell, often turning pleasant scents foul.

Common causes include upper respiratory tract infections, nasal polyps, aging, and traumatic head injury where shearing forces damage delicate fibers traversing the cribriform plate.
