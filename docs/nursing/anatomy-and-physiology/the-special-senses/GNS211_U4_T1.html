The Eye

Introduction and Accessory Structures of the Eye

The human eye sits safely within the bony orbit of the skull, cushioned by adipose tissue which protects it from physical shock. Several accessory structures work continuously to shield, lubricate, and move the eyeball.

  • Eyelids (Palpebrae): Cover the anterior surface of the eye, protecting it from foreign objects and intense light through the blink reflex.
  • Conjunctiva: A clear mucous membrane lining the inner eyelids and folding over the white of the eye, keeping the surface moist.
  • Lacrimal Apparatus: Produces and drains tears containing mucus, antibodies, and lysozyme to clean and lubricate the eye.
  • Extrinsic Eye Muscles: Six skeletal muscles attached to the outer surface of the eyeball, allowing precise, coordinated movements in all directions.

Layers of the Eyeball: Fibrous and Vascular Tunics

The wall of the eyeball consists of three distinct layers. The outer fibrous tunic provides structural shape and protects delicate internal parts. It includes the opaque, white sclera, which covers most of the eye, and the transparent cornea at the very front, which bends incoming light rays.

Beneath the fibrous layer lies the middle vascular tunic, or uvea. This layer contains the choroid, a heavily pigmented membrane supplying blood to the retina. Toward the front, the choroid thickens into the ciliary body, which holds the lens and controls its shape. The colored part of the eye, the iris, regulates the size of the central opening known as the pupil, thereby controlling the amount of light entering the eye.

Internal Cavities, Humors, and Intraocular Pressure

The lens and ciliary body divide the interior of the eye into two main cavities. The anterior cavity sits in front of the lens and contains a watery fluid called aqueous humor. The ciliary body continuously secretes this fluid, which nourishes the cornea and lens before draining through the scleral venous sinus, also known as the canal of Schlemm.

Blockage in the drainage of aqueous humor leads to a dangerous buildup of pressure inside the eyeball, a condition called glaucoma. Behind the lens lies the much larger posterior cavity, filled with a transparent, jelly-like substance named the vitreous body. This gel maintains the spherical shape of the eyeball and presses the retina firmly against the choroid layer.

The Retina and Phototransduction

The innermost layer of the eye is the retina, which contains the photoreceptor cells responsible for converting light energy into electrical nerve signals.

  • Rods: Highly sensitive to light, functioning best in dim conditions, but only perceive shades of gray.
  • Cones: Require bright light to function, responsible for sharp color vision and detailed central sight.

When light hits photopigments inside these receptor cells, it triggers a chemical change that hyperpolarizes the cell, altering neurotransmitter release. This signal passes to bipolar cells and then to ganglion cells, whose axons bundle together to form the optic nerve leaving the eye at the optic disc, commonly known as the blind spot.

Physiology of Vision: Refraction and Accommodation

For a clear image to form on the retina, light rays entering the eye must be bent, or refracted, so they converge at a single focal point. The cornea performs the majority of this bending, while the adjustable lens fine-tunes the focus for objects at varying distances.

When focusing on close objects, the ciliary muscle contracts, releasing tension on the suspensory ligaments. This allows the elastic lens to bulge into a more convex shape, increasing its refractive power, a process called accommodation. When viewing distant objects, the ciliary muscle relaxes, pulling the ligaments taut and flattening the lens.

The Visual Pathway to the Brain

Nerve impulses generated in the retina travel along the axons of ganglion cells, which converge to form the optic nerve (Cranial Nerve II). At the base of the brain, the two optic nerves meet at the optic chiasm, where fibers from the medial half of each retina cross over to the opposite side, while lateral fibers stay on the same side.

Beyond the chiasm, the fibers continue as the optic tracts, carrying visual information to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus. From there, neurons project through the optic radiations to the primary visual cortex located in the occipital lobe, where conscious perception of sight finally occurs.
