Types of Joints

Introduction to Joints and Their Functions

A joint (or articulation) is the point of contact between bones, between bone and cartilage, or between bone and teeth. Joints serve two main purposes: they hold the skeleton together securely, and they give the skeleton mobility. Without joints, our bodies would be rigid structures incapable of locomotion, manipulation, or posture adjustment.

Every joint's structure determines its function. A joint designed for immense stability, such as the sutures of the skull, allows virtually no movement. Conversely, a joint designed for wide-ranging mobility, like the shoulder, sacrifices some structural stability and relies heavily on surrounding muscles and ligaments for support. Nurses must understand this balance between stability and mobility when assessing musculoskeletal injuries or planning patient care.

Structural and Functional Classification of Joints

Joints are classified using two distinct systems: structural and functional. The structural classification looks at what material binds the bones together and whether a fluid-filled cavity is present. This system divides joints into fibrous, cartilaginous, and synovial types.

The functional classification categorizes joints based on the amount of movement they allow:

  • Synarthrosis: An immovable joint, suited for areas requiring maximum protection.
  • Amphiarthrosis: A slightly movable joint, providing modest give and stability.
  • Diarthrosis: A freely movable joint, characteristic of the limbs.

Fibrous Joints (Synarthroses)

In fibrous joints, the adjacent bones are joined by hard, collagenous fibrous connective tissue. These joints have no joint cavity, and most permit little to no movement.

There are three primary types of fibrous joints:

  • Sutures: Irregular, interlocking edges of bone bound tightly by short connective tissue fibers, found exclusively in the skull.
  • Syndesmoses: Bones connected exclusively by ligaments or a broad sheet of fibrous tissue (interosseous membrane), allowing slight give, such as the distal tibiofibular joint.
  • Gomphoses: Peg-in-socket fibrous joints that fasten teeth into their bony alveolar sockets using periodontal ligaments.

Cartilaginous Joints (Amphiarthroses)

Bones in cartilaginous joints are connected entirely by cartilage. Like fibrous joints, they lack a joint cavity and allow little or no movement.

  1. Synchondroses: Primary cartilaginous joints where bones are bound by hyaline cartilage. A classic example is the epiphyseal plate in growing long bones and the sternocostal joint of the first rib. Many of these eventually ossify.
  2. Symphyses: Secondary cartilaginous joints where the bone surfaces are covered by hyaline cartilage, but a robust pad of compressible fibrocartilage connects them. The pubic symphysis and intervertebral discs are prime examples, providing shock absorption and limited flexibility.

Synovial Joints: Structure and Characteristics

Synovial joints are distinguished by the presence of a fluid-filled joint cavity (synovial cavity). They are nearly all freely movable diarthroses and make up most joints in the appendicular skeleton.

Every typical synovial joint shares five structural traits:

  • Articular cartilage: A thin layer of smooth hyaline cartilage covering bone ends to reduce friction and absorb shock.
  • Joint cavity: A potential space containing a small amount of lubricating synovial fluid.
  • Articular capsule: A two-layered enclosure consisting of a tough outer fibrous layer and an inner synovial membrane.
  • Synovial fluid: A viscous, egg-white-like fluid that nourishes articular cartilage and lubricates contact surfaces.
  • Reinforcing ligaments: Dense regular connective tissue bands that strengthen and stabilize the joint.

Subtypes of Synovial Joints

Synovial joints are categorized into six structural subtypes based on the shape of their articular surfaces and the movement axes they allow:

  • Plane (Gliding) joint: Flat articular surfaces allowing sliding movements, such as intercarpal joints.
  • Hinge joint: A cylindrical projection fits into a trough-shaped surface, allowing movement in a single plane, like the elbow.
  • Pivot joint: A rounded end protrudes into a ring of bone and ligament, allowing rotation, such as the atlantoaxial joint.
  • Condylar (Ellipsoid) joint: An oval articular surface fits into a complementary depression, allowing angular movement in two planes, like metacarpophalangeal joints.
  • Saddle joint: Both surfaces have concave and convex areas shaped like a saddle, allowing greater freedom, such as the carpometacarpal joint of the thumb.
  • Ball-and-socket joint: A spherical head fits into a cup-like socket, permitting movement in all axes, including rotation, like the hip and shoulder.

Types of Body Movements at Synovial Joints

Movements allowed by synovial joints are grouped into four main categories: gliding, angular, rotation, and special movements.

  • Gliding: Flat bone surfaces sliding past one another.
  • Flexion and Extension: Bending movements that decrease or increase the angle between bones. Hyperextension extends a joint beyond its normal anatomical position.
  • Abduction and Adduction: Moving a limb away from or toward the midline of the body.
  • Circumduction: Moving a limb in a cone-shaped circle.
  • Rotation: Turning a bone around its own longitudinal axis, such as shaking the head "no."
  • Special movements: Includes pronation and supination of the forearm, inversion and eversion of the foot, dorsiflexion and plantar flexion of the ankle, and opposition of the thumb.

Clinical Relevance and Common Joint Pathologies

Joints are vulnerable to mechanical wear, autoimmune attacks, and traumatic injury due to their constant physical role. Nurses encounter several major joint pathologies in clinical practice:

  • Osteoarthritis (OA): The most common chronic arthritis, often called wear-and-tear arthritis. It results from the gradual breakdown of articular cartilage, leaving bone ends exposed and rubbing together.
  • Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA): A chronic, systemic inflammatory autoimmune disease where the body attacks joint tissues, causing painful inflammation of the synovial membrane, pannus formation, and eventual joint deformity.
  • Gouty Arthritis: Deposition of uric acid crystals in joint cavities, causing sudden, severe attacks of pain and inflammation, typically in the big toe.
  • Sprains and Dislocations: Stretching or tearing of reinforcing ligaments (sprain) or the complete displacement of bones out of their normal joint alignment (dislocation).
