Compact Bone Tissue

Introduction and Structural Importance of Compact Bone

Compact bone, also known as cortical bone, forms the hard, dense outer layer of most bones. It provides structural support, protects delicate internal organs, and bears the weight of the body. While it appears solid to the naked eye, microscopic examination reveals a highly organized network of tunnels and channels. This tissue makes up about 80 percent of the total bone mass in an adult human skeleton, with the remaining 20 percent consisting of spongy bone.

Microscopic Anatomy: The Osteon and Haversian System

The functional unit of compact bone is the osteon, or Haversian system. Each osteon is a cylindrical structure that runs parallel to the long axis of the bone.

  • Haversian Canal: The central channel containing blood vessels and nerve fibers.
  • Lamellae: Concentric rings of calcified extracellular matrix surrounding the central canal.
  • Lacunae: Small spaces nestled between the lamellae that house mature bone cells.
  • Canaliculi: Tiny microscopic canals connecting the lacunae to each other and to the central canal for nutrient exchange.

Cellular Components of Compact Bone

Compact bone contains four main types of specialized cells, each fulfilling distinct roles in formation, maintenance, and resorption.

  • Osteogenic cells: Stem cells that divide and differentiate into bone-forming cells.
  • Osteoblasts: Active cells responsible for synthesizing and depositing new bone matrix.
  • Osteocytes: Mature bone cells trapped in lacunae that maintain daily tissue health.
  • Osteoclasts: Large, multinucleated cells derived from white blood cells that break down bone tissue during remodeling.

Extracellular Matrix and Mineralization

The exceptional hardness of compact bone comes from its unique extracellular matrix, which is divided into organic and inorganic portions.

The organic part consists mainly of collagen fibers, providing flexibility and tensile strength. The inorganic part is composed of hydroxyapatite crystals, formed from calcium phosphate and calcium hydroxide. These mineral crystals deposit around the collagen fibers, hardening the tissue so it can withstand heavy compressive loads without breaking.

Vascular and Neural Supply via Volkmann's Canals

Because the calcified extracellular matrix prevents nutrient diffusion, compact bone requires a dedicated transport network. Blood vessels and nerves travel from the periosteum into the bone through perforating canals, also known as Volkmann's canals. These canals run at right angles to the central Haversian canals, connecting the blood supply of the medullary cavity and periosteum with the interior osteons.

Comparison with Spongy Bone Tissue

Compact bone and spongy bone differ significantly in structure, location, and function.

FeatureCompact BoneSpongy Bone
StructureDense and solidPorous trabeculae network
LocationOuter layer of bonesInterior of bones and epiphyses
Primary FunctionWeight-bearing and protectionRed marrow housing and lightness

Despite these differences, both tissues share the same types of bone cells and extracellular matrix components.

Physiological Functions and Mechanical Support

Compact bone is built to withstand mechanical stress, particularly compression and torsion forces encountered during movement. Beyond mechanical support, the cortical bone acts as a vital storage depot for minerals like calcium and phosphorus, releasing them into the bloodstream when systemic levels drop. The outer periosteum covering this tissue also serves as an attachment site for tendons and ligaments.
