Skeletal Muscle

Introduction and Functions of Skeletal Muscle

Skeletal muscle is one of three major muscle types in the human body, alongside cardiac and smooth muscle. These muscles attach directly or via tendons to bones, pulling on the skeletal framework to create movement. Beyond voluntary locomotion, skeletal muscle supports critical physiological roles.

  • Movement: Generates force to walk, run, write, and manipulate objects.
  • Posture: Sustains constant tonic contraction to keep the body upright against gravity.
  • Joint Stability: Pulls across skeletal joints to stabilize and protect vulnerable articulations.
  • Thermogenesis: Produces body heat through metabolic activity and shivering when exposed to cold temperatures.

Gross Anatomy and Organization

A whole skeletal muscle is a discrete organ structured in nested layers of connective tissue. This hierarchical design provides structural integrity and allows smooth transmission of force.

  • Epimysium: The dense irregular connective tissue sheath surrounding the entire muscle.
  • Perimysium: Fibrous connective tissue that partitions the muscle into bundles called fascicles.
  • Endomysium: Fine areolar connective tissue surrounding individual muscle cells, or fibers.

Blood vessels and motor neurons travel through these connective tissue layers to supply every muscle fiber with oxygen, nutrients, and electrical signals.

Microscopic Anatomy of Muscle Fibers

A skeletal muscle fiber is a long, cylindrical multinucleated cell. Its specialized plasma membrane is the sarcolemma, and its cytoplasm is the sarcoplasm. Running through the sarcoplasm are hundreds of cylindrical organelles called myofibrils, which occupy most of the cell volume.

Invaginations of the sarcolemma called T-tubules tunnel deep into the cell, carrying electrical impulses. Closely associated with T-tubules is the sarcoplasmic reticulum, a specialized smooth endoplasmic reticulum that stores and releases calcium ions essential for contraction.

The Sarcomere and Sliding Filament Theory

The sarcomere is the functional contractile unit of a skeletal muscle fiber, extending from one Z-line to the next. Myofibrils consist of alternating protein filaments that give skeletal muscle its striated appearance.

  • Thick Filaments: Composed primarily of the protein myosin, featuring head groups that bind to actin.
  • Thin Filaments: Composed mainly of actin, along with regulatory proteins tropomyosin and troponin.

According to the sliding filament theory, muscle contraction occurs when myosin heads attach to actin, swivel to pull the thin filaments toward the center of the sarcomere, and shorten the overall muscle length without changing the actual length of the individual filaments.

Neuromuscular Junction and Excitation-Contraction Coupling

Skeletal muscle fibers do not contract without stimulation from a motor neuron. The site where a motor neuron meets a muscle fiber is the neuromuscular junction.

When an electrical impulse travels down the motor neuron, it triggers the release of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine into the synaptic cleft. Acetylcholine binds to receptors on the sarcolemma, generating an electrical action potential that sweeps across the membrane and down the T-tubules. This signal causes the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release calcium ions, which bind to troponin, shift tropomyosin away, and expose binding sites on actin so contraction can begin.

Energy Metabolism and Muscle Fatigue

Skeletal muscle requires continuous ATP supply for both contraction and relaxation. Since muscles store only small amounts of ATP, they rely on three primary pathways to regenerate it:

  1. Creatine Phosphate: Provides rapid, short-term energy transfer for the first few seconds of intense exertion.
  2. Anaerobic Glycolysis: Breaks down glucose without oxygen, yielding ATP quickly but producing lactic acid, which contributes to muscle soreness and fatigue.
  3. Aerobic Respiration: Requires oxygen to break down glucose and fatty acids in mitochondria, producing large amounts of ATP for sustained endurance activity.

Muscle fatigue occurs when a muscle can no longer generate expected force, often due to ATP depletion, ionic imbalances, or metabolic byproduct accumulation.

Types of Skeletal Muscle Fibers

Skeletal muscle fibers are classified into distinct functional types based on how fast they contract and how they generate ATP.

  • Slow Oxidative (Type I): Red fibers rich in myoglobin and capillaries, specialized for endurance and resistance to fatigue.
  • Fast Oxidative-Glycolytic (Type IIa): Intermediate fibers that contract quickly and use both aerobic and anaerobic metabolism.
  • Fast Glycolytic (Type IIx): White fibers with low myoglobin content, specialized for powerful, rapid bursts of movement but fatigue very quickly.
