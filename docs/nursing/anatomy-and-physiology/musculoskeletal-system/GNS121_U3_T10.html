Cardiac Muscle

Introduction and Characteristics of Cardiac Muscle

Cardiac muscle, or myocardium, forms the thick middle layer of the heart wall. It pumps blood through the circulatory system by sustained, rhythmic contractions without conscious effort. Unlike skeletal muscle, cardiac muscle is involuntary and innervated by the autonomic nervous system.

  • Autrhythmicity: The ability to generate its own electrical impulses without external nervous stimulation.
  • Branching fibers: Individual cardiocytes split and connect with neighboring cells, forming a complex, interwoven network.
  • Single nucleus: Most cardiac muscle cells contain one centrally located nucleus, unlike multinucleated skeletal muscle cells.

Histology and Ultra-Structure of Cardiocytes

Cardiocytes are short, thick, branched cells packed with cellular machinery designed for continuous aerobic work. A large portion of the cell volume is taken up by mitochondria, reflecting the heart's constant need for ATP and high resistance to fatigue. The contractile proteins actin and myosin are arranged in repeating units called sarcomeres, giving the tissue a striated appearance under the microscope.

Sarcoplasmic reticulum is less developed in cardiocytes compared to skeletal muscle, meaning extracellular calcium ions play a major role in triggering contraction.

Intercalated Discs and Functional Syncytium

Cardiocytes are joined end-to-end by thick, dark staining regions called intercalated discs. These structures contain two vital mechanical and electrical elements:

  1. Desmosomes: Strong physical junctions that anchor cells together so they do not pull apart during forceful contractions.
  2. Gap junctions: Specialized channels that allow ions to pass freely from one cell to the next, creating low-resistance electrical pathways.

Because of gap junctions, electrical stimulation applied to any single part of the tissue spreads rapidly to all neighboring cells. This allows the atria or ventricles to contract as a single coordinated unit, known as a functional syncytium.

Electrophysiology and Action Potential in Cardiac Muscle

The cardiac action potential is prolonged compared to nerve or skeletal muscle, lasting up to 300 milliseconds. This extended duration prevents tetany, ensuring the heart has time to relax and fill with blood between beats.

  • Phase 0 (Depolarization): Voltage-gated sodium channels open, causing a rapid influx of sodium ions and a spike in membrane potential.
  • Phase 2 (Plateau): Slow L-type calcium channels open, allowing calcium in while potassium leaks out, maintaining a depolarized plateau.
  • Phase 3 (Repolarization): Calcium channels close, potassium channels remain open, restoring the negative resting membrane potential.

Excitation-Contraction Coupling Mechanism

Excitation-contraction coupling is the process by which an electrical action potential triggers physical muscle shortening. When an action potential sweeps across the sarcolemma and down the T-tubules, it triggers voltage-gated calcium channels to open. Calcium enters the cell from the extracellular fluid and stimulates the release of even more calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum, a process called calcium-induced calcium release.

Free calcium binds to troponin, shifting tropomyosin away from binding sites on actin. Myosin heads then attach to actin, pulling the filaments inward to shorten the sarcomere.

Myocardial Metabolism and Energy Supply

The heart muscle has an absolute requirement for aerobic metabolism to sustain its continuous work cycle. Under normal resting conditions, the myocardium derives the majority of its energy from fatty acids, though it readily utilizes glucose, lactate, and ketone bodies depending on availability and metabolic state.

Because of its heavy reliance on oxygen, any interruption in coronary blood flow quickly leads to ischemia, ATP depletion, and cellular injury within minutes.

Regulation of Cardiac Muscle Contractility

Contractility refers to the strength of cardiac muscle contraction at any given muscle fiber length. This property is heavily influenced by the autonomic nervous system and circulating hormones.

  • Sympathetic stimulation: Releases norepinephrine, increasing intracellular calcium levels and producing stronger, faster contractions (positive inotropy).
  • Parasympathetic stimulation: Via the vagus nerve, primarily decreases heart rate with minor effects on ventricular contractility.
  • Frank-Starling law: States that within physiological limits, the greater the stretch of the cardiac muscle during filling, the greater the force of the subsequent contraction.
