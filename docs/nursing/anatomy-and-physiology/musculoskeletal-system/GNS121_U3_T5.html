Joints and Articulations

Introduction and Functional Classification of Joints

A joint, or articulation, is any point where two bones, bone and cartilage, or bone and teeth meet. Joints hold the skeleton together and give the rigid body the mobility required for daily tasks. Instead of viewing joints as mere mechanical hinges, nurses must understand them as dynamic structures tied to overall musculoskeletal health.

Physicians and anatomists classify joints based on how much movement they allow.

  • Synarthrosis: An immovable joint that offers maximum protection to internal structures, such as the sutures between skull bones.
  • Amphiarthrosis: A slightly movable joint that provides a mix of strength and limited flexibility, seen in the pubic symphysis and intervertebral discs.
  • Diarthrosis: A freely movable joint dominant in the appendicular skeleton, enabling walking, lifting, and self-care activities.

Structural Classification of Joints

Structural classification relies on the material binding the bones together and the presence or absence of a joint cavity. This grouping includes three distinct categories:

  1. Fibrous joints: Bones are connected by dense irregular connective tissue rich in collagen fibers, lacking any joint cavity. Most are immovable.
  2. Cartilaginous joints: Bones are united by hyaline cartilage or fibrocartilage, also lacking a joint cavity, allowing limited movement.
  3. Synovial joints: Bones are separated by a fluid-filled joint cavity, enclosed within a capsule, and specialized for extensive movement.

Anatomy of Synovial Joints

Synovial joints share a common structural plan designed to reduce friction and withstand mechanical stress during movement.

Bursae and tendon sheaths are often associated with synovial joints. These closed sac-like structures lined with synovial membrane act as cushions to reduce friction where tendons or muscles rub against bone.

Types of Synovial Movements

The structural design of a synovial joint dictates the movements it can execute. These movements occur across specific planes and axes:

  • Gliding: Flat bone surfaces slide past one another, as seen in the intercarpal joints.
  • Angular movements: Change the angle between bones, including flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, and circumduction.
  • Rotation: The turning of a bone around its own long axis, common in the atlas-axis neck joint.
  • Special movements: Actions unique to specific body areas, such as supination and pronation of the forearm, or opposition of the thumb.

Structural Subtypes of Synovial Joints

Synovial joints are further categorized into six structural subtypes based on the shapes of their articulating surfaces.

  1. Plane joint: Articular surfaces are flat, allowing slipping or gliding movements (e.g., intertarsal joints).
  2. Hinge joint: A cylindrical projection fits into a trough-shaped surface, permitting flexion and extension in one plane (e.g., elbow).
  3. Pivot joint: A rounded end protrudes into a sleeve or ring of bone, allowing uniaxial rotation (e.g., proximal radioulnar joint).
  4. Condylar joint: An oval articular surface fits into an oval depression, allowing biaxial movement (e.g., metacarpophalangeal knuckles).
  5. Saddle joint: Both surfaces have concave and convex areas, allowing greater freedom of movement (e.g., carpometacarpal joint of the thumb).
  6. Ball-and-socket joint: A spherical head articulates with a cup-like socket, permitting multiaxial movement in all directions (e.g., shoulder and hip).

Joint Stability and Range of Motion Factors

Joint stability and range of motion maintain an inverse relationship: joints that are highly stable usually have a restricted range of motion, while highly mobile joints are more prone to dislocation.

Three main factors influence joint stability:

  1. Articular surfaces: Shape determines how well bones fit together, though bones like the shoulder ball-and-socket offer minimal bony stability.
  2. Ligaments: Unite bones and prevent excessive or undesirable motion. The more ligaments a joint has, the stronger it becomes, though stretched ligaments do not snap back easily.
  3. Muscle tone: The most important stabilizing factor for many joints. Muscle tendons kept under resting tension across joints pull tight and maintain joint integrity.

Common Joint Pathologies

Joints are susceptible to wear, trauma, and systemic disease. Recognizing these conditions helps nurses monitor mobility and manage pain effectively.

  • Osteoarthritis (OA): The most common chronic arthritis, often called wear-and-tear arthritis. It results from the breakdown of articular cartilage over time, leading to bone rubbing on bone, pain, and reduced joint movement.
  • Rheumatoid arthritis (RA): A chronic, inflammatory autoimmune disease where the immune system attacks joint tissues, causing painful synovial inflammation, pannus formation, and eventual joint deformity.
  • Gouty arthritis: A painful condition caused by the deposition of uric acid crystals in joint cavities, triggering severe inflammatory responses, frequently starting in the great toe.
  • Sprains and Dislocations: Sprains occur when ligaments reinforcing a joint are stretched or torn. Dislocations happen when bones are forced out of their normal alignment during trauma.
