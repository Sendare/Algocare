Bony Pelvis

Anatomical Structure and Bones of the Pelvis

The bony pelvis is a sturdy basin-shaped ring of bone that connects the spine to the lower limbs, supporting the upper body weight and protecting internal pelvic organs. It consists of four main bones working closely together:

  • Hip bones (Coxal bones): Two large, irregularly shaped bones, each formed by the fusion of three parts during childhood: the ilium, ischium, and pubis.
  • Sacrum: A wedge-shaped bone composed of five fused vertebrae located at the back of the pelvis.
  • Coccyx: The tailbone, formed by four fused vertebrae at the very base of the spinal column.

At the meeting point of the ilium, ischium, and pubis lies the acetabulum, a deep cup-shaped socket that forms the hip joint with the head of the femur. A common misconception is that the pelvis is a single rigid structure; however, its individual bone components allow for slight movement, especially during pregnancy due to hormonal changes.

Joints and Ligaments of the Pelvic Girdle

The stability of the pelvic girdle relies on strong joints and dense bands of ligaments that limit excessive movement while permitting slight flexibility, particularly during childbirth.

  1. Sacroiliac joints: Strong synovial and syndesmotic joints linking the sacrum to the ilium on both sides, transferring weight from the spine to the lower limbs.
  2. Pubic symphysis: A cartilaginous joint located anteriorly where the two pubic bones meet, cushioned by an interpubic disc.
  3. Sacrococcygeal joint: A slight movable joint connecting the sacrum and the coccyx.

Key ligaments reinforce these joints significantly. The sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments convert the greater and lesser sciatic notches of the hip bone into foramina, allowing major nerves and blood vessels to pass through to the lower extremities.

Division of the Pelvis: False and True Pelvis

An imaginary plane called the pelvic brim (or linea terminalis) divides the bony pelvis into two distinct regions with very different anatomical and clinical functions.

The false pelvis (greater pelvis) lies superior to the pelvic brim. It is bounded by the abdominal walls in front and the iliac fossae laterally. Although called 'false' because it cradles lower abdominal organs like the intestines, it is structurally part of the abdominal cavity.

The true pelvis (lesser pelvis) lies inferior to the pelvic brim. It forms a bony canal with a definite inlet, cavity, and outlet. The true pelvis contains the urinary bladder, rectum, and reproductive organs, and it plays a direct role in guiding the fetus through the birth canal during labor.

Pelvic Planes: Inlet, Cavity, and Outlet

Understanding the dimensions of the true pelvis requires examining three imaginary planes that a fetus must navigate during birth: the inlet, the cavity, and the outlet.

  • Pelvic Inlet: The entrance to the true pelvis, bounded posteriorly by the sacral promontory, laterally by the linea terminalis, and anteriorly by the pubic crest. Its shape determines whether engagement of the fetal head can occur easily.
  • Pelvic Cavity: A curved canal situated between the inlet and the outlet. It is bounded by the sacrum behind and the curved walls of the ischium and pubis in front. The fetal head typically rotates within this roomy space.
  • Pelvic Outlet: The diamond-shaped exit bounded by the tip of the coccyx posteriorly, the ischial tuberosities laterally, and the inferior pubic arch anteriorly. Midwife and nursing assessments pay close attention to the distance between the ischial spines and tuberosities to ensure adequate outlet space.

Classification of Pelvic Types (Caldwell-Moloy)

The Caldwell-Moloy classification categorizes female pelves into four main morphological types based on the shape of the pelvic inlet. This classification helps clinicians anticipate labor progress.

  • Gynecoid pelvis: The classic female pelvis with a rounded or slightly oval inlet. It offers the most favorable dimensions for normal vaginal delivery.
  • Android pelvis: Resembling a male pelvis, it features a heart-shaped inlet with a narrow forepelvis and prominent ischial spines. This shape often leads to arrested labor or difficult fetal descent.
  • Anthropoid pelvis: Characterized by an oval inlet where the anteroposterior diameter is longer than the transverse diameter. Women with this type usually deliver facing forward (occiput posterior position).
  • Platypelloid pelvis: A flat, kidney-shaped pelvis with a wide transverse diameter but a very short anteroposterior diameter, frequently causing obstruction at the pelvic inlet.

Sexual Dimorphism: Differences Between Male and Female Pelvis

The pelvis displays some of the most pronounced skeletal differences between sexes due to the reproductive and childbearing demands placed on the female anatomy.

FeatureFemale PelvisMale Pelvis
General StructureBroad, shallow, and lightNarrow, deep, and heavy
Pelvic InletOval or roundHeart-shaped
Subpubic AngleWide (greater than 90 degrees)Narrow (less than 90 degrees)
SacrumWider, shorter, and less curvedNarrow, longer, and more curved

These structural adaptations allow the female pelvis to accommodate fetal development and childbirth, whereas the male pelvis is built for greater upper body leverage and physical exertion.

Clinical Significance in Obstetrics and Nursing Care

A thorough understanding of pelvic anatomy is vital for nurses and midwives managing labor, assessing pelvic adequacy, and identifying potential complications. Pelvimetry—the measurement of pelvic dimensions—can be performed clinically or through imaging.

During labor, nurses monitor the descent of the fetal presenting part in relation to the ischial spines, a milestone known as zero station. Narrow pelvic diameters or abnormal pelvic shapes can lead to prolonged labor, fetal distress, or the need for instrumental delivery (such as forceps or vacuum extraction) or cesarean section. Nursing care also involves encouraging proper maternal positioning to help open the pelvic outlet and facilitating mobility to ease fetal rotation through the birth canal.
