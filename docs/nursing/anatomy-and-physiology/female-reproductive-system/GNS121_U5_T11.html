Pelvic Floor

Anatomy and Boundaries of the Pelvic Floor

The pelvic floor forms the inferior boundary of the abdominopelvic cavity, stretching like a muscular hammock from the pubic bone anteriorly to the coccyx posteriorly. Laterally, it attaches to the ischial spines and obturator fascia. This funnel-shaped structure closes the pelvic outlet, separating the pelvic cavity from the perineum below. It features openings for the urethra, vagina in females, and rectum, which traverse the structure while maintaining closure through constant muscular tone.

Muscles of the Pelvic Diaphragm

The primary muscular component of the pelvic floor is the pelvic diaphragm, a broad and thin muscular layer.

  • Levator ani: The largest muscle group, consisting of the pubococcygeus, iliococcygeus, and puborectalis. It acts as a sling to pull the rectum and vagina forward, maintaining fecal and urinary continence.
  • Coccygeus: Located posteriorly, assisting the levator ani in supporting the pelvic viscera and flexing the coccyx.
  • Transverse perinei: Superficial muscles that stabilize the perineal body and support urogenital structures.

Fascial Layers and Ligaments

Muscles alone cannot maintain pelvic organ position against intra-abdominal pressure. Dense connective tissue sheets and ligaments provide passive suspension. The endopelvic fascia continuous with the parietal pelvic fascia anchors the uterus, bladder, and vagina to the bony pelvis. Key structures like the uterosacral and cardinal ligaments prevent uterine prolapse by securing the cervix and upper vagina to the sacrum and lateral pelvic walls.

Physiological Functions

The pelvic floor performs multiple vital roles in daily human function:

  • Sphincteric control: Maintains closure of the urethral and anal sphincters at rest, relaxing voluntarily during elimination.
  • Organ support: Resists downward gravity and increases in intra-abdominal pressure from coughing, lifting, or sneezing.
  • Sexual function: Contributes to sexual arousal and orgasm through rhythmic reflex contractions of the bulbocavernosus and levator ani muscles.
  • Obstetric support: Guides the fetal head through the birth canal during labor by resisting and then redirecting descent.

Common Dysfunctions and Pathologies

Weakness, hypertonicity, or injury to the pelvic floor leads to distinct clinical syndromes. Pelvic organ prolapse occurs when fascial and muscular supports fail, allowing the bladder, uterus, or rectum to bulge into the vaginal canal. Urinary incontinence—either stress, urge, or mixed—frequently stems from urethral hypermobility or sphincter weakness. Conversely, pelvic floor hypertonicity causes chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia, and obstructive voiding because the muscles cannot relax properly.

Nursing Assessment

Assessing the pelvic floor requires a thorough nursing history and focused physical evaluation. Nurses screen for urinary leakage, bowel complaints, sexual dysfunction, and chronic pelvic pain. Palpating the abdomen and inspecting the perineum can reveal signs of atrophy, scarring, or obvious prolapse during a Valsalva maneuver. Nurses also assess the patient's ability to voluntarily contract and relax the pelvic floor muscles during digital examination performed by trained clinicians.

Nursing Management and Rehabilitation

Management of pelvic floor disorders begins with conservative, non-surgical approaches. Pelvic floor muscle training, commonly known as Kegel exercises, helps improve strength, endurance, and resting tone. Nurses play a crucial role in teaching patients how to isolate these specific muscles without contracting the gluteus or abdominal muscles. Biofeedback, electrical stimulation, and lifestyle modifications such as weight management and treating chronic constipation further support functional recovery before surgical options are considered.
