Physiology of Lactation

Mammary Gland Anatomy and Mammogenesis

The female breast is structured to produce and deliver milk to an infant. Inside the breast tissue, branching networks of lactiferous ducts converge toward the nipple, ending in clusters of secretory cells known as alveoli. These alveoli are the functional units where milk is synthesized and stored. Surrounding each alveolus are myoepithelial cells, specialized muscle cells that contract to push milk into the ductal system.

During pregnancy, rising hormone levels drive mammogenesis, preparing the glandular tissue for milk production. Estrogen stimulates the growth and branching of the ductal system, while progesterone promotes the development of the alveolar secretory structures. High levels of circulating estrogen and progesterone during gestation inhibit actual milk secretion, keeping the gland in a preparatory state until delivery occurs.

Lactogenesis: Stages and Onset of Milk Secretion

Lactogenesis is the process through which the mammary gland becomes competent to secrete milk. It is divided into distinct phases occurring before and after parturition.

  • Lactogenesis I (Mid-Pregnancy): The breasts differentiate and develop the capacity to synthesize small amounts of milk components, such as lactose and protein, forming colostrum.
  • Lactogenesis II (Postpartum): Triggered by the delivery of the placenta and the sudden drop in circulating progesterone and estrogen, copious milk secretion begins. Prolactin takes the primary role, and the volume of milk increases significantly, usually by the second to fourth day after birth.

Hormonal Regulation: Prolactin and Oxytocin

Two key pituitary hormones govern the production and release of breast milk, working through a delicate balance of supply and demand.

Prolactin, secreted by the anterior pituitary, drives milk synthesis in the alveolar cells. Every time an infant suckles, sensory impulses travel from the nipple to the hypothalamus, causing a sharp surge in prolactin levels that signals the alveoli to manufacture milk for the next feeding.

Oxytocin, released by the posterior pituitary, is responsible for milk ejection. The baby's suckling triggers oxytocin release, which travels through the bloodstream to contract the myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli, squeezing milk down into the ducts.

The Milk Ejection Reflex

The milk ejection reflex, commonly known as the let-down reflex, is a neuroendocrine response. Sensory nerve endings in the nipple are stimulated by the infant's suckling. These signals travel via the spinal cord to the hypothalamus, prompting the posterior pituitary gland to release oxytocin into the bloodstream.

This reflex can be conditioned and is easily influenced by maternal emotions. Stress, anxiety, or pain can trigger the release of epinephrine, which constricts mammary blood vessels and blocks oxytocin from reaching the breast. Conversely, the sight, sound, or thought of the baby can trigger spontaneous milk let-down.

Galactopoiesis and Milk Maintenance

Galactopoiesis is the maintenance of established milk production. Once lactation is successfully initiated, continued milk secretion relies primarily on autocrine control—the supply-and-demand mechanism.

A protein called Feedback Inhibitor of Lactation (FIL) plays a central role here. When milk accumulates and is not removed from the alveoli, FIL builds up and suppresses further milk synthesis by the secretory cells. Regular removal of milk through nursing or expressing clears FIL and prompts the alveoli to produce more milk. If milk removal stops, increased pressure and FIL accumulation lead to involution of the mammary tissue.

Composition of Breast Milk

Human breast milk changes dynamically over time to meet the growing infant's nutritional and immunological requirements.

  • Colostrum: Produced during the first few days postpartum, this thick, yellowish fluid is rich in secretory IgA, white blood cells, and proteins, providing crucial immune protection.
  • Transitional Milk: Secreted from around day four to two weeks postpartum, this milk gradually increases in fat, lactose, and water-soluble vitamins, shifting in volume.
  • Mature Milk: Reaching stability after two weeks, mature milk divides into foremilk (watery and rich in lactose at the start of a feed) and hindmilk (higher in fat content towards the end of a feed).

Clinical Challenges in Lactation

Various complications can disrupt the normal physiology of lactation, causing distress for both mother and infant.

  • Engorgement: Overfilling of the breasts with milk and fluid, leading to edema and tight, painful tissue that prevents the baby from latching properly.
  • Mastitis: Inflammation of the breast tissue, often associated with milk stasis and bacterial infection, presenting with localized tenderness, warmth, and flu-like symptoms.
  • Sore Nipples: Frequently caused by incorrect infant positioning and latching, which damages the delicate skin integrity of the nipple surface.
