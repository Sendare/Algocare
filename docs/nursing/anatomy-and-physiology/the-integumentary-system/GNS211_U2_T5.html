Functions of the Skin

Introduction to the Integumentary System and Skin

The skin is the largest organ of the human body, forming the primary boundary between internal physiological systems and the external environment. Structurally, it consists of two main layers: the superficial epidermis, composed of stratified squamous epithelium, and the deeper dermis, composed of dense irregular connective tissue. Beneath these lies the hypodermis (or subcutaneous layer), which anchors the skin to underlying muscle and bone while providing insulation and fat storage.

  • Epidermis: Avascular outer layer responsible for barrier formation and melanin production.
  • Dermis: Vascularized middle layer containing hair follicles, sweat glands, blood vessels, and nerve endings.
  • Hypodermis: Loose connective and adipose tissue layer cushioning internal organs.

Protection and Immune Defense

The skin protects underlying tissues from mechanical trauma, chemical exposure, microbial invasion, and ultraviolet radiation. The stratum corneum acts as a physical and chemical barrier, reinforced by keratin and surface lipids that inhibit water loss and pathogen entry. The slightly acidic skin pH, known as the acid mantle, discourages bacterial colonization.

Immune defense relies heavily on resident Langerhans cells within the epidermis. These specialized dendritic cells phagocytose antigens and present them to T-lymphocytes, initiating an adaptive immune response. Additionally, melanin shields deeper cellular DNA from UV-induced mutations.

Thermoregulation and Temperature Control

Body temperature homeostasis depends heavily on cutaneous blood flow and sweat production, regulated by the hypothalamus. When core body temperature rises, dermal blood vessels undergo vasodilation, increasing heat loss through radiation and convection. Simultaneously, eccrine sweat glands secrete fluid; its subsequent evaporation cools the skin surface.

Conversely, when exposed to cold environments, vasoconstriction minimizes blood flow to the skin, trapping heat in the body's core. Contraction of arrector pili muscles produces goosebumps, a minor vestigial heat-conservation mechanism in humans.

Cutaneous Sensation and Sensory Reception

The skin functions as a massive sensory organ packed with specialized nerve receptors that detect environmental stimuli. These receptors translate mechanical, thermal, and nociceptive signals into electrical impulses for the central nervous system.

  • Meissner corpuscles: Detect light touch and low-frequency vibrations, concentrated in hairless skin.
  • Pacinian corpuscles: Respond to deep pressure and high-frequency vibration.
  • Merkel discs: Register sustained touch and spatial texture.
  • Free nerve endings: Sense pain, temperature, and crude touch.

Metabolic Functions: Vitamin D Synthesis

Beyond acting as a physical barrier, the skin serves as an endocrine and metabolic site. When epidermal keratinocytes are exposed to ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation, they convert a cholesterol precursor into cholecalciferol (Vitamin D3).

This cholecalciferol enters the bloodstream and travels to the liver and kidneys, where it undergoes sequential hydroxylation to become calcitriol (the active form of Vitamin D). Calcitriol is essential for dietary calcium and phosphorus absorption in the gastrointestinal tract, supporting bone mineralization and neuromuscular function.

Excretion and Secretion

The skin plays a secondary excretory role by eliminating small amounts of metabolic wastes through glandular secretions. Eccrine sweat glands secrete a hypotonic fluid composed primarily of water, sodium chloride, urea, uric acid, and ammonia.

Additionally, sebaceous glands secrete sebum, an oily substance composed of lipids, triglycerides, and wax. Sebum lubricates the hair and stratum corneum, preventing skin cracking and providing mild antimicrobial action, though excessive production can contribute to acne.

Blood Reservoir and Absorption

The extensive vascular network of the dermis allows the skin to act as a blood reservoir, holding roughly five percent of the body's total blood volume. When vigorous exercise or systemic stress increases demand in skeletal muscles or internal organs, dermal blood vessels constrict to shunt blood toward those active sites.

While the skin is largely impermeable to water and most toxins, certain lipid-soluble substances, gases, and transdermal medications (such as hormone patches or analgesics) can cross the stratum corneum and enter the dermal capillaries through passive diffusion.

Clinical Relevance for Nursing Practice

Understanding skin functions directly guides bedside nursing care and patient assessment. Alterations in skin color, turgor, or temperature frequently signal systemic pathology, such as dehydration, hypoxia, or infection. For instance, impaired thermoregulation or decreased sensation places immobile patients at high risk for pressure injuries, as they cannot feel localized tissue ischemia.

Furthermore, nurses must monitor skin integrity during topical drug administration and assess for signs of Vitamin D deficiency in vulnerable populations with limited sunlight exposure.
