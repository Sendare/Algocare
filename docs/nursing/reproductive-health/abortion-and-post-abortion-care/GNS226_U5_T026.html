Problems of Unsafe Abortion

Introduction and Legal Context of Unsafe Abortion in Nigeria

Unsafe abortion remains a major public health concern in Nigeria, contributing significantly to maternal morbidity and mortality. The World Health Organization defines an unsafe abortion as a procedure for terminating an unintended pregnancy carried out either by persons lacking the necessary skills or in an environment that does not conform to minimal medical standards, or both. In Nigeria, restrictive abortion laws, deeply rooted in the Criminal Code and Penal Code, permit the procedure only to save the life of the pregnant woman. Because legal channels are severely restricted, many women and girls resort to clandestine procedures performed by unqualified practitioners using crude methods, ranging from the insertion of foreign objects to the ingestion of toxic concoctions and unprescribed medications. This clandestine nature pushes vulnerable populations into secrecy, delaying timely interventions and increasing the severity of resulting complications.

Immediate Physical Complications and Emergencies

The physical trauma following an incomplete or unsafe abortion often manifests as acute surgical and medical emergencies. Nurses working in emergency and gynaecological wards must recognize these life-threatening presentations promptly.

  • Haemorrhage: Excessive bleeding resulting from incomplete evacuation, uterine atony, or severe lacerations of the cervix and myometrium.
  • Sepsis: Systemic infection arising from unsterile instruments, introduction of bacteria into the uterine cavity, or delayed treatment of retained products of conception.
  • Visceral Injury: Perforation of the uterine wall, bladder, or bowel caused by probes, curettes, or other foreign implements.
  • Toxicity: Systemic poisoning resulting from the ingestion of dangerous substances such as herbal preparations, excessive doses of oxytocics, or caustic chemicals.

Long-Term Reproductive Health Consequences

Survivors of unsafe abortion frequently face chronic physical ailments that impair their future reproductive health and overall quality of life. Scarring of the inner uterine lining from aggressive curettage can lead to intrauterine adhesions, a condition known as Asherman syndrome, which presents with amenorrhea and secondary infertility. Chronic pelvic inflammatory disease frequently develops from ascending infections, resulting in chronic pelvic pain, dyspareunia, and tubal blockage. Tubal scarring substantially increases the risk of future ectopic pregnancies, where a fertilized egg implants outside the uterine cavity, posing another severe threat to maternal life. Furthermore, cervical incompetence resulting from traumatic mechanical dilatation can cause recurrent mid-trimester miscarriages or premature deliveries in subsequent desired pregnancies.

Psychological and Social Impacts on the Patient

The psychological burden of unsafe abortion is exacerbated by societal stigma, moral condemnation, and fear of legal repercussions. Patients frequently experience acute stress disorders, anxiety, depression, and prolonged grief, particularly when facing life-threatening physical complications or the permanent loss of fertility. In many Nigerian communities, pregnancy outside marriage or termination of pregnancy carries severe social ostracization, forcing women to suffer in silence without familial or community support networks. This lack of a supportive environment deters individuals from seeking psychological counseling or follow-up medical care, deepening their isolation and compounding their mental health distress.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of Risk Factors

Clinical assessment requires a high index of suspicion, non-judgmental communication, and systematic data collection. Nurses must evaluate vital signs for early signs of hypovolemic shock or systemic infection. A thorough obstetric and gynecological history should be taken, noting the date of the last menstrual period, details of any interventions attempted, and current symptoms such as abnormal bleeding, lower abdominal pain, or fever. Nurses must maintain strict confidentiality to build trust, ensuring patients feel safe enough to disclose vital clinical details without fear of judgment or police reporting. Recognizing subtle signs of sepsis or internal bleeding during these assessments prevents rapid clinical deterioration.

Post-Abortion Care (PAC) and Medical Management

Post-Abortion Care is an emergency intervention strategy designed to manage complications of both safe and unsafe induced or spontaneous abortions. The core components of PAC include emergency treatment for complications, contraceptive counseling and services, and linkage to broader reproductive health care. Medical management often involves the use of uterotonics such as misoprostol or surgical uterine evacuation via Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA) to clear retained products of conception safely. Antibiotic therapy is initiated promptly if signs of infection are present. Crucially, PAC services must be provided regardless of the legal status of the abortion or the patient's ability to pay, emphasizing the nurse's ethical duty to preserve human life.

Prevention Strategies and the Role of the Nurse

Preventing unsafe abortion requires a multi-faceted approach centered on primary healthcare, education, and community engagement. Nurses play a pivotal role in expanding access to modern, effective contraception, providing unbiased family planning information to adolescents and adults alike. Community outreach programs led by nurses help dismantle myths surrounding contraceptives and reduce the stigma associated with seeking sexual health services. Advocating for comprehensive sexuality education in schools equips young people with accurate knowledge about their bodies and reproductive health. By fostering open, supportive dialogues within healthcare settings, nurses empower patients to make informed choices, ultimately reducing reliance on clandestine and dangerous abortion practices.
