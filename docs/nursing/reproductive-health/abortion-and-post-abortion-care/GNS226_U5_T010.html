Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA)

Introduction and Definition of Manual Vacuum Aspiration (MVA)

Manual Vacuum Aspiration is a safe and highly effective surgical procedure used to empty the uterine cavity using a specially designed syringe to create a vacuum. Unlike electric vacuum pumps, the MVA system relies on a handheld plastic aspirator that holds a vacuum created manually by the operator before the procedure begins. This makes it particularly valuable in low-resource healthcare settings, rural health clinics, and emergency obstetric care units across Nigeria where reliable electricity supply can be a major challenge.

The technique is primarily valued for its simplicity, quiet operation, and portability. Patients typically experience less anxiety because there is no loud motor noise humming in the background. Clinical guidelines recommend MVA for uterine evacuation up to the 12th to 14th week of pregnancy, making it a cornerstone technology in reducing maternal morbidity and mortality associated with unsafe abortions and incomplete miscarriages.

Indications and Contraindications for MVA

Clinical application of MVA requires careful patient selection to ensure safety and therapeutic success. Knowing when to use the procedure—and when to avoid it—prevents severe pelvic infections, uterine perforation, and uncontrolled hemorrhage.

  • Indications: Management of incomplete abortion, missed abortion, induced abortion within legal gestational limits, molar pregnancy management, and endometrial biopsy.
  • Contraindications: Gestational age beyond 14 weeks, acute pelvic inflammatory disease, known bleeding disorders, severe cervical stenosis, and suspected uterine perforation.

Components of the MVA Instrument and Assembly

The MVA kit consists of reusable and disposable components that must be meticulously inspected and assembled before any clinical procedure. Understanding each part ensures rapid assembly and immediate troubleshooting if a vacuum leak occurs.

  • Aspirator body: A clear plastic cylinder with a volume capacity of 60 cc, marked with graduation lines to measure evacuated tissue and blood loss.
  • Plunger and piston: The internal rod and rubber seal that pull back to create and maintain the vacuum inside the cylinder.
  • Buttons and valves: The pinch valves and button locks that hold the plunger in place and release the vacuum when pressed.
  • Cannulae: Flexible or rigid plastic tubes ranging from sizes 4mm to 12mm, matched to the gestational age in weeks.
  • Silicone lubricant: Applied sparingly to the piston ring to ensure smooth movement and maintain an airtight seal.

Pre-Procedure Preparation and Patient Counseling

Comprehensive preparation reduces patient anxiety, confirms clinical eligibility, and prepares the pelvic anatomy for safe instrument passage. Nurses must conduct a thorough physical and psychological assessment before the patient is brought to the treatment room.

  1. Counseling: Explain the procedure in clear, simple language, discuss pain management options, and provide emotional reassurance.
  2. Informed Consent: Ensure the patient signs the institutional consent form voluntarily after all questions are answered.
  3. Bladder Emptying: Ask the patient to empty her bladder completely to reduce uterine displacement and discomfort.
  4. Bimanual Examination: Perform a pelvic exam to assess uterine size, position (anteverted or retroverted), and cervical consistency.
  5. Medication Administration: Administer prophylactic antibiotics and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or paracervical block as ordered.

Step-by-Step Procedure for Performing MVA

Executing MVA requires sterile technique, precise anatomical navigation, and continuous observation of the patient's vital signs and verbal responses.

  • Exposure and Antisepsis: Place the patient in the lithotomy position, insert a speculum, and clean the cervix and vagina using an antiseptic solution.
  • Cervical Stabilization: Grasp the anterior lip of the cervix gently with a tenaculum forceps.
  • Uterine Sounding: Gently insert a uterine sound to measure cavity depth and confirm uterine position.
  • Cervical Dilatation: Pass graduated dilators if necessary to accommodate the chosen cannula size.
  • Cannula Insertion and Evacuation: Introduce the prepared MVA cannula through the internal os until it touches the fundus, withdraw slightly, attach the pre-vacuumed syringe, release the pinch valves, and rotate the syringe 360 degrees while moving gently back and forth until the cavity is empty.

Post-Procedure Care and Monitoring

Immediate recovery monitoring prevents delayed complications such as hemorrhage or uterine atony. Once the procedure is complete, the extracted tissue is inspected to ensure completeness of products of conception.

Tissue inspection involves floating the specimen in a shallow basin of clean water or saline to identify chorionic villi, gestational sacs, or fetal parts. Nurses monitor vital signs every 15 minutes for the first hour, assess vaginal bleeding amounts, and check uterine firmness. Patients receive counseling on warning signs, hygiene, nutritional support, and contraception before discharge. Post-abortion contraception is initiated immediately unless the patient declines, as fertility returns rapidly within two weeks.

Recognition and Management of Complications

Although MVA is remarkably safe, vigilant nursing staff must remain alert to potential adverse events. Early identification saves lives.

  • Uterine Perforation: Caused by forceful sounding or cannula passage. Symptoms include severe lower abdominal pain out of proportion to recovery, unusual depth on sounding, or sudden internal bleeding. Stop the procedure immediately and monitor closely.
  • Incomplete Evacuation: Persistent heavy bleeding and cramping. Requires repeat aspiration or medical management.
  • Hemorrhage: Excessive bleeding due to uterine atony or retained tissue. Treat with bimanual massage, uterotonic drugs like oxytocin or misoprostol, and fluid resuscitation.
  • Infection: Fever, foul-smelling discharge, and pelvic tenderness managed with broad-spectrum antibiotics.

Instrument Processing, Cleaning, and Sterilization

Proper reprocessing of MVA instruments prevents healthcare-associated infections and ensures equipment longevity. All items must be handled wearing heavy-duty utility gloves.

  1. Decontamination: Immediately submerge used cannulae and syringes in a 0.5% chlorine solution for 10 minutes to kill bloodborne pathogens.
  2. Cleaning: Wash all components thoroughly with brush, soap, and clean water, flushing the lumina of cannulae to remove organic debris.
  3. High-Level Disinfection or Sterilization: Process instruments via autoclaving, chemical soaking in glutaraldehyde, or high-level boiling according to institutional standards.
  4. Storage: Dry completely and store in clean, covered containers to prevent dust and insect contamination.
