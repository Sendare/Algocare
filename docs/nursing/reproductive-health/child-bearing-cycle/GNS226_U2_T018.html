Multiple Pregnancy

Introduction and Definition of Multiple Pregnancy

A multiple pregnancy occurs when a woman carries two or more fetuses simultaneously during the same gestation. The incidence has risen significantly over recent decades, driven largely by the widespread use of assisted reproductive technology and delayed childbearing. Twins represent the vast majority of multiple births, categorized broadly into dizygotic and monozygotic types. Dizygotic twins arise from the independent fertilization of two separate ova by two individual spermatozoa, resulting in genetically distinct siblings who share no more genetic similarity than ordinary siblings. Monozygotic twins develop from the division of a single fertilized ovum into two embryos, producing genetically identical offspring. Recognizing these foundational concepts is critical for nurses caring for expectant mothers in Nigerian healthcare facilities, as twin gestations carry inherently higher risks than singleton pregnancies.

Zygosity, Chorionicity, and Amnionicity

Understanding the placental arrangements in multiple pregnancies is clinically more important than knowing genetic zygosity alone. Chorionicity refers to the number of placentas, while amnionicity refers to the number of amniotic sacs.

  • Dichorionic Diamniotic (DCDA): Each fetus has its own placenta and amniotic sac. This is typical of all dizygotic and about one-third of monozygotic twins.
  • Monochorionic Diamniotic (MCDA): Fetuses share a single placenta but occupy separate amniotic sacs. This occurs exclusively in monozygotic twins.
  • Monochorionic Monoamniotic (MCMA): Fetuses share both the same placenta and the same amniotic sac, carrying the highest risk due to potential cord entanglement.

Shared vascular connections in monochorionic placentas predispose the fetuses to unique complications like twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome.

Diagnosis and Antenatal Assessment

Early and accurate diagnosis of multiple gestation transforms antenatal outcomes by allowing timely surveillance. Clinical signs often raise initial suspicion: uterine size exceeding dates, excessive weight gain, or palpating multiple fetal parts during abdominal examination. However, definitive confirmation relies entirely on early obstetric ultrasonography.

Ultrasound performed in the first trimester establishes chorionicity with the highest accuracy by mapping placental masses and evaluating the membrane-placenta junction (the lambda or T-sign). Subsequent antenatal visits require more frequent monitoring intervals compared to singleton pregnancies. Nurses must schedule serial growth scans to track fetal growth velocity and screen for growth discordance, amniotic fluid volume abnormalities, and structural anomalies.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Multiple pregnancies place immense physiological stress on the mother and introduce distinct pathological risks for the fetuses.

  • Maternal risks: Hyperemesis gravidarum, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, antepartum and postpartum hemorrhage, and operative delivery.
  • Fetal and neonatal risks: Prematurity, intrauterine growth restriction, congenital anomalies, low birth weight, and twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome.

Twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome arises in monochorionic pregnancies due to unbalanced vascular anastomoses in the shared placenta, leading to one twin becoming oliguric and growth-restricted while the co-twin becomes polyuric and hydropic.

Antenatal Management and Nutritional Care

Clinical management requires a multidisciplinary team approach focusing on heightened surveillance and aggressive nutritional support. Pregnant women carrying multiples need increased caloric intake, higher protein distribution, and intensified supplementation of iron and folic acid to prevent severe maternal anemia, which is highly prevalent in resource-constrained settings.

Antenatal visits are scheduled every two weeks in the second trimester and weekly thereafter for monochorionic pregnancies. Nurses play a pivotal role in educating the mother on signs of preterm labor, such as pelvic pressure, uterine tightening, and vaginal discharge changes. Prophylactic measures, including cervical length screening and progesterone therapy, may be implemented to reduce the risk of spontaneous premature delivery.

Labor, Delivery, and Intrapartum Nursing Care

Intrapartum care for multiple pregnancies demands intense vigilance, preparation, and the presence of skilled neonatal resuscitation teams. The mode of delivery depends on fetal presentation, chorionicity, and gestational age. While dichorionic diamniotic twins with a vertex-vertex presentation can often achieve a safe vaginal delivery, non-vertex presentations or monochorionic monoamniotic twins usually mandate a planned cesarean section.

During labor, continuous electronic fetal monitoring of all fetuses is mandatory to detect acute hypoxia promptly. The delivery suite must be fully equipped with separate resuscitation stations for each baby. After the birth of the first twin, the nurse immediately assists the obstetrician in monitoring the second twin's lie, heart rate, and contractions while preparing for the rapid delivery of the second baby.

Postpartum Care and Prevention of Complications

The immediate postpartum period carries a sharp rise in maternal morbidity, primarily due to uterine atony and subsequent postpartum hemorrhage. Overdistension of the myometrium from multiple gestations impairs effective muscle contraction following delivery.

Nurses must maintain rigorous postpartum surveillance, including frequent checks of uterine tone, vaginal blood loss, blood pressure, and pulse rates. Active management of the third stage of labor using uterotonic agents is standard practice. Beyond physical recovery, the nurse provides emotional support and practical guidance to the mother and family regarding tandem breastfeeding, neonatal care routines, and recognizing signs of postpartum depression.
