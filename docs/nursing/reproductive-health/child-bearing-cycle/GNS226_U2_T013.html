Heartburn in Pregnancy

Introduction and Physiological Causes of Heartburn in Pregnancy

Heartburn, or pyrosis, is an extremely common complaint during pregnancy, affecting up to eighty percent of expectant mothers, particularly in the third trimester. The primary driver behind this discomfort is the hormone progesterone, which relaxes smooth muscles throughout the body. This systemic relaxation includes the lower esophageal sphincter (LES), the muscular valve meant to keep stomach contents from rising. When the LES remains partially open, gastric acid easily flows backward into the esophagus, causing a burning sensation behind the sternum.

As the pregnancy progresses, the growing uterus creates mechanical intra-abdominal pressure. This physical crowding pushes the stomach upward, altering normal anatomical alignments and exacerbating the reflux of acid. Understanding that this is a normal physiological response helps nurses reassure anxious mothers, though proper management remains necessary to prevent severe distress.

Clinical Presentation and Assessment

Pregnant women typically describe a burning retrosternal sensation that often radiates toward the throat, usually worsening after meals or when lying flat. Other associated symptoms include bitter fluid regurgitation, excessive salivation, and occasional bloating. Nurses must conduct a thorough subjective assessment by asking about the frequency, timing, and severity of these episodes.

During assessment, differentiate normal pregnancy heartburn from more serious conditions. Red flag symptoms that require medical provider referral include:

  • Dysphagia: Difficulty or pain when swallowing food.
  • Hematemesis: Vomiting blood or material that resembles coffee grounds.
  • Melena: Passing dark, tarry stools indicating gastrointestinal bleeding.
  • Persistent vomiting: Leading to dehydration or inadequate weight gain.

Non-Pharmacological Management and Lifestyle Changes

First-line management for pregnancy-induced heartburn relies heavily on lifestyle modifications and dietary adjustments. Nurses should guide patients to implement these safe, effective strategies before considering medications.

  • Dietary habits: Eat smaller, frequent meals instead of three large portions to prevent stomach overdistension.
  • Postural care: Remain upright for at least one to two hours after eating and elevate the head of the bed by six inches.
  • Trigger avoidance: Limit intake of fatty, spicy, acidic foods, caffeine, and chocolate, which relax the lower esophageal sphincter or irritate the esophageal mucosa.
  • Clothing choices: Wear loose-fitting garments around the waist to avoid adding external pressure to the abdomen.

Safe Pharmacological Interventions

When lifestyle modifications fail to provide relief, healthcare providers may recommend safe medications. Antacids containing calcium carbonate are usually the first choice because they neutralize existing stomach acid quickly. However, nurses must advise patients to avoid antacids high in sodium or magnesium trisilicate, as excessive use can lead to electrolyte imbalances or adverse fetal effects.

If antacids are ineffective, H2-receptor antagonists such as famotidine may be prescribed. Proton pump inhibitors are generally reserved for severe, refractory cases of gastroesophageal reflux disease. Self-medication with over-the-counter remedies without consulting a midwife or obstetrician must be strongly discouraged.

Nursing Guidance and Complication Prevention

Unmanaged, chronic acid reflux can lead to complications such as esophagitis, mucosal erosion, and chronic sleep disturbances that affect maternal well-being. Nurses play a crucial role in empowering patients through targeted education and reassurance.
