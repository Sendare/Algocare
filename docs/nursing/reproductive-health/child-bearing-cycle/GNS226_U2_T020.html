Anaemia in Pregnancy

Introduction and Definition of Anaemia in Pregnancy

Anaemia during pregnancy remains a major public health challenge across Nigeria, contributing significantly to maternal morbidity and perinatal mortality. World Health Organization guidelines define anaemia in pregnant women as a haemoglobin concentration below 11.0 g/dL. However, the Nigerian Federal Ministry of Health often highlights a regional threshold of less than 10.0 g/dL for severe local context, though clinical practice standardly adopts the 11.0 g/dL cutoff for early intervention. This condition impairs oxygen delivery to maternal tissues and the developing fetus, sparking a cascade of obstetric complications if left unmanaged.

Physiological Haemodilution and Normal Haematological Changes

During gestation, maternal blood volume expands dramatically by roughly 40 to 50 percent to meet the metabolic demands of the uteroplacental unit and prepare for anticipated blood loss during delivery. Plasma volume increases much more than red blood cell mass, creating a relative dilution known as physiological haemodilution or pseudoanaemia. This mismatch causes a normal drop in haemoglobin and haematocrit levels, especially during the second trimester. Midwives and nurses must recognize this process to avoid misinterpreting normal pregnancy adaptation as true pathological deficiency.

Classification and Etiology of Anaemia in Pregnancy

  • Iron deficiency anaemia: The most prevalent cause globally and locally, driven by depleted iron stores to support fetal growth.
  • Folate deficiency anaemia: Results from inadequate intake of green leafy vegetables or increased cellular division demands, leading to megaloblastic changes.
  • Malaria-induced anaemia: Haemolysis of red blood cells caused by Plasmodium falciparum infection, highly endemic in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Haemoglobinopathies: Inherited conditions like sickle cell disease (HbSS or HbSC) that cause chronic haemolytic states during pregnancy.

Clinical Manifestations and Assessment in Pregnant Women

Mild anaemia can remain entirely silent, but as haemoglobin levels fall, women present with classic signs of tissue hypoxia.

  • General symptoms: Persistent fatigue, generalized weakness, dizziness, and shortness of breath upon mild exertion.
  • Physical signs: Pallor of the conjunctiva, nail beds, and palmar creases.
  • Advanced signs: Tachycardia, palpitations, ankle edema, and angular cheilitis in severe cases.

Diagnostic Investigations and Laboratory Findings

Accurate diagnosis requires laboratory confirmation beyond clinical appearance. A baseline complete blood count measures haemoglobin and haematocrit levels. Further evaluation often includes red cell indices such as mean corpuscular volume to classify the type of anaemia. Serum ferritin testing offers the most accurate assessment of stored iron levels, though acute-phase inflammation can falsely elevate it. In malaria-endemic regions, routine screening via blood film microscopy or rapid diagnostic tests is standard practice during antenatal booking.

Maternal and Fetal Complications of Anaemia

Uncorrected anaemia carries grave risks for both mother and baby. For the mother, reduced oxygen reserves lower tolerance to postpartum hemorrhage, increasing the likelihood of shock and maternal death. It also raises vulnerability to puerperal sepsis due to compromised immune function. Fetal impacts include intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, preterm birth, and diminished Apgar scores at delivery.

Medical and Pharmacological Management

Management strategies focus on correcting deficiencies and treating underlying triggers. Routine prophylaxis involves daily oral supplementation with ferrous salts and folic acid for all pregnant women attending antenatal clinics. Therapeutic doses are prescribed when confirmed anaemia is diagnosed. Intravenous iron preparations may be utilized if oral iron is poorly tolerated or if severe anaemia presents late in pregnancy. Intermittent preventive treatment for malaria and insecticide-treated bed nets remain core preventative measures in endemic zones.

Nursing Care Plan and Health Education in Antenatal Care

Nurses play a frontline role in mitigating anaemia through targeted health education and continuous monitoring. Care plans include assessing dietary habits to encourage the intake of local iron-rich foods like dark green vegetables, beans, and lean meats, while discouraging substances like tea or coffee that inhibit iron absorption. Nurses counsel clients on taking iron supplements with vitamin C sources, such as orange juice, to enhance uptake. Furthermore, educating women on recognizing warning signs and complying with scheduled malaria prevention strategies ensures holistic protection.
