Circulatory Changes at Birth

Foetal Circulation Overview

Before birth, the foetus relies entirely on the placenta for oxygen and nutrients because the lungs are filled with fluid and non-functional. Oxygenated blood returns from the placenta via the umbilical vein, bypassing the immature liver through the ductus venosus to reach the inferior vena cava. As blood enters the right atrium, a large portion is shunted directly into the left atrium through the foramen ovale, ensuring that the brain and coronary vessels receive well-oxygenated blood. Blood that does enter the right ventricle and pulmonary artery is largely diverted away from the lungs by the ductus arteriosus, flowing straight into the descending aorta. Understanding this parallel circuit is vital for recognizing why neonatal transition requires such dramatic pressure shifts.

Triggers for Circulatory Transition

The sudden shift from placental to independent cardiopulmonary function is triggered by two main events: clamping of the umbilical cord and the baby's first breath. When the cord is tied, the low-resistance placental circuit is removed, causing a sharp rise in systemic vascular resistance and systemic blood pressure. Simultaneously, inflation of the lungs with air drastically reduces pulmonary vascular resistance, opening up the pulmonary capillary bed. Oxygen acts as a powerful local vasodilator in the pulmonary vessels, promoting rapid blood flow into the lungs. These simultaneous pressure changes flip the heart from a parallel circuit into a series circuit.

Closure of the Foramen Ovale

The foramen ovale is a flap-like valve between the right and left atria that allowed blood to bypass the lungs in utero. At birth, the sudden rush of blood returning from the newly aerated lungs floods the left atrium, raising left atrial pressure above right atrial pressure. This pressure differential forces the septum primum tightly against the septum secundum, shutting the flap mechanically. Crying or straining can temporarily reverse this pressure gradient in the first few hours of life, sometimes causing a brief, harmless murmur or mild cyanosis. Permanent anatomical fusion of the flap usually occurs over the first few months of life.

Obliteration of the Ductus Arteriosus

  1. Functional Closure: Occurs within 10 to 15 hours after birth due to smooth muscle contraction in response to high arterial oxygen tension and the withdrawal of maternal prostaglandins.
  2. Anatomical Obliteration: Complete fibrous sealing and transformation into the ligamentum arteriosum takes place over several weeks.
  3. Patency Issues: In preterm infants, immature oxygen-sensing mechanisms and high local prostaglandin levels often keep the vessel open, resulting in patent ductus arteriosus (PDA).

Closure of Ductus Venosus and Umbilical Vessels

With the cessation of placental blood flow, the umbilical arteries and vein undergo immediate functional contraction to prevent exsanguination. The distal portions of the umbilical arteries become the medial umbilical ligaments, while the proximal parts remain open as the superior vesical arteries supplying the bladder. The umbilical vein shrivels into the ligamentum teres (round ligament of the liver). Similarly, the ductus venosus collapses within days of clamping, eventually fibrosing into the ligamentum venosum. These remnants serve as permanent anatomical landmarks of the prenatal circulation.

Establishment of Adult-Type Circulation

Once all foetal shunts close, the cardiovascular system operates as a true dual circuit in series. Deoxygenated blood returns from the body via the venae cavae into the right atrium, passes to the right ventricle, and is pumped through the pulmonary arteries exclusively to the lungs for gas exchange. Oxygenated blood returns via the pulmonary veins to the left atrium, moves to the left ventricle, and is pumped out through the aorta to perfuse all systemic tissues. This separation ensures maximum efficiency in oxygen delivery, matching the high metabolic demands of extrauterine life.

Neonatal Assessment and Delayed Transition Recognition

Midwives and nurses must monitor transitioning neonates closely for signs of abnormal adaptation, known as persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN) or failure of shunt closure. Key assessment parameters include respiratory rate, heart rate, peripheral perfusion, and oxygen saturation measured via pulse oximetry. Central cyanosis, tachypnea, grunting, or persistent murmurs indicate that pulmonary vascular resistance has failed to drop or that shunts remain open abnormally. Timely identification allows for interventions such as oxygen therapy, thermal care, and medical or surgical management.
