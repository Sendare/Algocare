Physiological Changes in Pregnancy

Introduction to Maternal Physiology

Pregnancy triggers profound systemic changes across every organ system to support fetal growth and prepare the mother for labor and lactation. These alterations are primarily driven by hormonal signals from the corpus luteum and later the placenta, alongside mechanical pressures from the enlarging uterus. Recognizing these physiological shifts is essential because many normal variants overlap with clinical signs of pathology. Nigerian midwives and nurses must distinguish between benign adaptation and true complications during antenatal care visits.

Cardiovascular and Hemodynamic Adaptations

The maternal cardiovascular system undergoes dramatic expansion. Plasma volume increases by about 40 to 50 percent, while red blood cell mass increases by only 20 to 30 percent. This mismatch leads to physiological anemia of pregnancy, characterized by a lowered hemoglobin concentration despite adequate iron stores. Cardiac output rises significantly early in the first trimester, driven by increases in both stroke volume and heart rate.

  • Blood Pressure: Systemic vascular resistance drops due to smooth muscle relaxation caused by progesterone and prostaglandins, causing blood pressure to fall in the second trimester before returning to baseline near term.
  • Aortocaval Compression: In the supine position, the heavy uterus compresses the aorta and inferior vena cava, reducing venous return and causing supine hypotensive syndrome.

Respiratory System Alterations

As the uterus enlarges, it pushes the diaphragm upward by about four centimeters, altering chest cavity geometry. While the respiratory rate remains largely unchanged, tidal volume and minute ventilation increase substantially under the influence of progesterone, which heightens the respiratory center's sensitivity to carbon dioxide.

This state of chronic hyperventilation leads to a mild respiratory alkalosis, which facilitates carbon dioxide transfer away from the fetus. Total lung capacity decreases slightly due to the elevated diaphragm, but inspiratory capacity expands.

Renal and Urinary Tract Modifications

Renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate (GFR) increase by up to 50 percent early in pregnancy, remaining elevated until term. This increase leads to higher clearance rates, resulting in lower serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen levels. Consequently, trace amounts of glucose and protein in the urine can sometimes be a normal finding, though persistent proteinuria warrants investigation for pre-eclampsia.

High progesterone levels cause smooth muscle relaxation throughout the urinary tract, leading to ureteral dilation and urinary stasis. This anatomical change increases the vulnerability of pregnant women to upper urinary tract infections, particularly asymptomatic bacteriuria.

Gastrointestinal and Metabolic Adjustments

Elevated progesterone relaxes smooth muscle throughout the gastrointestinal tract, slowing gastric emptying and decreasing intestinal motility. This sluggish movement promotes increased nutrient absorption but frequently causes constipation, heartburn, and bloating. Additionally, increased human chorionic gonadotropin and estrogen contribute to nausea and vomiting during the first trimester.

Metabolically, pregnancy is an anabolic state in early gestation followed by a catabolic state in late pregnancy, prioritizing glucose delivery to the developing fetus.

Endocrine and Hormonal Shifts

The placenta acts as a temporary endocrine powerhouse, secreting large quantities of estrogen, progesterone, human placental lactogen, and relaxin. Human placental lactogen induces maternal insulin resistance, ensuring a steady supply of glucose for the fetus. The thyroid gland also enlarges slightly due to increased metabolic demands and human chorionic gonadotropin stimulation, maintaining normal thyroid function despite elevated thyroxine-binding globulin levels.

Musculoskeletal and Dermatological Changes

Joint laxity increases markedly due to the hormone relaxin, which softens connective tissue and pelvic ligaments to prepare the pelvis for birth. This ligamentous relaxation shifts the maternal center of gravity forward, resulting in lordosis and the characteristic waddling gait of late pregnancy.

Dermatological alterations include hyperpigmentation, such as the linea nigra on the abdomen and chloasma on the face, alongside striae gravidarum caused by stretching of the dermal elastic fibers.

Nursing Assessment and Patient Education

Antenatal nursing care relies on educating women about expected physiological changes so they can differentiate normal discomforts from danger signs. Nurses must teach warning signs like severe headache, visual disturbances, or sudden swelling, which may indicate hypertensive disorders. Counseling should also cover nutritional needs, hydration, and safe management of minor ailments like nausea and constipation without unsupervised drug use.
