Vomiting in Pregnancy

Introduction and Physiological Basis

Nausea and vomiting affect up to eighty percent of pregnant women, usually beginning in the first trimester. This symptom complex stems from surging hormonal levels, particularly human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and estrogen. High levels of progesterone also relax the smooth muscle of the gastrointestinal tract, slowing gastric emptying and contributing to persistent nausea. While often termed morning sickness, symptoms can strike at any time of day or night. Evolutionary theories even suggest it may serve a protective role by deterring ingestion of potentially harmful foods during early embryonic development.

Clinical Presentation and Hyperemesis Gravidarum

Standard pregnancy sickness involves mild to moderate nausea with occasional vomiting that does not severely disrupt daily activities or nutritional status. Conversely, hyperemesis gravidarum represents the extreme end of the spectrum. It involves intractable vomiting, inability to retain any food or fluids, significant weight loss exceeding five percent of pre-pregnancy weight, and severe electrolyte imbalances. Recognizing the boundary between normal physiological adaptation and pathological dehydration is critical for prompt nursing intervention.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Workup

Comprehensive nursing assessment requires evaluating the frequency, trigger factors, and exact volume of vomitus. Nurses must check vital signs for postural hypotension and tachycardia, which signal dehydration.

  • Urinalysis: Screens for urinary ketones indicating starvation ketosis.
  • Electrolyte Panel: Checks for hypokalemia and hyponatremia resulting from profound fluid loss.
  • Obstetric Ultrasound: Confirms intrauterine pregnancy and rules out multi-fetal gestation or gestational trophoblastic disease, which raise hCG levels.

Dietary and Lifestyle Management

Initial management centers on conservative lifestyle modifications. Patients should eat small, frequent meals rather than three large ones to keep the stomach lightly occupied. Dry carbohydrates like crackers consumed before rising can settle morning acidity. Ginger preparations have proven clinical efficacy as natural antiemetics. Furthermore, separating solid food intake from liquids prevents gastric distension and subsequent vomiting episodes.

Pharmacological Interventions

When conservative measures fail, safe antiemetic medications become necessary. First-line therapy typically combines pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) with doxylamine. For persistent hyperemesis, clinicians prescribe stronger agents such as metoclopramide, ondansetron, or promethazine. Nurses must monitor patients receiving these drugs for potential side effects like drowsiness, extrapyramidal symptoms, or prolonged QT intervals depending on the specific agent administered.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Uncontrolled vomiting carries significant health risks if left untreated. Severe dehydration leads to acute kidney injury and electrolyte imbalances. Prolonged nutrient deficiency can cause Wernicke's encephalopathy due to severe thiamine depletion. From a fetal perspective, chronic maternal starvation and severe weight loss may result in intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, and preterm birth, highlighting why aggressive medical management is paramount.

Patient Education and Follow-Up

Discharge teaching must empower the patient to recognize warning signs that require immediate re-admission. Nurses should instruct women to track daily fluid intake and report signs of worsening dizziness, dark urine, or inability to keep medications down for twelve hours. Reassurance is equally vital, as many women experience psychological distress and guilt regarding their inability to eat normally during pregnancy.
