Gender Based Violence in Humanitarian Settings

Introduction to Gender-Based Violence in Humanitarian Settings

Gender-based violence (GBV) refers to harmful acts directed against an individual based on their biological sex or socially constructed gender roles. In humanitarian settings—such as natural disasters, armed conflicts, and displacement crises—the risk of GBV increases exponentially. Rule of law breaks down, traditional support structures collapse, and populations are forced into overcrowded camps or makeshift shelters where privacy and security are severely compromised. Women and girls bear the heaviest burden, though men, boys, and sexual minorities also face targeted violence.

Humanitarian workers must recognize that GBV is entirely preventable yet pervasive during emergencies. Power dynamics shift when families lose livelihoods and assets, rendering vulnerable members more susceptible to exploitation. Addressing GBV requires viewing it not merely as a byproduct of chaos, but as a life-threatening medical and human rights emergency that demands immediate, coordinated action from all healthcare personnel.

Vulnerability Factors and Risk Drivers in Crises

  • Displacement and Overcrowding: Living in congested camps with inadequate lighting and poorly segregated sanitation facilities creates fertile ground for assaults.
  • Economic Dependency: Loss of livelihoods forces survivors into survival sex, transactional relationships, or exploitative labor.
  • Breakdown of Law Enforcement: Impunity reigns when police forces and judiciary systems flee or become corrupt during conflicts.
  • Normalisation of Violence: Prolonged exposure to community violence desensitizes populations, making abusive behaviors culturally accepted or ignored.

Types and Manifestations of GBV in Emergencies

  1. Sexual Violence: Includes rape, attempted rape, forced impregnation, and sexual assault perpetrated without consent, frequently used as a weapon of war.
  2. Physical Violence: Encompasses beating, slapping, mutilation, and acid attacks resulting in bodily harm.
  3. Emotional and Psychological Abuse: Involves severe verbal threats, harassment, humiliation, and forced isolation that destroy self-worth.
  4. Harmful Traditional Practices: Child marriages and female genital mutilation often surge as families seek economic security or adherence to strict customs during distress.
  5. Denial of Resources or Services: Withholding food, medical care, or shelter to coerce individuals into compliance.

Health and Psychosocial Consequences for Survivors

The aftermath of GBV extends far beyond immediate physical injuries. Survivors frequently present with lacerations, fractures, internal trauma, and unwanted pregnancies. Sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, pose a severe threat when barrier protection is absent during an assault.

Psychological impacts run deep, manifesting as severe post-traumatic stress disorder, chronic anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation. Stigma and social rejection within the community often compound this trauma, leading to profound isolation. Nurses must view these physical and mental health issues as interconnected crises requiring empathetic, holistic intervention.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of Survivors

Nurses are frequently the first healthcare professionals to encounter GBV survivors. Identifying victims requires a high index of suspicion, especially when patients present with vague somatic complaints like chronic pelvic pain, headaches, or insomnia. A common clinical mistake is focusing solely on visible physical wounds while ignoring emotional distress.

Assessment must occur in a private, secure environment away from family members or onlookers who might compromise disclosure. Nurses should use calm, non-judgmental language, allowing the patient to speak at their own pace. Never pressure a survivor to share details they are not ready to reveal; creating a safe space encourages voluntary disclosure.

Clinical Management and Immediate Care Protocols

Immediate medical care must follow established clinical protocols within 72 hours of an assault for maximum effectiveness.

  • Emergency Contraception: Offered to prevent unwanted pregnancy if presented within five days.
  • PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis): Administered within 72 hours to reduce the risk of HIV transmission.
  • STI Prophylaxis: Antibiotics given to prevent infections like chlamydia, gonorrhea, and syphilis.
  • Wound Care: Cleaning, suturing, and tetanus shots for physical trauma.
  • Forensic Documentation: Carefully recording injuries with patient consent using standardized body maps, preserving clothing or evidence if legal action is desired.

Ethical Considerations, Confidentiality, and Safety

Confidentiality is the cornerstone of GBV care. Nurses must never share a survivor's identity or details of their ordeal with anyone—including family, community leaders, or colleagues—without explicit, informed consent. Breaching confidentiality can endanger the survivor's life and destroy trust in healthcare systems.

Safety planning forms an integral part of ethical nursing practice. Before a survivor leaves the facility, the nurse must assess whether it is safe for them to return home or to their camp shelter. If danger persists, connecting the patient with safe temporary housing or protection services is essential to prevent repeat victimization.

Referral Pathways and Inter-Agency Collaboration

No single nurse or healthcare facility can manage all the complex needs of a GBV survivor alone. Effective care relies on a multi-sectoral referral pathway linking medical services, psychosocial counseling, legal assistance, and safe shelter.

Nurses must be familiar with local referral networks operating within their humanitarian zone. When transferring a patient, ensure direct communication with the receiving agency while maintaining strict data protection protocols. Coordinated teamwork guarantees that survivors receive comprehensive support without having to retell their traumatic stories repeatedly to multiple strangers.

Prevention Strategies and Community-Based Support in Humanitarian Action

Effective humanitarian action goes beyond reactive clinical treatment by actively engaging communities in prevention. Nurses can participate in awareness campaigns that educate displaced populations on human rights, gender equality, and available support services.

Community-based safety committees, involving trusted local leaders and women's groups, help identify high-risk areas like dark pathways or isolated water points and advocate for practical improvements such as solar lighting and community patrols. Empowering communities to protect their vulnerable members builds resilience and reduces the overall incidence of gender-based violence during protracted crises.
