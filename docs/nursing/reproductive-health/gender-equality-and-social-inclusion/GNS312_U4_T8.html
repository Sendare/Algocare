Gender Based Violence

Understanding Gender-Based Violence

Gender-based violence refers to harmful acts directed at an individual based on their gender. It is rooted in systemic gender inequality, abuse of power, and harmful societal norms. While it affects people of all genders, women and girls bear a disproportionate burden globally and locally in Nigeria. Recognizing this violence requires looking beyond physical marks to understand how power imbalances dictate control, coercion, and fear within relationships and communities.

Types and Manifestations

  • Physical violence: Inflicting bodily harm through hitting, slapping, burning, or acid attacks.
  • Sexual violence: Forced sexual intercourse, attempted rape, and unwanted sexual touching.
  • Psychological abuse: Constant insults, isolation, threats, and emotional manipulation.
  • Economic deprivation: Controlling financial resources, withholding money, or denying access to employment.
  • Harmful traditional practices: Child marriage, female genital mutilation, and widowhood rites.

Socio-Cultural Risk Factors in Nigeria

Cultural beliefs, patriarchal structures, and religious misinterpretations heavily drive gender-based violence across many Nigerian communities. Traditional expectations often demand that women remain submissive, making it difficult to report abuse. Economic dependence on male partners traps many survivors in violent homes. Silence is frequently enforced by extended families and community elders who prioritize family reputation over the safety of the individual.

Health Consequences and Clinical Presentation

Survivors present with a mix of acute injuries and chronic health problems. Physical signs include bruises, lacerations, fractures, and sexually transmitted infections. Mental health struggles are profound, featuring depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder, and suicidal ideation. Somatic complaints like chronic pelvic pain, headaches, and gastrointestinal distress often bring survivors to the clinic when they are not yet ready to disclose the violence they experienced.

Nursing Assessment and Identification

Nurses must maintain a high index of suspicion, especially when patients present with vague symptoms or injuries that do not match the given history. Assessment requires a private, safe environment away from accompanying family members who might be the perpetrators. Asking direct, non-judgmental screening questions helps build trust. Documenting findings objectively using exact patient quotes and body maps is critical for future legal proceedings.

Immediate Management and Emergency Care

Emergency care prioritizes physical stabilization and safety. Treating life-threatening injuries takes precedence over gathering evidence. For sexual assault survivors presenting within 72 hours, emergency contraception and post-exposure prophylaxis for HIV must be initiated promptly. Forensic evidence collection requires specialized kits and strict chain of custody protocols. Nurses must offer compassionate reassurance while obtaining informed consent before any procedure.

Psychosocial Support and Counseling

Healing extends beyond physical recovery. Psychological first aid helps stabilize survivors by offering practical care and assessing immediate needs without forcing disclosure. Professional trauma counseling assists survivors in processing grief, rebuilding self-esteem, and developing coping mechanisms. Peer support groups provide a safe space where survivors realize they are not alone, reducing isolation and fostering emotional resilience.

Legal Frameworks and Reporting

Nigeria operates under various legal instruments designed to protect individuals from violence, notably the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act. Nurses must understand their legal obligations regarding mandatory reporting, especially concerning minors or incapacitated adults. Balancing patient confidentiality with legal requirements requires careful navigation. Collaborating with social workers, law enforcement, and legal aid organizations ensures survivors receive comprehensive protection.

Prevention and Community Advocacy

Ending gender-based violence requires community-level intervention. Nurses play a vital role as advocates by educating communities about human rights, challenging harmful gender stereotypes, and promoting gender equality. Engaging local leaders, youth groups, and men in advocacy creates a culture of zero tolerance for violence. Preventive education in schools helps young people build healthy relationship habits and respect for boundaries from an early age.
