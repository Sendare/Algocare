Oral Contraceptives

Mechanism of Action and Types of Oral Contraceptives

Oral contraceptives are hormonal medications used primarily to prevent pregnancy. They generally fall into two main categories: Combined Oral Contraceptives (COCs) and Progestin-Only Pills (POPs).

  • Combined Oral Contraceptives: Contain synthetic estrogen (usually ethinyl estradiol) and a progestin. Their primary mechanism is suppressing ovulation by inhibiting the release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus, which blocks the surge of luteinizing hormone. Secondary mechanisms include thickening cervical mucus to impede sperm penetration and altering the endometrium to prevent implantation.
  • Progestin-Only Pills: Contain only a progestin hormone and are often prescribed when estrogen is contraindicated, such as during breastfeeding. They primarily thicken cervical mucus and thin the endometrium, while ovulation suppression is less consistent compared to COCs.

Non-Contraceptive Health Benefits

Beyond pregnancy prevention, oral contraceptives offer several therapeutic and prophylactic advantages for women. Medical providers frequently prescribe them to manage various gynecological disorders.

  • Menstrual Regulation: Reduces dysmenorrhea by decreasing prostaglandin production and creates predictable, lighter withdrawal bleeding.
  • Hyperandrogenism Management: Lowers free testosterone levels, helping control moderate acne and hirsutism.
  • Reduction of Benign Conditions: Lowers the incidence of functional ovarian cysts, iron-deficiency anemia from heavy blood loss, and benign breast disease.
  • Long-term Protection: Confers significant protection against endometrial and ovarian cancers, with risk reduction persisting years after discontinuation.

Contraindications and Clinical Risk Factors

Safety screening is paramount because hormonal contraceptives carry serious systemic risks for certain patient populations. The World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria guide these safety evaluations.

Absolute contraindications for combined oral contraceptives include a history of thromboembolic events, cerebrovascular disease, ischemic heart disease, known or suspected breast cancer, severe liver disease, and complicated valvular heart disease. Furthermore, women over age 35 who smoke heavily face sharply elevated cardiovascular risks from estrogen therapy. Progestin-only pills have far fewer absolute contraindications, making them safer for patients with cardiovascular risk factors or those with a history of migraines with aura.

Client Assessment and Screening Protocols

Before dispensing oral contraceptives, the nurse must conduct a thorough health assessment. This process identifies underlying health problems and ensures the chosen formulation is safe and effective.

The initial evaluation includes taking a detailed medical, surgical, and family history, alongside an accurate blood pressure measurement and weight check. A routine pelvic exam or breast exam is not strictly required before initiating hormonal contraception, provided baseline screening questions rule out active pathologies. Nurses must screen specifically for recent migraines with aura, undiagnosed abnormal uterine bleeding, and potential drug interactions with liver enzyme-inducing medications like certain anticonvulsants or rifampicin.

Patient Counselling and Initiation Guidance

Effective counselling ensures high continuation rates and reduces the risk of accidental discontinuation due to manageable side effects. Nurses explain how to take the pills correctly and what to expect during the first few months of use.

Clients are instructed to take one pill daily at approximately the same time. For combined pills, various packaging options exist, such as 21 active pills followed by 7 inactive pills, or continuous dosing regimens. The nurse should explain that unscheduled spotting or light bleeding is common during the first three months as the endometrium adjusts to exogenous hormones. Clear instructions regarding backup contraception during the first week of a new pack are crucial if initiation does not coincide with the first days of menses.

Managing Missed Doses and Side Effects

Mistakes happen with daily medication regimens, and clear protocols must be followed to maintain contraceptive efficacy. Managing side effects proactively prevents unnecessary dropouts from family planning programs.

  • Missed Combined Pills: If one active pill is missed, take it as soon as remembered and continue the rest as normal. If two or more active pills are missed, take the most recent pill immediately, discard earlier missed pills, and use backup contraception for seven days. Emergency contraception may be considered if unprotected intercourse occurred in the preceding five days.
  • Missed Progestin-Only Pills: If a pill is taken more than three hours late, take it immediately and use backup contraception for forty-eight hours.
  • Side Effect Management: Nausea can be minimized by taking the pill with food or at bedtime. Persistent headaches, severe leg pain, or chest pain require immediate evaluation to rule out severe vascular complications.
