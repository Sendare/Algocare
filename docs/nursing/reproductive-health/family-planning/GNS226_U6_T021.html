Emergency Contraceptive Methods

Introduction and Definition of Emergency Contraception

Emergency contraception (EC) refers to methods used to prevent pregnancy after unprotected sexual intercourse, contraceptive failure, or sexual assault. It acts as a secondary form of birth control and does not protect against sexually transmitted infections. EC is intended for emergency situations only and should not replace regular, ongoing contraceptive methods.

Indications and Timing for Emergency Contraceptive Use

Timing is the single most critical factor determining the success of emergency contraceptive pills.

  • Unprotected Intercourse: Any act of sexual intercourse where no barrier method or hormonal contraceptive was used.
  • Contraceptive Mishap: Condom breakage, slippage, or incorrect withdrawal.
  • Missed Pills: Missing multiple consecutive combined oral contraceptive pills.
  • Sexual Assault: Providing urgent prophylaxis when regular contraception status is uncertain.

Types of Emergency Contraceptive Pills and Mechanisms

Emergency contraceptive pills (ECPs) function primarily by delaying or inhibiting ovulation. They do not disrupt an established pregnancy.

The Copper Intrauterine Device (Cu-IUD) as Emergency Contraception

The copper-bearing intrauterine device is the most effective form of emergency contraception available. When inserted within 120 hours of unprotected intercourse, it prevents fertilization by producing a spermicidal environment through copper ion release. An added clinical advantage is that it can remain in place to provide ongoing, highly effective contraception for years.

Clinical Assessment, History, and Counseling

Before dispensing emergency contraception, the nurse must conduct a brief, focused clinical assessment. Inquire about the exact time of the unprotected intercourse and the date of the last menstrual period to rule out an existing pregnancy. Assess for contraindications, explain potential side effects such as nausea or irregular spotting, and counsel the client on starting a regular ongoing contraceptive method.

Side Effects, Contraindications, and Follow-up Care

Emergency contraceptives are generally safe, but clients may experience temporary side effects like nausea, fatigue, headache, and lower abdominal pain. Menstrual irregularities, such as early or delayed bleeding, are common in the cycle following ingestion. There are virtually no medical conditions for which the risks of emergency pills outweigh the benefits, except for a known, confirmed pregnancy.

Addressing Myths, Barriers, and the Nurse's Role in Nigeria

In Nigerian clinical practice, nurses frequently encounter cultural and religious barriers regarding emergency contraception. Many clients harbor misconceptions, believing EC causes permanent infertility, encourages promiscuity, or acts as an abortifacient. Nurses must provide non-judgmental, confidential education, dispel these myths with scientific facts, and ensure friendly access to reproductive health services.
