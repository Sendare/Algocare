Injectable Contraceptives

Introduction and Overview of Injectable Contraceptives

Injectable contraceptives are hormonal medications administered via intramuscular or subcutaneous injection to prevent pregnancy. They offer discreet, highly effective, and reversible family planning. In Nigeria, where maternal health advocacy relies heavily on community-level interventions, injectables are among the most popular modern contraceptive methods due to their ease of use and privacy.

  • Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA): Given every three months.
  • Norethisterone Enanthate (NET-EN): Given every two months.

Client confidentiality makes this method attractive to young women and individuals who prefer keeping their contraceptive use private from partners or family members.

Mechanisms of Action

Progestin-only injectables work through three primary physiological pathways to prevent conception:

  1. Suppression of ovulation: Hormones act on the hypothalamus and pituitary gland to inhibit the mid-cycle surge of luteinizing hormone, preventing the release of an ovum.
  2. Thickening of cervical mucus: Progestin alters cervical secretions, creating a physical barrier that impedes sperm penetration into the upper reproductive tract.
  3. Endometrial thinning: The lining of the uterus becomes atrophic, making it unfavorable for the implantation of a fertilized egg.

These combined actions yield a typical-use failure rate of less than one percent when injections are taken strictly on schedule.

Eligibility and Client Assessment (WHO MEC)

The World Health Organization Medical Eligibility Criteria (WHO MEC) guides nurses in determining whether a client can safely use injectable contraceptives. The criteria categorize conditions from Category 1 (no restriction) to Category 4 (unacceptable health risk).

  • Category 1: Conditions for which there is no restriction for the use of the contraceptive method.
  • Category 2: Conditions where the advantages of using the method generally outweigh the theoretical or proven risks.
  • Category 3: Conditions where the theoretical or proven risks usually outweigh the advantages of using the method.
  • Category 4: Conditions that represent an unacceptable health risk if the contraceptive method is used.

Injectables are generally safe for breastfeeding mothers, women with controlled hypertension, and those with uncomplicated diabetes. However, active breast cancer, severe liver disease, and unexplained vaginal bleeding require thorough medical evaluation before initiation.

Administration Techniques and Schedules

Proper administration ensures maximum drug efficacy and minimizes local tissue trauma at the injection site. For intramuscular DMPA, shake the vial gently, select the ventrogluteal or deep deltoid muscle, and use a sterile needle.

  • Crucial Nursing Rule: Never massage the injection site after administering intramuscular DMPA. Massaging can accelerate the absorption rate, shortening the duration of contraceptive protection.
  • Grace Period: Most guidelines allow a grace period of up to two to four weeks for late return visits, provided pregnancy is reasonably excluded.

Side Effects and Common Misconceptions

Users frequently experience changes in bleeding patterns, which is the leading reason for discontinuation. Amenorrhea (absence of menses), irregular spotting, or prolonged bleeding are normal physiological responses to continuous progestin exposure.

Other reported effects include mild weight gain, mood fluctuations, and temporary headaches. A widespread misconception in many Nigerian communities is that injectable contraceptives cause permanent infertility or accumulate dirt in the womb. Nurses must actively dispel these myths during counseling, assuring clients that fertility returns after discontinuation, though sometimes with a delay.

Nursing Management and Client Counseling

Effective counseling builds trust and improves continuation rates. Nurses should use the BRAIDED framework (Benefits, Risks, Alternatives, Inquiries, Disadvantages, Explanation, Documentation) to structure counseling sessions.

  • Assess the client's reproductive goals and medical history.
  • Provide clear information on expected side effects, particularly menstrual changes.
  • Give written or visual reminder cards with the exact date for the next scheduled injection.
  • Encourage open dialogue for any concerns that arise between clinic visits.

Return to Fertility and Long-Term Considerations

A notable characteristic of progestin-only injectables is the delayed return to ovulation after discontinuation. While oral contraceptives clear the body within days, depot formulations can linger in fat tissue.

  • Delay duration: Ovulation and fertility can take an average of 4 to 10 months after the last injection wears off, though some women conceive much sooner.
  • Bone Mineral Density: Long-term use of DMPA is associated with a temporary, reversible decrease in bone mineral density. Current guidelines do not recommend routine bone density scans for typical users, but nurses should encourage adequate calcium intake and weight-bearing exercise for adolescents and long-term users.
