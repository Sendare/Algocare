Intrauterine Device (IUD)

Introduction and Overview of Intrauterine Devices

An intrauterine device (IUD) is a small, T-shaped contraceptive frame inserted into the uterine cavity by a trained healthcare provider. It offers long-acting, reversible contraception (LARC), making it one of the most effective birth control methods available. Once placed, an IUD can remain in the uterus for several years depending on the specific model, providing continuous pregnancy prevention without requiring daily compliance from the user. Return to fertility is immediate upon removal, which appeals to women wishing to delay childbearing rather than achieve permanent sterilization.

Mechanisms of Action: Copper vs Hormonal IUDs

Different types of IUDs prevent pregnancy through distinct physiological pathways:

  • Copper IUDs: Release copper ions that create a sterile inflammatory reaction inside the endometrium. This environment is toxic to sperm and ova, impairing sperm motility and preventing fertilization.
  • Hormonal IUDs: Local release of progestin thickens cervical mucus to block sperm entry, thins the uterine lining to prevent implantation, and can suppress ovulation in some users.

Types of IUDs Available in Nigerian Healthcare Settings

In Nigerian clinical practice, public and private health facilities primarily stock two main categories of intrauterine devices.

  • Copper-bearing IUDs: Devices such as Copper T 380A are widely distributed through government hospitals and primary health care clinics due to their long lifespan (up to 12 years) and affordability.
  • Levonorgestrel-releasing IUDs: Brands like Mirena release synthetic progestin locally and are often chosen by clients seeking lighter menstrual flows and reduced dysmenorrhea, though they have a higher acquisition cost.

Client Selection, Contraindications, and Clinical Evaluation

Thorough clinical screening before IUD insertion protects client health and ensures method satisfaction. Nurses must take a detailed reproductive and medical history.

  1. Absolute Contraindications: Current pregnancy, active pelvic infection (PID), unexplained vaginal bleeding, distorted uterine cavity, and cervical or uterine malignancy.
  2. Relative Contraindications: Severe dysmenorrhea, heavy menstrual bleeding (for copper IUDs), history of ectopic pregnancy, and certain immunosuppressive states.
  3. Clinical Evaluation: Bimanual examination to assess uterine size, position, and tenderness, alongside a speculum examination to check for signs of sexually transmitted infections.

Nursing Procedures for IUD Insertion and Removal

IUD insertion and removal require strict adherence to aseptic technique. During insertion, the clinician cleans the cervix with an antiseptic solution, uses a uterine sound to measure uterine depth, and gently introduces the loaded IUD into the cavity, trimming the threads to about 2 to 3 centimeters. For removal, the nurse applies steady, gentle traction on the exposed threads using ring forceps. Sudden or excessive pulling must be avoided to prevent thread breakage or trauma to the cervical os.

Client Education, Follow-Up, and Management of Side Effects

Effective client education ensures high continuation rates and early detection of problems. Clients should expect mild cramping and irregular spotting during the first few weeks after insertion. For pain relief, mild analgesics like paracetamol or ibuprofen are recommended. Nurses must teach clients how to check for IUD threads vaginally, advising them to report immediately if they feel the hard plastic stem of the device. A standard follow-up visit is scheduled after the first menses or between four to six weeks post-insertion.

Recognition and Management of IUD Complications

Although IUDs are safe, nurses must remain vigilant for potential complications.

  • Expulsion: The device slips out of the uterus, usually within the first few months.
  • Perforation: The IUD punctures the uterine wall during insertion, requiring surgical removal if it enters the peritoneal cavity.
  • Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID): An infection usually introduced during insertion or related to underlying sexually transmitted infections, treated promptly with broad-spectrum antibiotics and device removal if infection persists.
  • Ectopic Pregnancy: Though rare due to high efficacy, any pregnancy occurring with an IUD in place carries a high risk of being ectopic.
