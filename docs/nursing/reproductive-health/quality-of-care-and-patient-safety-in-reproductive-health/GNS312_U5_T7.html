Reasons for Occurrence of Harm

Defining Harm and Adverse Events in Clinical Settings

Patient harm in healthcare refers to unintended physical or psychological injury resulting from or contributed to by medical management rather than the underlying disease process. In busy Nigerian clinical environments, recognizing the boundary between disease progression and healthcare-associated injury is vital for accurate auditing and quality improvement.

  • Adverse Event: An unintended injury caused by medical care that results in temporary or permanent disability, prolonged hospital stay, or death.
  • Preventable Harm: An adverse event that could have been avoided if standard care guidelines or safety protocols had been followed.
  • Near Miss: An unsafe situation or clinical error that was intercepted before reaching the patient, causing no actual harm.

Human Factors and Cognitive Errors

Clinical work relies heavily on human cognition, making healthcare professionals vulnerable to mental shortcuts and fatigue-induced mistakes. Long shifts, staff shortages, and heavy patient loads in secondary and tertiary health facilities across Nigeria often exhaust cognitive reserves.

  1. Confirmation Bias: Focusing only on signs that prove an initial diagnosis while ignoring contradictory clinical evidence.
  2. Automation Bias: Over-relying on monitors or automated lab machines, missing subtle manual assessment changes.
  3. Premature Closure: Settling on a diagnosis too quickly and stopping the collection of further clinical data.
  4. Fatigue and Stress: Reduced alertness due to consecutive shifts, leading to calculation errors in medication administration.

Systemic and Organizational Failures

Many clinical errors do not stem from individual incompetence, but from flawed work environments and institutional processes. James Reason's Swiss Cheese Model explains that hazards exist within layers of a system. Harm occurs only when holes in multiple defensive layers align.

  • Resource Scarcity: Lack of basic consumables like gloves, running water, or functional oxygen cylinders forcing unsafe workarounds.
  • Poor Staffing Ratios: High patient-to-nurse ratios that prevent adequate monitoring and timely medication administration.
  • Inadequate Training: Lack of regular, mandatory orientation programs for new or rotating clinical personnel.

Communication Breakdowns and Handoff Errors

Clear communication forms the backbone of safe nursing practice. During shift changes, patient transfers, or emergency referrals between primary health centers and general hospitals, critical information is frequently lost.

Verbal orders, illegible handwritten charts, and informal handoffs without structured tools like ISOBAR (Introduction, Situation, Observation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) invite disaster. When a midwife fails to communicate a rising postpartum blood pressure reading to the incoming shift, timely intervention is delayed, directly contributing to preventable maternal morbidity.

Technological and Equipment-Related Causes

Modern healthcare relies heavily on biomedical devices, from infusion pumps to fetal monitors and suction machines. When these tools malfunction or are used without proper orientation, they become vectors for patient harm.

Common issues include uncalibrated blood pressure cuffs, expired single-use consumables, dead backup batteries during power outages, and complex device interfaces that confuse tired operators. Routine maintenance schedules and pre-use checks are mandatory safeguards against equipment-induced injuries.

Strategies for Prevention and Mitigation

Mitigating clinical harm requires moving away from a punitive culture toward a constructive blamemehe culture that encourages transparency and system redesign.

  • Standardized Checklists: Implementing surgical safety checklists and safe childbirth checklists in all maternity units.
  • Double-Checking High-Risk Medications: Requiring two nurses to independently verify calculations for oxytocin or insulin infusions.
  • Regular Simulation Training: Conducting emergency drills for postpartum hemorrhage and eclampsia management to build muscle memory.
