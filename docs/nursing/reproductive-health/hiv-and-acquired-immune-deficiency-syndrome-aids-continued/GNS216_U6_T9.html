Prevention of HIV Infection

Introduction to HIV Prevention

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) prevention forms the bedrock of public health nursing. The primary goal is to interrupt the transmission cycle of the virus by targeting known routes of spread: unprotected sexual contact, exposure to infected blood, and vertical transmission from mother to child. Nigeria carries a significant portion of the global HIV burden, making localized prevention strategies critical for nursing practice across primary, secondary, and tertiary health facilities. Effective prevention requires a multi-layered approach combining behavioral, biomedical, and structural interventions tailored to specific vulnerable populations.

The ABCD Strategy of HIV Prevention

The classic ABCD framework remains a fundamental cornerstone for behavioral risk reduction counseling in community health nursing:

  • A - Abstinence: Choosing not to engage in sexual intercourse is the absolute most effective way to avoid sexually transmitted HIV.
  • B - Be faithful: Maintaining a mutual, mutually faithful relationship with an uninfected partner eliminates exposure risk.
  • C - Condoms: Consistent and correct use of male and female barrier methods significantly reduces transmission risks during intercourse.
  • D - Delay/Drugs: Delaying sexual debut among adolescents and avoiding recreational drug or alcohol abuse that impairs sound judgment.

Biomedical Interventions: PrEP and ART as Prevention

Biomedical approaches have revolutionized HIV prevention by utilizing antiretroviral drugs to protect uninfected individuals and stop transmission from those living with the virus.

  • Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP): Daily oral antiretroviral medication taken by HIV-negative individuals at high risk to prevent acquiring the virus.
  • Treatment as Prevention (TasP): Maintaining a patient on Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) until their viral load becomes undetectable.

Undetectable equals Untransmittable (U=U) is a proven scientific principle confirming that individuals with a sustained suppressed viral load cannot sexually transmit HIV to partners.

Universal Precautions and Occupational Safety

Healthcare workers face constant occupational exposure risks to bloodborne pathogens. Universal precautions dictate treating all patient blood and body fluids as potentially infectious, regardless of known diagnosis. Key nursing actions include wearing appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE), practicing safe injection techniques, and implementing rigorous hand hygiene before and after every patient interaction. Safe disposal of sharps in puncture-resistant safety boxes remains mandatory to prevent accidental needle-stick injuries in busy clinical wards.

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)

Post-Exposure Prophylaxis is an emergency medical intervention for individuals who experience a high-risk exposure to HIV, such as a needle-stick injury or sexual assault. To be effective, PEP must be initiated as soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours and no later than 72 hours after exposure. The standard regimen involves a 28-day course of multi-drug antiretroviral therapy. Nurses play a vital role in providing immediate first aid to the wound site, conducting risk assessments, and counseling patients on adherence to the full medication course.

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT)

Vertical transmission of HIV from a mother to her child can occur during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding. Comprehensive PMTCT programs aim to reduce this risk to less than two percent through a cascade of interventions. Pregnant women are screened during antenatal care, and those diagnosed receive immediate ART. Additional preventive measures include offering elective Caesarean sections where indicated, managing safe infant feeding practices, and providing infant prophylaxis syrup after birth.

Community Education and Nursing Roles in HIV Prevention

Nurses act as frontline educators, counselors, and advocates in the fight against HIV. Community outreach helps dismantle persistent myths, reduces social stigma, and promotes voluntary counseling and testing (VCT). By fostering supportive environments, nurses encourage marginalized groups to access healthcare services without fear of discrimination. Effective health teaching empowers individuals to make informed choices regarding safe sex practices, medication adherence, and regular screening.
