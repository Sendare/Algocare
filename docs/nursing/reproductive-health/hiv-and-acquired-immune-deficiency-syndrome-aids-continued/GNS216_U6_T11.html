Prevention of Mother to Child Transmission (PMTCT)

Introduction to PMTCT and Global Context

Prevention of Mother-to-Child Transmission (PMTCT) encompasses a comprehensive package of interventions designed to stop the transmission of the Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) from an infected mother to her child during pregnancy, labor, delivery, or breastfeeding. Globally, sub-Saharan Africa bears the highest burden of paediatric HIV, making effective PMTCT implementation a vital public health priority for Nigerian nurses and midwives. Without interventions, the risk of transmission ranges from 15% to 45%. Effective PMTCT programs drastically reduce this risk to below 5%, paving the way for an HIV-free generation.

Pathophysiology of Mother-to-Child Transmission

Mother-to-child transmission, also known as vertical transmission, occurs when HIV passes from a mother living with HIV to her baby. This can happen at three distinct physiological periods:

  • In utero: Transplacental passage of the virus, particularly during the third trimester or when maternal viral load is high due to primary infection or advanced disease.
  • Intrapartum: Exposure of the infant to infected maternal blood, vaginal secretions, and amniotic fluid during uterine contractions and passage through the birth canal.
  • Postnatal: Ingestion of infected breast milk containing free viral particles and cell-associated virus during breastfeeding.

Factors such as high maternal viral load, low CD4 count, placental inflammation, and prolonged rupture of membranes significantly increase transmission probability.

The 4-Pronged Strategy of PMTCT

The World Health Organization utilizes a comprehensive 4-pronged approach to address maternal and child health regarding HIV:

  1. Prong 1: Primary prevention of HIV infection among women of childbearing age.
  2. Prong 2: Prevention of unintended pregnancies among women living with HIV.
  3. Prong 3: Prevention of HIV transmission from pregnant and breastfeeding women to their infants.
  4. Prong 4: Provision of appropriate treatment, care, and support to mothers living with HIV, their children, and their families.

This holistic model ensures that PMTCT extends beyond the delivery room to encompass community-wide reproductive health and family support.

Antenatal Care, Counseling, and HIV Testing

Routine antenatal care (ANC) serves as the primary gateway for PMTCT services. During the first booking visit, nurses must offer routine, opt-out HIV counseling and testing (HCT) to all pregnant women in a confidential environment. Pre-test counseling explores risk reduction and prepares the client for the test result, while post-test counseling delivers the result with empathy. For women who test negative, prevention messages and repeat testing during the third trimester are vital due to the risk of seroconversion during pregnancy. For those who test positive, immediate linkage to ART is initiated.

Antiretroviral Therapy Regimens for Pregnant and Breastfeeding Women

Lifelong Antiretroviral Therapy (ART) is initiated for every pregnant or breastfeeding woman diagnosed with HIV, regardless of her CD4 cell count or clinical staging. The standard first-line regimen typically consists of a Tenofovir, Lamivudine, and Dolutegravir (TLD) combination, which is safe, highly effective, and rapidly suppresses viral load. Achieving and maintaining viral suppression is the single most important factor in preventing vertical transmission. Nurses play a crucial role in assessing medication adherence, managing minor side effects, and reinforcing the importance of daily, uninterrupted dosing.

Intrapartum and Labor Management in PMTCT

Intrapartum care requires specific precautions to minimize infant exposure to maternal blood and genital secretions. Healthcare workers must strictly adhere to standard universal precautions during all examinations and deliveries. Elective caesarean section may be recommended for women with high or unknown viral loads near term. Unnecessary invasive procedures, such as artificial rupture of membranes, routine episiotomies, and instrumental deliveries, should be avoided when possible to reduce mucosal trauma. Continuing maternal oral ART throughout labor remains essential.

Postnatal Care and Infant Prophylaxis

Postnatal interventions begin immediately after birth to protect the exposed newborn. Infants born to mothers living with HIV receive antiretroviral prophylaxis—such as syrup Nevirapine or Zidovudine—starting within hours of delivery to neutralize any virus acquired during birth. Prophylaxis duration depends on maternal viral load and risk stratification. Early infant diagnosis (EID) using Dried Blood Spot (DBS) PCR testing is performed at specific intervals, typically at 6 weeks of age, to determine the infant's HIV status. Nurses support mothers with immunization schedules, growth monitoring, and adherence counseling.

Infant Feeding Options in the Context of HIV

Infant feeding counseling balances the risk of HIV transmission through breast milk against the life-threatening risks of malnutrition, diarrhea, and pneumonia associated with unsafe replacement feeding. Current WHO guidelines recommend exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life, accompanied by maternal ART, followed by the introduction of complementary foods while continuing breastfeeding up to 12 months or longer. Mixed feeding—giving breast milk and other foods or liquids concurrently—is strictly discouraged because it damages the infant's gut mucosa and dramatically increases HIV transmission rates.

Nursing Roles and Community Support in PMTCT

Nurses act as educators, counselors, clinical care providers, and community advocates within PMTCT programs. They address stigma and discrimination, which remain major barriers to testing, treatment retention, and exclusive feeding practices. Partner involvement is actively encouraged, as male support improves maternal adherence and retention in care. Community health workers bridge the gap between health facilities and local communities by tracing defaulters, offering peer support, and promoting maternal and child health services.
