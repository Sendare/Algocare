Puerperium

Introduction and Definition of the Puerperium

The puerperium is the critical postnatal period that begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six weeks, or forty-two days. During this timeframe, the maternal anatomical and physiological systems return to their pre-pregnant state. While most texts focus on the physical recovery, it marks a profound psychological transition for the mother as she embraces her new role. Nurses in Nigerian primary health clinics often encounter mothers who neglect their own recovery due to excessive focus on the newborn. Comprehensive postnatal care during these weeks reduces maternal morbidity significantly.

Uterine Involution and Lochia

Uterine involution is the rapid shrinking of the uterus back to its non-pregnant size and pelvic position. Immediately after delivery, the fundus is palpable midway between the symphysis pubis and the umbilicus, then rises to the level of the umbilicus within twenty-four hours. It should descend by approximately one centimeter every day.

  • Lochia rubra: Dark red discharge occurring from day one to day four postpartum, containing blood, decidual tissue, and mucus.
  • Lochia serosa: Pinkish-brown discharge lasting from day five to day nine, composed of fewer red blood cells, serum, leukocytes, and cervical mucus.
  • Lochia alba: Yellowish-white discharge appearing from day ten to day fourteen, consisting mainly of leukocytes, epithelial cells, and bacteria.

Physiology of Lactation and Breastfeeding Support

Lactation is driven by hormonal shifts following the delivery of the placenta. A sharp drop in progesterone and estrogen triggers prolactin release from the anterior pituitary gland, stimulating milk production. Oxytocin, released by the posterior pituitary during infant suckling, causes the contraction of myoepithelial cells around the alveoli, resulting in the milk ejection reflex or let-down.

Colostrum is secreted in the first few days, providing concentrated immunoglobulins and antibodies. Nurses must teach mothers correct latching techniques to prevent cracked nipples and mastitis, emphasizing exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months of life.

Routine Postnatal Assessment and Care

Systematic evaluation of the mother prevents overlooked complications. Nurses use the BUCKS or APGAR-style mental frameworks for checking vital signs, lochia, perineal integrity, bladder function, and emotional state.

Psychological Adjustments and Mental Health

The transition to motherhood involves major emotional vulnerability. Up to eighty percent of women experience the maternity blues, characterized by tearfulness, anxiety, and mood instability within the first week, resolving without formal psychiatric intervention.

In contrast, postpartum depression persists past two weeks, presenting with severe despondency, guilt, and inability to bond with the infant. Nurses must actively screen for psychological distress, as cultural stigma in Nigeria often forces women to suffer in silence without reporting depressive symptoms.

Common Puerperal Complications and Red Flags

Postnatal complications can deteriorate rapidly if not identified. Puerperal sepsis remains a leading cause of maternal mortality in sub-Saharan Africa, often signaled by foul lochia, lower abdominal pain, and pyrexia. Secondary postpartum hemorrhage occurs after twenty-four hours due to subinvolution or infection.

Thromboembolic events also threaten postpartum women due to pregnancy-induced hypercoagulability and venous stasis. Nurses must educate patients on red flags: heavy vaginal bleeding, severe calf pain, chest pain, and high fever.

Postnatal Counseling, Nutrition, and Contraception

Recovery demands adequate nutrition, rest, and careful spacing of future pregnancies. Nursing mothers require increased caloric intake, high protein diets, and iron supplementation to recover from delivery blood loss.

Contraceptive counseling must begin before hospital discharge. Progestin-only methods, barrier methods, and lactational amenorrhea method (LAM) are safe during breastfeeding. Combined oral contraceptives are generally avoided in the early weeks due to increased thromboembolic risk and potential suppression of milk supply.
