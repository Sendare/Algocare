Postnatal Care

Introduction and Definition of Postnatal Care

Postnatal care, often called the puerperium, begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six weeks (42 days). This critical period addresses the rapid physical, psychological, and social adjustments a mother experiences after childbirth. In many Nigerian clinical settings, mothers are discharged early due to bed shortages, making targeted postnatal education essential before they leave the facility. The primary goal is to monitor maternal recovery, promote newborn health, and establish a supportive environment for the growing family.

Physiological Changes and Involution of the Puerperium

Involution describes the anatomical process where the reproductive organs return to their non-pregnant state. The uterus shrinks rapidly, dropping by roughly one centimeter per day below the umbilicus, and should become a pelvic organ again by day ten. Lochia changes character over these weeks, moving from bright red ruba, to brownish serosa, and finally to yellowish-white alba. A common misconception among new mothers is that retained clots cause sudden heavy bleeding weeks later, whereas uterine atony or subinvolution is usually the culprit.

Immediate Postnatal Assessment and Monitoring

The first two hours following placental delivery carry the highest risk of postpartum hemorrhage. Nurses must check vital signs, uterine tone, and vaginal bleeding every fifteen minutes for the first hour, then every thirty minutes for the second hour. A boggy, relaxed uterus requires immediate fundal massage to stimulate contraction and expel accumulated clots. Bladder distention frequently pushes the uterus upward and sideways, worsening bleeding, so encouraging spontaneous voiding is a priority.

Routine Postnatal Care Schedules and Assessments

Structured contact schedules ensure timely detection of late complications. Standard touchpoints include the first 24 hours, day three, days six through fourteen, and the final six-week checkup. Each visit requires a head-to-toe clinical evaluation:

  • Vital signs: Check for silent postpartum preeclampsia or infection.
  • Perineum: Inspect episiotomy or tear sites for hematoma, gaping, or foul discharge.
  • Breasts: Assess for engorgement, nipple cracks, or early mastitis signs.
  • Emotional state: Screen for baby blues versus clinical depression.

Breastfeeding Support and Lactation Management

Exclusive breastfeeding for the first six months provides unmatched nutritional and immunological protection for the infant. Early initiation within the first hour of birth stimulates prolactin and oxytocin release. Nurses must observe a full feed to ensure proper latching, preventing sore, cracked nipples and inadequate milk transfer. Engorgement occurs when breasts overfill with milk and lymph, which is best managed through frequent feeding, warm compresses before nursing, and cold packs afterward.

Identification and Management of Postnatal Complications

Postpartum hemorrhage, puerperal sepsis, and thromboembolism remain leading causes of maternal mortality in Nigeria. Vigilant monitoring saves lives by catching subtle shifts in clinical baselines.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage: Blood loss exceeding 500ml for vaginal births or 1000ml for cesarean sections.
  • Puerperal Sepsis: Genital tract infection marked by fever, lower abdominal pain, and foul lochia.
  • Thromboembolism: Leg swelling, warmth, and pain due to hypercoagulability and immobility.

Mental Health and Postpartum Emotional Well-being

Postpartum psychological adjustments range from transient baby blues to debilitating clinical depression and psychosis. Baby blues affect many women around day three to five, resolving spontaneously with rest and family support. Conversely, postpartum depression manifests as persistent sadness, severe anxiety, and inability to bond with the baby, requiring professional intervention. Nurses must screen mothers using tools like the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale without judgment, keeping in mind that cultural stigma often hides these symptoms.

Postnatal Nutrition, Hygiene, and Self-Care

Recovery requires adequate caloric intake, increased protein, and proper hydration, especially for lactating mothers. Micronutrient supplementation, particularly iron and folic acid, should continue for at least three months to replenish stores lost during birth. Perineal hygiene prevents ascending infections; mothers should wash the area with clean water from front to back after every voiding or bowel movement. Gentle postnatal exercises help strengthen the pelvic floor muscles, reducing long-term risks of urinary incontinence.

Family Planning and Postpartum Contraception

Ovulation can return as early as three weeks postpartum, even before the resumption of normal menstruation, making birth spacing critical. Lactational Amenorrhea Method (LAM) works effectively only if breastfeeding is exclusive, the baby is under six months old, and menses has not returned. Progestin-only contraceptives, such as injectables, implants, and pills, do not interfere with milk supply and are safe to start immediately. Combined estrogen-progestin methods are generally delayed because they can reduce milk volume and increase thromboembolic risk in the early weeks.

Discharge Planning, Community Follow-up, and Immunization

Comprehensive discharge planning bridges the gap between hospital care and home life. Before leaving, the mother must receive clear warning signs for both herself and her newborn, emergency contact details, and scheduled appointment cards. Infant immunizations, such as the BCG and oral polio vaccines given at birth, are verified or administered. Community health extension workers play a vital role through home visits, ensuring continuity of care and early identification of danger signs in remote settings.
