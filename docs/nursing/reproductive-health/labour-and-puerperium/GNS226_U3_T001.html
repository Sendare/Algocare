Labour

Introduction and Definition of Labour

Labour is the physiological process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina to the outside world. Clinically, it is defined as painful, regular uterine contractions that increase in frequency and intensity, accompanied by progressive effacement and dilation of the cervix. When this process occurs spontaneously between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation, it is termed term labour.

A common misconception in clinical practice is that any uterine tightening or mild discomfort before 37 weeks constitutes true labour; however, Braxton Hicks contractions are irregular, non-progressive, and do not cause cervical change. True labour requires objective evidence of cervical remodeling. Recognizing the distinct boundary between false and true labour prevents premature interventions and unnecessary maternal anxiety.

Causes and Physiology of the Onset of Labour

The exact trigger for human labour remains a complex interplay of endocrine, paracrine, and mechanical factors. As pregnancy reaches term, several physiological shifts occur to transition the uterus from a relaxed, quiescent state to a highly contractile organ.

  • Progesterone Withdrawal: Progesterone maintains uterine relaxation. While maternal blood levels do not drop, functional withdrawal occurs due to changes in receptor types within the myometrium.
  • Estrogen Dominance: Estrogen levels rise relative to progesterone, increasing the sensitivity of myometrial cells and stimulating the synthesis of gap junctions.
  • Oxytocin Release: The posterior pituitary gland releases oxytocin, and local uterine sensitivity to oxytocin peaks at term, prompting strong muscular contractions.
  • Prostaglandin Synthesis: Fetal membranes and decidua produce prostaglandins (especially PGE2 and PGF2alpha), which promote cervical ripening and myometrial stimulation.

Normal Anatomy and Mechanisms of Normal Labour

The passage of the fetus through the pelvis requires precise anatomical adjustments known as the cardinal movements of labour. These mechanical steps allow the irregularly shaped fetal head to navigate the varying diameters of the maternal bony pelvis.

  1. Engagement: The widest diameter of the fetal presenting part passes through the pelvic inlet.
  2. Descent: Downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal, driven by uterine contractions and maternal pushing.
  3. Flexion: The fetal head bends forward against the pelvic floor, presenting the smallest suboccipitobregmatic diameter.
  4. Internal Rotation: The head rotates, usually bringing the occiput to the anterior position beneath the pubic symphysis.
  5. Extension: The fetal head pivots upward and emerges from the vagina as the occiput passes under the pubic symphysis.
  6. Restitution and External Rotation: The head aligns back with the shoulders, and the shoulders rotate internally to fit the pelvic outlet.
  7. Expulsion: The anterior shoulder delivers, followed quickly by the posterior shoulder and the rest of the body.

The Four Stages of Labour and Clinical Management

Labour is divided into distinct chronological phases to guide nursing care and medical interventions.

StageDescriptionKey Nursing Responsibilities
First StageOnset of true labour to full cervical dilation (10 cm)Monitor contractions, track partograph, assess vital signs, support coping
Second StageFull cervical dilation to delivery of the infantAssist with maternal pushing, monitor fetal heart rate, prepare delivery tray
Third StageDelivery of the infant to delivery of the placentaAdminister uterotonics, inspect placenta for completeness, assess bleeding
Fourth StageThe first one to two hours immediately following placental deliveryMonitor postpartum hemorrhage risk, check uterine tone, promote bonding

Maternal and Fetal Assessment During Labour

Continuous surveillance ensures maternal safety and detects early signs of fetal compromise. Nurses utilize both physical assessments and technological tools to evaluate progress and well-being.

Maternal observations include regular tracking of blood pressure, pulse, temperature, and urine output. Elevated temperature can indicate maternal infection or chorioamnionitis. Blood pressure monitoring helps identify gestational hypertension disorders.

Fetal well-being is monitored using intermittent auscultation with a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler ultrasound, or via continuous Electronic Fetal Monitoring (EFM). The baseline fetal heart rate normally ranges between 110 and 160 beats per minute. Accelerations signal a healthy, reactive nervous system, while decelerations—especially late or variable decelerations—warn of potential fetal hypoxia requiring immediate clinical action.

Pain Management and Supportive Care in Labour

Labour pain is severe and multidimensional, combining physical tissue stretching with emotional anxiety. Effective nursing care integrates both non-pharmacological comfort measures and pharmacological options.

Non-pharmacological techniques include continuous one-on-one labour support, maternal mobility, position changes, hydrotherapy, massage, breathing exercises, and sacral counter-pressure. These methods enhance coping and reduce the perception of pain without introducing drug side effects.

Pharmacological relief ranges from systemic opioids, such as pethidine or tramadol, to regional anesthesia like epidural or spinal blocks. Nurses must monitor for side effects such as maternal respiratory depression, hypotension, or urinary retention following regional anesthesia administration.

Complications of Labour and Emergency Nursing Interventions

Even a normally progressing labour can turn into an emergency. Swift identification and team-based intervention save maternal and neonatal lives.

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding following delivery, most commonly caused by uterine atony. Immediate interventions include uterine massage, administering oxytocin, and monitoring vital signs.
  • Cord Prolapse: The umbilical cord slips ahead of the presenting part, compressing blood vessels and cutting off fetal oxygen. Nurses must manually elevate the presenting part off the cord and position the mother in a knee-chest position while rushing her for emergency Cesarean section.
  • Shoulder Dystocia: The fetal shoulder impacts against the maternal symphysis pubis after the head is delivered. Nursing staff must immediately execute specific maneuvers, such as the McRoberts maneuver, while avoiding direct fundal pressure.
