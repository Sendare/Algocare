Immediate Care of the Newborn

Introduction and Objectives of Immediate Newborn Care

The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life is the most critical physiological adjustment a human being makes. At birth, the newborn must instantly shift from a fluid-filled, dependent system to an independent existence where the lungs breathe air, the gut digests food, and the thermoregulatory system maintains body temperature.

The primary goals of immediate newborn care are:

  • Establishing effective respiration: Clear the airway and initiate spontaneous breathing.
  • Maintaining normal body temperature: Prevent rapid heat loss through evaporation, conduction, convection, and radiation.
  • Preventing immediate complications: Administer prophylactic interventions like eye ointment and vitamin K.
  • Promoting bonding: Support early skin-to-skin contact and breastfeeding initiation.

Most newborns transition smoothly without intervention. However, midwives and nurses must remain vigilant because roughly ten percent require some assistance to breathe, and about one percent need extensive resuscitation. Assessment begins the moment the baby emerges.

Immediate Assessment and Apgar Scoring

The Apgar score provides a quick, standardized method for evaluating the physical condition of a newborn at birth. It is assessed at exactly 1 minute and 5 minutes after birth. If the score at five minutes is below seven, additional assessments are typically performed every five minutes for up to twenty minutes.

The tool evaluates five distinct physiological parameters, each scored from 0 to 2:

A common misconception is that the Apgar score guides immediate resuscitation decisions. Resuscitation must begin immediately based on heart rate and breathing before the one-minute Apgar score is even assigned.

Establishing and Maintaining a Patent Airway

Most newborns clear their airway spontaneously by coughing and crying. However, gentle physical clearance is often necessary. Nurses should position the infant on their back or side with the neck slightly extended in a neutral or 'sniffing' position. Hyperextension or flexion of the neck can obstruct the immature trachea.

When secretions are present in the mouth and nose, suctioning should be performed gently. Always suction the mouth first and then the nose. Suctioning the nose before the mouth can startle the baby, causing them to gasp and aspirate oral secretions into the lower airway.

Bulb syringes are standard tools for suctioning. The bulb must be compressed before insertion into the mouth or nose to avoid pushing mucus deeper into the respiratory tract. Suctioning should be brief and performed only when necessary, as vigorous mechanical stimulation of the posterior pharynx can trigger bradycardia via vagal nerve stimulation.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Hypothermia

Newborns are highly susceptible to rapid heat loss due to a high ratio of surface area to body mass, thin skin, and a lack of subcutaneous fat. They cannot shiver effectively to generate heat; instead, they rely on the metabolism of brown adipose tissue (BAT), also known as brown fat.

Heat transfer occurs through four main mechanisms in the delivery room:

  1. Evaporation: Heat loss when amniotic fluid on the skin turns to gas. Prevented by drying the infant immediately at birth.
  2. Conduction: Heat loss from direct contact with cold surfaces like scales or unheated tables. Prevented by using pre-warmed blankets and radiant warmers.
  3. Convection: Heat loss from air currents passing over the skin. Prevented by keeping delivery rooms free of drafts and closing doors.
  4. Radiation: Heat loss to nearby cold solid surfaces that are not in direct contact. Prevented by placing cribs away from outside cold walls and windows.

Cold stress triggers a dangerous cascade: increased metabolic rate leads to high oxygen consumption, peripheral vasoconstriction, hypoglycemia, and metabolic acidosis.

Prevention of Infection and Ophthalmia Neonatorum

During passage through the birth canal, a newborn may be exposed to maternal pathogens such as *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* and *Chlamydia trachomatis*. If left untreated, these organisms can cause severe conjunctivitis known as ophthalmia neonatorum, which can rapidly lead to corneal ulceration and permanent blindness.

Prophylactic treatment is mandatory by law in many jurisdictions. A single application of an antimicrobial agent is instilled into the lower conjunctival sac of each eye within one hour of birth.

  • Erythromycin (0.5%) ointment is the standard prophylactic agent used globally because it protects against gonococcal ophthalmia without causing chemical irritation.
  • Tetracycline (1%) ointment is an alternative.
  • Silver nitrate (1%) drops, historically used, are largely obsolete due to high rates of chemical conjunctivitis.

The nurse should clean the eyelids with sterile water or normal saline before instilling the ointment. The application tube should not touch the eye structure to prevent accidental trauma. Excess ointment can be gently wiped away after one minute, but the medication should not be washed out immediately.

Vitamin K Administration and Hemorrhagic Disease Prevention

Newborn infants enter the world with a sterile intestinal tract devoid of the normal bacterial flora required to synthesize vitamin K. Vitamin K is an essential cofactor hepatic synthesis of coagulation factors II, VII, IX, and X. Without adequate levels, neonates are at significant risk for Vitamin K Deficiency Bleeding (VKDB), a condition marked by spontaneous internal or external hemorrhages, particularly intracranial bleeding.

To prevent this, a single intramuscular dose of phytomenadione (synthetic vitamin K1) is administered shortly after birth, usually within the first hour.

  • Route and Site: Administered strictly via the intramuscular route into the anterolateral thigh muscle (vastus lateralis). The dorsogluteal site must never be used in infants due to the risk of sciatic nerve injury.
  • Dosage: Typically 1 mg for term infants and 0.5 mg for preterm infants weighing less than 1,500 grams.

Parents sometimes express concern about injections, making prenatal and postpartum education regarding the life-saving nature of this prophylaxis an important nursing duty.

Identification and Anthropometric Measurements

Accurate identification is a critical safety priority in the delivery suite. Immediately after birth, before the infant leaves the delivery room, matching identification bands are placed securely on the newborn's wrist and ankle, and a matching band is placed on the mother. Footprints and sometimes maternal thumbprints are also documented according to institutional policy.

Anthropometric measurements are taken to establish baseline physical data and screen for gestational growth abnormalities:

  • Weight: Measured naked on a calibrated scale, shielding the scale pan with a clean paper sheet. Normal term weight ranges from 2,500 to 4,000 grams.
  • Length: Measured from the crown of the head to the heel of the extended leg on a flat measuring board.
  • Head Circumference: Measured at the widest occipitofrontal diameter, passing just above the eyebrows and around the prominent occipital bone.
  • Chest Circumference: Measured at the level of the nipple line.

Initiation of Breastfeeding and Skin-to-Skin Contact

Uninterrupted skin-to-skin contact (SSC), often called kangaroo care, should begin immediately after birth for stable newborns and continue for at least one hour. The naked infant is placed prone directly on the mother's bare chest, covered with a warm dry blanket.

This practice stabilizes the infant's temperature, heart rate, and blood sugar while reducing maternal and neonatal stress hormones. During this period, the newborn goes through predictable stages of alertness, culminating in the first feed.

Early initiation of breastfeeding takes advantage of the newborn's peak alert state during the first hour of life. The infant uses innate reflexes like rooting and sucking to attach to the breast. Colostrum, the first milk, provides critical passive immunity and high concentrations of antibodies. Nurses should assist with positioning and latching without rushing the mother or baby.

Ongoing Monitoring and Preparation for Transfer to Mother

Before transferring the mother and baby to the postnatal ward, the nurse performs a final systematic check to ensure stability. Vital signs—including heart rate, respiratory rate, and temperature—are re-evaluated and documented. The nurse inspects the umbilical cord stump to confirm that all three vessels (two arteries and one vein) are present and that the clamp is secure with no active bleeding.

The initial physical examination also screens for gross congenital anomalies, such as cleft palate, imperforate anus, or limb defects. The infant's general tone, color, and activity level are confirmed normal.

Finally, the nurse provides clear discharge or transfer instructions to the mother, emphasizing warning signs that require immediate notification, such as respiratory distress, poor feeding, lethargy, or fever. Documenting all interventions, medications administered, and parental responses concludes the immediate care phase.
