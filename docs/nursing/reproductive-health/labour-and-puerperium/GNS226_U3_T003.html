Abnormal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Abnormal Labour

Abnormal labour, clinically referred to as dystocia or difficult labour, is characterized by abnormally slow progress of labour or failure to deliver vaginally without mechanical obstruction. Normal labour follows a predictable pattern of cervical dilation and fetal descent. When contractions fail to dilate the cervix or the fetus fails to descend despite adequate uterine activity, the labour becomes abnormal. Recognizing this deviation early prevents prolonged obstructed labour, a major driver of maternal morbidity and obstetric fistula in low-resource settings.

The Four Ps of Labour

Labour progress depends on four interacting components. Any abnormality in one or more of these elements leads to dystocia:

  • Power: Uterine contractions and maternal pushing efforts that expel the fetus.
  • Passenger: The fetus, its size, presentation, position, and anatomical anomalies.
  • Passage: The maternal bony pelvis and soft tissues of the birth canal.
  • Psyche: The emotional state, anxiety levels, and psychological readiness of the mother.

Classification and Types of Abnormal Labour

Abnormal labour manifests in distinct patterns of progress failure.

  • Prolonged latent phase: Cervical dilation proceeding slower than normal during the initial phase of labour.
  • Active phase arrest: Cease of cervical dilation for four hours or more with adequate contractions, or six hours with inadequate contractions.
  • Secondary arrest of descent: No further downward movement of the fetus in the second stage for at least two hours.
  • Precipitate labour: Labour that finishes in less than three hours from the onset of regular contractions.

Causes and Risk Factors of Dystocia

Dystocia arises from mechanical obstructions, functional abnormalities, or maternal characteristics. Cephalopelvic disproportion occurs when the fetal head is too large for the maternal pelvis. Malpositions like occipitoposterior position or malpresentations like breech and face presentation frequently obstruct smooth descent. Weak uterine contractions, known as uterine inertia, stem from fatigue, overdistension from polyhydramnios or multiples, or regional anesthesia. Advanced maternal age and high body mass index further increase the likelihood of dysfunctional labour.

Clinical Assessment and Monitoring Using the Partograph

The partograph is a graphical tool used to monitor labour progress and maternal-fetal well-being. It features an alert line and an action line drawn four hours to the right of the alert line. When cervical dilation crosses the alert line, the patient requires close observation and transfer to a comprehensive facility. Crossing the action line demands immediate medical evaluation and active intervention, such as oxytocin augmentation or cesarean section. Nurses record cervical dilation, fetal heart rate, contraction frequency, descent, and maternal vital signs on this chart hourly or four-hourly as indicated.

Nursing Management and Interventions

Nurses play a central role in caring for women experiencing abnormal labour. Continuous support reduces anxiety and improves birth outcomes. Nurses monitor vital signs, hydration status, electrolyte balance, and bladder distension, encouraging regular voiding every two hours. Changing maternal positions relieves cord compression, enhances uterine perfusion, and encourages fetal rotation. Administering prescribed intravenous fluids prevents dehydration and ketoacidosis during prolonged labours.

Medical and Surgical Management

Medical management of dystocia often involves oxytocin infusion to stimulate stronger contractions when uterine inertia is diagnosed. Healthcare providers perform artificial rupture of membranes, or amniotomy, to augment slow labour. If mechanical obstruction is present, or if medical augmentation fails, surgical intervention via vacuum extraction, forceps delivery, or emergency cesarean section becomes necessary to safeguard maternal and fetal health.

Complications and Maternal-Fetal Outcomes

Prolonged and abnormal labour carries severe risks for both mother and baby. Maternal complications include postpartum hemorrhage, uterine rupture, severe exhaustion, and genital tract infections. Prolonged pressure on soft tissues can result in obstetric fistulae. Fetal risks encompass asphyxia, meconium aspiration syndrome, intracranial hemorrhage, and neonatal sepsis. Prompt recognition and timely intervention remain the key strategies to minimize these adverse outcomes.
