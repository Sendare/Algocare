Physiology of Normal Labour

Introduction and Definition of Normal Labour

Normal labour, also known as parturition, refers to the physiological process by which the fetus, placenta, and membranes are expelled from the uterus through the vagina. To be clinically classified as a normal or spontaneous vaginal delivery, several specific criteria must be met:

  • Onset: Spontaneous in onset without artificial induction.
  • Gestational Age: Term delivery occurring between 37 and 42 completed weeks of gestation.
  • Presentation: Cephalic presentation, meaning the baby's head enters the pelvis first.
  • Duration: Completed without undue prolongation.
  • Outcome: Results in the birth of a single live baby with no major complications for the mother or newborn.

A common misconception in clinical practice is that any delivery ending vaginally is a normal labour. If oxytocin augmentation or instrumental assistance like forceps are required, the process deviates from strict normality. Understanding this baseline definition helps nurses in Nigerian maternity units quickly identify deviations that require medical intervention.

The Four Ps of Labour

The progress and outcome of any delivery depend on the interplay of four primary factors, commonly known as the Four Ps of labour:

  1. Power: Refers to the forces that expel the product of conception, primarily uterine contractions and maternal pushing efforts.
  2. Passage: Refers to the maternal pelvis and soft tissues of the birth canal through which the fetus must pass.
  3. Passenger: Refers to the fetus, its size, presentation, attitude, and position.
  4. Psyche: Refers to the emotional state, anxiety level, past experiences, and coping mechanisms of the mother.

When these four elements are in harmony, labour progresses smoothly. Obstruction or failure in even one of these components often leads to prolonged labour or obstructed labour, a major cause of maternal morbidity and mortality in resource-limited settings.

Hormonal Control in the Initiation of Labour

The exact trigger for human labour remains a complex interaction of endocrine signals rather than a single event. As pregnancy reaches term, the endocrine balance shifts from maintaining quiescence to promoting uterine contractility.

  • Progesterone Withdrawal: Progesterone acts as a uterine relaxant throughout pregnancy. Near term, its local effect decreases, removing the block on myometrial contractions.
  • Estrogen Dominance: Rising estrogen levels increase the sensitivity of the myometrium and stimulate the production of gap junctions between muscle cells.
  • Oxytocin Secretion: Released from the posterior pituitary, oxytocin binds to specific receptors on uterine cells to stimulate powerful contractions via a positive feedback loop.
  • Prostaglandins: Produced locally within the decidua and fetal membranes, prostaglandins promote cervical ripening and enhance myometrial contractility.

Stages of Normal Labour

Normal labour is divided into four distinct clinical stages, each characterized by specific physiological milestones and nursing care requirements:

  1. First Stage: Begins with the onset of true uterine contractions and ends with complete cervical dilatation at 10 centimeters. It is subdivided into latent and active phases.
  2. Second Stage: Begins with complete cervical dilatation and ends with the delivery of the baby.
  3. Third Stage: Begins immediately after the birth of the infant and ends with the expulsion of the placenta and membranes.
  4. Fourth Stage: The immediate postpartum recovery period, lasting roughly two hours after placental delivery, where maternal stabilization occurs.

Mechanism of Normal Labour

As the fetus travels through the birth canal, it performs a series of passive movements known as the cardinal movements of labour. These adjustments allow the irregularly shaped fetal head to navigate the varying diameters of the maternal pelvis.

  • Engagement: The widest transverse diameter of the fetal head passes down into the pelvic inlet.
  • Descent: Downward movement of the fetus through the birth canal.
  • Flexion: The fetal chin bends inward against the chest, presenting a smaller diameter to the pelvis.
  • Internal Rotation: The head rotates, usually bringing the occiput to the front beneath the pubic symphysis.
  • Extension: As the head reaches the perineum, it pivots upward and emerges from the vagina.
  • Restitution and External Rotation: The head turns back to align with the shoulders as they rotate inside the pelvis.
  • Expulsion: The anterior shoulder is born, followed quickly by the posterior shoulder and the rest of the body.

Maternal Physiological Adaptations During Labour

Labour places tremendous physical demands on the mother's body, triggering widespread systemic adaptations across major organ systems.

  • Cardiovascular System: Each contraction shunts approximately 300 to 500 milliliters of blood back into the systemic circulation, temporarily increasing cardiac output and maternal blood pressure.
  • Respiratory System: Hyperventilation is common due to pain and anxiety, leading to an increased respiratory rate and potential risk of respiratory alkalosis.
  • Gastrointestinal System: Gastric motility and emptying are significantly reduced during labour, which increases the risk of nausea, vomiting, and aspiration if general anesthesia is required.
  • Renal System: Pressure from the presenting part can cause urinary stasis or trauma, while the kidneys work to filter increased metabolic waste from muscular exertion.

Nursing Assessment and Monitoring

Continuous and systematic nursing assessment ensures the safety of both mother and fetus throughout labour. The cornerstone of intrapartum monitoring is the partograph, a graphical record of the progress of labour and maternal-fetal condition.

  • Fetal Monitoring: Assess fetal heart rate regularly using intermittent auscultation with a Pinard stethoscope or Doppler, or continuous electronic fetal monitoring where available.
  • Uterine Contractions: Evaluate frequency, duration, and intensity by abdominal palpation.
  • Maternal Vital Signs: Monitor blood pressure, pulse, and temperature routinely to detect early signs of exhaustion, dehydration, or infection.
  • Cervical Progress: Perform vaginal examinations when indicated to assess cervical dilatation, effacement, and fetal station.
