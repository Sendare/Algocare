Breast Conditions in Puerperium

Physiology of Lactation and Puerperal Breast Changes

The puerperium brings rapid hormonal shifts that transform the breasts from pregnancy preparation to active milk production. Drop in progesterone and estrogen levels following placental delivery removes the inhibition on prolactin, triggering copious milk secretion by day three or four postpartum.

  • Colostrum: The thick, yellowish initial milk rich in immunoglobulins and proteins, secreted in small volumes to match the newborn stomach capacity.
  • Transitional milk: Appears around days 4 to 10 postpartum, marking the blend of colostrum and mature milk components.
  • Mature milk: Established by the end of the second week, providing balanced nutrients and active immunological factors for the infant.
  • Let-down reflex: Oxytocin stimulates the myoepithelial cells surrounding the alveoli to contract, forcing milk down the ducts toward the nipple in response to infant suckling or maternal cue thoughts.

Normal Engorgement versus Pathological Complications

Breast engorgement is a common condition resulting from lymphatic congestion, increased vascularity, and milk accumulation as lactation is established. True engorgement involves both breasts feeling heavy, warm, and uniformly firm, sometimes accompanied by a mild systemic temperature rise that resolves quickly.

Pathological complications arise when stasis persists. Unlike normal fullness, pathological engorgement features tense, shiny skin over the breasts where the infant cannot latch onto the flattened nipple. If unrelieved, increased intra-ductal pressure compresses blood vessels, leading to localized tissue ischemia and predisposing the mother to mastitis. Nursing interventions focus on gentle breast massage, expression of small amounts of milk to soften the areola, and frequent breastfeeding to clear the ducts.

Sore and Cracked Nipples: Causes, Prevention, and Care

Nipple trauma is predominantly caused by incorrect infant positioning and poor attachment at the breast. When the baby takes only the nipple instead of a large portion of the areola, the delicate skin is subjected to friction against the hard palate, resulting in abrasions and fissures.

Management centres on correcting the latch technique rather than applying harsh drying agents. Nurses should encourage mothers to express a few drops of breastmilk after feeding and rub it over the nipples for its natural healing and antimicrobial properties. Air-drying and avoiding tight synthetic bras reduce moisture buildup and friction. For severe pain, short breaks from direct feeding combined with gentle expression maintain milk supply while the skin heals.

Blocked Milk Ducts and Lactocele Formation

A blocked duct occurs when a specific area of the breast fails to drain completely during feeding. It presents as a tender, palpable lump with normal overlying skin temperature and no systemic illness.

  • Causes: Incomplete emptying, tight clothing or bra wires, infrequent feeds, or a sudden change in feeding schedules.
  • Lactocele: A retention cyst containing milk that forms when a duct remains obstructed for a prolonged period, appearing as a smooth, movable, painless swelling.

Nursing care involves warm compresses applied before nursing to dilate ducts, followed by feeding with the baby's chin pointed toward the lump to facilitate drainage. Massaging the lump gently toward the nipple during feeds helps dislodge thickened milk plugs.

Puerperal Mastitis: Pathophysiology and Management

Mastitis is an inflammation of breast tissue that may involve bacterial infection, typically Staphylococcus aureus entering through cracked nipples. It manifests with localized redness, severe pain, swelling, and marked systemic symptoms including high fever, chills, and flu-like malaise.

Prompt medical management is essential to prevent abscess formation. Treatment combines effective milk removal through continued breastfeeding with appropriate oral antibiotics. Analgesics such as paracetamol reduce pain and inflammation. Contrary to outdated practices, stopping breastfeeding during mastitis increases complications; continued emptying of the breast clears infectious organisms and speeds recovery.

Breast Abscess: Identification and Surgical Intervention

A breast abscess represents a severe progression of untreated or inadequately managed mastitis. It develops as a walled-off collection of pus within the breast tissue, characterized by a soft, fluctuant central mass, intense localized throbbing pain, and persistent high fever despite antibiotic therapy.

When an abscess forms, antibiotic therapy alone is insufficient because the drug cannot penetrate the pus-filled cavity. Surgical management involves incision and drainage under local or general anaesthesia, or repeated ultrasound-guided needle aspiration. Postoperative nursing care includes sterile dressing changes, pain management, monitoring for wound healing, and supporting the mother to maintain lactation in the unaffected breast while managing expression safely on the affected side.

Preventive Nursing Interventions and Health Education

Preventive care in the antenatal and early postnatal periods significantly reduces the incidence of puerperal breast complications. Nurses play a vital role in educating mothers before and immediately after delivery.

  • Antenatal assessment: Inspecting nipple structure and teaching proper breast hygiene without using harsh soaps that dry natural oils.
  • Postnatal support: Observing early breastfeeding sessions to ensure proper latch and positioning before discharge.
  • Self-care guidance: Advising mothers on wearing supportive, non-restrictive bras, maintaining adequate hydration and nutrition, and recognizing early warning signs of stasis.
  • Prompt reporting: Encouraging mothers to report localized pain, hard lumps, or flu-like symptoms immediately rather than waiting for scheduled check-ups.
