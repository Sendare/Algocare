Congenital Anomalies

Introduction and Definition of Congenital Anomalies

Congenital anomalies, often referred to as birth defects, are structural or functional alterations that develop during intrauterine life. These conditions can be identified prenatally, at birth, or later in infancy. They represent a significant global health concern and contribute heavily to neonatal and infant morbidity and mortality rates, especially in developing regions like Nigeria where diagnostic and therapeutic resources may face constraints.

Etiology and Risk Factors in the Nigerian Context

The exact cause of many congenital anomalies remains unknown, but several genetic, infectious, and environmental factors contribute significantly. In the Nigerian healthcare setting, specific local risk factors compound the incidence of these conditions.

  • Nutritional Deficiencies: Inadequate maternal intake of essential micronutrients, particularly folic acid, increases neural tube defect risks.
  • Infections: High prevalence of endemic infections such as malaria and rubella during early pregnancy teratogenesis.
  • Consanguinity: Marriages between close blood relatives in certain cultural communities raise the probability of autosomal recessive genetic disorders.
  • Herbal Remedies: Unregulated use of traditional herbal preparations during the first trimester exposes the fetus to unknown teratogenic agents.

Classification: Structural versus Functional Anomalies

Congenital anomalies are broadly divided into two major categories based on their clinical presentation and pathophysiology:

FeatureStructural AnomaliesFunctional Anomalies
DefinitionPhysical malformations of body parts or organsMetabolic, neurological, or sensory impairments
ExamplesCleft lip, spina bifida, clubfootSickle cell disease, congenital hypothyroidism, deafness
DetectionOften visible via physical exam or ultrasoundOften requires biochemical testing or newborn screening

Immediate Midwifery and Nursing Management at Birth

When an infant is born with a visible congenital anomaly, the midwife or attending nurse must act swiftly to stabilize the newborn and offer compassionate care. The initial priorities include ensuring a patent airway, maintaining adequate thermoregulation, and protecting exposed tissues.

  • Airway Clearance: Suctioning secretions gently if facial or upper airway anomalies obstruct breathing.
  • Protecting Defects: Covering open neural tube defects with sterile, warm, saline-soaked gauze to prevent infection and moisture loss.
  • Thermal Control: Placing the baby under a radiant warmer, as infants with open defects lose heat rapidly.
  • Initial Assessment: Conducting a rapid Apgar score evaluation while noting any obvious syndromic features.

Multidisciplinary Care and Parental Support

Managing congenital anomalies extends far beyond the delivery room. It requires a collaborative team consisting of neonatologists, pediatric surgeons, nurses, physiotherapists, and social workers. Parents experience profound shock, grief, and guilt upon discovering their newborn has a defect. Nurses play a pivotal role in bridging clinical care and emotional support by providing honest, empathetic communication and connecting families with available community resources.
