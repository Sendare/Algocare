Postnatal Follow-up

Introduction to Postnatal Care and Follow-up

The postnatal period begins immediately after the delivery of the placenta and extends for six weeks, or forty-two days. During this critical window, a woman undergoes profound anatomical, physiological, and psychological transitions while adapting to motherhood. Postnatal follow-up aims to monitor these recovery processes, prevent complications that could lead to maternal morbidity or mortality, and support successful infant feeding and care. In many resource-constrained settings across Nigeria, this period represents a high-risk phase where maternal and neonatal deaths frequently occur due to delayed detection of postpartum hemorrhage, sepsis, or hypertensive disorders. Structured follow-up bridges the gap between hospital delivery and community reintegration, ensuring both mother and baby receive timely clinical evaluation and health promotion guidance.

Schedule of Routine Postnatal Visits

A structured timeline ensures ongoing evaluation of the mother and infant during the puerperium.

  • First Contact: Within 24 hours of birth, whether delivery occurred in a health facility or at home.
  • Second Visit: Between days 4 and 7 post-delivery to assess breastfeeding and check for early infections.
  • Third Visit: At 6 weeks postpartum for a comprehensive physical, psychological, and reproductive health evaluation.

Additional visits are scheduled immediately if clinical warning signs arise, regardless of the routine timeline.

Comprehensive Maternal Assessment

Evaluating a postpartum woman requires a systematic head-to-toe physical examination alongside a targeted history. The nurse checks vital signs, paying special attention to blood pressure to rule out late-onset preeclampsia and elevated temperature to detect puerperal sepsis. Palpation of the abdomen confirms uterine involution, ensuring the fundus is firm and decreasing steadily in size. The perineum or abdominal wound is inspected for signs of hematoma, dehiscence, or infection. Lochia is assessed for color, amount, odor, and presence of large clots; foul-smelling or persistent bright red bleeding signals pathology. Breast examination checks for engorgement, cracked nipples, and early signs of mastitis.

Newborn Evaluation and Neonatal Care

Neonatal assessment runs parallel to maternal care during follow-up visits. The nurse evaluates feeding success, verifying proper latch and infant weight gain compared to birth weight. Umbilical cord stump status is checked for dryness, foul odor, or surrounding skin redness. Jaundice is evaluated visually by pressing the skin from head to toe, noting how far down the yellow discoloration extends. Vital signs, skin color, muscle tone, and elimination patterns—urine and stool frequency—provide immediate data on infant health. Nurses also reinforce safe sleep practices and educate caregivers on recognizing danger signs like lethargy or poor suckling.

Identification and Management of Postnatal Complications

Prompt recognition of danger signs saves lives during the postnatal period. Postpartum hemorrhage can occur late due to retained products of conception or subinvolution. Puerperal sepsis presents with fever, chills, lower abdominal pain, and abnormal vaginal discharge. Thromboembolic disorders manifest as unilateral leg swelling, pain, and warmth. Nurses must educate women and their families on these red flags so they seek care immediately rather than waiting for scheduled appointments. Management involves stabilization, vital sign monitoring, intravenous access, and prompt referral to medical officers for definitive treatment like uterotonics or antibiotics.

Mental Health Screening and Postpartum Depression

Postpartum psychological distress ranges from mild 'baby blues' to severe postpartum depression and psychosis. The 'baby blues' are common, transient, and resolve within two weeks without medical treatment. Conversely, postpartum depression persists beyond two weeks, impairing the mother's ability to function and bond with her infant. Nurses use screening tools, such as the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale, to detect mood changes early. Compassionate listening, validation of feelings, psychoeducation, and appropriate psychiatric referral are fundamental nursing responsibilities that safeguard both maternal mental health and infant development.

Family Planning and Contraceptive Counseling

Postnatal family planning counseling helps couples achieve optimal birth spacing, improving both maternal and child health outcomes. Nurses initiate discussions about future reproductive intentions during pregnancy and continue them during postnatal visits. Lactational Amenorrhea Method provides temporary natural contraception for exclusively breastfeeding women whose menses have not returned. For those desiring modern methods, options include progestin-only pills, injectables, implants, intrauterine devices, or barrier methods. Counseling must be tailored to individual preferences, medical eligibility criteria, and breastfeeding goals without coercion.

Nutrition, Self-Care, and Health Education

Recovery from childbirth requires adequate nutritional intake, particularly for lactating mothers. The diet must include increased caloric intake, high-quality proteins, iron-rich foods, and calcium to replenish physiological stores. Hydration is vital for maintaining adequate milk production. Nurses educate women on proper perineal hygiene, rest schedules, pelvic floor exercises to prevent future urinary incontinence, and gradual return to physical activity. Addressing cultural myths surrounding postpartum food restrictions is essential in Nigerian contexts to prevent maternal malnutrition and promote holistic recovery.
