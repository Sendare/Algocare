Care of the Newborn

Immediate Assessment and Resuscitation

The birth of a newborn demands rapid evaluation to ensure the transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life is successful. Immediately upon delivery, the midwife or nurse places the baby in a warm, dry environment and evaluates three quick parameters: gestational age, breathing or crying, and good muscle tone. If these three indicators are positive, the infant stays with the mother for skin-to-skin contact. If the baby is gasping, apneic, or limp, immediate resuscitation steps begin under a radiant warmer, following the principles of clear airway, effective ventilation, and sometimes chest compressions.

Apgar Scoring and Physiological Adaptation

The Apgar score provides a standardized numerical evaluation of the newborn's overall condition at 1 and 5 minutes after birth.

  • Appearance (Color): Evaluates central cyanosis and pallor versus a completely pink body.
  • Pulse (Heart Rate): Assessed via auscultation or palmar pulse at the base of the umbilical cord.
  • Grimace (Reflex Irritability): Measured by the infant's response to tactile stimulation or catheter suctioning.
  • Activity (Muscle Tone): Observed through flexion and resistance of the extremities.
  • Respiration (Respiratory Effort): Based on the strength and regularity of the cry and breathing movements.

A score of 7 to 10 indicates good transition, 4 to 6 means moderate depression requiring intervention, and 0 to 3 signals severe distress needing aggressive resuscitation.

Thermoregulation and Heat Loss Prevention

Newborns are highly susceptible to rapid drops in body temperature due to a high surface-area-to-mass ratio and thin layers of subcutaneous fat.

  • Evaporation: Heat loss occurs when amniotic fluid on the skin turns into vapor; drying the baby immediately prevents this.
  • Conduction: Heat transfers away when the infant touches a cold surface, like an unheated scale or table.
  • Convection: Cool drafts from open windows or air conditioners carry heat away from the skin.
  • Radiation: Heat radiates from the infant's body to cooler surrounding walls or solid objects.

Nurses prevent cold stress by drying infants thoroughly, using pre-warmed blankets, placing babies skin-to-skin with mothers, and keeping delivery rooms free of drafts.

Routine Prophylactic Care

Standardized interventions protect newborns from infection and hemorrhagic disease immediately after birth. Eye prophylaxis using tetracycline 1% ointment prevents ophthalmia neonatorum caused by maternal gonococcal or chlamydial infections acquired during passage through the birth canal. Vitamin K is administered via an intramuscular injection to prevent vitamin K deficiency bleeding, since the sterile newborn gut cannot yet synthesize clotting factors. Hepatitis B vaccination is also given before discharge to confer early active immunity against viral transmission.

Breastfeeding Initiation and Support

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life maximizes nutritional and immunological benefits for the neonate. Colostrum, the thick yellowish initial milk, provides high concentrations of antibodies and laxative properties that help clear meconium. Proper latching involves ensuring a wide mouth opening that takes in a large portion of the areola, not just the nipple, to prevent maternal nipple soreness and ensure efficient milk transfer. Rooming-in allows demand feeding, which stimulates adequate maternal milk supply and fosters maternal-infant bonding.

Systematic Physical Examination

A thorough neonatal examination head-to-toe detects congenital anomalies and assesses general vitality. The nurse inspects the head for molding, caput succedaneum, or cephalhematoma. Fontanelles should be soft and flat, with the anterior diamond-shaped and the posterior triangular. Breath sounds are clear and equal bilaterally, and femoral pulses are checked to rule out coarctation of the aorta. The umbilical cord is inspected for the presence of two arteries and one vein. The spine is checked for dimples or tufts of hair that might indicate spina bifida occulta.

Danger Signs and Complications

Early recognition of neonatal illness saves lives, especially in resource-constrained settings where infections can escalate rapidly. Nurses educate mothers to watch for signs such as poor suckling, lethargy, persistent vomiting, high fever or hypothermia, and fast or difficult breathing. Jaundice appearing in the first 24 hours of life or extending down to the palms and soles is pathological and demands urgent medical review. Any umbilical redness extending to the abdominal skin or foul-smelling discharge points toward omphalitis, which requires systemic antibiotics.

Discharge Planning and Maternal Education

Preparation for discharge bridges hospital care and home management in the Nigerian context. The nurse teaches mothers about umbilical cord hygiene using dry cord care, avoiding the application of saliva, toothpaste, or dirty concoctions that introduce tetanus spores. Warmth maintenance, exclusive breastfeeding for six months, and recognizing danger signs are reinforced. Follow-up clinic appointments for immunization schedules and growth monitoring are scheduled before the mother and baby leave the health facility.
