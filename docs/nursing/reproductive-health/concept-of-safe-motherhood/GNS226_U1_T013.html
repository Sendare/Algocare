Maternal Morbidity

Introduction to Maternal Morbidity and Safe Motherhood

Maternal morbidity refers to any physical or psychological condition that results from or is aggravated by pregnancy and childbirth, failing to result in death but causing lasting health problems. Safe motherhood initiatives aim to ensure that all women receive the necessary care during pregnancy, childbirth, and the postpartum period to prevent these debilitating conditions. In Nigeria, the burden of maternal ill-health remains high, placing immense pressure on healthcare facilities and families. Nurses must recognize that survival alone does not indicate complete health, as millions of women live with chronic pain, infection, or disability following delivery.

Severe Acute Maternal Morbidity and Maternal Near Miss

A maternal near miss is defined as a woman who nearly died but survived a complication that occurred during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy. Studying near-miss cases provides valuable clinical insights because survivors share many characteristics with maternal deaths, offering a larger sample size for hospital audits. Severe Acute Maternal Morbidity (SAMM) encompasses life-threatening conditions involving organ dysfunction or failure. Using standardized WHO criteria to identify these cases helps nursing staff evaluate the quality of emergency obstetric care in clinical settings.

Major Direct Causes of Maternal Morbidity in Nigeria

Direct obstetric causes stem directly from obstetric complications of pregnancy, labor, and the puerperium, or from interventions, omissions, or incorrect treatment. In Nigeria, the leading direct causes follow well-established pathological patterns:

  • Postpartum Hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding after birth, often due to uterine atony.
  • Hypertensive Disorders: Pre-eclampsia and eclampsia leading to seizures or organ damage.
  • Pregnancy-Related Infections: Puerperal sepsis following prolonged rupture of membranes or unsafe abortion.
  • Obstructed Labor: Impeded fetal descent leading to uterine rupture or obstetric fistulae.

Indirect Causes and Contributing Socioeconomic Factors

Indirect obstetric causes result from previously existing disease or disease that developed during pregnancy and was aggravated by the physiological effects of gestation. Conditions such as severe anemia, malaria, heart disease, and HIV significantly increase vulnerability. Socioeconomic factors compound these medical risks profoundly. Poverty limits access to nutritious foods and paid transport. Cultural practices, traditional beliefs, and low educational status often delay healthcare-seeking behavior. When a pregnant woman reaches the hospital late due to financial constraints or transportation failure, an otherwise manageable complication quickly progresses to severe morbidity.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of High-Risk Conditions

Early identification of high-risk pregnancies relies on systematic clinical assessment. Nurses must conduct thorough baseline evaluations during antenatal visits, checking for chronic hypertension, previous surgical scars, history of PPH, and extreme parity. During labor, vigilance is paramount. Monitoring vital signs frequently allows the detection of subtle shifts before shock or eclamptic seizures manifest. The use of the partograph is vital for tracking labor progress and spotting prolonged labor early. Any deviation in blood pressure, pulse rate, temperature, or urine output demands immediate escalation to medical staff.

Nursing Management and Emergency Interventions

When severe maternal morbidity strikes, rapid and decisive nursing actions save lives. Management focuses on stabilization using emergency protocols. For hemorrhage, nurses must secure large-bore intravenous access, administer prescribed uterotonics, and monitor fluid replacement. In cases of severe pre-eclampsia, controlling blood pressure and administering magnesium sulfate prevents progression to eclamptic fits. Continuous monitoring of intake and output via an indwelling urinary catheter helps assess renal perfusion. Multidisciplinary teamwork and clear communication are indispensable during these critical crises.

Long-Term Physical and Psychological Complications

Surviving severe acute maternal morbidity does not mean a return to full health. Many women face chronic physical sequelae such as chronic pelvic pain, infertility, urinary or fecal incontinence from fistulae, and severe anemia. Psychologically, near-miss survivors experience high rates of post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, depression, and grief, especially if the infant did not survive. These hidden burdens affect family dynamics, child care, and maternal earning capacity. Comprehensive postnatal care must look beyond wound healing to address these enduring mental and physical challenges.

Prevention Strategies and the Role of the Midwife

Preventing maternal morbidity requires proactive intervention at multiple levels of the healthcare system. Midwives and nurses serve as frontline educators, promoting family planning to reduce high-risk and unwanted pregnancies. Ensuring universal access to skilled birth attendants and timely referral networks breaks the chain of delays. Community outreach programs help dispel harmful cultural myths and encourage early antenatal booking. By advocating for women's health and maintaining high competency in emergency obstetric care, nurses play a definitive role in lowering the burden of maternal morbidity across Nigeria.
