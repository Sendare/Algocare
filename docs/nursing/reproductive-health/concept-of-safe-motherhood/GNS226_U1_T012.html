Maternal Mortality

Introduction and Definitions

Maternal mortality represents a critical indicator of healthcare system quality and women's social standing. The World Health Organization defines a maternal death as the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and site of the pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes.

  • Direct obstetric deaths: Result from obstetric complications of the pregnant state, interventions, omissions, or incorrect treatment.
  • Indirect obstetric deaths: Result from previous existing disease or disease that developed during pregnancy and was not due to direct obstetric causes, but was aggravated by physiological effects of pregnancy.
  • Late maternal death: The death of a woman from direct or indirect obstetric causes more than 42 days but less than one year after termination of pregnancy.
  • Pregnancy-related death: The death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy, irrespective of cause.

Epidemiological Context in Nigeria

Nigeria carries a disproportionate share of the global maternal mortality burden. Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for approximately two-thirds of all maternal deaths worldwide, with Nigeria alone contributing a significant percentage. The maternal mortality ratio in Nigeria remains unacceptably high, estimated at over 500 deaths per 100,000 live births, though rates vary starkly between the northern and southern geopolitical zones.

Disparities stem from uneven distribution of health resources, infrastructural decay, and varying levels of female education and empowerment. Urban centers typically feature lower ratios due to better physical access to comprehensive emergency obstetric care, whereas rural communities suffer from geographic isolation and poverty. Understanding these epidemiological patterns helps nursing students direct public health advocacy and community mobilization efforts where vulnerability peaks.

Direct and Indirect Causes of Maternal Death

Clinical causes of maternal mortality are broadly categorized into direct obstetric complications and pre-existing medical conditions worsened by gestation. In Nigerian clinical practice, a few dominant pathologies account for the vast majority of hospital admissions and fatalities.

  • Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH): Excessive bleeding following delivery, often due to uterine atony, retained tissue, or lacerations.
  • Pregnancy-induced hypertension: Conditions like pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, characterized by high-pressure complications and seizures.
  • Sepsis: Severe systemic infection originating from the genital tract following delivery or unsafe abortion.
  • Unsafe abortion: Complications arising from clandestine procedures performed in unhygienic environments by unskilled providers.
  • Indirect causes: Malaria, anemia, HIV/AIDS, and cardiac disease, which are heavily influenced by tropical disease burdens and baseline nutritional status.

Socio-Cultural and Systemic Determinants

Medical interventions alone cannot solve maternal mortality without addressing root societal drivers. Cultural norms, gender inequality, poverty, and weak health infrastructure heavily shape health-seeking behavior in Nigeria. Many women require spousal or familial permission to access healthcare facilities, creating fatal delays when emergencies arise at night or in remote villages.

Traditional beliefs often lead families to patronize traditional birth attendants (TBAs) for complicated deliveries instead of recognized maternity centers. While some TBAs cooperate with clinics, many lack the skills to manage severe hemorrhage or obstructed labor. Furthermore, out-of-pocket payment systems force impoverished families to delay care while sourcing funds, turning treatable obstetric emergencies into tragedies.

The Three Delays Model

The Three Delays framework explains how socioeconomic and systemic factors culminate in maternal death by breaking down the timeline of an obstetric emergency into distinct bottlenecks.

  1. Delay in deciding to seek care: Caused by failure to recognize danger signs, low status of women, financial constraints, or reliance on traditional remedies.
  2. Delay in reaching a health facility: Caused by poor road networks, lack of transport, geographical distance, and high transportation costs.
  3. Delay in receiving adequate treatment: Caused by poorly equipped facilities, lack of skilled personnel, stock-outs of essential drugs, and poor staff attitudes.

Assessment and High-Risk Identification

Nurses play a frontline role in screening pregnant women during antenatal care to catch risk factors before they escalate into emergencies. Routine assessments include monitoring blood pressure, testing urine for protein, checking hemoglobin levels, and plotting fundal height growth.

Nursing Interventions and Safe Motherhood

Safe motherhood initiatives focus on ensuring that every pregnant woman receives the care she needs to remain healthy throughout pregnancy, childbirth, and the postnatal period. Nurses implement these strategies through direct bedside care, health education, and community advocacy.

Active management of the third stage of labor (AMTSL) serves as a core nursing intervention to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. This includes administering an uterotonic drug such as oxytocin immediately after delivery of the baby, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage. Nurses also educate mothers on recognizing danger signs like severe headache, blurred vision, vaginal bleeding, and reduced fetal movement, encouraging immediate health facility visits when these symptoms appear.

Strategies for Reduction and Policy Implications

Addressing maternal mortality permanently requires multisectoral policy reforms and strengthening of the health system. Task-shifting policies allow trained community health extension workers to administer life-saving uterotonics and magnesium sulfate where professional midwives are scarce. Universal health coverage schemes help eliminate out-of-pocket costs that delay emergency care seeking.

Infrastructure improvements must ensure reliable electricity, clean water supply, and functional blood banking services in primary health centers. Furthermore, investing in female education and girl-child retention in schools raises health literacy and delays early marriage, creating long-term resilience against maternal vulnerability across Nigerian communities.
