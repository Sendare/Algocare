Strategies of Safe Motherhood

Introduction to Safe Motherhood and Maternal Health in Nigeria

Safe motherhood encompasses the strategies, interventions, and services designed to ensure that women experience healthy pregnancies, safe deliveries, and positive postnatal outcomes. In Nigeria, maternal mortality ratios remain a major public health challenge due to a combination of clinical, geographical, and socioeconomic factors. Addressing this burden requires structured approaches that span from community levels up to tertiary healthcare facilities.

The three delays model often explains why Nigerian mothers face severe complications: delay in deciding to seek care, delay in reaching a health facility, and delay in receiving appropriate treatment once at the facility. Effective safe motherhood strategies directly target these three bottlenecks through coordinated health education, infrastructural development, and prompt clinical intervention.

Core Pillars and Objectives of Safe Motherhood Initiatives

Safe motherhood initiatives rely on four foundational pillars established by international health bodies, adapted to local contexts to maximize survival rates.

  • Family Planning: Ensures individuals have the information and means to plan their families, preventing unintended pregnancies and high-risk births.
  • Antenatal Care: Monitors pregnancy progression, detects early signs of complications, and provides preventive treatments like malaria prophylaxis and tetanus toxoid.
  • Obstetric Care: Guarantees access to skilled birth attendants and emergency facilities for complicated deliveries.
  • Postnatal Care: Focuses on the critical weeks following birth to manage postpartum hemorrhage, infection, and neonatal adjustment.

Antenatal Care (ANC) and Routine Maternal Assessment

Antenatal care serves as the primary contact point between pregnant women and the healthcare delivery system. Current guidelines recommend a minimum of eight contacts throughout gestation to allow for timely surveillance and intervention. Routine assessment involves tracking blood pressure for pre-eclampsia, testing urine for protein and glucose, screening for infections such as HIV, syphilis, and malaria, and providing iron and folic acid supplementation.

A common misconception is that ANC is only necessary when a woman feels unwell. Nurses must educate clients that routine visits are preventive, aiming to catch silent conditions like gestational hypertension before they progress to eclampsia.

Skilled Birth Attendance and Intrapartum Care Strategies

The presence of a skilled birth attendant—such as a midwife, nurse, or doctor with midwifery skills—during labor and delivery drastically reduces maternal and neonatal mortality. Intrapartum strategies focus on monitoring labor progress using the partograph, maintaining aseptic technique during delivery, and managing the third stage of labor actively to prevent postpartum hemorrhage.

  • Partograph utilization: Plots cervical dilation and maternal vitals to spot prolonged labor early.
  • Active management of the third stage: Administering a uterotonic agent, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage.
  • Immediate newborn assessment: Checking apgar scores and ensuring prompt initiation of breathing and breastfeeding.

Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care (EmONC)

Despite optimal antenatal care, unpredictable emergencies occur during pregnancy and childbirth. Emergency Obstetric and Newborn Care services are categorized into Basic EmONC and Comprehensive EmONC facilities. BEmONC facilities handle administration of parenteral antibiotics, uterotonics, and anticonvulsants, manual removal of the placenta, and vacuum extraction. CEmONC facilities provide all BEmONC functions plus blood transfusions and caesarean sections.

Timely referral networks between primary health centers and comprehensive hospitals remain critical for saving women who suffer from obstructed labor, uterine rupture, or severe hemorrhage.

Postnatal Care and Prevention of Postpartum Complications

The postnatal period, particularly the first forty-eight hours after birth, is a high-risk window when most maternal and neonatal deaths occur. Postnatal care protocols require assessments at specific intervals: within twenty-four hours, on day three, at two weeks, and at six weeks postpartum.

Nurses evaluate uterine involution, lochia color and amount, perineal wound healing, and emotional wellbeing. Early identification of postpartum hemorrhage, puerperal sepsis, and postpartum depression prevents long-term morbidity and mortality. Educating families on danger signs ensures swift readmission if complications arise after discharge.

Family Planning and Interconception Care

Family planning extends beyond limiting family size; it protects maternal health by promoting healthy birth spacing. Interconception care focuses on the time between pregnancies, addressing maternal nutritional deficiencies, screening for chronic illnesses, and providing psychological support before a subsequent conception.

Short birth intervals of less than twenty-four months increase the risk of preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal depletion syndrome. Integrating counseling on modern contraceptive methods during postnatal visits empowers women to regain their physiological reserves before embarking on another pregnancy.

Community Mobilization and Overcoming Barriers to Maternal Healthcare

Clinical interventions alone cannot eliminate maternal mortality if cultural, financial, and logistical barriers keep women away from health facilities. Community mobilization involves engaging traditional rulers, religious leaders, and men to support maternal health initiatives. Establishing community emergency transport schemes helps solve the second delay in reaching healthcare centers.

Nurses play a vital role as health educators, bridging the gap between biomedical practices and community beliefs. By working alongside traditional birth attendants to redirect clients toward skilled facilities, maternal health programs gain local trust and higher utilization rates.
