Overview of Newborn Management

Immediate Assessment and Transition of the Newborn

The transition from intrauterine to extrauterine life requires rapid physiological adjustments, most notably the initiation of independent breathing and the closure of fetal shunts. Immediately at birth, the midwife or nurse performs a rapid primary assessment to determine if the baby requires immediate resuscitation or routine care.

  • Apgar Scoring: Evaluates heart rate, respiratory effort, muscle tone, reflex irritability, and color at 1 and 5 minutes after birth.
  • Breathing and Cry: A vigorous cry indicates a patent airway and adequate lung expansion.
  • Heart Rate: Must be assessed immediately; a rate below 100 beats per minute signals the need for positive pressure ventilation.

Routine Immediate Care and Resuscitation

Once the initial transition is confirmed stable, routine care centers on maintaining airway clearance, ensuring warmth, and preventing infection. Suctioning should be reserved for babies with obvious airway obstructions, as deep routine suctioning can cause laryngospasm or bradycardia.

For infants who fail to breathe spontaneously, the neonatal resuscitation program guidelines direct providers to dry, stimulate, position the airway, and administer supplemental oxygen or ventilation as indicated by the heart rate response.

Thermoregulation and Prevention of Hypothermia

Newborns possess a high surface-area-to-mass ratio and lack significant subcutaneous fat, making them exceptionally vulnerable to rapid heat loss through evaporation, conduction, convection, and radiation. Hypothermia triggers metabolic acidosis, hypoglycemia, and respiratory distress.

  • Evaporation: Heat loss when amniotic fluid on the skin turns to vapor; combat by drying thoroughly at birth.
  • Conduction: Direct transfer of cold surfaces; combat by pre-warming radiant warmers, scales, and blankets.
  • Convection: Drafts of cool air; combat by keeping delivery rooms draft-free and using incubators.
  • Radiation: Transfer to cooler solid objects not in direct contact; combat by placing cribs away from cold windows and external walls.

Systematic Head-to-Toe Physical Examination

A comprehensive physical assessment must be performed within the first twenty-four hours of life to detect congenital anomalies and birth trauma. The nurse or midwife inspects every body system methodically, noting variations between normal findings and pathological signs.

  • Head: Assess fontanelles; the anterior is diamond-shaped and closes by 18 months, while the posterior is triangular and closes much earlier.
  • Chest: Listen for clear bilateral breath sounds and regular heart rhythms; slight murmurs may disappear as shunts close.
  • Abdomen: Check for a soft contour and the presence of two arteries and one vein in the umbilical cord.
  • Spine: Inspect the entire length for dimples, tufts of hair, or masses indicating spina bifida occulta.

Prophylactic Care and Immunizations

Prophylactic interventions protect the newborn from specific infectious agents and hemorrhagic conditions common in the immediate neonatal period. Administering these medications is a standard component of institutional delivery care.

  • Vitamin K: Given intramuscularly in the vastus lateralis to prevent hemorrhagic disease of the newborn, as infants lack intestinal flora to synthesize clotting factors.
  • Eye Prophylaxis: Tetracycline or erythromycin ointment instilled into the conjunctival sac to prevent ophthalmia neonatorum caused by gonococcal or chlamydial exposure.
  • Hepatitis B Vaccine: Administered per local protocol to guard against vertical transmission from infected mothers.

Initiation and Promotion of Breastfeeding

Early initiation of breastfeeding within the first hour of life maximizes bonding, supplies colostrum rich in immunoglobulins, and stimulates maternal milk production. Proper positioning and a wide latch are crucial to prevent nipple trauma and ensure efficient milk transfer.

  • Skin-to-Skin Contact: Promotes thermal stability, stabilizes blood glucose, and encourages natural rooting and sucking reflexes.
  • Latch Assessment: The infant's mouth should cover a large portion of the areola, not just the nipple, with lips flared outward.

Identification of Danger Signs and Discharge Education

Equipping parents with the knowledge to recognize neonatal illness is essential before hospital discharge. Midwives and nurses must explain warning signs clearly and verify maternal understanding of daily care routines like cord hygiene and temperature monitoring.

  • Fever or Hypothermia: Axillary temperatures above 37.5°C or below 36.5°C.
  • Feeding Difficulties: Refusing consecutive feeds or excessive lethargy during wake windows.
  • Respiratory Changes: Persistent grunting, chest retractions, or fast breathing rates above 60 breaths per minute.
  • Jaundice: Yellowing of the skin or sclera appearing within the first 24 hours of life.
