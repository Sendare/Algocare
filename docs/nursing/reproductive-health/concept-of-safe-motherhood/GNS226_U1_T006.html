Prenatal Care

Introduction to Prenatal Care and Safe Motherhood

Prenatal care, commonly referred to as antenatal care (ANC), encompasses the comprehensive medical and nursing supervision given to a pregnant woman from conception until the onset of labor. The primary aim is to monitor the normal progression of pregnancy and promote the health of both mother and fetus. Safe motherhood initiatives depend heavily on accessible, quality antenatal services, particularly in resource-limited settings across Nigeria where maternal mortality remains a major public health challenge. Nurses serve as the frontline educators, screeners, and coordinators of this care, establishing vital trust and continuity for expectant mothers.

Objectives and Scheduled Antenatal Visits

Structured antenatal visits are scheduled to detect and manage potential complications early. The World Health Organization recommends a minimum of eight contacts for a positive pregnancy experience, replacing the older four-visit model.

  • First Contact: Up to 12 weeks gestation for baseline health evaluation and dating.
  • Second Contact: Between 20 and 26 weeks for anomaly scanning and growth monitoring.
  • Third Contact: At 30, 34, 36, 38, and 40 weeks for close monitoring of fetal well-being and birth preparedness.

Key objectives include screening for pre-eclampsia, managing anemia, administering tetanus toxoid vaccination, and providing malaria prophylaxis.

Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

Pregnancy induces profound anatomical and physiological adaptations across every body system to accommodate the growing fetus.

  • Cardiovascular System: Blood volume increases by up to 40-50%, leading to physiological anemia of pregnancy because plasma volume expands faster than red blood cell mass.
  • Respiratory System: Tidal volume and oxygen consumption increase due to progesterone stimulation and elevation of the diaphragm by the enlarging uterus.
  • Gastrointestinal System: Smooth muscle relaxation caused by progesterone slows gastric motility, frequently resulting in constipation and heartburn.
  • Renal System: Glomerular filtration rate increases significantly, which commonly causes frequency of micturition.

Comprehensive Antenatal Assessment and History Taking

The initial antenatal booking visit requires a meticulous history and physical examination. The nurse must gather biographical data, medical history, surgical history, family history, and a detailed obstetric history summarized using the TPAL or GPA system (Gravida, Para, Abortion). Obstetric history must explore past labor complications, postpartum hemorrhages, or previous cesarean sections. The physical examination includes baseline vital signs, weight, height, systemic examination, and an abdominal examination to estimate gestational age via fundal height measurement once past 20 weeks.

Nutrition, Supplementation, and Health Promotion

Adequate nutrition supports fetal development and maternal tissue expansion. Pregnant women require an increase in daily caloric intake, balanced across carbohydrates, proteins, and healthy fats. Standard antenatal supplementation includes folic acid in the first trimester to prevent neural tube defects, and iron and folate combinations thereafter to prevent anemia. Health promotion must also cover safe water consumption, adequate rest, avoidance of self-medication, and the use of insecticide-treated bed nets to prevent malaria.

Identification of Pregnancy Complications and Risk Factors

Nurses must remain vigilant in identifying danger signs that signal severe obstetric complications. Any high-risk condition—such as multiple gestation, malpresentation, severe hypertension, vaginal bleeding, or severe abdominal pain—demands prompt escalation or referral to a higher-level facility.

  • First Trimester Danger Signs: Persistent vomiting, vaginal bleeding, fever.
  • Second and Third Trimester Danger Signs: Visual disturbances, severe headache, swelling of the face and hands (pre-eclampsia), decreased fetal movement, and leaking liquor.

Birth Preparedness and Complication Readiness

Birth preparedness and complication readiness (BPCR) is a strategy aimed at reducing delays in obtaining emergency obstetric care. Every pregnant woman and her family must make concrete plans before labor begins. This involves identifying a skilled birth attendant, choosing a health facility, arranging emergency transport, securing funds for potential medical costs, and identifying a compatible blood donor if needed. Nurses guide families through these plans to ensure swift action when labor starts or if a life-threatening emergency occurs.
