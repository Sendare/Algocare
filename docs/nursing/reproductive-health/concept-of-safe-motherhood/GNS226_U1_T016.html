Estimation of Maternal Mortality

Introduction and Core Definitions of Maternal Mortality

Maternal mortality remains a critical public health indicator, reflecting the overall effectiveness of a country's healthcare system. According to the World Health Organization, a maternal death is the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy, irrespective of the duration and site of the pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes.

Key metrics used to measure this burden include:

  • Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR): The number of maternal deaths per 100,000 live births, which measures the obstetric risk associated with pregnancy and childbirth.
  • Maternal Mortality Rate (MMRTE): The number of maternal deaths per 100,000 women of reproductive age (typically 15-49 years), combining the risk of pregnancy with the fertility rate.
  • Lifetime Risk of Maternal Death: The probability that a 15-year-old female will eventually die from a maternal cause, accounting for both the cumulative risk of pregnancy and the mortality risk per pregnancy.

Importance and Objectives of Maternal Mortality Estimation

Accurate estimation of maternal mortality is essential for health planning, resource allocation, and policy formulation. Without reliable data, governments and international agencies cannot track progress toward global targets like the Sustainable Development Goals.

Primary objectives for measuring these statistics include:

  • Identifying major clinical causes of death such as hemorrhage, sepsis, and obstructed labor.
  • Evaluating the impact of safe motherhood programs and emergency obstetric care services.
  • Highlighting health inequities between rural and urban populations or among different socioeconomic groups.
  • Guiding budget allocation toward high-impact maternal and child health interventions.

Data Sources: Civil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS)

A well-functioning Civil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS) system is the gold standard for capturing all births and deaths in a population continuously. When functioning properly, CRVS registers every maternal death alongside its underlying medical cause.

In many low- and middle-income countries, including Nigeria, civil registration systems are incomplete. Many deaths occur at home and are never officially registered, leading to significant underreporting. Consequently, reliance shifts to alternative methods and statistical modeling to estimate the true burden of maternal mortality.

Household Surveys and Census Approaches

When vital registration systems are weak, household surveys serve as a vital alternative for gathering health data. Instruments such as the Demographic and Health Surveys utilize full birth histories or sibling history modules to capture mortality data retrospectively.

Censuses can also include specific questions about recent deaths in the household over the past 12 months. While these approaches capture data from communities with low facility-use rates, they suffer from recall bias, large sampling errors, and wide confidence intervals, making year-to-year trend analysis difficult.

Indirect Estimation: The Sisterhood Method

The sisterhood method is an indirect technique developed specifically to measure maternal mortality in settings where data is scarce and sample sizes needed for direct surveys are too large. By asking adult respondents about the survival status of all their sisters who reached reproductive age, researchers can calculate estimates reflecting mortality conditions from several years prior to the survey.

The main advantage of the sisterhood method is that it requires a relatively small sample size and avoids complex clinical evaluations. However, it provides a retrospective estimate representing a midpoint time period rather than current annual figures.

Challenges in Measuring Maternal Mortality in Nigeria

Measuring maternal mortality in Nigeria presents unique systemic and cultural hurdles. Many births and deaths occur outside formal healthcare facilities, often attended by traditional birth attendants where events go unrecorded.

Additional challenges include:

  • Misclassification of causes of death on medical certificates due to lack of diagnostic tools or incomplete clinical notes.
  • Cultural practices where deaths, especially maternal ones, may be stigmatized or concealed.
  • Poor integration between community-level reporting systems and national health management information systems.

The Role of Nurses and Midwives in Data Recording and Reporting

Nurses and midwives sit at the frontline of maternal healthcare delivery and data collection. Their day-to-day documentation directly feeds into the Health Management Information System (HMIS) and contributes to national statistics.

Clinical responsibilities for accurate data include:

  • Maintaining meticulous labor ward registers and admission logs.
  • Recording accurate immediate and underlying causes of obstetric complications on patient charts.
  • Participating in maternal death surveillance and response (MDSR) audits to review facility-based mortality.
  • Ensuring confidentiality while capturing complete demographic and clinical details for every client.
