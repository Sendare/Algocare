STI Information and Counselling (Male)

Introduction and Common Types of STIs in Males

Sexually transmitted infections represent a significant public health challenge among male clients in Nigeria. Many infections present asymptomatically, leading to delayed seeking of care and continued transmission within communities. Healthcare providers must recognize both bacterial and viral pathogens frequently encountered in clinical practice.

  • Gonorrhoea: Caused by *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, presenting typically with purulent urethral discharge and dysuria.
  • Chlamydia: Caused by *Chlamydia trachomatis*, often milder than gonorrhoea but a leading cause of non-gonococcal urethritis.
  • Syphilis: Caused by *Treponema pallidum*, characterized by painless primary chancres progressing through systemic stages if untreated.
  • Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV): A viral infection attacking the immune system, requiring lifelong antiretroviral management.
  • Genital Herpes: Caused by Herpes Simplex Virus, manifesting as painful vesicular lesions and recurrent outbreaks.

Clinical Presentation and Recognition of Symptoms

Recognizing the physical manifestations of sexually transmitted infections allows nurses to initiate prompt diagnostic workups and prevent long-term complications such as strictures or infertility. Male clients frequently report localized urogenital symptoms that require careful physical assessment.

Common presenting complaints include:

  • Urethral discharge ranging from clear to thick yellow-green pus.
  • Burning or pain during urination, clinically known as dysuria.
  • Scrotal swelling, pain, or heaviness indicating epididymitis or orchitis.
  • Painless or painful sores, ulcers, and blisters on the penile shaft or scrotum.
  • Inguinal lymphadenopathy resulting from localized immune response.

Comprehensive STI Assessment and History Taking

Taking a thorough sexual history requires building strong rapport and maintaining absolute confidentiality. Nurses must ask direct, non-judgmental questions regarding sexual practices, number of partners, and previous infection history. Clients often feel shame or anxiety, making a supportive clinical environment essential for accurate disclosures.

The nursing assessment must cover recent sexual exposures, condom use consistency, and symptoms experienced by regular partners. Physical examination should include inspection of the genitalia, palpation of regional lymph nodes, and collection of appropriate swabs or blood samples for laboratory confirmation before starting empirical therapy.

Principles of Pre-Test and Post-Test Counselling

Counselling remains a cornerstone of sexually transmitted infection care, empowering clients to understand their diagnosis, adhere to treatment, and modify risk behaviors. Pre-test counselling explores the client's readiness for testing, assesses personal risk, and explains the screening procedure.

Post-test counselling translates laboratory results into actionable health steps. For positive results, the nurse provides emotional support, explains the prescribed regimen, emphasizes the necessity of abstinence during treatment, and discusses partner notification. For negative results, counselling focuses on reinforcing safer sex practices and the correct use of barrier methods.

Partner Notification and Contact Tracing Strategies

Contact tracing breaks the chain of transmission by ensuring that individuals exposed to an infection receive prompt evaluation and treatment. Nurses play a pivotal role in facilitating partner notification, balancing client confidentiality with public health obligations.

Methods of partner notification include:

  • Patient referral: The client voluntarily informs their partners and encourages them to seek testing.
  • Provider referral: Trained healthcare staff notify partners confidentially without revealing the index client's identity.
  • Contract referral: An agreement where the client is given a specific timeframe to notify partners before the clinic steps in.

Medical Management and Treatment Compliance

Effective medical management requires administering evidence-based antimicrobial therapy tailored to the specific pathogen identified. Nurses must educate male clients on completing the full course of prescribed drugs, even after symptoms resolve, to prevent antimicrobial resistance and treatment failure.

Management also involves advising clients to refrain from sexual activity until both they and their partners have completed treatment and follow-up evaluations confirm clearance. Nurses should screen clients for concurrent infections, as individuals with one STI are at a higher risk of contracting others, including HIV.

Preventive Education and Risk Reduction in Nursing Practice

Prevention remains the most effective strategy in controlling the spread of sexually transmitted infections. Nursing practice must incorporate health education addressing behavioral modifications, consistent barrier methods, and routine screening for high-risk populations.

Core preventive measures include correct and consistent condom use, limiting the number of sexual partners, and promoting timely vaccination against preventable viral STIs like Human Papillomavirus and Hepatitis B. Nurses should also discuss harm reduction strategies and provide accessible supplies of condoms and lubricants during clinical encounters.
