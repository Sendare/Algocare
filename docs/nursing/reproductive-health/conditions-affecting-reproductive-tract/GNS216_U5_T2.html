Sexually Transmitted Infections (Female)

Introduction to Sexually Transmitted Infections in Females

Sexually transmitted infections represent a major public health concern globally, disproportionately affecting women due to biological and socioeconomic factors. The female reproductive tract has a large, moist mucosal surface area, which easily traps pathogens during unprotected sexual contact. Microscopic tears during intercourse create direct entry points for infectious agents. Young women face higher vulnerability because cervical ectopy—where columnar cells are exposed on the outer cervix—provides an easier target for organisms like chlamydia and gonorrhoea. Hormonal contraceptives and vaginal douching can alter the protective lactobacilli flora, raising the risk of acquiring these infections.

Pathophysiology and Modes of Transmission

Pathogens responsible for sexually transmitted infections enter the body primarily through sexual mucous membrane contact, blood, or vertical transmission from mother to foetus. Once inside, bacteria, viruses, or parasites attach to host epithelial cells, multiply, and trigger local inflammatory responses.

  • Ascending infection: Many lower genital tract infections move upward through the endocervix into the uterus, fallopian tubes, and pelvic cavity.
  • Mucosal disruption: Micro-abrasions from sexual friction allow organisms to bypass the skin barrier and enter local blood vessels or lymphatics.
  • Vertical transmission: Infected pregnant women can pass pathogens to the infant across the placenta, during vaginal delivery, or via breast milk.

Bacterial and Parasitic Infections: Chlamydia, Gonorrhoea, and Trichomoniasis

Bacterial and parasitic infections are among the most prevalent curable sexually transmitted conditions in women.

  • Chlamydia trachomatis: Often asymptomatic, causing mild mucopurulent cervical discharge and dysuria if symptoms appear.
  • Neisseria gonorrhoeae: A gram-negative diplococcus producing yellow-green cervical discharge, pelvic pain, and urinary frequency.
  • Trichomonas vaginalis: A flagellated protozoan causing frothy yellow-green, foul-smelling vaginal discharge, intense pruritus, and strawberry cervix.

Prompt diagnosis and antibiotic treatment prevent chronic pelvic inflammatory disease and subsequent infertility.

Viral STIs: HPV, Herpes, and HIV

Viral sexually transmitted infections establish lifelong residence in the host, requiring ongoing management rather than simple cure.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

A thorough clinical assessment begins with a sensitive, non-judgmental sexual history, detailing recent partners, contraceptive use, and specific symptoms such as abnormal discharge, pelvic pain, or dyspareunia. Physical examination involves inspecting the external genitalia, perineum, and perianal region for lesions, erythema, or swelling. A speculum examination allows direct visualization of the vaginal walls and cervix.

Diagnostic confirmation relies on nucleic acid amplification tests, which offer high sensitivity for chlamydia and gonorrhoea using endocervical swabs or urine samples. Wet mount microscopy quickly identifies trichomoniasis or candidiasis, while serological blood tests screen for syphilis and HIV.

Nursing Management and Pharmacological Treatment

Pharmacological intervention depends on the specific causative organism identified during diagnostic testing. Bacterial infections respond to appropriate oral or injectable antibiotics, such as azithromycin or ceftriaxone. Antifungals treat candidiasis, while antivirals manage herpes outbreaks and suppress viral loads in HIV.

Nurses play a critical role in promoting treatment adherence and ensuring partner notification. Patients must complete the full course of medications even if symptoms resolve quickly. Sexual intercourse should be avoided until treatment is completed and symptoms disappear, and sexual partners must be evaluated and treated simultaneously to prevent reinfection.

Complications of Untreated Infections in Females

Neglecting sexually transmitted infections can lead to severe, irreversible damage to the female reproductive system. Ascending bacterial infections often result in pelvic inflammatory disease, causing chronic pelvic pain, scar tissue formation, and ectopic pregnancies. Tubal factor infertility frequently arises when fallopian tubes become blocked or structurally damaged by chronic inflammation. Furthermore, mucosal inflammation and ulcers compromise the natural epithelial barrier, significantly increasing the probability of acquiring or transmitting human immunodeficiency virus.

Patient Education, Prevention, and Public Health Strategies

Primary prevention strategies focus on community education, consistent condom use, and targeted vaccination programs, such as human papillomavirus immunisation for young adolescents before sexual debut. Nurses conduct health talks in schools and clinics to dispel myths regarding contraception and infection transmission. Secondary prevention involves routine screening for sexually transmitted infections during antenatal visits and family planning consultations. Public health nurses also advocate for confidential testing services to reduce social stigma that often deters women from seeking early medical care.
