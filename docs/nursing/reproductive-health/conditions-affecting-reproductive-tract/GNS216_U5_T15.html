Cervical Polyps

Introduction and Definition of Cervical Polyps

Cervical polyps are common, finger-like, benign growths that attach to the surface of the endocervix or ectocervix. They typically measure a few millimeters to a couple of centimeters in length, often appearing bright red or cherry-pink with a soft, fragile texture. While most polyps are non-cancerous, a small percentage can undergo malignant changes, making clinical evaluation necessary upon discovery during a routine pelvic examination.

Anatomy and Pathophysiology of the Cervix

The cervix consists of the ectocervix, covered by stratified squamous epithelium, and the endocervix, lined by columnar epithelium. Polyps usually arise from the endocervical canal as a result of chronic inflammation, localized congestion of cervical blood vessels, or hyperplastic responses of the cervical mucosa. Hormonal stimulation, particularly elevated estrogen levels, also plays a significant role in promoting localized tissue proliferation.

Etiology and Risk Factors

The exact cause of cervical polyps remains multifactorial, strongly linked to chronic inflammation of the cervix or endocervical lining. Common predisposing factors include:

  • Chronic Cervicitis: Persistent infections or inflammation of the cervix.
  • Hormonal Imbalance: High circulating levels of estrogen during reproductive years or pregnancy.
  • Cervical Congestion: Localized vascular congestion in the pelvic blood vessels.
  • Age: Women of reproductive age, particularly those who have given birth and those perimenopausal, are most frequently affected.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Many women with cervical polyps are entirely asymptomatic, discovering the condition during routine speculum exams. When symptoms do appear, they reflect the fragile nature of the vascular growths.

  • Intermenstrual Bleeding: Spotting between normal menstrual periods.
  • Postcoital Bleeding: Bleeding triggered by friction during sexual intercourse.
  • Leukorrhea: Increased yellowish or whitish mucous vaginal discharge.
  • Postmenopausal Bleeding: Bleeding occurring after the cessation of menses, requiring prompt evaluation.

Nursing Assessment and Diagnostic Procedures

Assessment begins with a detailed menstrual and obstetric history, focusing on abnormal bleeding patterns. During a physical examination, visualization via a speculum usually reveals a red or purple growth protruding from the cervical os. Diagnostic confirmation involves:

  • Pelvic Examination: Direct visualization and gentle palpation.
  • Colposcopy: Magnified examination of the cervix.
  • Polypectomy and Biopsy: Removal of the lesion for histological analysis to rule out malignancy.

Medical and Surgical Management

Cervical polyps are primarily managed through minor surgical removal, as medical therapy does not resolve existing growths. The standard procedure is an avulsion polypectomy, where the clinician gently grasps the polyp base with ring forceps and twists it off. The base may then be cauterized with silver nitrate or electrocautery to prevent recurrence and control minor bleeding.

Nursing Care Plan and Patient Education

Post-procedure nursing care focuses on monitoring for complications such as hemorrhage or infection. The nurse instructs the patient to expect mild cramping and light spotting for a few days. Discharge teaching includes advising the patient to avoid sexual intercourse, tampon use, and douching for one to two weeks to allow the cervix to heal properly. The nurse also stresses the importance of attending follow-up appointments to review biopsy results.
