Vulval Tumour

Introduction and Anatomical Background

Vulval tumours involve abnormal growths on the external female genitalia, which include the labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, and perineal glands. While relatively rare compared to other gynaecological cancers, these growths demand careful clinical attention because they significantly affect both physical comfort and psychological well-being. The vulva features rich vascular and lymphatic networks, meaning malignant cells can metastasize to inguinal and femoral lymph nodes relatively early in the disease process. Understanding this local anatomy helps nurses anticipate why patients often present with localized swelling, persistent ulcers, or regional lymphadenopathy during initial assessments.

Classification: Benign versus Malignant Tumours

Tumours of the vulva divide broadly into benign growths and malignant neoplasms. Benign lesions do not invade surrounding tissues or spread to distant sites, whereas malignant tumours are locally invasive and potentially metastatic.

  • Benign Tumours: Includes cysts (such as Bartholin gland cysts), fibromas, lipomas, and condyloma acuminata caused by human papillomavirus.
  • Malignant Tumours: Squamous cell carcinoma accounts for the vast majority of cases. Other less common types include malignant melanoma, basal cell carcinoma, and vulval sarcomas.

Aetiology and Risk Factors

The development of vulval malignancies stems from a combination of viral, inflammatory, and lifestyle factors. Persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus strains, particularly HPV 16, strongly correlates with younger women developing vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia and subsequent squamous cell carcinoma. Conversely, older women often develop non-HPV-related vulval cancer through chronic inflammatory dermatoses like lichen sclerosus. Tobacco smoking also acts as an independent risk factor by suppressing local immune responses in the genital mucosa.

Clinical Presentation and Patient Assessment

Patients with vulval tumours often delay seeking medical care due to embarrassment or by mistaking symptoms for common infections or dermatitis. A thorough nursing history and physical inspection are critical for early detection.

  • Pruritus: Persistent, unremitting itching is the most common presenting symptom.
  • Lump or Mass: A visible or palpable growth on the labia.
  • UlCERATION: Non-healing sores that may bleed or discharge foul-smelling fluid.
  • Pain or Burning: Localized discomfort exacerbated by walking, urination, or sexual intercourse.

Diagnostic Investigations and Biopsy

Confirming a diagnosis of a vulval tumour requires direct visual inspection and tissue sampling. Nurses prepare the patient for clinical examination, which may involve colposcopy to inspect suspicious areas under magnification with acetic acid or iodine stains. A punch biopsy is performed under local anaesthesia to obtain definitive histological confirmation of the tissue type and grading. Imaging studies like pelvic MRI or CT scans are subsequently ordered to assess the depth of invasion and check for lymph node involvement.

Medical and Surgical Management

Treatment strategies depend heavily on the stage of the tumour, its histological type, and the patient's overall health status. Surgery remains the primary treatment modality for most localized vulval cancers, ranging from wide local excision to radical vulvectomy with inguinal lymph node dissection. Radiation therapy and chemotherapy may be used as neoadjuvant treatments to shrink large tumours before surgery, or as definitive therapy for patients who cannot tolerate surgical procedures.

Nursing Care and Patient Support

Nursing care for patients with vulval tumours addresses both physical wound healing and profound psychological distress. Postoperative care focuses on meticulous perineal hygiene, preventing wound breakdown, and monitoring for signs of infection or lymphoedema in the lower limbs. Nurses must also create a supportive, non-judgmental environment because surgeries in the genital region deeply impact body image, sexual health, and self-esteem. Providing empathetic counselling and referrals to support groups helps patients navigate these sensitive challenges.
