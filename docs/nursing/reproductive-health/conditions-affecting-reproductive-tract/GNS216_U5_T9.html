Vulval Varicose Veins

Introduction and Definition of Vulval Varicose Veins

Vulval varicose veins are dilated, tortuous veins located on the labia majora and minora, often extending from pelvic or lower limb varicosities. While less common than leg varicose veins, they frequently develop during pregnancy due to distinct hemodynamic shifts. These veins can range from small, asymptomatic cosmetic concerns to large, painful clusters that cause significant discomfort for the patient. Understanding this condition allows the nurse to provide early reassurance and appropriate clinical monitoring.

Anatomy and Physiological Changes During Pregnancy

Pregnancy triggers profound anatomical and circulatory alterations that directly promote pelvic varicosities.

  • Blood Volume: Total maternal blood volume increases by up to 50 percent to support the growing fetus.
  • Venous Pressure: The enlarging uterus exerts direct mechanical pressure on the inferior vena cava and pelvic veins, obstructing venous return.
  • Hormonal Influence: Elevated progesterone levels cause smooth muscle relaxation in the vascular walls, reducing vessel tone and promoting dilation.

Etiology and Risk Factors

Certain predisposing factors increase the likelihood of a pregnant woman developing vulval varicosities. Multiparity is a primary risk factor because previous pregnancies cause cumulative damage and stretching to the pelvic vein valves. A personal or family history of chronic venous insufficiency or deep vein thrombosis also heightens vulnerability. Furthermore, jobs requiring prolonged standing exacerbate venous pooling in the lower pelvis.

Clinical Manifestations and Symptoms

Patients with vulval varicose veins present with a spectrum of localized complaints.

  • Visible Swelling: Soft, blue, or purple nodular clusters or grape-like structures on the labia.
  • Discomfort: A heavy, aching, or full sensation in the perineum, often worsening after standing for long periods or at the end of the day.
  • Dyspareunia: Pain or discomfort during sexual intercourse due to tissue congestion.
  • Pruritus: Itching around the affected area secondary to skin stretching and local congestion.

Nursing Assessment and Physical Examination

Assessment requires a delicate balance of clinical thoroughness and patient privacy. The nurse should inquire about symptom onset, exacerbating factors, and previous pregnancies. During physical examination, inspect the vulval area in a well-lit room with the patient in the lithotomy position if comfortable. Palpate gently to assess tenderness, warmth, and the compressibility of the varicosities. Document the size, exact location, and whether the varicosities extend to the buttocks or lower limbs.

Conservative Management and Patient Education

Management for vulval varicosities is predominantly conservative because surgical intervention during pregnancy is generally avoided.

  • Rest and Elevation: Encourage the patient to lie on her left side to relieve inferior vena cava compression and promote venous return.
  • Support Garments: Recommend specialized vulval support supports or maternity compression shorts designed to apply gentle pressure.
  • Activity Modification: Advise against prolonged standing or sitting, and encourage frequent short walks to stimulate the skeletal muscle pump.
  • Hygiene: Promote gentle washing and avoidance of tight synthetic clothing to prevent skin irritation.

Intrapartum Care and Delivery Considerations

Labor and delivery present specific risks regarding vulval varicosities, particularly the risk of severe hemorrhage if engorged vessels tear during the second stage. Clinicians must handle the perineum with care during examinations and delivery. Episiotomies should be avoided if possible or carefully placed away from large varicosities to prevent massive bleeding. Assisted deliveries using forceps or vacuum must be executed with extreme vigilance to protect the fragile perineal tissues.

Potential Complications and Postpartum Follow-Up

Although most vulval varicosities resolve spontaneously after childbirth, complications can occur. Thrombophlebitis may develop, presenting as a hard, tender, red cord along the vein. Postpartum hemorrhage remains a risk if torn vessels fail to contract effectively. During postpartum checks, the nurse should inspect the perineum for involution and thrombosis. Reassure the mother that varicosities typically shrink significantly within six weeks following delivery, though subsequent pregnancies may see their return.
