Amenorrhoea

Introduction and Definition of Amenorrhoea

Amenorrhoea is the absence of menstruation. It is a symptom of an underlying condition rather than a disease itself. Clinically, it is important to distinguish between normal physiological states of absent menses—such as pregnancy, lactation, and prepuberty—and pathological disruptions. Recognizing this sign early allows nurses to guide patients toward timely investigations and prevent long-term health consequences.

Physiology of Normal Menstruation and the HPO Axis

Normal menstruation relies on the coordinated feedback loop of the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Ovarian (HPO) axis.

  • Hypothalamus: Secretes Gonadotropin-Releasing Hormone (GnRH) in a pulsatile manner.
  • Pituitary Gland: Responds to GnRH by releasing Luteinizing Hormone (LH) and Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH).
  • Ovaries: Produce estrogen and progesterone in response to LH and FSH, stimulating the endometrium.

Disruption at any of these three levels halts the cycle and causes amenorrhoea.

Classification: Primary versus Secondary Amenorrhoea

Classification guides the diagnostic approach in clinical practice.

  • Primary Amenorrhoea: Absence of menarche by age 15 in the presence of normal secondary sexual characteristics, or by age 13 if secondary sexual development is absent.
  • Secondary Amenorrhoea: Absence of menses for three consecutive cycles, or six months in a woman who previously had regular menstrual periods.

Aetiology and Risk Factors

The causes of amenorrhoea are categorized by anatomical compartment.

Risk factors include extreme weight loss, eating disorders, chronic stress, and endurance athletic training.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnostic Evaluation

Evaluating a patient with amenorrhoea requires a thorough history, physical examination, and targeted investigations. The nurse plays a key role in preparing the patient for diagnostic tests.

Initial investigations always include a pregnancy test to rule out gestation. Further evaluation involves checking serum hormone levels including TSH, prolactin, FSH, and LH. Pelvic ultrasound helps visualize uterine structure and ovarian morphology.

Medical and Nursing Management

Management focuses on treating the underlying cause, restoring normal hormonal balance, and preserving future fertility. Medical therapy may include hormone replacement therapy (HRT) or oral contraceptive pills to protect the endometrium, or dopamine agonists for hyperprolactinaemia.

Nursing care involves administering prescribed medications, monitoring for side effects, and supporting lifestyle modifications related to nutrition and exercise.

Potential Complications and Long-Term Implications

Untreated amenorrhoea, particularly when caused by hypo-oestrogenism, carries significant long-term health risks. Prolonged low estrogen levels lead to decreased bone mineral density and early osteoporosis. Women with chronic anovulation, such as those with untreated Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, face an increased risk of endometrial hyperplasia and future infertility.

Patient Education and Psychosocial Support

A diagnosis of amenorrhoea often triggers anxiety regarding body image, fertility, and womanhood. Nurses provide essential counseling by explaining the diagnostic process in clear language and dispelling myths. Encouraging balanced nutrition, healthy weight management, and stress reduction helps patients regain physiological stability and emotional well-being.
