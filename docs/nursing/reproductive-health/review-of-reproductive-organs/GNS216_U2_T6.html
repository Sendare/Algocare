Dysmenorrhoea

Introduction and Definition of Dysmenorrhoea

Dysmenorrhoea is the medical term for painful menstrual cramps that occur immediately before or during menses. It stands as one of the most common gynecological complaints among adolescent girls and women of reproductive age in Nigeria. Many individuals experience mild discomfort, but severe cramping can disrupt daily academic, social, and occupational activities. Healthcare providers evaluate this condition by assessing the intensity and timing of the pelvic pain relative to the menstrual cycle.

Classification: Primary versus Secondary Dysmenorrhoea

Clinical classification divides dysmenorrhoea into two distinct types based on the presence or absence of underlying pelvic pathology.

  • Primary Dysmenorrhoea: Painful menstruation in the absence of identifiable pelvic disease, typically starting shortly after menarche.
  • Secondary Dysmenorrhoea: Painful menstruation caused by an organic pelvic pathology, such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, or uterine fibroids, often appearing later in reproductive life.

Pathophysiology and Hormonal Triggers

The physiological root of primary dysmenorrhoea lies in excessive uterine activity driven by local chemical messengers. As progesterone levels drop right before menstruation, the endometrium releases high amounts of prostaglandins, specifically PGF2alpha. These fatty acid compounds trigger intense, coordinated myometrial contractions and vasoconstriction. This process restricts blood flow to the uterine muscle, creating temporary ischemia that stimulates pain nerve fibers.

Clinical Presentation and Associated Symptoms

Pain usually begins a few hours before or just as bleeding starts, peaking within the first twenty-four hours. The discomfort centers in the lower abdomen and frequently radiates to the lower back and thighs. Beyond cramping, many women experience systemic symptoms due to circulating prostaglandins.

  • Gastrointestinal: Nausea, vomiting, and loose stools.
  • Neurological: Fatigue, dizziness, and tension headaches.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Risk Factors

Diagnosing primary dysmenorrhoea relies heavily on a thorough menstrual and medical history. Clinicians look for specific risk factors that increase the likelihood of severe cramps, such as early menarche, prolonged or heavy menstrual flows, smoking, and a positive family history. A physical examination, including a pelvic exam where appropriate, helps rule out secondary causes when pain persists or worsens over time.

Medical and Non-Pharmacological Management

Management strategies combine pharmacological agents and comfort measures to block prostaglandin action and relax uterine muscles. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen serve as first-line medical therapy because they inhibit cyclooxygenase and reduce prostaglandin synthesis. Hormonal contraceptives are also effective by suppressing ovulation and thinning the endometrium. Non-pharmacological interventions include applying local heat to the lower abdomen, regular physical exercise, and practicing relaxation techniques.

Nursing Care and Patient Education

Nurses play a key role in supporting patients with dysmenorrhoea through holistic care and targeted health education. Nursing interventions involve assessing pain severity, teaching proper medication timing—such as starting NSAIDs just before menses begin—and demonstrating comfort measures like heat therapy. Health education should also address common misconceptions about menstruation and encourage prompt reporting of unusual pain patterns that could indicate secondary causes.
