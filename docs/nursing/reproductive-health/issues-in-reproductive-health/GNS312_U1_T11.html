Burden of Early and Forced Marriage

Introduction and Prevalence

Early and forced marriage refers to any union where one or both parties are under eighteen years of age, and where consent is compromised by cultural, economic, or familial pressure. This practice is globally recognized as a fundamental violation of human rights, disproportionately affecting young girls in developing regions, including parts of rural Nigeria.

  • Child Bride: A female minor entered into a marital union before reaching legal adulthood.
  • Forced Marriage: A union lacking the free and informed consent of either partner due to coercion, threats, or severe social pressure.
  • Legal Age of Consent: The statutory age defined by national law below which a person cannot legally consent to marriage.

Socioeconomic Drivers and Root Causes

The persistence of early marriage is fueled by complex socioeconomic factors deeply rooted in poverty, patriarchal norms, and cultural traditions. In many resource-poor communities, daughters are viewed as economic burdens or assets that can be exchanged through bride price to alleviate household debt.

  • Poverty: Families marry off young daughters to reduce household expenses and secure financial support.
  • Cultural Tradition: Deeply ingrained societal expectations prioritize female chastity and early motherhood over formal schooling.
  • Insecurity: Conflict and displacement drive parents to marry off daughters early to protect them from sexual violence or to secure alliances.

Physical and Reproductive Health Consequences

Adolescent bodies are biologically unprepared for pregnancy and childbirth, leading to severe obstetric complications. Pelvic bones that are not fully developed increase the risk of prolonged and obstructed labor, which frequently results in obstetric fistula. Furthermore, young mothers face higher rates of maternal mortality, severe postpartum hemorrhage, and unsafe abortions if pregnancies are unwanted.

  • Obstetric Fistula: An abnormal opening between the birth canal and bladder or rectum caused by prolonged pressure during labor.
  • Anemia: Iron deficiency exacerbated by frequent pregnancies and poor nutritional intake during adolescence.
  • Immaturity: Incomplete pelvic growth that severely obstructs the normal passage of the fetus.

Psychological and Emotional Toll

The abrupt transition from childhood to adult marital responsibilities inflicts deep psychological scars. Child brides experience high rates of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder due to isolation, forced sexual initiation, and separation from peers and family support networks. Lacking coping mechanisms, many young girls exhibit suicidal ideation or a profound sense of helplessness.

  • Isolation: Total separation from friends, schoolmates, and familiar community support systems.
  • Coercion: Emotional and physical pressure that strips the minor of personal autonomy.
  • Trauma: Lasting psychological distress resulting from abuse and premature adult roles.

Educational and Economic Impact on Communities

Forcing a girl into marriage abruptly ends her formal education, locking her and her future children in an intergenerational cycle of poverty. Without literacy and vocational skills, these girls cannot secure gainful employment, severely limiting household income and community development.

  • Intergenerational Poverty: The cycle where lack of education and resources passes from parents to children.
  • Dropout Rate: The premature cessation of schooling due to marital or domestic obligations.
  • Economic Stagnation: Reduced community productivity resulting from uneducated and underutilized female populations.

Nursing Assessment and Identification of At-Risk Minors

Nurses working in community health centers and antenatal clinics are strategically positioned to identify signs of early and forced marriage. Routine assessment should include screening young clients for unexplained injuries, signs of forced sexual activity, malnutrition, and sudden school withdrawal.

  • Behavioral Cues: Extreme submissiveness, fearfulness, or reluctance to speak when a spouse or older relative is present.
  • Physical Signs: Frequent untreated injuries, chronic pelvic pain, and early or repeated pregnancies in minors.
  • Social Indicators: Discontinuation of education and lack of personal identification documents.

Interventions, Advocacy, and Preventive Strategies

Addressing early and forced marriage requires a multi-sectoral approach involving healthcare workers, community leaders, legal enforcement, and educators. Nurses play a crucial role as advocates by conducting community sensitization, empowering girls with reproductive health knowledge, and reporting suspected cases of child abuse to appropriate social welfare agencies.

  • Sensitization: Educating traditional and religious leaders on the health harms of child marriage.
  • Empowerment: Providing young girls with life skills, health education, and safe spaces.
  • Reporting: Collaborating with social workers and legal authorities to protect minors at imminent risk.
