Health Consequences of Female Genital Mutilation

Understanding Female Genital Mutilation and Its Classifications

Female Genital Mutilation encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. The World Health Organization categorizes these practices into four distinct types to guide clinical documentation and research.

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV (Unclassified): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Cultural beliefs often drive the practice, linking it to chastity, purity, and social acceptance. Nurses working in Nigerian healthcare settings must understand these classifications accurately to provide non-judgmental care and proper documentation during admission assessments.

Immediate Physical and Surgical Complications

The performance of Female Genital Mutilation, often carried out in unhygienic environments using crude instruments like razor blades, kitchen knives, or broken glass without anesthesia, triggers severe acute trauma. Nurses in emergency and primary care settings frequently encounter patients presenting with these immediate surgical complications.

  • Severe Hemorrhage: Injury to the deep branches of the pudendal artery can lead to life-threatening blood loss and hypovolemic shock.
  • Acute Pain: Intense, unmanaged nociceptive pain stimulates the sympathetic nervous system, causing tachycardia and hypertension.
  • Local Infection: Contaminated cutting tools introduce pathogens, setting the stage for cellulitis, abscess formation, and tetanus.
  • Urine Retention: Swelling, pain, and mechanical obstruction from tissue trauma prevent normal micturition, requiring catheterization.

Prompt stabilization involves fluid resuscitation, meticulous wound care, and prophylactic antibiotics to prevent systemic sepsis.

Long-Term Urological and Gynecological Sequelae

Women who survive the immediate trauma of FGM often live with chronic physical morbidity that affects their daily lives. Scar tissue formation, nerve entrapment, and anatomical distortion create persistent urological and gynecological challenges.

Chronic urinary tract infections are frequent due to anatomical distortion and obstructed urine flow, particularly in women with Type III infibulation where the urinary meatus may be partially covered. Dysmenorrhea occurs because menstrual blood cannot escape freely behind scar tissue or restrictive bridges, leading to hematocolpos. Furthermore, chronic pelvic pain often stems from neuroma formation where nerve endings become trapped in scar tissue during the healing process. Gynecological examinations can be extremely difficult and painful, requiring a gentle, trauma-informed approach from nursing staff.

Obstetric Complications and Risks During Childbirth

Childbirth for a woman with a history of FGM, particularly Type III, presents significant clinical challenges for both mother and fetus. Scar tissue lacks the elasticity of normal perineal tissue, increasing the risk of severe tearing and prolonged labor.

  • Prolonged Second Stage: Rigid scar tissue obstructs fetal descent, exhausting the mother and increasing the risk of fetal distress.
  • Obstetric Fistula: Pressure necrosis from prolonged obstructed labor can create abnormal openings between the vagina and the bladder or rectum.
  • Postpartum Hemorrhage: Delayed expulsion of the placenta and extensive perineal lacerations elevate maternal blood loss risks.
  • Fetal Asphyxia: Extended compression in the birth canal deprives the newborn of adequate oxygen, requiring immediate neonatal resuscitation.

Healthcare providers in labor wards must anticipate these risks, prepare for possible surgical interventions such as an anterior defibulation during labor, and ensure skilled neonatal support is present.

Psychological, Emotional, and Sexual Health Impacts

The consequences of FGM extend far beyond physical scars, deeply affecting mental well-being and sexual function. Victims often experience complex psychological trauma stemming from the betrayal of trust, the violent nature of the procedure, and ongoing physical pain.

Anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder are prevalent among affected women. Many report feelings of incompleteness, shame, and chronic fear regarding sexual intimacy and future childbirth. Sexual dysfunction is a direct anatomical consequence; the removal of sensitive erectile tissue and the presence of painful scar tissue often result in dyspareunia, lack of sexual desire, and the inability to achieve orgasm. Nurses must approach these sensitive topics with empathy, providing a safe environment for patients to express their emotional distress without fear of stigma.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Effective clinical care begins with thorough and sensitive assessment. Nurses in pediatric, gynecological, and obstetric wards must be vigilant in identifying signs of FGM, both recent and historical, during routine health evaluations.

Assessment requires establishing a strong rapport and ensuring absolute privacy. When taking a health history, nurses should ask direct, non-judgmental questions regarding genital surgeries or traditional practices. Physical examination requires explicit informed consent, clear explanation of each step, and a gentle touch. The nurse should document specific anatomical findings, noting the absence of structures or presence of scar tissue without displaying shock or disgust. Recognizing these signs early allows the multidisciplinary team to offer appropriate psychological, urological, and obstetric support.

Holistic Management and Patient Care Strategies

Managing the health consequences of FGM requires a multidisciplinary, patient-centered approach that addresses physical, psychological, and social needs. Nurses coordinate care across various specialties to improve the patient's quality of life.

Surgical interventions, such as defibulation, may be necessary for women with Type III infibulation experiencing severe urinary or obstetric obstruction. Psychotherapy and counseling help patients process trauma, manage depression, and rebuild body confidence. Pelvic floor physiotherapy assists with chronic pain and muscle dysfunction. Throughout these interventions, patient education remains vital, helping women understand their anatomy, manage recurrent infections, and prepare for safe pregnancies. Compassionate nursing care validates their experiences and supports their journey toward healing.

Prevention, Advocacy, and the Role of the Nurse in Nigeria

Eradicating Female Genital Mutilation in Nigeria requires active community engagement, robust legal frameworks, and passionate advocacy by healthcare professionals. Nurses are frontline educators who can influence community attitudes and protect vulnerable girls.

Nurses must collaborate with local leaders, religious figures, and women's groups to debunk myths linking FGM to hygiene, morality, or fertility. Understanding national legislation, such as the Violence Against Persons Prohibition Act, empowers nurses to report violations and advocate for victims. Educational campaigns in schools and antenatal clinics help raise awareness about the severe health risks. By speaking out against the practice and offering culturally sensitive counseling, Nigerian nurses play a pivotal role in ending FGM for future generations.
