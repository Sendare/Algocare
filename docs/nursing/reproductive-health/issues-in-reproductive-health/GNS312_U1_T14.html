Elimination of Early and Forced Marriage

Introduction and Definition

Early and forced marriage refers to any union where one or both parties have not attained the age of eighteen years, or where consent is not freely given due to coercion, threats, or immaturity. While often framed as a cultural practice, public health experts and human rights advocates categorize it as a severe violation of fundamental human rights. In Nigeria, the prevalence varies markedly by region, driven heavily by entrenched patriarchal traditions and poverty. Recognizing this practice requires looking beyond marital status to understand the systemic stripping of a child's autonomy and future potential.

Socio-Cultural and Economic Drivers

Multiple interlocking factors sustain the practice of early and forced marriage across Nigerian communities:

  • Poverty: Families facing extreme destitution sometimes view marrying off a daughter as a way to reduce household financial burdens or secure bride wealth.
  • Gender Inequality: Deeply rooted beliefs that value boys over girls lead to the undervaluation of a girl's education and future independence.
  • Religious and Cultural Misinterpretations: Distorted teachings or rigid traditional customs are frequently invoked to justify marrying girls off at puberty.
  • Insecurity and Conflict: Displacement and community instability push vulnerable families to marry off daughters early for perceived protection.

Physical and Psychological Health Consequences

Child brides face devastating health outcomes stemming from biological immaturity and forced sexual activity. Obstetric fistula remains one of the most harrowing physical consequences, caused by obstructed labor in pelvises that are not yet fully developed. Sexually transmitted infections, including HIV, occur at higher rates due to power imbalances that prevent young girls from negotiating safe sex. Psychologically, these adolescents experience high rates of depression, anxiety, and post-traumatic stress disorder, often feeling trapped with no avenue for escape.

Legal Frameworks and Child Rights Acts

Legislative measures provide the formal backbone for protecting minors, though enforcement remains inconsistent across Nigerian states.

  • Child Rights Act (2003): Sets the legal age of marriage at eighteen years, though adoption by all 36 states has faced political and cultural hurdles.
  • Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act: Criminalizes harmful traditional practices and forced marriages in the Federal Capital Territory and adopting states.
  • Customary and Sharia Law Intersections: Legal pluralism creates loopholes where religious or customary courts sometimes override federal statutory protections.

Nursing Identification and Clinical Assessment

Nurses and midwives are frequently the first health professionals to interact with victims of early and forced marriage during antenatal visits, immunizations, or emergency care. Recognizing subtle red flags is vital for timely intervention. A young girl accompanied everywhere by an older husband who answers all questions on her behalf warrants quiet, confidential assessment. Unexplained injuries, frequent urinary tract infections, signs of repeated forced sexual trauma, and extreme reluctance to speak openly should prompt suspicion of coercion.

Community Advocacy and Prevention Strategies

Sustainable elimination of early marriage requires grassroots engagement that respects local context while challenging harmful norms. Nurses play a pivotal role in community dialogues, working alongside traditional rulers, religious leaders, and youth groups. Advocacy efforts focus on keeping girls in school, as educational attainment is one of the strongest deterrents against early unions. Public awareness campaigns must emphasize the health risks of adolescent childbearing rather than merely lecturing communities, fostering local ownership of child protection initiatives.

Survivor Support, Rehabilitation, and Care

Caring for survivors of early and forced marriage demands a multi-sectoral approach that addresses medical healing, psychological trauma, and social reintegration. Healthcare facilities must offer compassionate, non-judgmental treatment for physical ailments like fistulas and sexually transmitted infections. Safe houses and temporary shelters provide immediate physical protection for girls fleeing abusive unions. Furthermore, vocational training and educational catch-up programs empower survivors to achieve financial independence, breaking the cycle of vulnerability.
