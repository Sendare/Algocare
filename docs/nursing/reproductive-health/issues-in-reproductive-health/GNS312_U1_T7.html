FGM Elimination Strategies and Policies in Nigeria

Overview of Female Genital Mutilation in Nigeria

Female Genital Mutilation involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia for non-medical reasons. In Nigeria, the practice is deeply rooted in cultural norms, tradition, and misconceptions regarding female chastity and hygiene. The prevalence varies widely across geopolitical zones, with higher rates typically recorded in the southern regions compared to the north.

  1. Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris.
  2. Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and labia minora.
  3. Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal opening through the creation of a covering seal.
  4. Type IV (Other): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, or scraping.

Legislative Frameworks and Federal Laws

Legal prohibition serves as a critical deterrent against harmful traditional practices. At the federal level in Nigeria, the landmark Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act was signed into law in 2015. This legislation explicitly prohibits female genital mutilation, prescribing jail terms and fines for perpetrators as well as anyone who incites or aids the practice.

Despite the existence of the federal VAPP Act, Nigeria operates under a federal system where state governments must domesticate the law before it becomes fully enforceable within their respective jurisdictions. This legislative gap historically slowed down coordinated nationwide prosecution of offenders.

State-Level Policies and Anti-FGM Laws

To bridge the gap left by federal legislation, numerous Nigerian states have domesticated the VAPP Act or enacted state-specific anti-FGM laws. States like Lagos, Osun, Ekiti, and Oyo have established specialized task forces and committees to monitor compliance, protect survivors, and prosecute offenders.

State-level laws empower local magistrate and high courts to try offenders, including traditional birth attendants and medical personnel who perform the procedure. However, enforcement remains uneven due to varying political will and resource allocation across different state governments.

National Action Plans and Inter-Agency Collaborations

Eliminating FGM requires coordinated multi-sectoral responses involving government ministries, international development partners, and civil society organizations. The Federal Ministry of Women Affairs, alongside the Ministry of Health, has spearheaded National Action Plans to mainstream anti-FGM strategies into public health systems and educational curricula.

Inter-agency collaborations facilitate resource pooling, joint public awareness campaigns, and standardized data collection. These partnerships ensure that legal reforms are supported by social welfare services, psychological support for survivors, and community education programs.

Community-Based Approaches and Grassroots Mobilization

Top-down legislation alone is rarely sufficient to eradicate deeply rooted traditional practices. Grassroots mobilization focuses on empowering local community members to drive behavioral change from within. Civil society organizations facilitate community dialogues, town hall meetings, and youth-led advocacy campaigns to challenge the myths supporting FGM.

Public declaration ceremonies, where communities collectively pledge to abandon the practice, have proven remarkably effective. These public commitments create social consensus, ensuring that families who abandon FGM do not face stigma or social isolation from their peers.

Role of Healthcare Professionals in Prevention

Healthcare workers occupy the front lines of FGM prevention, management, and advocacy. Nurses, midwives, and physicians must educate expectant mothers and families on the severe obstetric and gynecological complications associated with the practice, such as chronic pain, keloid formation, and obstructed labor.

A disturbing trend in some regions is the medicalization of FGM, where healthcare professionals perform the procedure under the false assumption that it is safer in a clinic. Professional nursing and medical councils in Nigeria strictly prohibit members from performing any form of FGM, classifying it as professional misconduct punishable by license revocation.

Engaging Traditional and Religious Leaders

Traditional and religious leaders hold immense moral authority in Nigerian communities. When these opinion leaders speak out against FGM, their followers listen. Many advocacy programs now focus on training emirs, obas, pastors, and imams to use their platforms to debunk religious justifications for the practice.

Misconceptions that holy texts require female cutting are actively corrected through dialogue and theological reviews. Enlisting these leaders ensures that anti-FGM messages resonate with cultural authenticity and spiritual backing.

Barriers to Policy Implementation

Despite the formulation of policies and laws, several barriers hinder effective FGM elimination in Nigeria. Deep-seated secrecy surrounding the practice makes it difficult for law enforcement agencies to detect and prosecute offenders. Practitioners often operate underground, conducting mutilations in secluded rural hideouts or at night.

Other systemic challenges include inadequate funding for enforcement agencies, poor infrastructure in rural settlements, and cultural resistance to external interference in family matters. Addressing these bottlenecks requires sustained political commitment and targeted resource distribution.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and the Path Forward

Tracking progress in the fight against FGM requires robust monitoring and evaluation frameworks. National demographic and health surveys provide statistical data on prevalence rates, shifting attitudes, and the age at which cutting occurs. This data guides policymakers in redirecting resources to high-burden areas.

The path forward depends on sustained educational investments, economic empowerment for women, and rigorous enforcement of existing legislation. Nurses and midwives must continue to document complications, report violations through appropriate channels, and advocate for total abandonment.
