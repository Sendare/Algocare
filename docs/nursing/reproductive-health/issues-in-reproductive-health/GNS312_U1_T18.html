Strategies for Elimination of Harmful Practices

Introduction to Harmful Traditional Practices in Reproductive Health

Harmful traditional practices persist across many communities, deeply rooted in historical customs and gender inequality. These behaviors directly threaten the reproductive health, bodily autonomy, and human rights of women and girls. Common examples include female genital mutilation, child marriage, and forced virginity testing. Nurses must recognize these acts not merely as cultural expressions, but as severe violations of physical integrity that cause lasting trauma.

  1. Female Genital Mutilation (FGM): Partial or total removal of external female genitalia for non-medical reasons, leading to chronic pain, obstetric fistulas, and psychological distress.
  2. Child Marriage: Formal marriage or informal union before age 18, which exposes young girls to early pregnancy risks, obstructed labor, and interrupted education.
  3. Son Preference: Cultural bias favoring male offspring, sometimes resulting in harmful neglect of female newborns or forced repeated pregnancies.

Socio-Cultural Drivers and Public Health Impacts

Cultural norms, patriarchal structures, and economic pressures powerfully sustain harmful practices. Communities often perpetuate these rituals to ensure social conformity, marriageability, or lineage purity. Understanding these underlying beliefs helps nurses approach affected populations with empathy rather than immediate judgment.

The public health fallout is immense. Survivors frequently endure chronic pelvic infections, painful intercourse, severe scar tissue formation, and profound psychological sequelae including depression and post-traumatic stress disorder. Healthcare systems carry a heavy burden managing emergency obstetric complications, neonatal morbidity, and long-term reconstructive needs tied directly to these preventable traditions.

International and National Legal Frameworks

Eradicating harmful practices requires robust legislative instruments that criminalize abusive traditions and protect vulnerable populations. International treaties such as the Maputo Protocol and the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women explicitly obligate governments to enact protective laws.

At the national level, many countries have established specific anti-FGM and child rights acts. However, passing laws alone is insufficient; enforcement remains weak in rural areas due to secrecy, corruption, and cultural complicity. Nurses must understand existing legal frameworks to guide victims appropriately, report mandatory cases safely, and advocate for policy implementation within their local health districts.

Community-Based Interventions and Grassroots Advocacy

Top-down legal mandates often fail if communities do not internalize the need for change. Sustainable elimination relies heavily on grassroots advocacy led by local actors, women's groups, and youth organizations. Public declarations of abandonment, where entire villages publicly pledge to stop practicing FGM or child marriage, have proven remarkably effective.

  • Peer Education Groups: Empowering young people to discuss reproductive rights openly without shame or cultural backlash.
  • Alternative Rite of Passage Programs: Celebrating a girl's transition into adulthood through education and mentorship instead of physical cutting.
  • Radio and Media Campaigns: Broadcasting dramatized stories in local languages to shift public perception and highlight survivor success stories.

The Role of Healthcare Professionals in Prevention and Support

Nurses occupy a frontline position in identifying, managing, and preventing harmful reproductive practices. Clinical encounters offer vital windows of opportunity to screen patients for signs of abuse, provide trauma-informed care, and educate families during antenatal or immunization visits.

Healthcare providers must maintain a non-judgmental attitude so patients feel safe disclosing trauma. Medicalization of FGM—where healthcare workers perform the procedure for supposed safety—is strictly unethical and illegal. Nurses must actively refuse any participation in these acts and report violations while maintaining patient confidentiality and trust.

Engaging Traditional and Religious Leaders as Change Agents

Traditional and religious leaders hold immense moral authority in many communities. Attempts to eradicate harmful practices without their buy-in often face fierce resistance and accusations of foreign cultural imperialism. Conversely, when respected clerics and chiefs speak out against child marriage or FGM, their followers listen and adapt.

Nurses and public health advocates should partner with these influential figures, using sacred texts and health data to demonstrate that these practices are not divinely mandated nor culturally irreplaceable. Engaging them transforms potential opposition into powerful advocacy, accelerating community-wide behavior change.

Monitoring, Evaluation, and Sustainable Elimination Strategies

Eliminating harmful practices requires systematic tracking of progress, resource allocation, and continuous evaluation of implemented programs. Ministries of health and nursing organizations must collect reliable data on prevalence rates, service utilization, and legal outcomes to measure what works.

Sustainability depends on integrating reproductive health education into school curricula, strengthening local child protection committees, and ensuring long-term financial support for grassroots NGOs. Without ongoing monitoring, communities may regress, or hidden practices may persist underground, undermining years of dedicated public health work.
