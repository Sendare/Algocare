Management of Female Genital Mutilation Complications

Clinical Overview and Classification of Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. Understanding the anatomical extent of the procedure guides clinical management. The World Health Organization classifies FGM into four distinct types:

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and appositioning the labia minora and/or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV (Other): All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterization.

Immediate and Long-Term Complications

The physical trauma of FGM triggers a cascade of acute and chronic health problems. Immediate complications typically occur during or shortly after the procedure. Chronic issues persist for years, deeply affecting physical and mental well-being.

  • Severe hemorrhage: Caused by damage to the clitoral artery or labial branches.
  • Acute infection: Resulting from unsterile cutting instruments, leading to tetanus or sepsis.
  • Chronic pain: Neuroma formation in the scar tissue can cause persistent local discomfort.
  • Urinary retention: Mechanical obstruction or severe swelling prevents normal voiding.
  • Keloid scars: Excessive fibrous tissue formation over the vulva causes chronic irritation.

Initial Assessment and History Taking

Conducting a clinical assessment for an FGM survivor requires high cultural sensitivity and a trauma-informed approach. Many patients experience profound anxiety when discussing their genital anatomy. Healthcare providers must establish trust before performing any physical examination. The history should document the specific type of FGM, age at procedure, and current symptoms such as dyspareunia, dysmenorrhea, or urinary difficulties. A gentle, non-judgmental physical examination helps identify scar tissue, cysts, or complete vaginal occlusion in infibulated women.

Surgical Management: Defibulation and Reconstruction

Surgical intervention is frequently required for women with Type III FGM (infibulation) to relieve physical obstructions. Defibulation, also known as deinfibulation, is the surgical procedure to open the fused labia. This procedure is commonly performed before marriage, during pregnancy, or at the time of labor to allow for normal childbirth, sexual intercourse, and menstruation.

Clitoral reconstruction is an elective surgical procedure aimed at restoring clitoral anatomy and sensation. It involves mobilizing the buried remnant of the clitoris and bringing it to a more anatomical position. Nurses play a crucial role in preparing the patient, managing postoperative wound care, and providing reassurance throughout the recovery process.

Psychological, Psychosexual, and Obstetric Care

The impact of FGM extends far beyond physical scars, frequently causing chronic depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, and severe sexual dysfunction. Painful intercourse, lack of lubrication, and psychological flashbacks require multidisciplinary care involving mental health specialists and specialized gynecologists.

During pregnancy and childbirth, women with FGM face higher rates of obstetric complications, including prolonged second-stage labor, obstetric lacerations, postpartum hemorrhage, and increased neonatal morbidity. Midwives and obstetric nurses must provide specialized intrapartum monitoring and avoid routine episiotomies unless specifically indicated, while ensuring timely defibulation when necessary.

Nursing Interventions and Community Prevention in Nigeria

Nurses and midwives occupy a front-line position in combating FGM through clinical care, patient advocacy, and community education. Healthcare facilities must remain safe spaces where survivors can seek help without fear of stigmatization. Advocacy involves educating families, traditional birth attendants, and community leaders about the severe health hazards of the practice.

In Nigeria, legislative measures such as the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act criminalize FGM at the federal level, with many states passing complementary laws. Nurses must understand these legal frameworks to support victims and report violations appropriately while promoting community-led eradication initiatives.
