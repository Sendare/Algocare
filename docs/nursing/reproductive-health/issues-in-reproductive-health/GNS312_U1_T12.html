Reasons for Early and Forced Marriage

Introduction and Definitions

Early marriage refers to any union where at least one party is under eighteen years of age. Forced marriage occurs when one or both parties enter the union without free and full consent, often due to coercion, threats, or manipulation by family members. While these terms frequently overlap, they stem from distinct social pressures. Recognizing this distinction helps nurses identify vulnerable young individuals during routine clinical assessments in community health centers and emergency departments.

Poverty and Economic Hardship

Severe financial distress remains a primary driver of child marriage across many vulnerable communities. Families living in extreme poverty sometimes view young daughters as economic burdens. Marrying off a girl early reduces household expenses and can bring in wealth through a bride price or dowry. In regions facing severe inflation or crop failure, parents mistakenly believe marriage will secure their child's daily survival and financial stability.

Cultural Traditions and Societal Norms

Deep-seated cultural beliefs often dictate that girls should transition directly from childhood to marriage and motherhood. These traditions pass down through generations, making community members view non-conformity as a violation of heritage. Peer pressure from community elders reinforces these practices, as families fear social isolation or ridicule if they deviate from established wedding timelines and local customs.

Gender Inequality and Patriarchal Systems

Patriarchal societies systematically undervalue the contributions of women and girls. When a community views females as inferior or subordinate, their education, health, and autonomy take secondary importance to male family members. Early marriage acts as a tool to control female sexuality, reinforce male dominance, and limit women's participation in public and economic spheres.

Protection of Family Honor and Social Status

In many conservative societies, family honor is closely tied to the perceived chastity and moral purity of its female members. Parents arrange early marriages to prevent premarital relationships that could supposedly bring shame upon the household. Additionally, marrying daughters into influential families can elevate the social standing, political alliances, and economic security of the entire extended family.

Insecurity and Humanitarian Crises

Armed conflicts, natural disasters, and displacement camps drastically increase the risk of child marriage. Disrupted social structures, lack of police protection, and the threat of sexual violence lead parents to marry off daughters as a perceived coping mechanism for safety. Families in refugee settings often believe a husband will protect a young girl from broader community dangers and physical abuse during displacement.

Lack of Education and Legal Protection

Educational attainment directly correlates with delayed marriage age; girls who remain in school are far less likely to marry early. When poverty or inadequate infrastructure prevents girls from attending secondary school, their future options narrow significantly. Furthermore, weak enforcement of statutory laws protecting children allows illegal child marriages to occur openly without legal consequences for perpetrators.

Implications for Nursing Practice and Community Advocacy

Nurses encounter the physical and psychological consequences of early marriage across antenatal, pediatric, and emergency wards. Young mothers face high risks of obstetric fistulas, severe hemorrhage, and mental health disorders like postpartum depression. Healthcare professionals must recognize red flags during clinical encounters, maintain confidentiality, provide non-judgmental care, and collaborate with social workers to protect vulnerable minors.
