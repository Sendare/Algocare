Overview of Harmful Traditional Practices

Introduction to Harmful Traditional Practices in Reproductive Health

Harmful traditional practices (HTPs) are deeply rooted behavioral patterns and customs that violate the fundamental human rights, health, and bodily integrity of individuals, particularly women and girls. In many Nigerian communities, these customs have persisted for generations because they are tied to cultural identity, social acceptance, and community survival. While some traditions promote communal well-being, HTPs inflict severe physical, psychological, and reproductive harm. Nurses encounter the consequences of these practices daily in antenatal clinics, labor wards, and emergency departments. Recognizing these customs as human rights violations rather than harmless folklore is the first step toward effective nursing intervention.

Socio-Cultural Drivers and Underlying Causes

The persistence of HTPs is sustained by a complex web of social, economic, and patriarchal factors. Understanding these drivers helps nurses approach affected patients with empathy instead of judgment. Key drivers include:

  • Patriarchy and Gender Inequality: Beliefs that place male authority over female autonomy, driving practices that control women's bodies and sexuality.
  • Poverty and Economic Dependence: Lack of financial independence forces families to rely on traditional safety nets or child marriage as economic relief.
  • Fear of Social Stigma: Communities enforce conformity through ostracization, making it difficult for individuals to abandon harmful rituals.
  • Lack of Formal Education: Low literacy rates correlate with limited awareness of human rights and the medical dangers associated with traditional rites.

Female Genital Mutilation: Types, Procedure, and Health Impact

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) involves the partial or total removal of external female genitalia for non-medical reasons. It is classified into four main types by the World Health Organization, ranging from type I (clitoridectomy) to type IV (all other harmful procedures such as piercing or scraping). The procedure is typically performed by traditional practitioners using unsterilized blades, knives, or broken glass without anesthesia.

Immediate complications include severe hemorrhage, excruciating pain, shock, and acute urine retention. Long-term consequences involve chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, keloid formation, painful sexual intercourse, and severe obstetric complications such as prolonged labor and perineal tearing during childbirth.

Child Marriage and Early Adolescent Pregnancy

Child marriage refers to any formal marriage or informal union where one or both parties are under eighteen years of age. In Nigeria, this practice is more prevalent in the northern geopolitical zones due to a combination of religious misinterpretations, poverty, and cultural norms.

When young adolescent girls become pregnant before their reproductive tracts are fully mature, their bodies face catastrophic risks. Pelvic underdevelopment leads directly to obstructed labor, which is a major precursor to obstetric fistula. Furthermore, young mothers face higher rates of eclampsia, maternal mortality, low birth weight, and neonatal death. The psychological toll includes depression, social isolation, and the abrupt termination of formal education.

Nutritional Taboos and Practices Affecting Pregnant Women

Many traditional societies enforce dietary restrictions on pregnant and lactating women based on unfounded myths. Common taboos forbid pregnant women from consuming protein-rich foods such as eggs, meat, snails, and certain fish. Communities often claim these foods cause babies to be born bald, drool excessively, or develop greedy personalities.

In reality, these nutritional taboos deprive mothers and developing fetuses of vital micronutrients and proteins. This direct deprivation contributes significantly to maternal anemia, intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, and poor postpartum recovery. Nurses must actively screen for these dietary restrictions during antenatal visits and provide culturally sensitive nutrition education.

Widowhood Rites and Reproductive Rights Violations

Widowhood rites are harsh cultural practices imposed on grieving women upon the death of their husbands. These rituals are built on the discriminatory assumption that the wife is responsible for her husband's death. Practices include forcing the widow to drink water used to wash the corpse, shaving her hair forcefully, sleeping on the bare floor, and isolating her for weeks.

From a reproductive health standpoint, these rites violate bodily autonomy and expose women to severe infections and psychological trauma. Furthermore, dispossession of property leaves widows and their children destitute, heightening their vulnerability to exploitation, poverty, and compromised health outcomes.

Clinical Assessment and Identification of Affected Patients

Identifying victims of HTPs requires sharp clinical acumen and a non-judgmental approach, as patients rarely report these practices voluntarily due to fear and shame. Nurses must look for subtle physical cues during routine examinations. For instance, pelvic examinations may reveal scar tissue, clitoral absence, or vaginal stenosis indicative of FGM.

During history taking, nurses should assess for unexplained chronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, prolonged labor history, or signs of severe psychological distress. Establishing a trusting, confidential nurse-patient relationship is essential. Asking open-ended questions about cultural practices helps patients feel safe enough to disclose their experiences.

Nursing Management, Psychological Support, and Rehabilitation

Management of individuals affected by HTPs goes beyond physical wound care; it requires holistic nursing rehabilitation. Physical care involves treating infections, managing chronic pain, and preparing patients for specialized surgical interventions such as deinfibulation or fistula repair.

Psychological support is equally critical. Nurses must provide trauma-informed care, actively listening without judgment and connecting patients with professional counselors or social workers. Rebuilding self-esteem, empowering women through health education, and involving supportive family or community leaders help patients reintegrate and regain control over their reproductive health.

Legal Frameworks, Advocacy, and Prevention Strategies in Nigeria

Combating HTPs requires robust legislative action alongside grassroots community engagement. At the national level, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act provides a vital legal framework criminalizing harmful practices such as FGM, forced isolation of widows, and harmful traditional practices. Many states have domesticated this act, though enforcement remains inconsistent.

Nurses play a powerful role in advocacy and prevention. By partnering with local chiefs, religious leaders, and women's groups, nurses can help demystify health myths and promote alternative rites of passage that preserve culture without causing harm. Community education and empowering young girls through schooling remain the most effective long-term deterrents.
