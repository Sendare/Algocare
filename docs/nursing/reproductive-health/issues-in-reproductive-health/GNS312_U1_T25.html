Sexual Dysfunctions in Females

Introduction and the Female Sexual Response Cycle

Female Sexual Dysfunction (FSD) encompasses a persistent disturbance in a woman's sexual desire, arousal, orgasm, or pain associated with sexual activity, causing personal distress. Understanding normal physiology is essential before identifying pathology. Masters and Johnson outlined the physiological stages of sexual response:

  • Desire: Psychological interest in sexual activity driven by hormones and thoughts.
  • Arousal: Vasocongestion of pelvic tissues, vaginal lubrication, and clitoral engorgement.
  • Orgasm: Peak rhythmic contractions of the pelvic floor muscles accompanied by intense pleasure.
  • Resolution: Return of the genital organs to their unaroused state accompanied by general relaxation.

Disruptions anywhere along this continuous cycle result in specific clinical dysfunctions.

Classifications and Types of Female Sexual Dysfunctions

Clinical presentations of FSD are categorized into four primary diagnostic domains based on the specific phase of the response cycle affected:

  1. Female Sexual Interest/Arousal Disorder (FSIAD): Absence or reduced frequency of sexual thoughts, receptivity, and physical arousal cues.
  2. Female Orgasmic Disorder: Marked delay, infrequency, or absence of orgasm, or substantially reduced intensity of orgasmic sensations.
  3. Genito-Pelvic Pain/Penetration Disorder (GPPPD): Encompasses dyspareunia (pain during intercourse), vaginismus (involuntary pelvic floor muscle contraction), and fear of pain or penetration.
  4. Substance/Medication-Induced Sexual Dysfunction: Symptoms directly attributable to pharmacological agents like Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) or antihypertensives.

Etiology and Predisposing Risk Factors

The origins of sexual dysfunction are rarely singular; they involve a complex interplay of biological, psychological, and sociocultural variables.

  • Biological Factors: Hormonal deficiencies (estrogen drops during menopause), chronic illnesses (diabetes, cardiovascular disease), neurological disorders, and pelvic surgeries.
  • Psychological Factors: Depression, anxiety, history of sexual trauma, body image disturbances, and performance anxiety.
  • Sociocultural Factors: Cultural taboos, religious beliefs regarding sex as purely reproductive, lack of sexual education, and relationship conflicts or lack of trust with a partner.

Nursing Assessment and History Taking

Comprehensive assessment requires a non-judgmental, empathetic, and culturally sensitive approach. Nurses must establish rapport to overcome the social stigma attached to discussing sexual health in Nigeria.

The interview should cover:

  • Onset, duration, and course of the dysfunction (lifelong versus acquired).
  • Situational factors (occurs with specific partners or all contexts).
  • Current medication profiles and medical history.
  • Quality of the partner relationship and recent life stressors.

Privacy and clear communication validate the patient's concerns and lay the foundation for effective care.

Diagnostic Evaluation and Screening Tools

Diagnostic evaluation aims to rule out organic causes and quantify the severity of the dysfunction. Initial evaluation includes a thorough physical and pelvic examination to assess for anatomical abnormalities, signs of estrogen deprivation, or localized tenderness.

Laboratory tests often include fasting blood glucose, lipid profiles, and hormone panels (thyroid-stimulating hormone, free testosterone, and prolactin levels). Validated self-report screening tools, such as the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI), assist clinicians in objectively measuring domains of desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction, and pain.

Nursing Management and Therapeutic Interventions

Interventions for FSD require a multidisciplinary approach tailored to the underlying etiology. Nursing care centers on patient education, coordination of care, and supportive therapies.

  • Pharmacological management: Managing underlying chronic conditions, adjusting offending medications, or utilizing topical estrogen creams for atrophic vaginitis. Lubricants and moisturizers are recommended for dyspareunia.
  • Physical interventions: Pelvic floor physical therapy, biofeedback, and vaginal dilators for GPPPD.
  • Referrals: Directing patients to sex therapists, gynecologists, or mental health professionals when psychological or complex anatomical issues predominate.

Patient Counseling and Psychosocial Support

Because sexual dysfunction deeply affects self-esteem, mental well-being, and marital harmony, psychosocial support is paramount. Nurses provide supportive counseling by validating feelings and dispelling myths.

Couples therapy is often integrated to improve communication patterns, encourage non-demand sensate focus exercises, and reduce performance anxiety. Normalizing the condition encourages adherence to treatment plans and fosters a healthier view of intimacy. Involving partners in education sessions bridges communication gaps and supports long-term recovery.
