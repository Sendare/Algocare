Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

Definition and Types of Female Genital Mutilation

Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) encompasses all procedures involving partial or total removal of the external female genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. The World Health Organization categorizes these procedures into four distinct types:

  • Type I (Clitoridectomy): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  • Type II (Excision): Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  • Type III (Infibulation): Narrowing of the vaginal orifice with creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the labia minora or majora, with or without excision of the clitoris.
  • Type IV: All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, such as pricking, piercing, incising, scraping, and cauterizing.

Socio-Cultural Drivers in Nigeria

In many Nigerian communities, the practice of FGM persists due to deeply entrenched cultural norms, beliefs about female purity, and societal expectations. Communities often view the procedure as a necessary rite of passage into womanhood and a way to control female sexuality, ensuring premarital virginity and marital fidelity. Economic factors also play a role, as traditional circumcisers rely on the practice for income and social standing. Peer pressure among women heavily reinforces the continuity of the tradition, since uncircumcised females may face stigmatization, mockery, or rejection in marriage markets.

Physical Health Complications

The physical consequences of FGM vary depending on the type performed, ranging from immediate surgical trauma to chronic, lifelong medical conditions.

TimelineCommon Complications
ImmediateSevere hemorrhage, acute pain, shock, urine retention, local infection
Long-termChronic pelvic pain, recurrent urinary tract infections, keloid formation, dyspareunia
ObstetricProlonged labor, obstetric fistula, perineal tearing, increased neonatal mortality

Infibulation carries the highest burden of long-term morbidity because scar tissue constantly obstructs normal urinary and reproductive tract drainage.

Psychological and Psychosexual Impacts

Beyond physical scars, survivors frequently experience profound psychological distress and psychosexual dysfunction. Anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) are common among women who recall the trauma of the procedure, especially when performed forcibly during childhood or adolescence. Psychosexually, the removal of sensitive genital tissue leads to reduced sexual responsiveness, anorgasmia, and severe pain during intercourse. These challenges frequently strain marital relationships and diminish the overall quality of life for affected women.

Nursing Assessment and Clinical Identification

Nurses play a vital role in identifying survivors during routine gynecological visits, antenatal care, or pediatric assessments. Clinical identification requires a sensitive, non-judgmental approach during physical examinations, maintaining patient privacy and building trust. During history-taking, nurses should screen for unexplained urinary symptoms, chronic pain, or difficulties during past deliveries. When inspecting the perineum, the nurse documents the absence or alteration of anatomical structures such as the clitoris or labia, noting any scar tissue or signs of infibulation without expressing shock or disgust.

Medical Management and Defimaxillary Procedures

Medical management focuses on treating immediate complications like hemorrhage and infection, as well as managing chronic conditions. For women with Type III FGM (infibulation), surgical intervention known as defibulation or de-infibulation may be necessary, particularly before childbirth or to relieve urinary and menstrual obstructions. This minor surgical procedure cuts open the fused scar tissue to restore normal anatomy and access to the vagina. Post-procedure care involves wound hygiene, pain management, and psychological counseling to help the patient adjust to the physical changes.

Legal Frameworks and the Nurse's Advocacy Role

In Nigeria, the Violence Against Persons Prohibition (VAPP) Act explicitly prohibits FGM, backed by various state-level laws carrying penalties of imprisonment and fines for perpetrators and those who abet the practice. Despite these laws, enforcement remains weak due to cultural secrecy and community complicity. Nurses occupy a unique position of trust within communities, making them frontline advocates for eradication. Through community health education, school outreach, and individual patient counseling, nurses challenge cultural myths, educate families on health harms, and support victims of abuse.
