Maternal Distress

Understanding Maternal Distress

Maternal distress refers to the extreme physical and psychological exhaustion, anxiety, and physiological strain a woman experiences during pregnancy, labour, or the immediate postpartum period. Labour places massive metabolic demands on the body, shifting blood flow, increasing oxygen consumption, and triggering intense hormonal fluctuations. When coping mechanisms fail or complications arise, the mother enters a state of distress that threatens both her well-being and the safety of the foetus. In Nigerian clinical practice, recognizing this state early prevents prolonged labour and severe birth trauma.

Triggers and Risk Factors

  • Prolonged Labour: Stretched uterine muscles lose efficiency, tiring the mother out.
  • Dehydration and Ketosis: Inadequate fluid intake during long labour hours depletes energy stores.
  • Severe Pain: Unmanaged discomfort accelerates catecholamine release, reducing uterine blood flow.
  • Pre-existing Anxiety: Fear and lack of continuous labor support heighten emotional panic.

Clinical Signs and Symptoms

Recognizing maternal distress requires observing both visible behaviour and vital sign alterations. Physically, the woman may display tachycardia, tachypnoea, diaphoresis, and exhaustion so severe she struggles to follow simple instructions. Psychologically, verbal cues shift from coping statements to expressions of utter hopelessness, panic, or fear of dying. A common clinical mistake is dismissing these emotional outbursts as mere low pain tolerance rather than recognizing a failing physiological reserve.

Assessment and Monitoring

Comprehensive assessment bridges physical observations with standardized tools. The partograph remains an essential instrument in Nigerian maternity units to track cervical dilation, contraction frequency, and maternal pulse alongside foetal heart rate. Midwives must regularly evaluate hydration status, urine output via catheterization or monitoring voids, and pain scores. Continuous communication with the mother yields vital clues about her coping ability that monitors alone might miss.

Nursing Interventions and Management

Immediate nursing care focuses on restoring energy, reducing anxiety, and optimizing physiological parameters. Position changes, such as lateral recumbent positioning, relieve vena caval compression and improve placental perfusion. Hydration must be managed carefully through oral fluids if permitted or intravenous normal saline or Ringer's lactate to correct ketosis. Providing a calm environment, verbal reassurance, and involving a trusted birth companion drastically lower stress hormones.

Medical and Emergency Care

When supportive nursing measures fail to reverse maternal distress, medical intervention becomes mandatory. Obstetricians evaluate the underlying cause, which may require augmenting slow contractions with oxytocin if cephalopelvic disproportion is ruled out, or expediting delivery via vacuum extraction, forceps, or emergency caesarean section. Pain management is escalated using regional anaesthesia like epidurals if available and appropriate. Close multidisciplinary collaboration between midwives, obstetricians, and anaesthetists safeguards maternal survival.
