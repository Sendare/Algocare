Sickle Cell Disease in Pregnancy

Pathophysiology and Pregnancy Impact

Sickle cell disease is a genetic blood disorder caused by a mutation in the beta-globin gene, resulting in abnormal hemoglobin S instead of hemoglobin A. Under conditions of low oxygen, dehydration, or infection, these red blood cells polymerize, distorting into rigid crescent shapes. These deformed cells cause vaso-occlusion in microvessels, leading to ischemia, severe pain, and tissue infarction. Pregnancy naturally creates a hypercoagulable state and increases oxygen demand, which dramatically raises the vulnerability to sickling episodes. The physiological rise in blood volume further adds hemodynamic stress to an already compromised cardiovascular system in these mothers.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Maternal risks during pregnancy include frequent vaso-occlusive crises, acute chest syndrome, urinary tract infections, pre-eclampsia, and severe anemia. Acute chest syndrome is a medical emergency characterized by chest pain, fever, and new infiltrates on a chest radiograph, and it carries high maternal mortality. Fetal complications stem from chronic uteroplacental insufficiency caused by microvascular infarctions in the placenta. These complications manifest as intrauterine growth restriction, premature delivery, low birth weight, and increased perinatal mortality rates.

Antenatal Assessment and Monitoring

Comprehensive antenatal care requires a multidisciplinary team including obstetricians, hematologists, and midwives. Regular clinic visits allow for close monitoring of maternal hemoglobin levels, renal function, and signs of infection. Serial ultrasound scans are mandatory to track fetal growth and assess amniotic fluid volume starting in the second trimester. Doppler ultrasound of the middle cerebral artery can also help evaluate fetal well-being where available.

Pharmacological Management and Prevention

Routine prophylaxis forms the backbone of antenatal management to prevent crises and complications.

  • Folic acid: High-dose supplementation is prescribed daily to support active erythropoiesis and prevent megaloblastic anemia.
  • Malaria prophylaxis: Intermittent preventive treatment and insecticide-treated nets are vital in malaria-endemic regions like Nigeria to prevent severe hemolysis.
  • Antibiotics: Prompt treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria and urinary tract infections prevents pyelonephritis and subsequent crisis triggers.
  • Blood transfusions: Prophylactic exchange transfusions may be reserved for women with a severe clinical history or recurrent crises.

Intrapartum Care and Crisis Management

Labour and delivery demand meticulous monitoring to avoid hypoxia, dehydration, and acidosis. Continuous electronic fetal monitoring is standard practice due to the high risk of intrapartum fetal distress. Oxygen therapy, aggressive intravenous hydration, and adequate pain relief are immediately initiated if a vaso-occlusive crisis occurs during labour. Vaginal delivery is preferred unless standard obstetric indications demand a Caesarean section, as surgery carries higher risks of infection and thrombosis.

Postpartum Care and Contraception

The postpartum period remains a high-risk window for thromboembolic events and delayed sickle cell crises. Nurses must monitor lochia, vital signs, and hemoglobin levels closely while ensuring early postpartum mobilization. Contraceptive counseling must be initiated before hospital discharge because closely spaced pregnancies pose severe health risks. Progestin-only pills, barrier methods, or intrauterine devices are generally safe and effective choices, whereas high-dose estrogen contraceptives are avoided due to increased thrombosis risks.
