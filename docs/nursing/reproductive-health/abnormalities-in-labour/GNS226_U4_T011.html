Lacerations

Introduction and Definition of Genital and Perineal Lacerations

A laceration is a tear of the skin, mucous membranes, or underlying muscles in the genital tract during childbirth. These injuries most commonly affect the perineum, which is the muscular area between the vaginal opening and the anus. Lacerations can also occur in the labia, clitoris, urethra, and cervix. Unlike a surgical episiotomy—which is a clean, intentional cut made with scissors—a laceration has jagged edges and occurs spontaneously due to the stretching forces of the foetal head passing through the birth canal.

Anatomy of the Female Perineum and Pelvic Floor

The female pelvic floor provides crucial support to the pelvic organs and maintains continence. The perineal body acts as a central tendon where multiple muscles converge, including the bulbospongiosus, superficial transverse perineal, and the external anal sphincter. During childbirth, these muscular structures must stretch significantly to accommodate the baby. Understanding this anatomy helps midwives recognize which tissue layers are involved when a tear occurs.

Classification and Degrees of Perineal Tears

Perineal tears are graded into four degrees based on the depth of tissue involvement:

  1. First-degree: Injury to perineal skin and vaginal mucosa only.
  2. Second-degree: Injury to perineal muscles as well as skin, without involving the anal sphincter.
  3. Third-degree: Injury to the perineal structures involving the anal sphincter complex (subdivided into 3a, 3b, and 3c).
  4. Fourth-degree: Injury to the perineal muscles, anal sphincter, and the rectal mucosa, exposing the lumen of the rectum.

Etiology and Risk Factors During Labour

  • Foetal macrosomia: Babies weighing over 4 kilograms stretch perineal tissues beyond normal limits.
  • Rapid expulsion: Uncontrolled second stage pushing does not allow tissues time to stretch gradually.
  • Operative vaginal delivery: The use of forceps or vacuum extractors increases mechanical trauma.
  • Position in labour: Lithotomy and semi-recumbent positions can put excessive direct pressure on the perineum.
  • Nulliparity: First-time mothers have rigid, unyielding tissues compared to multiparous women.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis of Lacerations

Following delivery of the placenta, a systematic inspection of the lower genital tract is mandatory for every woman who had a vaginal birth. The midwife must position the client comfortably, ensure adequate lighting, and use sterile gauze to gently wipe away lochia for clear visualization. Every structure—labia, vagina, cervix, and perineum—is inspected systematically. A digital rectal examination is essential to rule out occult third- or fourth-degree tears by assessing the tone of the anal sphincter.

Immediate Midwifery Management and Repair

Once a tear is identified and classified, repair should be undertaken promptly to minimize blood loss and reduce infection risks. Local infiltration with a local anaesthetic agent provides adequate pain control during suturing. Anatomical alignment is critical: deeper muscle layers and the sphincter must be approximated accurately before closing the vaginal mucosa and skin. Using synthetic absorbable suture materials prevents tissue reaction and promotes rapid healing.

Postoperative Care and Pain Management

Good hygiene and effective pain relief are the cornerstones of postpartum recovery following a perineal repair. Cold packs applied to the perineum during the first twenty-four hours reduce localized swelling and numb nerve endings. Regular oral analgesics such as paracetamol or ibuprofen manage discomfort effectively. Midwives instruct women to clean the area from front to back after every toilet use and to change maternity pads frequently to prevent wound contamination.

Potential Complications and Long-term Sequelae

Unrecognized or poorly healed lacerations can lead to significant short-term problems like wound breakdown, hematoma formation, and severe localized infection. Over the long term, women may experience dyspareunia, pelvic organ prolapse, or chronic urinary and fecal incontinence. These persistent physical issues severely impact emotional well-being and daily functioning, highlighting why meticulous initial repair and supportive follow-up care remain vital in midwifery practice.
