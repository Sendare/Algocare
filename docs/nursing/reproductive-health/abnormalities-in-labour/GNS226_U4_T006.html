Diabetes Mellitus in Pregnancy

Pathophysiology and Classification

Pregnancy is naturally a diabetogenic state characterized by progressive insulin resistance, especially in the second and third trimesters. Placental hormones such as human placental lactogen, cortisol, and progesterone antagonize maternal insulin to ensure a continuous supply of glucose for the developing fetus. When maternal pancreatic beta-cells fail to compensate for this increased demand, hyperglycemia ensues.

Classification separates the condition into two distinct clinical groups:

  • Pregestational Diabetes Mellitus (PGDM): Women with diagnosed type 1 or type 2 diabetes who become pregnant or are diagnosed during the first trimester.
  • Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM): Carbohydrate intolerance of varying severity first recognized during pregnancy, typically manifesting after the 20th week of gestation.

Screening and Diagnosis

Early detection of abnormal glucose metabolism prevents adverse pregnancy outcomes. Universal screening protocols typically take place between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation, utilizing standard oral glucose tolerance tests.

  1. One-Step Approach: A 75-gram oral glucose tolerance test is administered after an overnight fast. Fasting, one-hour, and two-hour plasma glucose thresholds determine the diagnosis.
  2. Two-Step Approach: A 50-gram non-fasting glucose challenge test is performed first. If blood glucose exceeds a specific threshold, a diagnostic 100-gram three-hour oral glucose tolerance test follows.

High-risk women, including those with a previous history of GDM, a strong family history of diabetes, or obesity, undergo screening at their initial antenatal booking visit.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Poorly controlled maternal blood glucose levels create a hostile intrauterine environment, directly impacting both mother and fetus. High glucose crosses the placenta freely, stimulating fetal pancreatic insulin production. Because insulin is a potent growth hormone, fetal hyperinsulinemia drives excessive fat deposition and organomegaly.

Medical and Glycemic Management

Achieving normoglycemia requires a multi-pronged approach combining medical nutrition therapy, structured physical activity, self-monitoring of blood glucose, and pharmacotherapy when lifestyle measures fail.

Dietary counseling focuses on controlled carbohydrate intake distributed across three meals and multiple snacks to prevent postprandial spikes and nocturnal hypoglycemia. Regular capillary blood glucose checks guide daily management, with targets generally maintained below specific fasting and postprandial thresholds.

When blood glucose goals are not met through diet and exercise alone, insulin remains the gold standard therapeutic agent because it does not cross the placenta in significant amounts. While certain oral hypoglycemic agents are increasingly utilized, traditional management often favors insulin regimens tailored to individual pharmacokinetic profiles.

Intrapartum Care and Labour Management

Careful planning during the intrapartum period minimises birth trauma and neonatal metabolic crises. Timing of delivery depends on glycemic control and fetal well-being; well-managed pregnancies may proceed to spontaneous onset near term, whereas complicated cases require planned induction or cesarean delivery between 37 and 39 weeks.

During active labor, meticulous glucose monitoring is essential. Intravenous insulin and glucose infusions are often titrated hourly to maintain maternal blood glucose within a tight physiological range, preventing maternal hyperglycemia that could otherwise induce severe neonatal hypoglycemia immediately after birth.

Postpartum and Neonatal Care

Delivery of the placenta immediately removes the primary source of anti-insulin hormones, leading to a dramatic drop in insulin requirements. For women with gestational diabetes, insulin or oral agents are usually discontinued immediately post-delivery.

Neonates born to mothers with diabetes require immediate assessment for birth injuries, polycythemia, hyperbilirubinemia, and respiratory distress syndrome. Capillary blood glucose checks are performed routinely in the immediate hours following birth to catch asymptomatic hypoglycemia early. Furthermore, women with a history of gestational diabetes require a fasting blood glucose test or oral glucose tolerance test at 6 weeks postpartum to reclassify their glucose status.

Nursing Interventions and Education

Nurses play a vital role in coordinating care, interpreting glucose logs, and delivering practical education. Teaching self-injection techniques, proper glucometer calibration, and recognition of hypo- and hyperglycemia symptoms empowers patients to manage their daily routines safely.

Nutritional guidance emphasizes portion control and label reading. Additionally, nurses provide emotional support, addressing the anxiety often associated with complex medication regimens and fetal surveillance tests. Long-term counseling highlights lifestyle modifications, such as regular exercise and healthy dietary habits, to lower the risk of recurrent gestational diabetes in future pregnancies and type 2 diabetes later in life.
