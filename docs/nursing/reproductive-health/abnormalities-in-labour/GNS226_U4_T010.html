Prolonged Labour

Definition and Normal Partographic Progress

Prolonged labour, often termed obstructed labour when mechanical failure is present, describes cervical dilation and fetal descent that fails to progress at a normal rate. In primigravidae, the active phase of labour should progress at a rate of at least 1 centimeter per hour. The WHO partograph provides a visual tool to monitor this progression using an alert line and an action line. When the patient's plotted cervical dilation crosses the alert line into the danger zone, vigilance must increase. Crossing the action line, typically set four hours to the right of the alert line, mandates urgent clinical intervention and transfer from primary health clinics to comprehensive emergency obstetric care facilities.

Pathophysiology and Underlying Causes

The progression of labour relies on the three Ps: power, passenger, and passage. Dysfunction in any of these components results in prolonged labour.

  • Power: Inadequate or uncoordinated uterine contractions fail to dilate the cervix effectively.
  • Passenger: Fetal macrosomia, malpresentations such as brow or face presentation, and malpositions like persistent occipitoposterior impede descent.
  • Passage: Cephalopelvic disproportion arises from a contracted maternal pelvis, often linked to childhood malnutrition or early adolescent pregnancy in resource-constrained settings.

Clinical Assessment and Diagnosis in Nigerian Practice

Assessing a woman with suspected prolonged labour in a Nigerian healthcare facility demands a systematic approach. The nurse must conduct a thorough abdominal examination to check lie, presentation, fetal head engagement, and contraction frequency. Vaginal examination evaluates cervical effacement, dilation, membrane status, and pelvic architecture. Vital signs must be monitored closely to detect maternal exhaustion, dehydration, or sepsis early. Ascertaining the exact duration of labour is vital, though often challenging due to inaccurate recall by patients who labour at home or in spiritual houses before arriving at the hospital.

Maternal and Fetal Complications

Extended duration of labour places both mother and baby at severe risk if left unmanaged. Maternal complications include uterine rupture, postpartum hemorrhage, obstetric fistula from prolonged soft tissue compression, and severe systemic infection or sepsis. Fetal distress frequently develops due to reduced uteroplacental perfusion during prolonged contractions, leading to hypoxia, meconium aspiration syndrome, low Apgar scores, and intrauterine or early neonatal death. Recognizing these risks drives the urgency of prompt nursing intervention.

Immediate Nursing Care and Supportive Management

Supportive care stabilizes the mother while diagnostic evaluations take place. Establish reliable intravenous access immediately to correct dehydration and electrolyte imbalances with warm crystalloid fluids. Maintain strict fluid balance charting and monitor urine output via an indwelling catheter to watch for signs of renal impairment or bladder trauma. Administer prescribed analgesia to manage pain and reduce maternal stress. Continuous psychological support, clear explanations of procedures, and encouraging a birth companion help alleviate anxiety for the patient and her family.

Medical and Surgical Interventions

Management shifts from supportive to definitive treatment depending on the underlying cause identified during assessment. If uterine inertia is the primary issue and cephalopelvic disproportion is ruled out, artificial rupture of membranes or an oxytocin infusion may be initiated under strict fetal heart rate monitoring. When mechanical obstruction is confirmed, oxytocin is strictly contraindicated, and the patient must be prepared for an emergency caesarean section. Assisted vaginal delivery using vacuum extractors or forceps may be performed if the fetal head is low in the pelvis and conditions are favorable.

Prevention Strategies and Partograph Utilization

Preventing prolonged labour requires community mobilization, proper antenatal education, and consistent clinical practices. Empowering pregnant women to seek hospital care early rather than relying on unassisted home births reduces delayed presentations. Healthcare providers must receive regular training on accurate partograph completion to identify abnormal labour patterns before complications arise. Strengthening referral networks between primary health centers and tertiary hospitals ensures rapid transport when complications occur.
